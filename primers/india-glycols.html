00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

India Glycols Limited (IGL) is not the petrochemical company its name suggests. It is a bio-refinery: it takes fermentable sugar — chiefly molasses (the sticky by-product of sugar-making) and increasingly grain — ferments it into ethanol, and then turns that renewable ethanol into a fan of very different products. From one column of sugar it makes green glycols and specialty chemicals (the historic core), fuel-grade ethanol for petrol blending, potable spirits (country liquor and premium IMFL), pharma actives and natural gums, and industrial gases. The company was founded by the Delhi-based Bhartia family and runs its flagship integrated complex at Kashipur, Uttarakhand.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Its founding claim to fame is a green-chemistry first. In 1989 IGL became, by its own account, the first company in the world to make mono-ethylene glycol (MEG) from a renewable feedstock — molasses — rather than from petroleum. That bio-route to ethylene oxide and glycols is still the spine of the business; but, as we'll see, the punchline is that IGL barely sells commodity MEG any more.
  • The headline revenue number is a trap. IGL's gross "revenue from operations" was roughly ₹9,827 Cr in FY26, but a huge slice of that is state excise duty on liquor and low-value pass-through fuel-ethanol sales. The number that reflects the actual business is net revenue of ~₹4,211 Cr, on which it earned ~₹654 Cr of EBITDA and ~₹293 Cr of net profit. Always read this company on net revenue.
  • It is about to become three companies. On 17 July 2026 the NCLT sanctioned a demerger that carves IGL into three separately listed entities — a chemicals company (retained IGL), a spirits-and-bio-fuel company (IGL Spirits), and a bio-pharma company (Ennature Bio Pharma). Understanding IGL today means understanding what each of those three pieces is worth on its own.
In plain terms

Start with a vat of fermenting sugar. That single stream is India Glycols' raw material, and the whole company is a set of decisions about what to do with it. Distil it and blend it into petrol, and you have a low-margin fuel business riding the government's ethanol-blending push. Distil it further, age it and bottle it, and you have a high-margin liquor business. React it into ethylene oxide and you have a specialty-chemicals business selling to L'Oréal, Dow and BASF. Extract and refine plant molecules alongside, and you have a small pharma-and-gums business. For thirty years these sat inside one listed shell. The defining event of 2026 is the decision to stop running them as one company and let the market price each on its own merits.

01

Bio vs petro: how you make a glycol from sugar

No chemistry degree needed — just the one process that makes this company unusual.

To understand India Glycols you need exactly one idea: there are two roads to the same molecule, and IGL took the road no one else in India took. Ethylene glycol (MEG) is the workhorse chemical behind polyester fibre, PET bottles, antifreeze and a whole family of derivatives. Almost everyone makes it from crude oil or natural gas. IGL makes it from molasses.

Two roads to the same glycol — the fork that defines the company
The green road · IGL
Molasses → ethanol → bio-ethylene → bio-ethylene oxide → glycols
Renewable sugar, fermented and dehydrated. IGL's founding process.
vs
The petro road · everyone else
Naphtha / gas → ethylene → ethylene oxide → glycols
Fossil feedstock, cracked. The route used by Reliance and global majors.
Both roads arrive at the same ethylene oxide (EO) — the reactive intermediate — and the same glycols. The difference is the feedstock: one starts in a sugar mill, the other in an oil refinery. That single choice explains IGL's identity, its cost curve and its strategic pivot.

The key stop on both roads is ethylene oxide (EO) — a highly reactive gas that is the branch-point of the whole business. Once you have EO, you can react it into MEG (and its cousins DEG, TEG), or into higher-value derivatives: glycol ethers (solvents), ethoxylates and surfactants (the active ingredients in detergents, shampoos and agrochemicals), and polyethylene glycols (PEGs, used in pharma and cosmetics). IGL runs one of India's larger EO complexes at Kashipur, and the strategic story of the last decade is the deliberate march away from selling plain MEG and toward selling these EO derivatives, where a maker's formulation skill and customer relationships earn a fatter, steadier margin.

The one dependency that shapes everything: it starts in a farm field

A petrochemical MEG maker's fate is tied to crude oil and gas. India Glycols' fate is tied to the sugar and grain harvest. Its raw material is molasses and grain, so its cost base moves with cane crops, monsoon, minimum support prices, and government rules on where molasses and ethanol may be diverted. That is a completely different risk profile from a fossil-fed rival — sometimes an advantage (a renewable, lower-carbon feedstock that customers increasingly want), sometimes a curse (when petro-feedstock is dirt cheap and sugar is dear, the green route is the expensive way to make a commodity). Hold that tension; it is the engine of the whole investment case.

02

A bio-refinery in four parts — what IGL actually sells

One feedstock, four very different businesses — with wildly different margins.

Because everything starts from the same fermentable sugar, India Glycols looks at first glance like one business. It is not — it is four, and they sit at opposite ends of the value spectrum. In FY26 the four reporting lines split net revenue almost evenly, but their profitability could hardly be more different. This one comparison is the most important picture in the primer.

Look at those two charts together and the company's real shape appears. On revenue, Bio-Fuel is the biggest line; on profit, it is nearly the smallest. Potable Spirits is the mirror image — a mid-sized revenue line that throws off the largest slug of segment EBIT because its margin (21.4%) dwarfs fuel-ethanol's (7.8%). The Chemicals segment sits in between: mid-teens on revenue rank, second on profit, and — crucially — its margin is rising even as its revenue dipped, because IGL keeps swapping commodity glycol volume for higher-value specialties. That mix-shift is the single most important trend inside the chemicals business, and we return to it in section 07.

The uneven-margins point most first-timers miss

If you only glance at the revenue split, you'd call India Glycols a fuel-ethanol company with a chemicals and liquor side. If you look at where the profit is made, you'd call it a spirits-and-specialty-chemicals company with a low-margin fuel volume attached. Both descriptions use the same numbers; they just weight revenue versus profit. This is exactly why the company is splitting itself up — a single share price cannot fairly value a 21%-margin premium-liquor business, an 8%-margin commodity-fuel business, and a mid-teens specialty-chemicals business all at once. Different buyers want different pieces.

03

The chemicals engine: the business that keeps the IGL name

Green glycols, EO derivatives, guar and gases — the piece that stays behind after the split.

After the demerger, the entity that keeps the India Glycols name is the Bio-based Specialities & Performance Chemicals (BSPC) business, plus industrial gases. This is the historic heart — the EO-and-glycols complex at Kashipur — but reshaped over a decade from a commodity-MEG maker into a specialties house. In FY26 it did roughly ₹1,203 Cr of net revenue at an 11.7% EBIT margin, and — tellingly — that margin rose about 239 basis points even though revenue slipped, the signature of a deliberate trade-down in volume for a trade-up in value.

What the chemicals segment actually makes

  • Green glycols (MEG, DEG, TEG) — the renewable-route glycols that gave IGL its name and its sustainability pitch. Increasingly sold where a customer specifically wants a bio-based, lower-carbon glycol (personal care, pharma, specialty polyester) rather than as bulk commodity MEG, where petro competition is brutal.
  • Glycol ethers & acetates — solvents used in paints, coatings, inks and cleaners. A steady industrial specialty line.
  • Ethoxylates & surfactants — the EO-derivative performance chemicals that are the active ingredients in detergents, shampoos, cosmetics, agrochemical formulations and textile processing. This is the growth-and-margin core of the segment, with blue-chip customers reportedly including L'Oréal, Dow and BASF.
  • Guar gum & derivatives — natural gums refined from the guar bean, used as thickeners and in oilfield fracturing fluids. A specialty niche that swings with oil-and-gas drilling activity.
  • Oilfield & carbon-smart chemicals — bespoke performance chemistries for the energy and industrial markets, part of the push up the value ladder.
  • Industrial gases — a small, steady utility-like line that stays with the retained IGL.

Why a "green" label is a real commercial asset here, not just marketing

Global consumer and personal-care giants have public commitments to cut the carbon footprint of their ingredients. A surfactant or glycol made from renewable molasses carries a lower embedded-carbon story than the identical molecule cracked from naphtha — and for a brand chasing a sustainability target, that is worth paying up for and designing in. India Glycols' renewable route, once a quirky cost disadvantage in a cheap-oil world, is turning into a genuine selling point precisely with the customers who pay the best margins. That is the strategic bet inside the retained chemicals company: that "bio-based" moves from a cost to a premium.

04

Spirits & bio-fuel: the two exits for a litre of alcohol

The same still feeds a premium-liquor business and a government-driven fuel business — at very different margins.

The demerger bundles Potable Spirits and Bio-Fuel together into one new listed company, IGL Spirits Limited — and that pairing makes sense, because both start from the same distillation of sugar into alcohol. The difference is entirely in the destination. Point the alcohol at an oil-marketing company and you earn a fuel margin; age it, flavour it and bottle it, and you earn a branded-consumer margin. In FY26 that was the gap between 7.8% and 21.4% EBIT.

Potable spirits — the surprise profit engine

  • Country liquor (IMIL) leadership. IGL is a leading maker of Indian-made Indian liquor in Uttar Pradesh and Uttarakhand, with the well-known "Bunty Bubbly" brand — reportedly around 18.4 million cases sold. This is a high-volume, licence-protected, cash-generative base.
  • Premiumisation via IMFL. The growth push is up-market: a partnership around the Amrut premium-whisky franchise and launches such as Amazing Vodka flavours, driving reported ~30% IMFL volume growth in FY26. Premium liquor carries far richer margins than country liquor or fuel-ethanol.
  • Why the margin is so high. Branded spirits earn on brand equity, distribution licences and pricing power, not on commodity spreads. That is why a mid-sized revenue line produced the largest segment EBIT in FY26.

Bio-fuel — the volume-and-policy engine

  • Ethanol for petrol blending. IGL sells fuel-grade ethanol to the oil-marketing companies under India's Ethanol Blended Petrol (EBP) programme. In FY26 this was the fastest-growing line — revenue up ~40.9% to ~₹1,470 Cr, with EBIT nearly doubling — riding India hitting its 20% blending target ahead of schedule.
  • Low margin, high strategic value. At 7.8% EBIT margin, fuel-ethanol is the thinnest-margin big line. But it is a guaranteed off-take channel that absorbs distillery capacity and de-risks the sugar-to-alcohol base — a volume floor beneath the higher-value uses.
  • Feedstock flexibility. IGL emphasises a "3×3" flexibility — the ability to swing between grain, molasses and other feedstocks — so it can chase whichever sugar source is cheapest each season.
The device that fits no other company: one feedstock, three destinies

Here is the mechanism that makes India Glycols genuinely unusual. A single litre of alcohol distilled from the same molasses can walk out of the Kashipur gate through three different doors, and the door decides the margin. Out the fuel door it earns roughly 8% as blending ethanol. Out the chemicals door — reacted into EO derivatives — it earns roughly 12%. Out the liquor door — aged and branded — it earns roughly 21%. Same molecule, same plant, up to three times the margin depending purely on where management sends it. The art of running IGL has always been allocating that one alcohol stream across three end-markets as their relative economics shift season to season. The demerger's quiet cost is that it hard-wires two of those doors (fuel and liquor) into one company and hands the third (chemicals) to another — trading integrated flexibility for focused clarity.

05

The company, the family, and who controls it

A Bhartia-family enterprise, decades in the making, now consolidating control through a holding company.

India Glycols was built by the Bhartia family of Delhi, and remains a promoter-controlled company. Its history is essentially the story of a single audacious bet — that you could make petrochemicals from sugar — followed by four decades of diversifying the uses of that sugar-to-alcohol platform.

Timeline

  • 1980s
    Incorporated (originally as U.P. Glycols) by the Bhartia family; the Kashipur, Uttarakhand complex is built to make glycols by an unconventional renewable route.
  • 1989
    World-first: IGL becomes, by its own account, the first company anywhere to manufacture MEG from molasses rather than from petroleum — the green-chemistry foundation of the whole company.
  • 1990s–2000s
    Lists on BSE & NSE; broadens from bulk glycols into EO derivatives, glycol ethers, ethoxylates, guar gum and natural gums, and builds the potable-spirits and industrial-gases lines off the same distillery base.
  • 2010s
    As cheap petro-MEG floods in, IGL pivots capacity toward performance chemicals and scales fuel-ethanol as the government's blending programme accelerates.
  • Dec 2024
    Kashipur Holdings Limited (KHL), a promoter-group company, lifts its stake to ~50.35% and becomes the holding company — consolidating family control ahead of the restructuring.
  • FY25–FY26
    Debt reduction, margin expansion and record profit; the board files a scheme to split the group three ways to unlock segment value.
  • 17 Jul 2026
    NCLT (Allahabad) sanctions the three-way demerger into retained IGL (chemicals), IGL Spirits (spirits + bio-fuel) and Ennature Bio Pharma; listing of the new entities to follow the record date.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters hold ~59.6% of IGL (Jun 2026), with public shareholders around a third and modest institutional holding (FIIs ~2.2%, DIIs ~5.2%). This is firmly a founder-controlled company.
  • Kashipur Holdings Limited (KHL) became the holding company in December 2024 with a ~50.35% stake — a step that tightened family control and, notably, KHL sits outside the demerger scheme itself, so the restructuring reorganises the operating businesses beneath a stable promoter apex.
  • Family in management: the Bhartia family remains active at board and executive level (for example, the re-designation of Pragya Bhartia Barwale as a non-executive director) — a reminder that this is a family enterprise being reshaped, not a professionally-controlled widely-held one.
Why the ownership shape matters to a minority investor: a three-way demerger inside a tightly held family group is value-accretive in theory — each business gets a clean balance sheet, its own management focus and a fair market price — but the mechanics (share-swap ratios, which assets and debts go where, how the holding company is positioned) are set by the controlling family. The published scheme gives shareholders 1 IGL Spirits share and 0.33 Ennature shares for every IGL share held, alongside their retained-IGL stake. The case rests on those allocations being fair and the three listed pieces trading up to the sum of their parts.
06

The great unbundling: one share becomes three

The defining event of 2026 — and the lens through which the stock must now be valued.

For most of its life India Glycols was a classic Indian conglomerate-in-miniature: several unrelated businesses stapled together because they shared a feedstock and a founder. The market's standard response to that shape is a conglomerate discount — it values the messy whole below the sum of its cleaner parts. The 2026 demerger is management's move to collapse that discount by simply un-stapling the businesses.

The three companies IGL becomes
New entityWhat it holdsCharacter & FY26 shape
India Glycols (retained)Bio-based specialty & performance chemicals (BSPC) + industrial gasesThe green-chemistry specialties house. ~₹1,203 Cr revenue, 11.7% EBIT margin and rising — a mix-shift growth story.
IGL Spirits LimitedPotable spirits (country liquor + IMFL) and the bio-fuel / ethanol businessA consumer-plus-fuel play: high-margin premiumising liquor (~21% EBIT) bolted to a fast-growing, low-margin fuel-ethanol volume.
Ennature Bio PharmaBio-pharma actives, nutraceuticals, natural gums / bio-polymersThe smallest piece (~₹208 Cr, thin margin today) but the highest-optionality — a specialty-pharma and gums business with room to re-rate on its own.

Why do this? Because the three businesses attract entirely different investors and deserve entirely different multiples. A premium-spirits business is valued like a branded-consumer company. A bio-based specialty chemicals business is valued like a mid-cap chemicals compounder. A bio-pharma-and-gums business is valued on its pipeline and niche leadership. Bundled into one share, each was dragged toward a bland blended multiple; separated, each can be priced by the buyers who understand it. The share-swap gives existing holders direct stakes in all three (their retained IGL share, plus 1 IGL Spirits and 0.33 Ennature share per IGL share).

The honest caveat on demerger arithmetic

Sum-of-the-parts stories are seductive precisely because you can make the total whatever you want by choosing generous multiples for each piece. The discipline is to ask what each stub can actually earn and be valued at as a standalone, listed company — with its own overheads, its own debt allocation and its own cyclicality laid bare. Demergers frequently do unlock value, but they also strip away the internal cross-subsidy that let a weak segment lean on a strong one and the feedstock flexibility of routing one alcohol stream to its best use. The retained chemicals company and IGL Spirits will each carry their own fixed costs and their own bad years with nowhere to hide. The unlock is real; the free lunch is not.

07

The twist: the pioneer of green MEG barely sells MEG

The counter-intuitive fact at the centre of the chemicals business — and what it teaches about the whole company.

Here is the fact that surprises almost everyone who learns India Glycols' story: the company whose entire fame rests on being first in the world to make MEG from a renewable feedstock has spent the last decade deliberately walking away from making MEG. The flagship became the afterthought — and understanding why explains the company's whole strategic posture.

The reason is brutally simple economics. Commodity MEG is a global, oversupplied, price-taking product, and the marginal producers are gigantic petrochemical complexes — think Middle Eastern gas crackers and Indian refiners like Reliance — that make ethylene oxide from cheap fossil feedstock at enormous scale. When petro-feedstock is cheap, IGL's molasses-based MEG is the higher-cost way to make an identical, undifferentiated molecule. Trying to sell commodity green-MEG into that market is a recipe for thin-to-negative margins and constant cyclical pain. So IGL made a choice: stop fighting the petro-giants on the one product where its green feedstock is a cost disadvantage, and redeploy its ethylene oxide into products where "bio-based" is a premium — ethoxylates, glycol ethers, specialty surfactants — and redeploy its ethanol into fuel-blending and liquor.

This pivot is why the chemicals segment's margin can rise while its revenue falls — a pattern that looks strange until you know the strategy. IGL is voluntarily shedding low-margin commodity glycol tonnes and replacing them with fewer, higher-value specialty tonnes. Reported revenue dips; profit per rupee of sales climbs; the quality of the business improves. Judging this segment by its top line would get exactly the wrong answer. The number that tells the truth is EBIT margin, and it went the right way (up ~239 bps to 11.7%) in FY26.

The lesson the MEG story teaches about all of IGL

The green-MEG twist is a microcosm of how this company thinks. It does not cling to a legacy product for sentiment; it constantly re-allocates its one sugar-to-alcohol platform toward whichever end-use pays best right now — specialties over commodity glycol, premium IMFL over country liquor, whichever feedstock is cheapest this season. That adaptability is the real asset, more than any single plant or product. The uncomfortable flip side is that it means IGL has no single durable franchise you can point to and say "that is the moat" — its edge is the flexibility of the platform itself, which is precisely what the demerger now partially dismantles by dividing the platform among three companies.

08

Feedstock and the ethanol tailwind

The farm-and-policy forces that set IGL's cost base and its biggest growth driver.

Because everything begins with fermentable sugar, two external forces dominate the whole group: the cost and availability of molasses and grain (the input), and the government's ethanol-blending push (the demand pull for the fuel line). Both are set well outside the company's walls, in cane fields and in Delhi policy.

The blending programme (EBP) is a genuine multi-year tailwind: a government mandate that oil companies buy ever-more ethanol to mix into petrol, guaranteeing off-take for distillers like IGL and underwriting investment in distillery capacity. It is the reason IGL's fuel-ethanol line grew ~41% in FY26. But it is a volume tailwind at a thin margin — the fuel business is about absorbing capacity and capturing policy-driven demand, not about earning rich returns. And it is exposed to policy risk in both directions: procurement prices, the permitted mix of feedstocks, and diversion rules can all be adjusted by the government season to season.

The feedstock squeeze that can undo a good year

The mirror image of the ethanol tailwind is a feedstock risk. A poor cane crop, a spike in molasses or grain prices, or a government decision to restrict sugar/molasses diversion to ethanol (to protect food or sugar prices) all raise IGL's cost base at once across every business — chemicals, fuel and spirits alike, because they all draw on the same fermented sugar. This is the structural vulnerability a fossil-fed chemicals rival simply does not share. IGL's "3×3" feedstock flexibility — swinging between grain, molasses and alternatives — is its main defence, letting it chase the cheapest sugar each season. But flexibility softens the blow; it does not remove the exposure. A bad harvest is a headwind to the whole company at once.

09

Financials & valuation

A record year, a repaired balance sheet, and a valuation that now hinges on the sum of the parts.

FY26 was the best year in India Glycols' recent history: net revenue up ~12% to ~₹4,211 Cr, EBITDA up ~24.5% to ~₹654 Cr (a 15.5% margin), and net profit up ~27% to ~₹293 Cr — all while paying down debt. The stock trades around 22× earnings — a full-ish multiple that already reflects both the strong year and the market's anticipation of value being unlocked by the demerger.

Two things stand out on the balance sheet. First, deleveraging did real work: cutting debt (to ~₹1,691 Cr) reduced interest expense and helped PAT grow even faster than EBITDA. Second, the reported ROE (~11%) and ROCE (~12%) are decent-but-not-spectacular for the blended group — which is itself an argument for the demerger, since a 21%-margin spirits business and a rising-margin specialties business are almost certainly earning better returns than the low-margin fuel line that dilutes the average. The one number that settles the thesis: chemicals-segment EBIT margin

At ~22× earnings, IGL is not cheap — the market is already paying for the demerger unlock and a strong FY26. So the case does not rest on a re-rating from a low base; it rests on the parts being worth more, and improving, on their own. For the piece that keeps the IGL name — the specialty chemicals business — the single number that proves or breaks the story is BSPC EBIT margin: it rose to 11.7% (up ~239 bps) in FY26 precisely because IGL is swapping commodity glycol volume for performance-chemical value, and the whole retained-company thesis is that this margin keeps climbing toward specialty-chemicals norms as the mix richens. Track it quarter by quarter. Alongside it, watch the spirits EBIT margin (can it hold ~21% as IMFL premiumises?) for the IGL Spirits stub. If BSPC margin keeps grinding higher and spirits holds its premium economics, the sum-of-the-parts is worth chasing; if the chemicals margin stalls once the easy commodity-shedding is done, the demerger delivers a re-labelling rather than a re-rating.

The industry and the rivals

No single competitor looks like IGL — each of its pieces faces a different field.

One consequence of being four businesses in one is that India Glycols has no true like-for-like rival. Instead, each segment competes against a different set of companies, which is another reason the market struggled to price the bundle — and another argument for the split.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Notice how differently each field behaves. Commodity MEG is a scale-and-feedstock war IGL sensibly declined to fight. Specialty surfactants reward formulation and customer intimacy, where a bio-based story is a real edge. Country liquor is a licence-and-distribution game with regional moats; premium IMFL is a brand-building slog against entrenched majors. Ethanol is a policy-and-capacity race among sugar groups. A single management team was straddling four unrelated competitive dynamics at once — an argument, in itself, for handing each piece to a focused team and letting the market pick the right peer set for each. Standalone, retained-IGL will finally be benchmarked against specialty-chemicals compounders rather than against sugar mills or liquor majors.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A record year and a value-unlock event — asked whether the parts really are worth more apart.

India Glycols enters FY27 in its best shape in years: rising margins, lower debt, a booming fuel-ethanol line, a premiumising spirits business, and a court-sanctioned plan to split into three focused, separately valued companies. What it cannot promise is that the sum-of-the-parts arithmetic clears the bar its ~22× multiple already sets, or that a poor sugar harvest won't raise the cost base under all three pieces at once. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Demerger value unlock — three focused, separately listed businesses can each attract the right buyers and multiples, collapsing the old conglomerate discount.
  • Margin mix-shift is working — chemicals EBIT margin rose ~239 bps even as commodity glycol was shed; premium IMFL grew ~30%; the quality of earnings is improving.
  • Ethanol tailwind — the EBP blending mandate is a multi-year, policy-backed demand pull that lifted bio-fuel revenue ~41% and underwrites distillery capacity.
  • "Green" turning from cost to premium — bio-based glycols and surfactants increasingly command a premium with sustainability-committed consumer and personal-care customers.
  • Balance-sheet repair — debt down to ~₹1,691 Cr and falling interest cost let profit outpace revenue; a cleaner base for each spun-off entity.

The bear case

  • Feedstock & harvest risk — a poor cane/grain crop or a molasses-diversion curb raises the cost base across chemicals, fuel and spirits simultaneously; there is nowhere to hide.
  • Priced for the unlock already — at ~22× earnings the market pays in advance for a clean demerger; disappointment on ratios, listing timing or standalone margins could sting.
  • Commodity-cycle exposure — the green-glycol and fuel-ethanol legs remain thin-margin, price-taking commodities vulnerable to petro-MEG cheapness and ethanol-price resets.
  • Loss of flexibility — splitting the platform hard-wires the alcohol stream's destinations and removes the internal cross-subsidy and feedstock optionality that softened bad years.
  • Family-controlled restructuring — swap ratios, debt allocation and holding-company positioning are set by the promoter; minorities must trust the split is even-handed.
The bottom line

India Glycols is a genuinely inventive company — it made petrochemicals from sugar before anyone else, and it has kept re-pointing that one sugar-to-alcohol platform at whatever end-market paid best, from commodity MEG to specialty surfactants to fuel-ethanol to premium whisky. FY26 caught several of those bets working at once. The next chapter is not about a single plant or product; it is about execution of the split. The concrete, datable test is whether, once the three entities list, the retained chemicals business keeps grinding its EBIT margin up from 11.7% toward specialty-chemicals territory while IGL Spirits defends its ~21% liquor margin as a standalone — with the first clean standalone quarterly numbers, arriving after the record date and listing, as the moment the sum-of-the-parts thesis is finally marked to market. Prove the parts earn more apart than together, and the split was worth doing; leave the fuel-ethanol leg diluting a re-labelled group, and FY26's applause will have run ahead of the substance.
