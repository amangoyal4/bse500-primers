Bandhan Bank, the microlender that became a bank

A complete walk through India's most unusual private-sector bank — one that grew out of the country's largest microfinance NGO, lends tiny unsecured loans to poor rural women, and is now fighting to turn itself into a diversified "universal" bank. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Bandhan Bank is a private-sector universal bank headquartered in Kolkata that started life as a microfinance NGO — lending tiny, unsecured loans to poor rural women in West Bengal. It won a full banking licence in 2015 (the first microlender ever to do so in India), listed in 2018, and today serves roughly 3.5 crore customers through more than 1,700 branches and thousands of banking outlets, with ~₹1.66 lakh crore of deposits and ~₹1.54 lakh crore of advances.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A high-return, high-volatility engine. Because its core product is small unsecured micro-loans that carry very high interest rates, Bandhan earns a net interest margin near 6–7% — roughly double a big private bank's — and in good years posts a return on assets a large bank would envy. But that same book blows up in bad years: FY26 profit fell ~55% to ~₹1,224 crore (from ₹2,745 crore in FY25) as an industry-wide microfinance stress cycle drove credit costs up.
  • A deliberate pivot from monoline micro-lender to diversified bank. The whole strategy is to shrink dependence on risky micro-loans by growing secured lending — housing finance (built on its 2019 acquisition of HDFC's GRUH Finance), plus commercial, retail, gold and MSME loans. Secured lending has crossed ~56% of the book, up from a sliver a few years ago. Safer, but structurally lower-margin.
  • The market prices the volatility, not the potential. Bandhan trades around 1.3× book value — cheap for a bank that can earn a 2%+ RoA in a clean year, but a discount the market applies for the earnings swings, the East-India concentration, a governance scare over guaranteed-loan claims, and a leadership transition still being proven. It is a genuine "high-return-but-high-risk, still-proving-the-turnaround" story.
In plain terms

Think of Bandhan as a bank with a Ferrari engine and worn tyres. The engine — micro-lending to millions of low-income women — generates margins most banks can only dream of, which is why in a good year it mints money. The worn tyres are the risk: those borrowers have no collateral and thin buffers, so a bad monsoon, a local shock, a politician promising a loan waiver, or too many lenders chasing the same households can send repayments — and profits — skidding. Bandhan's entire modern strategy is a bet that it can keep the powerful engine while fitting steadier tyres: more secured, collateral-backed loans and a proper deposit bank, so the ride stops throwing everyone through the windscreen every few years.

01

Microfinance banking 101: how tiny loans make big margins

The one idea you must understand before anything else about Bandhan makes sense.

Microfinance (MFI) means lending very small, unsecured loans to low-income borrowers — usually women, usually rural or peri-urban, often for a tiny business: a sewing machine, a few goats, seed and fertiliser, a roadside stall. A typical loan is a few tens of thousands of rupees, repaid weekly or fortnightly over a year or two. The borrowers have no collateral, no credit history, and no bank branch nearby. Conventional banks historically wouldn't touch them. Microfinance was invented to serve exactly this "unbanked" bottom of the pyramid.

The trick that makes it work: the group (joint-liability) loan

If there's no collateral, how does a lender get repaid? The classic answer, and Bandhan's founding method, is the joint-liability group. Women organise into small groups; loans are given to individuals, but the group is collectively responsible. Peer pressure and mutual accountability replace physical collateral: your neighbours vouch for you, attend the weekly repayment meeting with you, and are on the hook if you default. It sounds fragile, but for two decades it produced repayment rates above 98% — better than many secured loan books.

Why micro-lending has such unusual economics
FeatureWhat it means — and why it matters
Tiny, unsecured loansNo collateral to seize. Repayment rests on the borrower's cash flow and group discipline, not on a house or gold — which is exactly why it can go wrong fast in a downturn.
Very high yieldsMicro-loans carry interest rates around ~22–24%. Servicing millions of tiny loans in person is costly, but the spread over the bank's own funding cost is huge — the source of Bandhan's 6–7% NIM, roughly double a large bank's.
High, cyclical credit riskIn normal times losses are low. But these borrowers have no buffers, so shocks — a bad monsoon, floods, a local economic hit, a pandemic, or a political loan-waiver rumour — can trigger mass defaults at once. Losses arrive in waves, not a steady trickle.
Labour-intensive, high-touchLoan officers physically visit villages, form groups, run weekly meetings and collect cash. It's a people business with thousands of field staff — a moat (relationships, local knowledge) but also a fixed cost.
The high-margin / high-risk bargain

Here is the whole trade-off in one line: you get paid a fat margin precisely because the loans are risky and hard to serve. In a good year that margin drops through to spectacular profit; in a bad year the same book delivers a spike in bad debts that wipes out much of the profit. This is why microfinance earnings look nothing like a normal bank's smooth line — they zig-zag. The two forces that determine which kind of year it is: the economic health of poor borrowers (weather, jobs, inflation) and the discipline of lenders — when too many MFIs pile loans onto the same households ("over-leverage"), the whole system gets fragile. Both matter enormously for Bandhan, and both turned against the sector in FY25–FY26.

The permanent political risk: because the borrowers are poor and numerous, their loans are a tempting target for politicians. A promised or actual loan waiver, or a sudden regulatory tightening, can collapse repayment culture overnight — borrowers stop paying in the expectation of forgiveness. Andhra Pradesh's 2010 microfinance crisis and Assam's 2019–21 stress both worked this way, and both hurt the sector Bandhan leads.
02

The bank born from an MFI — company & owners

From a one-room NGO in a Bengal village to a listed universal bank.

Bandhan's origin is genuinely unusual in Indian banking. It was not spun out of an industrial house or a foreign bank — it was built by a social entrepreneur, Chandra Shekhar Ghosh, a sweet-shop owner's son from Tripura, who started an NGO to lend to poor women and, over two decades, turned it into a bank big enough to list on the stock exchange.

Timeline — 25 years from micro-credit to universal bank

  • 2001
    Chandra Shekhar Ghosh founds Bandhan-Konnagar, a not-for-profit in West Bengal, lending micro-credit to marginalised women — starting in Bagnan, a village ~60 km from Kolkata. ("Bandhan" means "bond/togetherness".)
  • 2006
    Acquires an NBFC and creates Bandhan Financial Services (BFSPL) to scale the micro-lending commercially.
  • 2010
    Becomes India's largest microfinance institution by borrowers and portfolio.
  • Jun 2015
    RBI grants a universal banking licence — the first MFI ever to become a full bank in India. Banking operations begin in August 2015 with a ready-made network of micro-borrowers.
  • Mar 2018
    IPO — one of India's largest bank listings (~₹4,470 Cr); lists on NSE & BSE. Youngest listed bank in the country.
  • 2019
    Acquires GRUH Finance, HDFC's affordable-housing finance arm, in an all-stock deal — a landmark step into secured housing loans and a way to dilute promoter holding toward RBI limits.
  • 2020–21
    COVID and Assam / West Bengal microfinance stress (floods, loan-waiver politics) drive painful spikes in bad loans and credit costs.
  • 2024
    Founder Ghosh retires as MD & CEO (April); a forensic audit of guaranteed micro-loan claims (CGFMU) makes headlines; RBI approves Partha Pratim Sengupta — an SBI-groomed veteran — as the new MD & CEO (takes charge Nov 2024).
  • FY25
    Recovery year: PAT ₹2,745 Cr (+23%). Secured lending pushes past half the book.
  • FY26
    Sector-wide MFI stress cycle hits: full-year PAT falls ~55% to ~₹1,224 Cr, though asset quality and profit rebound sharply by Q4 (₹534 Cr).

Who owns it now

  • Promoter — Bandhan Financial Holdings Ltd (BFHL): the holding company that traces back to the original NGO/MFI structure. Its stake has been coming down from ~40% toward ~38% (open-market sales through FY26) — partly to meet RBI's promoter-dilution rules and to allow legacy investors to exit. A distinctive feature: BFHL's shares in the bank have never been encumbered/pledged.
  • Founder's role: Chandra Shekhar Ghosh stepped down from the bank's executive leadership in 2024 but remains associated with the wider Bandhan group. His departure ended a founder-led era and is itself part of the investment debate.
  • Institutional base: the free float is held by domestic mutual funds and foreign investors; the stock is an NSE/BSE large-mid-cap name (NSE: BANDHANBNK, BSE: 541153).
  • Balance-sheet scale: a full universal bank — ~₹1.66 lakh crore of deposits funding ~₹1.54 lakh crore of advances, with a strong ~18% capital ratio (see §03 and §10).
Why the GRUH deal was pivotal: acquiring GRUH Finance did three things at once — it gave Bandhan an instant, secured, lower-risk affordable-housing loan book; it diluted the promoter's stake toward regulatory limits (RBI had penalised the bank for not reducing it fast enough); and it planted the seed of the whole "diversify away from microfinance" strategy that defines Bandhan today.
03

Bank metrics, decoded for a newcomer

Eight abbreviations run the whole story. Learn them once here.

A bank is, at heart, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (deposits) and lends it out dearer (loans), pocketing the difference — minus whatever it loses on loans that go bad. Almost every number analysts quote is a way of measuring one of those moving parts. Here's the whole vocabulary you need for Bandhan, in plain English.

The bank dashboard — and Bandhan's reading on each

How these fit together

Read left to right, it tells a clean story. Bandhan earns a huge NIM (~6–7%) because it lends at micro-loan rates — that's the Ferrari engine. It funds that with deposits, but its CASA is only ~27% (a young deposit franchise still leaning on costlier term deposits), so its funding isn't as cheap as an old, branch-heavy bank's. The swing factor is credit cost: in a clean year it's low and the fat NIM drops to a 2%+ RoA; in a stress year credit cost spikes, devouring the margin and crushing RoA/RoE — exactly what happened in FY26 (RoA fell to ~0.6%). Capital (~18% CAR) is strong, so the bank can absorb the shocks; the debate is purely about how often and how hard the shocks come.

04

The loan book: from monoline micro to a mixed bank

The central strategic story — and the single most important chart in this primer.

For most of its life Bandhan was effectively a monoline microfinance lender: almost every rupee it lent was an unsecured group micro-loan. That is a fabulous business in a boom and a terrifying one in a bust. The entire modern strategy — under both the founder and the new CEO — is to diversify the asset side into secured, collateral-backed lending, so a single micro-loan shock can no longer sink the whole bank.

EEB vs the secured book — what those words mean

  • EEB — "Emerging Entrepreneurs Business": Bandhan's own name for its microfinance book — the unsecured group and individual micro-loans to low-income (mostly women) borrowers. High-yield, high-risk, the historic core. It has been deliberately shrunk — down ~15% in FY26 as the bank pulled back amid sector stress.
  • Secured / non-EEB book: everything collateral-backed or lower-risk — housing finance (the GRUH legacy plus affordable home loans), commercial banking (loans to NBFCs, SMEs), retail (personal, gold, loans against property), and MSME. Lower yield, but far steadier losses.

The core trade-off of diversification

Diversifying lowers risk but also lowers the sky-high MFI margins. A home loan or an SME loan is far safer than an unsecured micro-loan, but it also yields far less — a housing loan might earn 9–11% against a micro-loan's ~22–24%. So as secured lending grows, Bandhan's blended NIM drifts down from its microfinance peak. That's the deliberate price of a smoother, more durable, more "trustworthy" bank. The bull hopes the pivot converts Bandhan from a boom-bust MFI into a steady compounder the market will re-rate; the bear worries it ends up stuck in the middle — margins eroded by the secured shift, yet still carrying enough micro-loan risk to keep blowing up. FY26 was a live test of that fear.

Also part of diversification — geography: alongside product mix, Bandhan is pushing to de-concentrate beyond East India (see §06). Growing housing and commercial lending in the South, West and North reduces both product and regional dependence at the same time — two forms of the same de-risking bet.
05

The deposit franchise it had to build from scratch

A micro-lender knows how to lend. Learning to gather deposits is a different game.

When Bandhan became a bank in 2015, it already had millions of borrowers — but almost no depositors. An MFI funds itself by borrowing wholesale; a bank must fund itself with public deposits. Building a deposit franchise from a standing start, in a country full of century-old banks, is one of the hardest things Bandhan has had to do — and it's still doing it.

Retail (stickier) deposits are a healthy ~74% of the book, but the cheap CASA slice (~27%) is modest versus established banks that run 40%+ — a sign the franchise is real but still young.
  • Why CASA matters: current and savings balances pay little or no interest, so a high CASA ratio means cheap funding, which supports margins and cushions the bank when loan yields fall. At ~27%, Bandhan pays up more for deposits than a top-tier bank — a drag it is working to close as its brand and branch network mature.
  • Retail vs bulk: ~74% of deposits are retail (many small savers) rather than lumpy corporate/bulk money. Retail deposits are stickier and less flight-prone — a quiet strength that has grown steadily since listing.
  • The strategic link: a stronger, cheaper deposit base is what lets Bandhan grow secured (lower-yield) lending profitably. You can't run a housing-loan book on expensive wholesale funding. So the deposit build-out and the asset diversification are two halves of the same "become a real bank" project.
In plain terms

Bandhan inherited the hard half of banking (finding good borrowers) and had to learn the other hard half (winning depositors' trust). It has done remarkably well — ₹1.66 lakh crore of deposits, three-quarters retail, in barely a decade. But the cheap-money advantage that established banks enjoy is still being built. Every point of CASA it adds is a point of margin it doesn't have to earn from risky lending — which is why deposit quality, not just deposit growth, is a number worth watching each quarter.

06

The East-India question — a concentration built in from birth

Bandhan's geography is both its founding advantage and its structural vulnerability.

Because Bandhan grew out of a West Bengal NGO, its roots — and much of its micro-loan book — are heavily concentrated in East India, above all West Bengal, with meaningful exposure to Assam and Bihar. That density gave it unmatched local knowledge and market share; it also means a shock in one region can hit the whole bank at once. This is the demand-side risk unique to Bandhan.

~42% of the EEB portfolio sits in a single state. The de-concentration push — growing secured and micro lending outside the East — is the direct answer to this risk.
  • The advantage: deep roots mean Bandhan knows its heartland borrowers intimately — decades of repayment data, dense branch coverage, trusted field relationships. In West Bengal and Assam it is often the dominant microfinance lender, with local scale rivals can't match.
  • The vulnerability: that same concentration means a regional shock lands on a big slice of the book simultaneously. Assam's 2019–21 microfinance turmoil (floods plus loan-waiver politics) and West Bengal's periodic stresses have each hit Bandhan disproportionately precisely because so much of its lending is there.
  • The response: geographic diversification runs in parallel with product diversification (§04) — pushing housing, commercial and even micro lending into the South, West and North so that no single state can dictate the bank's results. It is slow, deliberate work, and success is measured in gradually falling concentration ratios.
The honest tension: Bandhan's East-India density is a genuine moat and a genuine hazard. You cannot fully un-concentrate a bank overnight without abandoning the very market that made it strong. The realistic path is to keep the profitable heartland while growing everywhere else faster — so the concentration ratio falls even as the heartland book stays large. Watch state-wise mix and collection efficiency in the East: that's where the next stress cycle, if one comes, would show up first.
07

The credit-cost roller-coaster

Why Bandhan's profit swings so violently — and what actually drives each swing.

If you only track one thing about Bandhan, track credit cost — the money it must set aside each year for loans going bad, as a percentage of its book. For a normal bank this is a boring, stable number. For a microfinance-heavy bank it is the single biggest swing factor in earnings, and it explains why Bandhan can earn ₹2,745 crore one year and ₹1,224 crore the next off a similar-sized balance sheet.

The ~55% FY26 drop wasn't a collapse in lending — deposits and advances both grew. It was elevated provisioning on the stressed micro-loan book eating the profit. That's the credit-cost cycle in one chart.

What drives the cycle

  • Borrower health shocks — floods, drought, inflation, local job losses. Poor households have no cushion, so a bad patch turns quickly into missed weekly repayments across whole groups.
  • Sector over-leverage — the FY25–FY26 stress was industry-wide: too many lenders had loaded the same borrowers with too much debt. The RBI-backed self-regulator (MFIN) imposed "guardrails" (caps on how many lenders per borrower and total exposure), which forced the whole industry — Bandhan included — to shrink micro-loan books and take losses on the excess.
  • Loan-waiver politics — even the expectation of forgiveness can dent repayment discipline in an election cycle.
  • The offset — provisioning & write-offs — Bandhan carries a high provision coverage (~85% incl. write-offs), so once it recognises the pain, the reported bad-loan ratios (GNPA ~3.3%, NNPA ~1.0% by Q4 FY26) come back down and profit rebounds — as it did sharply in Q4 (₹534 Cr, up 68% YoY).

Why the mix shift is meant to tame the ride

Here is where §04 and §07 connect. The reason Bandhan is so keen to grow secured lending is that secured loans have far lower and steadier credit costs than micro-loans. If the book becomes majority-secured (it now is, ~56%), then a future micro-loan stress cycle hits a smaller share of assets — so the earnings swing should shrink over time. Management's guidance for credit cost to settle around 1.5–1.6% over the next couple of years is precisely a bet that the diversified book will be less volatile than the old monoline one. Whether that guidance holds through the next downturn is the central thing the market is waiting to see proven — and the reason the stock trades cheap despite its high-return potential.

08

Governance, the CGFMU episode & the leadership handover

The trust discount, explained honestly.

Part of why Bandhan trades cheaply is not the numbers at all — it's trust. Over 2023–24 the bank went through a bruising sequence: a governance scare over how it accounted for guaranteed micro-loan claims, the retirement of its founder-CEO, and the arrival of an outsider chief executive. None was fatal, but together they left a "prove it" discount on the stock that has yet to fully lift.

The CGFMU / NCGTC forensic-audit episode

Bandhan had insured a large chunk of its stressed micro-loan portfolio under a government scheme called CGFMU — the Credit Guarantee Fund for Micro Units, which guarantees collateral-free micro-loans (up to ₹10 lakh) made under the Mudra/PMMY programme. When those loans went bad, Bandhan filed guarantee claims to recover its losses. The trustee that runs the scheme, NCGTC, did not simply pay — it ordered a forensic audit of the claimed portfolio first, to check the loans genuinely qualified.

  • What the audit found: after review, NCGTC assessed the bank's total eligible claim at about ₹1,231 crore (net of recoveries) as of March 2024. Bandhan had already been paid ~₹916 crore of that; NCGTC agreed to pay an additional ~₹315 crore.
  • Why it rattled investors: the very fact that a government trustee ran a forensic audit before honouring the claims raised a governance and disclosure question — were the guaranteed loans as clean as reported? The audit ultimately largely validated the claims (and even topped up the payout), which was reassuring — but the episode dented confidence at a sensitive moment and lingers in the "trust discount".

Founder exit & the new CEO

  • Chandra Shekhar Ghosh retired as MD & CEO in April 2024, ending the founder era. An executive director, Ratan Kumar Kesh, served as interim CEO during the search.
  • Partha Pratim Sengupta was approved by RBI and took charge as MD & CEO in November 2024. He is a career banker with ~four decades of experience — groomed at State Bank of India (where he rose to Deputy Managing Director & Chief Credit Officer) and formerly MD & CEO of Indian Overseas Bank. His background in credit and large-bank governance is widely read as exactly the risk-and-controls profile Bandhan needs for its next phase.
  • Why the transition matters: a founder-built, founder-run bank is different from a professionally-managed one. Bandhan is now testing whether the institution — its systems, its risk culture, its diversification strategy — can outlast the visionary who created it. Continuity of strategy (the secured-lending pivot has carried straight through the handover) is a positive sign; but a leadership change at a microfinance-heavy bank, mid-stress-cycle, is inherently something the market watches closely.
The point of this section: the governance and leadership events don't change Bandhan's earning power, but they change how much the market is willing to pay for it. A bank runs on confidence; two years of headlines about audits, a founder's exit and a new hand on the wheel translate directly into a lower multiple until a clean track record rebuilds the trust.
09

The industry and the rivals

Where Bandhan sits among the MFI-origin and small-finance banks.

Bandhan belongs to a distinctive cohort of Indian lenders that started at the bottom of the pyramid and moved up — microfinance institutions and small-finance banks (SFBs) chasing higher-margin, underserved segments. They all share the same DNA: high NIMs, high credit risk, and a strategic urge to diversify into safer, secured lending as they grow.

What the peer valuations tell you

Notice the pattern: CreditAccess Grameen (~2.9× book) and Ujjivan (~2.1×) trade at clear premiums to Bandhan (~1.3×), even though Bandhan is the largest and the only one with a full universal-bank licence and a big deposit base. The market is effectively saying it will pay more for smaller, cleaner, more predictable microfinance operators than for Bandhan's bigger-but-lumpier franchise. That gap is the whole investment debate in a nutshell: bulls see a scale leader with a deposit moat unfairly discounted for past volatility; bears see a bank that keeps earning that discount every time the cycle turns. The re-rating case rests on Bandhan proving its diversified book really is less volatile than its history suggests.

Industry backdrop: microfinance in India is regulated by the RBI and self-regulated via MFIN's "guardrails" (limits on lenders-per-borrower and total micro-debt), tightened during the FY25–26 stress to curb over-lending. Tighter guardrails mean slower micro-loan growth industry-wide — painful short-term, but healthier long-term, and a tailwind for diversified players like Bandhan that have other engines to grow.

Why P/B is the right lens — and why it's low

For a bank, price-to-book is the master valuation ratio: a bank is essentially a pile of loans funded by deposits and equity, so what you're buying is its net worth (book) and the return it can earn on it. The rule of thumb: a bank that can sustainably earn a high return on equity deserves a high multiple of book, and a low-RoE bank deserves near or below 1×. Bandhan's oddity is that its potential RoE (in a clean year) is high — worthy of 2–3× book — yet it trades at only ~1.3×. The market is applying a volatility-and-trust discount: it doesn't fully believe the high returns are durable, given the credit-cycle swings, the East-India concentration, and the recent governance/leadership disruption. Resolve those doubts — a few years of steady, diversified, low-volatility returns — and the same ~₹1,224 Cr (rising) earnings on the same book could command a materially higher multiple. Fail to, and the discount stays. That is the investment case, stated fairly for both sides.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A powerful engine, mid-overhaul — priced for the doubt, not the promise.

Bandhan is a rare thing: a bank that can genuinely out-earn its peers through the cycle, wrapped in enough volatility and history that the market won't pay up for it. The next few years are about whether the turnaround — more secured lending, wider geography, a professional management team, a rebuilt deposit franchise — finally converts a boom-bust microlender into a steady compounder.

The bull case

  • High-return engine — a 6–7% NIM and 2%+ through-cycle RoA potential that few banks can match, funding growth and losses alike.
  • The diversification is working — secured lending is now the majority of the book (~56%); each year of mix-shift should dampen the earnings swings.
  • Cheap on book — ~1.3× P/B for the scale leader of the MFI-origin cohort, well below smaller, pricier peers — real re-rating room if trust returns.
  • Well capitalised & growing — ~18% CAR, deposits and advances both compounding double-digit, ~3.5 crore customers.
  • Professionalised leadership — an SBI-groomed, credit-focused CEO and continuity of strategy through the founder handover.

The bear case

  • Credit-cost volatility — the FY26 ~55% profit fall shows the micro-loan book can still whipsaw earnings; the "normalised" 1.5–1.6% guidance is unproven through a full downturn.
  • East-India concentration — ~42% of the micro book in West Bengal; a regional or political shock hits a big slice at once.
  • Margin erosion — the safer secured mix structurally lowers NIM; the risk of ending up "stuck in the middle" — lower margin, still-lumpy risk.
  • Trust discount — the CGFMU forensic-audit episode and modest ~27% CASA keep a lid on the multiple until a clean track record rebuilds.
  • Execution & politics — a new CEO mid-cycle, a still-young deposit franchise, and the ever-present microfinance loan-waiver risk.
The bottom line

Bandhan is a high-return, high-risk bank in the middle of proving a turnaround. The business quality is real — few lenders earn its margins — but so is the volatility, and the market has chosen to price the volatility rather than the potential, leaving the stock cheap on book. The story over the next few years will be written in two numbers: credit cost (does the diversified book really deliver a steadier, ~1.5% run-rate through the cycle?) and the secured/CASA mix (is Bandhan genuinely becoming a durable universal bank, or a microlender in a bank's clothes?). Get those right and a ~1.3× P/B has room to re-rate meaningfully; get them wrong and the discount is deserved. That honest tension — powerful engine, unproven durability — is exactly why Bandhan is one of the most debated names in Indian financials.

The engine was never the problem. The whole question is whether Bandhan can make the ride smooth enough for the market to finally trust the horsepower.
