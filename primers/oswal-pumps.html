00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Oswal Pumps makes water pumps and the electric motors that drive them — the machines that lift water out of the ground for a farmer's field, a household's tank or an industrial plant. It is based in Karnal, Haryana, sells under the Oswal brand, and for most of its life was an ordinary, mid-sized pump-and-motor company. What changed everything is solar: Oswal became one of the largest suppliers of solar-powered agricultural pumps under the government's PM-KUSUM scheme, which pays to put off-grid solar irrigation pumps on Indian farms. That one policy took a steady business and, in two years, roughly tripled its profit — and then, in June 2025, took it public.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A policy scheme, not a product cycle, made the numbers. In the nine months to December 2024, over 87% of Oswal's revenue came from PM-KUSUM solar pumping systems, where the company holds an estimated ~38% market share (direct plus indirect supply). Revenue nearly doubled to ~₹1,430 Cr in FY25 and net profit jumped ~187% to ~₹281 Cr. That is a spectacular ramp — and it rests on one government programme.
  • It makes its own motors, and now its own solar panels. Oswal builds the pump, winds the electric motor inside it, and has moved backward into manufacturing solar modules — the panels that power a solar pump. Owning more of the bill of materials is the lever behind an EBITDA margin that jumped to ~29.5% in FY25, unusually fat for a pump maker.
  • It is a very young listed company on a rich, cyclical base. The June-2025 IPO raised fresh capital to expand capacity and repay debt, but it also means a short public track record against earnings that are, by the company's own disclosure, heavily concentrated in one subsidised end-market. The closest listed mirror is Shakti Pumps, the other big PM-KUSUM winner — the two are constantly compared.
In plain terms

Oswal Pumps is a competent pump-and-motor manufacturer that caught a policy wave at exactly the right moment. The government wants to move India's farm irrigation off diesel and grid power onto the sun — so it subsidises solar pumps, and it buys them through state tenders. Oswal turned itself into a factory tuned to win those tenders: it makes the pump, the motor and increasingly the solar panel in-house, so it can bid low and still keep a fat margin. The upside is obvious in the FY25 numbers. The question the whole primer circles back to is simpler than it looks — a business where nearly nine rupees in ten come from one subsidy programme is only as durable as the programme, and the programme runs on annual budgets and state-by-state tenders that can slow at any time.

01

Pumps & motors 101: how water gets lifted

No engineering degree needed — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

A water pump is a machine that moves water from where it is to where you want it — usually up, out of a well or borehole, against gravity. To do that it needs a source of rotation, which is the electric motor. Spin the motor, and it turns an impeller (a bladed disc) that flings water outward and pushes it up a pipe. Nearly everything Oswal sells is a variation on that idea, powered three different ways: from the grid, from a diesel set, or — the growth story — from a solar panel. Six terms carry the whole business.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Submersible pumpA pump-and-motor unit that sits underwater at the bottom of a borehole and pushes water up. The workhorse of farm and drinking-water supply in India, and Oswal's core product.
Monoblock pumpA pump and its motor built into one sealed block, mounted above ground (near a well or tank). Common in homes and small farms.
Electric motorThe part that actually spins — induction motors (grid/diesel) and submersible motors. Oswal winds its own motors in-house, which is unusual and important to margin.
HP (horsepower)The size/power of a pump — a home pump may be 1 HP, a farm solar pump 3–10 HP. Bigger HP means a bigger, higher-value machine.
Solar pumpA pump run by electricity from solar panels instead of the grid — no diesel, no power bill. Needs panels, a controller and a motor tuned to variable solar power. This is the product PM-KUSUM subsidises.
VFD / controllerThe electronics that take fluctuating solar power and feed the motor a usable, steady drive so the pump runs smoothly as clouds pass. The "brain" that makes a solar pump work.
Why a solar pump is a different sale from a normal pump

An ordinary pump is a commodity — a farmer walks into a dealership and buys one for a few thousand rupees, and dozens of brands compete on price and reliability. A solar irrigation pump is a system: panels sized to the pump, a controller matched to the motor, mounting structures, and installation at the farm, all certified to a government specification. That bundle costs far more — often ₹2–5 lakh for a farm system — and it is bought not by the farmer alone but by a state agency that pays most of the bill through a subsidy. So Oswal's real customer for its biggest product isn't a shopkeeper's counter; it is a government tender. That single shift — from selling a part to delivering a subsidised system — is what turned a modest pump maker into a company growing profit at triple-digit rates.

Who buys pumps

  • Farmers & state agencies (via PM-KUSUM) — the dominant buyer today. State nodal agencies run tenders to supply subsidised solar pumps to farmers; the winning manufacturer supplies, installs and often commissions them. This is Oswal's engine.
  • Households & builders — the traditional retail market for domestic monoblock and submersible pumps, sold through dealers and distributors. Steadier, lower-margin, brand-driven.
  • Industry, agriculture & exports — plants, farms and overseas buyers needing pumps and motors for irrigation, water supply and process use. The diversification leg that reduces reliance on any one scheme.
02

What Oswal actually makes: one factory, four things it builds itself

The integrated bill of materials — read this once and the margin makes sense.

Oswal's edge is that it makes most of a solar pumping system under its own roof in Karnal. A rival that buys motors from one supplier, panels from another and controllers from a third is stitching together other people's margins. Oswal builds the pump, winds the motor, and now presses its own solar modules — so more of every rupee a tender pays lands inside the company rather than leaking out to vendors. That single design choice runs through the whole financial story.

The product lines, decoded

  • Grid & solar submersible pumps — the core catalogue: pumps that sit down a borehole and push water up, sold both grid-connected and (increasingly) in solar-powered versions. The solar variant is the one PM-KUSUM buys in volume.
  • Electric motors — induction and submersible motors, made in-house. Winding your own motor is the difference between assembling a pump and building one; it lets Oswal control cost, quality and delivery on the single most important component.
  • Solar modules — the backward-integration move. By making the panels a solar pump needs, Oswal captures a slice of value that used to go to module importers or third-party suppliers — the clearest lever on its unusually high margin, and a hedge against panel-price swings.
  • Monoblocks, sewage & specialty pumps + spares — the wider retail and industrial range that keeps dealers stocked and gives the brand a base beyond the subsidy tender.
Why "integrated" shows up in the margin, not the brochure

Most pump companies quote gross margins in the teens to low twenties; Oswal posted an EBITDA margin near 29.5% in FY25. A pump is not a high-technology object, so a number that high has to come from somewhere structural — and here it comes from owning the components. When Oswal wins a solar-pump tender, it isn't paying a motor vendor's markup, a panel importer's margin and a controller supplier's fee; it books much of that itself. The flip side is the mirror image of the wind-and-cement lesson: vertical integration is a margin engine when volumes are high and a fixed-cost millstone when they aren't. A panel line and a motor shop have to be fed — if tender volumes slow, those in-house plants sit under-utilised, and the same integration that lifted margins on the way up drags on them on the way down.

03

The company, the family, and a very fresh listing

A Karnal promoter business that went from dealership brand to IPO in one policy cycle.

Oswal Pumps is a promoter-family business from Karnal, Haryana, that grew for years as a regional pump-and-motor brand before the solar-agri boom pulled it onto the national stage and, in June 2025, onto the exchanges. The promoter group retains a controlling majority — roughly 75%+ after the IPO — so this is very much a founder-run company, with all the alignment and the key-person concentration that implies.

Timeline

  • 2010s

  • FY23–FY25

  • 2024–25
    Backward integration into solar modules — Oswal adds in-house panel capacity, widening the value it captures per solar-pump system.
  • Jun 2025
    IPO on NSE & BSE (fresh issue plus offer-for-sale, ~₹1,387 Cr), funding capacity expansion, debt repayment and the solar-module ramp. The public track record begins here.
  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the founding family holds roughly 75%+ of the company post-listing — a tight, controlling grip typical of a first-generation manufacturing promoter. Concentrated ownership aligns incentives but concentrates key-person and governance risk; watch related-party dealings and any promoter pledging in the filings.
  • Manufacturing base: operations centre on Karnal, Haryana, across pump, motor and (newly) solar-module lines. The IPO proceeds are earmarked partly for capacity expansion — the physical bet that tender volumes keep growing.
  • Balance sheet: the IPO's fresh issue was used in part to repay debt, leaving a cleaner balance sheet to fund the working capital that government orders demand — important, because tender receivables tie up cash for months.
The listing changes the risk profile as much as the capital. Before June 2025, Oswal's concentrated, subsidy-driven earnings were a private matter. As a listed company on a rich multiple, that same concentration is now the market's daily question — and there is barely a year of public reporting to judge how the business behaves across a full tender cycle. A short track record on top of a policy-dependent earnings base is the single most important caveat a first-time reader should carry through everything that follows.
04

PM-KUSUM: the scheme that wrote the P&L

The single most important idea in the entire investment case — and it is a government programme, not a product.

You cannot understand Oswal Pumps without understanding PM-KUSUM (Pradhan Mantri Kisan Urja Suraksha evam Utthaan Mahabhiyan), the central scheme launched in 2019 to move India's farm irrigation onto solar power. It exists because farm irrigation is a fiscal and environmental headache: farmers get heavily subsidised or free grid electricity to run pumps, and where the grid doesn't reach they burn diesel. Solar pumps replace both — no diesel bill, no strain on the discom, no daytime power cut stopping the water. The government pays a large share of the cost; the farmer pays a fraction; a manufacturer supplies and installs the system. That manufacturer, in a plurality of cases, has been Oswal.

By its own disclosure, roughly seven-eighths of Oswal's revenue in the run-up to the IPO came from a single subsidy programme. This is the defining fact of the company — the source of the growth and the source of the risk, in the same bar.

The mechanism is worth spelling out because it explains both the scale and the fragility. PM-KUSUM runs largely through state nodal agencies, which float tenders to supply a target number of solar pumps in a state. Manufacturers bid; winners supply, install and commission the systems, and get paid partly by the central and state subsidy and partly by the farmer's contribution. Win a big state's tender and revenue steps up sharply; a lull in tendering — a state between allocations, a budget delay, an election-year pause — and the order flow stalls. This is not smooth, recurring demand; it is lumpy, allocation-driven demand set by a policy calendar the company does not control.

The device that only fits Oswal (and Shakti): market share of a subsidy, not a market

Most companies you value on their share of a market — customers freely choosing a product. Oswal's headline number is its ~38% share of PM-KUSUM supply — a share of a subsidy programme. That distinction is the whole thesis. A 38% share of an organically growing market is a moat; a 38% share of a scheme is a claim on a government budget line. It grows when Delhi and the states expand allocations and tender aggressively, and it shrinks the moment they don't — regardless of how good Oswal's pumps are. So the metric that actually drives the stock isn't Oswal's product win-rate; it is the annual pace of PM-KUSUM tendering and allocation, a number set in budget documents, not in Karnal. Read Oswal's growth off the scheme's disbursement calendar and you will usually be reading it right.

05

Vertical integration: the margin lever

Why owning the motor and the panel is worth more than owning the pump.

A pump on its own is a low-margin, competitive product. Oswal's fat margins come from a deliberate strategy of making the expensive parts itself — first the electric motor, and now the solar module. Each step backward down the supply chain captures a margin that a less-integrated rival has to pay away to a vendor. Understanding this is understanding why Oswal earns nearly 30% EBITDA on what is, on paper, a commodity product.

Each rung Oswal climbs — pump, then motor, then panel — pulls another supplier's margin inside the company. The solar-module line is the newest and the reason a pump maker can post a ~29.5% EBITDA margin.

The economics compound in two directions. On cost, making the motor and the panel removes vendor markups and insulates Oswal from price spikes in bought-in components — when module prices swing, an integrated maker feels it far less than an assembler. On competitiveness, a lower internal cost lets Oswal bid lower in a tender and still keep margin, which is precisely how you win share in a price-driven government auction. Integration, done at scale, is both a shield and a sword.

The catch integration always carries

The solar-module line is a real asset in a boom and a real liability in a lull. Panel manufacturing is capital-intensive and needs high utilisation to pay off; it only earns its keep if the pumps it feeds keep shipping in volume. If PM-KUSUM tendering slows, Oswal is left not just with idle pump capacity but with an idle panel plant too — a bigger fixed-cost base than a pure assembler would carry into the same downturn. So the integration that makes Oswal exceptional on the way up also makes its margins more operationally geared to volume on the way down. The 29.5% margin is not a permanent trait of the business; it is what integration delivers at current utilisation. Track the margin against tender volumes, not in isolation.

06

The concentration risk, told straight

What happens to the numbers when the tender flow slows — and the working-capital trap underneath.

Every strength in this business has a shadow, and it is the same shadow: one buyer, one scheme, one policy calendar. A company earning ~87% of revenue from PM-KUSUM is not diversified in any meaningful sense — it is a highly efficient supplier to a single, subsidised, government-run programme. That is fine while the programme expands. The investment question is entirely about what the earnings look like if, or when, it doesn't.

There are three distinct edges to the concentration, and they are worth separating rather than lumping into a vague "policy risk":

  • Volume risk — the tap. PM-KUSUM allocations are set annually and disbursed state-by-state. A slow tendering year, a budget squeeze, a shift in scheme priorities, or a state pausing between allocations can cut order flow sharply and suddenly — and there is no large non-scheme business to cushion the drop.
  • Price risk — the squeeze. Tenders are competitive and price-driven. As more manufacturers add solar-pump capacity to chase the same subsidy, bids get keener and margins can compress even if volumes hold. Today's ~29.5% margin assumes both healthy volumes and rational pricing; new capacity threatens the second.
  • Cash risk — the receivable. Government orders pay slowly. Oswal must buy materials, build systems and install them before the subsidy and farmer contributions arrive, tying up working capital for months. Rapid scheme-driven growth can therefore consume cash even as reported profit soars — which is exactly why the IPO's debt-repayment and the clean balance sheet matter so much.
Why concentration and working capital are the same problem

These two risks are usually discussed separately, but for Oswal they are one. Because the single customer is a government programme, the receivables are large, lumpy and slow — and because the business is concentrated, there is no steady retail cash flow to fund that gap. Grow fast into government tenders and you must finance an ever-larger pile of unpaid installations; hit a payment delay in a big state and the strain lands with no offsetting income stream. The scheme that drives the growth is the same scheme that stretches the balance sheet. A first-time reader should hold both facts together: the 87% is not just an earnings-quality question, it is a cash-conversion question, and the two compound each other.

The honest framing: none of this means the growth is fake — the pumps are real, the share is real, the margins are real. It means the earnings are higher-beta to a single policy line than the headline growth rate suggests. A diversified pump maker growing 30% is a different animal from a scheme supplier growing 88%, even at the same reported margin. The valuation has to price the concentration, not just the growth.
07

Exports and the retail base: the diversification that has to grow

The small, unglamorous businesses that decide whether Oswal is a pump company or a scheme trade.

If the concentration is the risk, the answer to it is the part of Oswal that isn't PM-KUSUM: its traditional retail pump-and-motor brand and its export business. Today these are small — together well under a fifth of revenue — but they are strategically the most important lines to watch, because they are what could turn Oswal from a subsidy supplier into a durable, cycle-resistant pump franchise.

The retail and distribution business is the original Oswal: household and agricultural pumps, monoblocks and motors sold through dealers under the brand. It is lower-margin and slower-growing than the tender business, but it has qualities the tender business lacks — recurring, cash-paying demand that doesn't depend on a government allocation and doesn't tie up months of working capital. A stronger retail base is the natural hedge against a PM-KUSUM slowdown.

The export business sells pumps and motors overseas, giving Oswal a demand pool entirely outside Indian policy. Exports are the cleanest form of diversification available to it — customers who buy on product and price, not subsidy — and building this leg is the most credible route to reducing the 87%. It is also where the vertical integration story travels well: an integrated, low-cost manufacturer is competitive in export markets in a way a high-cost assembler is not.

The number to watch that nobody leads with

If that share climbs over the next few years, Oswal is using the solar windfall to build a broader, self-funding pump franchise, and the business deserves to be valued as more than a scheme play. If it stays stuck in single digits while total revenue rides the subsidy, then however large the company gets, it remains a leveraged bet on one budget line. Growth can be flattering; diversification of that growth is the tell. This is the metric that separates the two futures.

08

Oswal vs Shakti: the head-to-head

The one comparison that matters — two winners of the same subsidy, built differently.

The natural comparison for Oswal is not a general pump maker like KSB or Kirloskar — it is Shakti Pumps, the other large PM-KUSUM beneficiary and the closest listed mirror. Both make pumps, motors and solar systems; both live substantially off the same scheme; both have seen earnings transformed by it. But they are not identical, and the differences are exactly what an investor is really choosing between.

The trade-off reads cleanly. Oswal offers the higher margin and the sharper growth, bought at the cost of deeper concentration and a shorter track record. Shakti offers a longer history and more diversification, at a lower margin. An investor choosing between them is really choosing between Oswal's integration-driven profitability-plus-concentration and Shakti's seasoned, broader-based but less profitable profile. They are not substitutes so much as two different risk-reward shapes on the same underlying theme.

The comparison that actually settles it

Put the two side by side and the real question is not "which grew faster" — in a scheme upcycle both grew fast — but "which is better placed when the scheme normalises." Shakti has already lived through a solar-pump cycle and carries more non-scheme revenue to absorb a lull; Oswal has a fatter margin and a cleaner post-IPO balance sheet but almost all its eggs in the KUSUM basket, and no public record of how it behaves in a down-tender year. Oswal's higher margin is the reward for its integration; its concentration is the risk that the very same integration amplifies. Whether that is a better bet than Shakti's steadier, lower-margin profile depends on one view: how long, and how smoothly, PM-KUSUM keeps disbursing.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded pump market, a narrower solar-tender arena.

India's pump market is large, old and competitive, with established brands spanning household, agricultural and industrial pumps. But the solar-tender arena Oswal actually competes in is narrower — it takes scale, in-house motors and solar know-how, and the appetite to fund slow-paying government receivables. Most legacy pump majors play mainly in the broader market; the PM-KUSUM fight is a smaller club.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, in the broad pump market Oswal is a mid-sized brand against giants like KSB and Kirloskar — it does not win on general-purpose scale. Second, in the solar-tender market it is a leader, because the specific mix it has assembled — in-house motors and panels, low bid cost, willingness to carry government receivables — is exactly what that arena rewards. The risk sits in the seam between those two facts: the very capacity that makes Oswal formidable in solar tenders is being copied. As legacy majors and new entrants add solar-pump lines to chase the same subsidy, the tender arena gets more crowded, bids get keener, and Oswal's exceptional margin faces its real test — not from a better pump, but from more bidders wanting the same allocation.

Financials & valuation

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A well-run integrator on a subsidy wave — asked to prove the wave is a tide.

Oswal enters its life as a listed company with genuine strengths: leadership in a growing solar-pump market, best-in-class margins from vertical integration, a clean post-IPO balance sheet, and a large addressable runway if India keeps solarising farm irrigation. The debate is not about whether the business is good — it clearly is, today. It is about whether earnings this concentrated in one policy programme deserve to be capitalised as durable.

The bull case

  • Leader in a structural shift — solarising farm irrigation is a multi-year national priority; Oswal has the largest share of the supply and a long tender runway ahead.
  • Best-in-class margins — ~29.5% EBITDA and mid-30s returns, powered by in-house motors and now solar modules that rivals must buy in.
  • Integration as a moat — owning the panel and the motor lets Oswal bid low and still keep margin, the exact edge a price-driven tender rewards.
  • Clean balance sheet — IPO proceeds repaid debt, giving room to fund the working capital that government orders demand.
  • Optionality beyond KUSUM — exports and retail can grow off the integrated, low-cost base, broadening the earnings if management pushes them.

The bear case

  • Single-scheme concentration — ~87% of revenue from PM-KUSUM; a tendering slowdown, budget squeeze or scheme change hits earnings with little cushion.
  • Margin under siege — as rivals add solar-pump capacity, competitive bidding can compress the very margin the valuation leans on.
  • Working-capital drag — government receivables are slow; fast scheme growth can consume cash even as reported profit soars.
  • Short public record — listed only since June 2025, with no visible history of how the business behaves in a down-tender year.
  • Peak-cycle risk — FY25 aligned peak volumes and peak integration benefit; the run-rate may prove hard to repeat once the scheme normalises.
The bottom line

Oswal Pumps is a well-run, deeply integrated pump maker that turned a government scheme into a remarkable two-year earnings surge — and then listed on the strength of it. The engineering is real, the margins are real, the share of PM-KUSUM is real. What is unproven is durability: whether a company earning nearly nine rupees in ten from one subsidy line can broaden its base before the scheme's cadence tests it. The next few years answer that in two checkable prints — the non-KUSUM share of revenue climbing off single digits, and the EBITDA margin holding through a full tender cycle as more bidders arrive. Watch those two figures each year; together they tell you whether Oswal is building a pump franchise that will outlast PM-KUSUM, or simply riding it exceptionally well while it lasts.
