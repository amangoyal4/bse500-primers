Borana Weaves, Surat's water-jet grey-fabric weaver

A complete walk through one of Surat's newest listed synthetic-fabric manufacturers — how a company turns crude-linked polyester yarn into undyed "grey" cloth on hundreds of high-speed water-jet looms, why the entire profit lives in a thin conversion spread multiplied by billions of metres, and how a five-year-old business rode the Surat man-made-fabric cluster from ₹42 crore to ₹389 crore of revenue and straight onto the exchange.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Borana Weaves makes synthetic grey fabric — plain, undyed, unfinished polyester cloth, sold by the metre. It is not a brand you will ever see on a shelf; it is the raw roll of fabric that a dyeing house, printer or garment maker buys and then colours, prints and cuts into saris, dress materials and dress-length "suit" fabrics. Borana buys polyester yarn, twists and heats it into a usable textured yarn, and then weaves that yarn into fabric on hundreds of high-speed water-jet looms in Surat, Gujarat — the town that produces the overwhelming majority of India's man-made-fibre fabric. It listed on the NSE and BSE in May 2025 after a heavily over-subscribed IPO, and it is very much a growth-and-capacity story rather than a mature, steady one.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a volume-and-spread business, not a brand. Grey fabric is a commodity — one weaver's plain polyester cloth is much like another's. Borana does not earn a fat margin on a differentiated product; it earns a thin conversion spread — the small gap between what its yarn costs and what its woven fabric sells for — multiplied across an enormous number of metres. That means the machine that matters is literally the loom: how many it runs, how busy it keeps them, and how cheaply it converts yarn to cloth.
  • The growth has been genuinely steep. Revenue went from about ₹42 crore in FY22 to ₹199 crore in FY24 and ₹389 crore in FY26 — roughly nine-fold in four years — with net profit rising even faster, to ₹64.6 crore in FY26 (up ~61% year on year). The shift from conventional weaving to modern water-jet looms, plus doing more of the yarn step in-house, is what widened the margin as the company scaled.
  • The whole story is capacity being added and filled. Borana had roughly 700 water-jet looms across three units at the time of listing, added a fourth unit through FY26, and has told investors it intends to roughly double capacity — adding around 5 crore metres of annual output — over about two years. The bet is straightforward: put more looms on the floor, run them hard, keep the spread intact. The risk stack is equally straightforward, and we spend the primer on it.
In plain terms

Borana Weaves is a high-volume job shop for the Surat fabric trade. It sits one step upstream of the colourful saris you actually see: it makes the blank canvas — plain white-ish polyester cloth — that thousands of Surat processors dye and print. Its profit is not a percentage of a fashion price tag; it is a few rupees of margin per metre, earned again and again on a loom that has to keep running. Scale it up while holding that per-metre spread and profits compound quickly, which is exactly what has happened. Let the yarn price spike without being able to raise cloth prices, or let a shiny new unit sit half-utilised, and the same maths runs painfully in reverse.

01

Grey fabric 101: how a plain roll of cloth earns money

No textile background needed — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

A length of woven fabric is made by interlacing two sets of threads at right angles — the lengthwise "warp" and the crosswise "weft" — on a machine called a loom. Before the fashion, the dye and the print, there is a plain, colourless roll of cloth straight off the loom. That raw, unfinished fabric is called grey fabric (also "greige"), and it is a product in its own right, traded in bulk. Borana sits on the making side of that step: it turns yarn into grey fabric and sells the roll onward. To follow the business, you need five terms.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)

Texturised yarn (PTY)POY that has been twisted and heat-set to become bulkier, stretchier and more fabric-like. Borana makes its own PTY in-house — a step back up the chain that both feeds its looms and is sold to others.

Conversion spreadThe margin between the cost of yarn going in and the price of grey fabric coming out. It is thin per metre and it is everything — a weaver's entire profit is this spread, held steady, times the volume of metres woven.
The economics that decide everything: spread per metre × metres

A grey-fabric weaver does not get paid for design, brand or finish — those margins belong to the dyers, printers and garment houses further down the chain. It gets paid a small, near-fixed conversion charge: the difference between yarn-in cost and fabric-out price, which mostly reflects the value of the weaving itself (machine time, power, labour, a modest profit). Because that per-metre number is small, the only way to make real money is volume — weave a great many metres — and the only way to protect the margin is efficiency: run modern looms, keep them running near full tilt, buy yarn cheaply, and waste as little as possible. That is why loom count, loom utilisation and yarn cost are the three levers that matter more than anything a marketer could do.

Who buys grey fabric

  • Dyeing & printing houses — the "processors" who take plain grey cloth and turn it into coloured, patterned fabric. In Surat these run in their thousands, and they are Borana's direct customers.
  • Traders & wholesalers — the fabric-market middlemen who buy grey or finished cloth in bulk and move it on to garment makers across India.
  • Garment & sari manufacturers — the end of the domestic chain, converting finished fabric into saris, dress materials, kurtis and dress-length "suit" sets — the mainstay of India's mass ethnic-wear market.
02

What Borana actually makes: yarn, texturising and the loom floor

The value chain — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Borana's business is a short, tightly linked chain: buy polyester yarn, make it fabric-ready, weave it into grey cloth, and hand that cloth to the Surat processing ecosystem. The company has quietly extended itself backwards a step — making its own texturised yarn — which both feeds its looms and becomes a second product to sell. Here is the flow.

Borana owns Stages 2 and 3 — texturising and weaving. It buys Stage 1 (yarn) and deliberately does not do Stage 4 (dyeing/printing), which is a different, effluent-heavy, capital-different business.

The two revenue lines, decoded

  • Grey fabric (~84% of revenue) — the core. Borana weaves plain synthetic cloth on its water-jet loom floor and sells it in bulk to processors and traders. This is the volume engine — the metres that fill the looms and drive the top line. It is also the commodity part: undifferentiated, price-sensitive, and dependent on running efficiently rather than pricing cleverly.
  • Texturised yarn / PTY (the balance) — the backward-integrated line. Borana texturises POY into PTY, uses most of it in its own looms, and sells the surplus. This does two jobs at once: it lowers the cost of the yarn feeding its fabric (capturing the texturiser's margin in-house) and adds a second saleable product. It is the reason the company's margins widened as it scaled.
Why the water-jet loom is the real advantage

Borana's edge is less about what it makes than how it makes it. Older Surat weaving ran on slower, higher-maintenance looms; Borana built out on modern water-jet looms, which shoot the weft across with a jet of water. For smooth polyester filament yarn, that means very high speed, uniform fabric with fewer defects, low waste and modest maintenance — in short, more good metres per machine per day at lower cost. On a product whose profit is a thin spread per metre, that efficiency is the margin. The move from conventional to water-jet weaving is precisely what took Borana's EBITDA margin from a razor-thin weaver's number up toward the low-twenties percent, and it is why "how many water-jet looms, running how hard" is the first question to ask about this company.

What Borana is not: it is not a dyeing, printing or garment company, and it does not sell a finished branded product. It stops at the grey roll. That keeps it out of the effluent-and-compliance headaches of processing and out of fashion risk — but it also means it has no brand, no consumer pricing power, and no downstream margin. It lives entirely on manufacturing efficiency in a plain-vanilla intermediate good.
03

The company, the family, and the IPO

A young, promoter-driven Surat business that scaled fast and then listed.

Borana Weaves is a recent, family-promoted company, not an old textile house. It was incorporated in October 2020, grew explosively through the post-COVID synthetic-textile boom, converted to a public company in 2024, and came to market in May 2025. Its short life is both its appeal — the growth is real and recent — and a caution: it has never yet weathered a full down-cycle as a listed company.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the business is controlled by the Borana family (the promoter group behind the Borana name in Surat), who retain the large majority of the equity after a fresh-issue IPO — meaning the promoters did not sell down; the company raised new money and diluted modestly. That keeps interests aligned but also concentrates control; watch related-party dealings closely (see below).
  • All-fresh IPO: the entire ₹144.9 Cr issue was new shares, so the cash went into the company (new unit + working capital), not into promoters' pockets. For a capacity-hungry weaver, that is the right shape of raise.
  • Balance sheet: net worth rose sharply to ~₹282 Cr at FY26 (from ~₹88 Cr a year earlier), lifted by the IPO proceeds and retained profit; total assets roughly ₹366 Cr. The company runs some working-capital debt, as weavers must, but the raise materially de-risked the balance sheet going into the expansion.
A related-party wrinkle worth flagging: Borana has historically sourced a meaningful share of its key raw material — POY yarn — from promoter-group entities, and its supplier base is geographically concentrated in Gujarat. That is common in tight-knit Surat trade, and it can aid supply reliability, but it also means an investor should read the related-party-transaction notes carefully: yarn is the single biggest cost line, so the price at which it is bought from connected parties directly shapes the reported conversion spread.
04

The Surat synthetic-fabric cluster and Borana's place in it

Why geography is destiny for this company — for better and worse.

You cannot understand Borana without understanding Surat. The city is the beating heart of India's man-made-fibre (MMF) textile industry — by common industry estimates it produces the large majority of the country's synthetic fabric — a dense, self-contained ecosystem of yarn makers, texturisers, weavers, processors (dyers and printers) and fabric traders packed into a few square kilometres. Everything a synthetic-fabric business needs is next door: yarn suppliers, thousands of processing houses, the wholesale fabric markets, skilled loom operators and machinery dealers. Borana is a weaver in that cluster — one of the volume producers of grey cloth that feeds the processors.

Being inside the cluster is a genuine operating advantage. Yarn arrives quickly and cheaply; a woven roll can be sold to a processor down the road with almost no logistics; machinery, spares and labour are all local; and demand signals travel fast through the market. It is why a young company could scale from ₹42 crore to nearly ₹400 crore so quickly — it plugged into an existing, humming supply chain rather than building one.

The double edge of the cluster

The same density that helps Borana also boxes it in. Roughly 98% of its revenue comes from within Gujarat — overwhelmingly the Surat trade. That is superb for logistics and working capital, but it means the company's fortunes are tied to one regional ecosystem: a demand slump in the Surat fabric market, a local power-cost shock, a labour or effluent-related disruption to nearby processors, or a burst of new loom capacity from rivals a street away all land directly on Borana with little geographic buffer. A weaver in the middle of the cluster enjoys the cluster's efficiency and inherits the cluster's concentration — there is no diversification to hide behind.

There is also a competitive-structure point. The Surat weaving base has historically been fragmented — vast numbers of small "powerloom" units, many on older machines. Borana's proposition is to be one of the organised, modern, water-jet players: bigger, more efficient, more bankable, and — post-IPO — better capitalised than the typical family loom shed. The opportunity it is chasing is the gradual shift of grey-fabric volume from thousands of tiny, sub-scale weavers toward a smaller number of efficient, larger ones. Whether that consolidation actually accrues to Borana, rather than to any of the other ambitious weavers doing the same thing, is one of the real open questions.

05

Looms × utilisation × spread: the whole company in one equation

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a commodity weaver.

Most companies you can size up with a story about pricing, brand or a product cycle. A grey-fabric weaver is refreshingly, brutally mechanical. Borana's profit is essentially the product of three numbers: how many looms it runs, how hard it runs them (utilisation), and the thin conversion spread it earns per metre. Get those three, and you have very nearly modelled the business. Everything else is detail.

All four units ran in the high-70s to mid-80s percent in FY26 — genuinely high for weaving. The newest unit (Unit 4) sits a touch lower as it ramps, which is exactly what you'd expect and exactly the number to watch as new capacity is added.

Read the equation left to right. Looms is capacity — Borana's ~700 water-jet looms are the denominator of everything, and adding looms (new units) is how it grows the metre count. Utilisation is discipline — a loom sitting idle still costs rent, interest and depreciation but weaves nothing, so keeping utilisation in the 80s (as FY26 showed) is what turns capacity into cash. And spread is the fragile bit — the few rupees of margin per metre between yarn cost and fabric price, which Borana can widen only through efficiency (water-jet looms, in-house yarn, low waste) because it has no power to simply charge more for a commodity.

The device that only fits a weaver like Borana

Value here is not a multiple on a brand; it is looms × utilisation × spread-per-metre, and each term breaks differently. Add looms but leave them under-filled, and utilisation falls — you have bought capacity that dilutes returns. Keep the looms full but let polyester spike without passing it on, and the spread compresses — you weave record metres for thinner profit. Hold spread and utilisation but stop adding looms, and growth simply stops. The reason FY26 worked so well is that all three moved the right way at once: more looms (Unit 4), high utilisation (high-70s to mid-80s), and a spread widened by in-house texturising. The reason a weaver is risky is that the same three terms can just as easily move the wrong way together — which is why this is a company you track quarter by quarter on capacity, utilisation and gross margin, not on a story.

A bear is betting that adding capacity that fast will, at some point, outrun demand (utilisation falls) or that a polyester swing will squeeze the spread before the new metres are even sold. There is no fudging it with narrative — the numbers show up every quarter.

06

The capacity ramp: the IPO growth story

Why this is a "build looms and fill them" stock — and where that can go wrong.

Borana raised fresh money for one overriding reason: to add capacity. The IPO was structured as an all-fresh issue with the bulk earmarked for a new manufacturing unit and for working capital to feed it. Post-listing, management has been explicit that the plan is to keep going — targeting a roughly doubled production base, on the order of an extra 5 crore metres a year, over about a two-year horizon. This is the engine of the entire equity story: more looms, more metres, more spread-dollars — provided the market absorbs the extra cloth at a healthy utilisation.

The appeal is obvious: a commodity weaver's profit scales with volume, so if Borana can install looms and keep them busy at a stable spread, profit compounds mechanically. FY26 is the proof of concept — Unit 4 was commissioned and, crucially, ran at ~78% utilisation in its first year while the older units stayed in the low-to-mid-80s. That is the pattern a capacity-led story needs: new capacity that fills up rather than sitting idle.

The risk that hides inside a growth ramp

Capacity expansion in a commodity is a double-edged sword, and it is worth being blunt about it. Every loom Borana adds, its neighbours in Surat can add too — the machines are available to anyone with capital, and the IPO has just given Borana the capital while the visible growth invites others in. If the whole cluster expands weaving capacity faster than downstream demand grows, the result is textbook: utilisation slips and the conversion spread thins for everyone, because plain grey fabric has no way to defend price. So the capacity plan is not automatically good news — it is good news only if Borana's new looms are filled at a spread that holds. The metric that tells you which world you're in is utilisation on the expanded base, quarter after quarter — not the size of the announced plan.

A genuine surprise: a chunk of Borana's reported profit has not come from weaving at all. In FY24, electricity-cost subsidies (a Gujarat state incentive for textile units) contributed on the order of ₹7.5 crore — roughly a third of that year's net profit. Power is a major cost for a loom floor, and the subsidy materially flatters the bottom line. It is a real benefit, but a policy-dependent one: an investor should mentally separate the "weaving" profit from the "subsidy" profit, because the two carry very different durability. If that incentive were trimmed, the reported margin would look meaningfully different even with the looms running exactly as well.
07

Backward integration into texturised yarn

The quiet move that widened the margin — and why it matters more than it looks.

A plain weaver buys finished, texturised yarn and simply converts it to cloth — earning only the weaving spread. Borana took a step back up the chain: it makes its own polyester textured yarn (PTY) in-house, from POY. That single decision is a big part of why its margins are better than a bare weaver's, and it changes how you should think about the business.

The mechanism is straightforward. Between raw POY and weavable yarn sits the texturising step — twisting and heat-setting the filament so it becomes bulkier and fabric-ready. Normally a weaver pays a texturiser for that; the texturiser keeps a margin. By owning texturising machines, Borana captures that margin itself, lowers the effective cost of the yarn feeding its looms, controls quality and timing, and — because it makes more PTY than it needs — sells the surplus as a second product line. On a business whose profit is a thin per-metre spread, capturing an extra slice of the chain is one of the few levers that genuinely moves the margin.

Why integration is the margin story, not a side note

Because grey fabric is a commodity, Borana cannot widen its spread by charging more — the market sets the cloth price. It can only widen the spread by lowering its own cost of making a metre, and there are just a handful of ways to do that: faster, more efficient looms (water-jet); low waste and high utilisation; and owning more of the yarn step so less margin leaks to suppliers. In-house PTY is the third of those. It is the difference between being a pure convert-and-hope weaver and a slightly more integrated manufacturer that keeps two margins instead of one. The flip side: integration also means Borana now carries yarn-processing risk and capital alongside weaving risk — when polyester prices move, it feels the swing on a larger portion of the chain, not just on the cloth.

The nuance to hold: in-house yarn is a margin enhancer, not a moat. Plenty of Surat players texturise their own yarn; it is good practice rather than a unique advantage. It helps explain why Borana's margins are where they are, but it does not, on its own, protect those margins from a cluster-wide capacity glut. Integration improves the odds on the "spread" term of the equation; it does not remove the risk that the term compresses if everyone weaves more at once.
08

Working capital and the crude-linked raw material

The two forces that can turn a good weaving year into a cash-strained one.

Two things sit underneath every weaver's results and quietly decide how comfortable the year feels: the price of polyester yarn (the raw material) and the working-capital cycle (how much cash is tied up in yarn, cloth and unpaid customer bills at any moment). Both are structural features of the business, not one-off events, and both cut against the smoothness of the growth story.

Raw material: polyester runs back to crude oil

Borana's dominant cost is polyester yarn (POY/PFY), and polyester is a petrochemical — its price traces back through polyester chips and their feedstocks to crude oil. So the single biggest input can swing with oil markets, petrochemical supply, and import dynamics, none of which Borana controls. When yarn prices fall, a weaver can enjoy a temporarily fatter spread (cloth prices are stickier than yarn for a while); when they rise sharply, the spread compresses unless and until higher cloth prices stick — and on a commodity, they may not. This is why Borana's margin is not a fixed dial: it flexes with the gap between yarn cost and cloth price, and that gap is partly hostage to crude.

Why working capital is the weaver's real ball-and-chain

Grey-fabric weaving is working-capital-hungry. Borana buys yarn, holds it as inventory, weaves it into cloth (more inventory), sells on roughly 30-day-ish credit, and only then collects. For a fast-growing weaver this is a treadmill: every extra crore of sales needs another slug of cash locked up in yarn, work-in-progress and receivables before the profit arrives. That is exactly why a slice of the IPO was earmarked for working capital, not just for machines. The danger as the company doubles capacity is that receivables and inventory balloon alongside revenue — so the number to watch is not just profit but whether cash conversion keeps pace. A weaver can look wonderfully profitable on the P&L while its cash is quietly disappearing into an ever-larger pile of unsold cloth and unpaid invoices.

There is a concentration angle here too. Borana's supplier base is heavily Gujarat-centred (with a meaningful share historically from promoter-group entities), and its customer base is overwhelmingly regional. That keeps the working-capital cycle short and local in good times, but it also means a regional shock — a payment squeeze rippling through the Surat trade, a yarn-supply disruption, a demand air-pocket in the fabric market — hits both ends of the cash cycle at once. Concentration cuts the working-capital cycle when things are calm and amplifies it when they are not.

09

The industry and the competitive shape

A fragmented weaving base, a modernising edge, and no pricing power to defend.

Grey-fabric weaving in Surat is not a consolidated industry with a few dominant names — it is the opposite: a highly fragmented base of thousands of small and mid-sized powerloom and water-jet units, plus a layer of larger, organised manufacturers. Borana competes to be firmly in that larger, modern layer. Understanding the competitive shape is really about understanding what Borana can and cannot defend.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two features define this market. First, fragmentation with low entry barriers to capacity — a water-jet loom is a buyable machine, so any well-funded player, Borana included, can add metres; that keeps the whole industry from ever earning fat, defensible margins on plain cloth. Second, no pricing power — because grey fabric is undifferentiated, no weaver can charge a premium; the only durable edge is being a lower-cost, better-run producer than the fragmented average. Borana's whole thesis is that modern looms, in-house yarn, scale and a clean post-IPO balance sheet let it sit comfortably below the industry cost curve and take share from sub-scale rivals. That can be true and still leave it exposed to cluster-wide overcapacity — being the most efficient weaver is worth a great deal in a tight market and much less in a glutted one.

A few things stand out in the record. The margin expansion is real and mechanistic — it traces to the move to water-jet looms and in-house texturising, not to a one-off. Returns on capital were unusually high pre-IPO (an FY24 ROE near 49%), which is rare for weaving and partly why the IPO drew ~149× demand; that ratio naturally steps down after raising fresh equity, but the underlying asset-efficiency is good. And the balance sheet is now clean — the fresh-issue proceeds funded the new unit and working capital, so the company enters its expansion self-funded rather than stretched.

The one number that settles the thesis: utilisation on the expanded loom base, read against polyester and receivables

At roughly ₹859 crore of market value and about 13× FY26 earnings, the market is paying a growth multiple for a plain-fabric weaver — which only makes sense if Borana keeps compounding as it doubles capacity. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is utilisation on the enlarged loom base — can the new looms be filled near the ~80% the old ones ran at? — read alongside two companions: the conversion spread / gross margin (is it holding as polyester moves and as cluster capacity grows?) and the cash-conversion cycle (are receivables and inventory rising faster than sales?). If new looms fill up at a steady spread and the cash keeps coming in, the profit ramp is durable and the multiple is fair. If utilisation on the fresh capacity sags, or the spread thins in a supply glut, or working capital swallows the growth, a growth-priced weaver re-rates quickly. Metres woven flatter the top line; filled looms at a held spread, turned into collected cash, are the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A fast, efficient young weaver being asked to prove it can double without breaking the maths.

Borana Weaves enters its next phase with real momentum: a proven ability to run modern looms at high utilisation, margins that widened as it scaled, a clean post-IPO balance sheet, and an explicit plan to roughly double capacity. The open question is not whether the business works — the record shows it does — but whether the three terms of the equation stay healthy through a doubling, in a commodity where nobody has pricing power and everyone can buy the same looms.

The bull case

  • Proven efficient operator — water-jet looms run at ~80%+ utilisation with margins that rose as the company scaled; a genuinely well-run weaver, not a promise.
  • Margin from integration — in-house texturised yarn captures an extra slice of the chain, lifting the spread above a bare weaver's.
  • Clean, funded balance sheet — an all-fresh IPO put cash into new looms and working capital; the expansion is self-funded, not debt-stretched.
  • Cluster advantage — sits inside Surat's dense MMF ecosystem, with fast, cheap access to yarn, labour, machinery and thousands of processor customers.
  • Consolidation runway — a fragmented, small-powerloom base leaves room for a modern, organised, well-capitalised player to take share.

The bear case

  • Commodity with no pricing power — plain grey fabric is undifferentiated; the spread is thin and defended only by efficiency, never by price.
  • Capacity-glut risk — the same looms are available to every rival; a cluster-wide build-out can sink utilisation and spread just as Borana adds its own metres.
  • Concentration — ~98% of revenue from Gujarat, a Gujarat-centric (and partly promoter-group) supplier base, and no long-term customer contracts.
  • Raw-material & working-capital swings — polyester is crude-linked and the biggest cost; growth locks up ever more cash in yarn, cloth and receivables.
  • Young, subsidy-aided, growth-priced — no full down-cycle track record; state power subsidy flattered past profit; a growth multiple on a commodity weaver has little cushion if utilisation or spread slips.
The bottom line

Borana Weaves is a rare thing — a commodity weaver that has actually run its machines well enough to widen margins while growing nine-fold, then listed with a clean balance sheet to do more of it. That is worth respecting; most Surat looms never escape thin, cyclical returns. But the company is now attempting the hardest version of its own model: doubling loom capacity in a market where extra cloth has no natural price defence, where polyester can move the spread from under it, and where growth ties up ever more cash. The next milestone that matters is concrete and near: the utilisation the newly added looms actually achieve as the ~5-crore-metre expansion comes on stream. If those fresh looms fill up near the ~80% the existing floor runs at, without the spread thinning, the ramp keeps compounding and the valuation earns itself. If they run half-empty, the market will learn — quickly and in the gross margin — that a commodity weaver's growth is only as good as the looms it can keep full.
