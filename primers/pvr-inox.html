00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

PVR INOX runs cinemas. Not studios, not streaming, not television — the physical business of selling a seat in a dark, air-conditioned room, and then selling the person in that seat a tub of popcorn and a cola. It was formed in March 2023 by merging PVR Ltd (India's first multiplex operator, founded by Ajay Bijli) with INOX Leisure (the Jain family's cinema arm), creating by a wide margin the country's largest multiplex chain — roughly 1,750+ screens across about 360 properties in 110-plus cities, several times the size of the next competitor. Around 150 million people walked through its doors in FY26.

Four facts frame everything that follows:

  • It keeps only about half of every ticket. Box office is the biggest revenue line (~₹3,650 Cr, approx., in FY26), but a large slice — commonly 45–50% of net ticket revenue — is paid straight back to the film's distributor as their share. The cinema is, in effect, renting out its screen and splitting the gate. What it does not split is the popcorn.
  • Food, drink and advertising carry the profit. Food & beverage was roughly a third of revenue in FY26 (~₹2,200 Cr, approx.) but runs at a gross margin north of 75%, and advertising (~₹450 Cr, approx.) is close to pure margin. Add them together and the concession counter plus the pre-show reel plausibly generate more gross profit than the films themselves — on well under half the revenue.
  • The cost base does not move. Mall rent, staff, electricity, projection and property maintenance are booked whether the auditorium is full or holds four people. That is severe operating leverage: FY26 revenue rose ~17% and profit swung by roughly ₹614 Cr — from a ₹281 Cr loss to a ₹333 Cr profit — on essentially the same screens, the same leases and the same payroll.
  • It doesn't choose the movies. The single biggest driver of a cinema chain's year is the film slate — how many big, wide-release titles the industry actually puts out, and whether audiences show up for them. PVR INOX has no control over that. It builds the theatres; other people decide what plays in them, and when.
The one-paragraph version

PVR INOX is closer to a hospitality-and-real-estate operator with a food business attached than to a media company. It signs 15–20 year leases on mall floorspace, fits it out with seats and projectors, and then waits for the film industry to give people a reason to come. When the slate is strong, the incremental admit walks past a popcorn counter with ~75% margins and drops almost straight into operating profit. When the slate is weak, the rent, the staff and the electricity bill arrive on exactly the same schedule. That asymmetry — fixed costs meeting variable demand it cannot influence — is the whole business, and it is why the same 1,750 screens can print a loss one year and a record profit the next.

01

Cinema exhibition 101: who takes what from a ₹300 ticket

The film value chain, and where an exhibitor actually sits in it.

A film moves through three distinct sets of hands before it reaches you, and each takes a cut. Understanding the split is the single most useful thing a first-time reader can learn about this sector, because it explains why a "blockbuster year" for Indian cinema does not translate one-for-one into profit for the company showing the blockbuster.

Money flows right-to-left on the ticket: the audience pays the cinema, the cinema pays the distributor a share, the distributor pays the producer. But food, drink and advertising money stops dead at Stage 3 — none of it is shared backwards. That single asymmetry drives most of what follows.

Why the sliding scale matters more than the headline gross

Distributor shares are not flat. A big film typically commands the highest share in its opening week — when the crowds are largest — and the share steps down each subsequent week. The perverse consequence for an exhibitor: the most crowded, most exciting week of a mega-release is also the week in which the cinema keeps the smallest proportion of each rupee. A film with long legs that keeps decent occupancy into weeks three and four can be worth more to PVR INOX per admit than a front-loaded event film that empties out after ten days — even though the second one dominates the headlines. Exhibitors quietly prefer a steady drumbeat of watchable mid-budget films to one gigantic release surrounded by empty weekends.

The Indian market, in context

  • India is under-screened, not over-screened. The country has on the order of 9,000 screens (approx.) for 1.4 billion people — a handful per million residents, against something like 120 per million in the United States and roughly 55 per million in China. On paper that is enormous headroom.
  • But most screens are single screens, and they are dying. The majority of India's screens are old stand-alone cinemas, many of them shutting each year. Multiplex screens have been growing while total screens have been flat or falling — the market is converting, not expanding.
  • PVR INOX is the consolidator. With ~1,750 screens it holds under a fifth of India's screen count but a far larger share of organised box office — its premium, urban, higher-ticket-price locations punch well above their number. It is the default release partner for Hollywood in India and for premium-format Hindi releases.
  • Ticket prices are geographically bifurcated. A metro premium format can carry a four-figure ticket; a tier-3 screen may run at a fraction of that. Blended ATP of ₹280 hides an enormous internal spread, and the mix between them moves the average as much as any price increase does.
02

Four revenue lines, four completely different margins

Read this once and the rest of the company falls into place.

PVR INOX reports its revenue in a handful of buckets, and the reason to care is that they behave nothing like each other. One is huge and half-shared. One is medium-sized and enormously profitable. One is small and almost pure profit. One is a rounding error that isn't quite a rounding error. Getting the mix right is most of the analysis.

1 · Box office — big, visible, and only half yours

Ticket sales are the headline. They are also the line over which the company has the least control: PVR INOX can set prices within limits and can programme its screens intelligently, but it cannot manufacture a hit. Box office revenue is reported net of GST but gross of the distributor's share, which then appears further down as "distributor's share" — a direct cost that scales almost exactly with the revenue. That accounting matters: a year in which box office revenue jumps 20% does not deliver 20% more gross profit, because roughly half of the increase walks straight out of the door to the film's owner.

2 · Food & beverage — the profit engine in a paper bucket

Popcorn, nachos, cola, coffee, and increasingly a full quick-service menu. The raw ingredient cost of a bucket of popcorn is famously trivial relative to its price; industry gross margins on cinema F&B run comfortably above 75%. Crucially, none of it is shared with the distributor. Every rupee of incremental F&B spend is the cinema's alone, and because the fixed cost of the counter and its staff is already paid, a large share of that gross margin falls to operating profit. This is why the industry obsesses over SPH — spend per head — and why PVR INOX keeps widening its menu, pushing combos at the point of ticket purchase, and installing self-order kiosks. FY26's SPH of ₹147 (up ~10%) and Q4's ₹165 (up ~32%) are, in profit terms, worth far more than the equivalent percentage move in ticket price.

3 · Advertising — small line, outsized profit

Before the film starts, PVR INOX sells four-ish minutes of a captive, demographically attractive, phone-down audience to brands. There is essentially no incremental cost to running an ad reel in a room that was going to be occupied anyway, so advertising revenue converts to profit at a very high rate — call it 85–90% (approx.). It is also the most cyclical and slate-sensitive line in the business: advertisers buy inventory when they know audiences will be there, so a quarter with a thin release calendar sees ad revenue fall harder than footfalls do. Q3 FY26 illustrated it precisely — ad revenue around ₹118 Cr, down ~21% year-on-year, explicitly attributed to fewer marketable films.

4 · Convenience fees and other income

Online booking fees, in-cinema sponsorship and brand activations, screen rentals for events and corporate use, and gaming/other ancillary formats. Individually small; collectively a few hundred crore of largely high-margin revenue. The convenience fee in particular is a recurring subject of consumer-forum and regulatory attention, so it should not be modelled as permanent at current rates.

The mix insight most first-timers miss

An investor reading only the revenue line will conclude that PVR INOX is a box-office business with a snack bar attached. The gross-profit line says something close to the opposite. Because roughly half of box office is contractually owed to distributors while F&B and advertising are retained almost in full, the company's gross profit is split something like evenly between "showing films" and "selling things to people who came to watch films". Every strategic move the company makes — bigger menus, premium formats, loyalty programmes, kiosks — is aimed at the second half, because that is the half it actually owns.

The five numbers that describe a cinema chain

Footfalls, ATP, SPH, occupancy, screens — and how they multiply together.

Exhibition has a refreshingly simple operating model. Almost the entire revenue line can be rebuilt from five disclosed numbers, which is unusual and very useful: it means you can sanity-check the company's results yourself, and it means a change in any one of them has a traceable, arithmetic consequence.

Multiply them and the P&L appears: admits × ATP = box office; admits × SPH = F&B revenue; add advertising and other income and you are within a few percent of the reported top line. Do that for FY26 — 150 million admits, ₹280 ATP (gross of ticket GST), ₹147 SPH — and you land close to the reported ₹6,646 Cr. The model is legible in a way that very few businesses are.

Why 28% occupancy is not the disaster it sounds like

A hotel running at 28% would be in administration. A cinema at 28% can be perfectly healthy, because the number is computed across every seat in every show all week — including the 11 a.m. Tuesday screening that nobody was ever going to attend. Demand is savagely concentrated into Friday evening through Sunday night and into holiday windows. The practical consequence is that the marginal weekday seat is nearly worthless while the marginal weekend seat is precious, which is why the industry has moved toward dynamic pricing, cheap-Tuesday schemes and subscription passes: the goal is not to fill the weekend (it fills itself when the film is good) but to put somebody in a Tuesday afternoon seat who will then buy popcorn at a 75% margin.

A useful per-screen frame

Divide 150 million admits by roughly 1,754 screens and 365 days and you get about 234 admits per screen per day (approx.) — call it a couple of hundred people through each auditorium daily, across four or five shows. That is the operating heartbeat of the company, and it is worth carrying into the next section, because the cost side can be expressed in exactly the same unit.

04

The merger, the group, and who controls it

Two rival chains, one boardroom, and a promoter group that owns less than a third.

PVR INOX exists because two competitors decided, during the worst stretch the Indian cinema business has ever had, that scale was the only defence. The merger completed in March 2023: INOX Leisure was merged into PVR Ltd, the combined entity renamed PVR INOX Limited, and the two founding families became joint promoters. The old PVR listing continued — which is why the BSE scrip code, 532689, still dates from PVR's original listing.

Timeline

  • 1997
    PVR opens India's first multiplex — a four-screen cinema at Saket, Delhi, as a joint venture between Ajay Bijli's Priya Exhibitors and Australia's Village Roadshow (hence P-V-R). It introduces Indian audiences to assigned seating, multiple simultaneous shows and a proper concession counter.
  • 1999–2002
    INOX Leisure is set up by the Inox / Gujarat Fluorochemicals group (the Jain family) and opens its first multiplex in Pune. A second national chain is born.
  • 2006
    PVR lists on the Indian exchanges — the origin of the BSE code 532689 that PVR INOX still trades under.
  • 2012–2018
    The consolidation decade. PVR acquires Cinemax (2012) and DT Cinemas (2016); INOX absorbs Satyam Cineplexes and others. Two chains pull decisively away from the rest of the field.
  • 2020–2021
    Covid. Cinemas shut for the best part of two years — the single most damaging event in the industry's history. Both chains burn cash, raise equity and debt, and watch studios send films directly to streaming platforms while the doors are bolted.
  • Mar 2023
    The merger completes. INOX Leisure merges into PVR; the entity becomes PVR INOX Ltd with ~1,700 screens — roughly four times its nearest rival — betting that scale delivers better distributor terms, better mall rents and central-cost synergies.
  • FY24–FY25
    The hard part. A patchy Hindi release calendar and post-Covid habit change leave the enlarged fixed-cost base badly under-fed. FY25 ends in a net loss of ~₹281 Cr. The company pivots to screen rationalisation (closing chronic underperformers), tight capex, debt reduction and an asset-light / franchise-operated expansion model.
  • FY26
    The slate delivers. Record revenue ₹6,646 Cr (+17%), reported EBITDA ~₹2,095 Cr, PAT ₹333 Cr versus the prior year's loss, admits ~150 mn, ATP ₹280, SPH ₹147. Net debt down to roughly ₹365 Cr at end-December 2025, more than ₹1,000 Cr lower than at the merger.

Ownership

  • A promoter group with a minority stake. The combined promoter group — the Bijli family (PVR's founders) plus the Jain family and associated Inox-group entities — holds roughly 27.5% (approx.). Within that, the Bijli family's own holding is small, in the region of 6% (approx.), following a partial stake sale in 2024. The rest of the register is institutional and public.
  • Why that matters. A company whose founders own a low-to-mid-twenties percentage collectively is materially more exposed to institutional pressure and, in theory, to corporate action than a typical Indian promoter-dominated firm. It also means governance and capital allocation are argued in public, on quarterly calls, rather than settled inside a family holding company.
  • The two cultures. The merger joined a Delhi-headquartered, brand-premium operator (PVR — Director's Cut, IMAX partnerships, Gold Class) with a Mumbai-headquartered, value-and-scale operator (INOX). The group is now run as a single national chain with multiple brand tiers, and the retained dual branding is deliberate: different formats, different price points, different catchments.
The merger's actual logic, stated plainly: an exhibitor's bargaining power comes from being unavoidable. A distributor releasing a film nationally cannot skip a chain that controls a fifth of India's screens and a much larger share of premium urban box office; a mall developer wants the anchor tenant that pulls weekend footfall through the whole property. Scale was meant to buy better distributor terms, better rent deals, one head office instead of two, and common procurement on everything from projectors to popcorn kernels. What scale explicitly could not buy is more films — which is why FY24 and FY25 were painful despite the combination working exactly as designed on costs.
05

The meter that runs whether anyone shows up

The core mechanic of this company, expressed in one unit: rupees per screen per day.

Everything difficult and everything exciting about PVR INOX comes from one structural fact: the cost of opening the doors is fixed, and the revenue that walks through them is not. The clearest way to feel it is to convert both sides of the P&L into the same unit — a single screen, a single day.

Take the fixed block. Across roughly 1,754 screens, PVR INOX carries an annual burden of mall rent and common-area maintenance, cinema employee costs, electricity and water, projection and content costs, repairs, insurance and property overheads. On a base of ~₹6,646 Cr of revenue, that fixed block plausibly runs in the region of ₹2,400–2,600 Cr a year (approx., estimated — the company does not disclose it in this form). Spread across 1,754 screens × 365 days — about 640,000 screen-days — that is somewhere around ₹38,000 per screen per day, before a single ticket is torn.

The bar that never moves is the first one. The device: 640,000 screen-days, priced in advance

PVR INOX begins each financial year having already committed to roughly 640,000 screen-days of rent, payroll and power. Those commitments were signed years earlier, in lease negotiations with mall developers, and they are indifferent to whether the coming twelve months contain four national blockbusters or none. The company then spends the year discovering — from the outside, along with everyone else — how many of those screen-days the film industry will actually fill. That is why the FY25 → FY26 comparison is so violent: the same screens, the same leases, the same staff, and a ₹614 Cr swing in profit, from a ₹281 Cr loss to a ₹333 Cr profit. Nothing structural changed inside the company between those two years. The release calendar changed.

This is operating leverage in its purest observable form, and it runs in both directions with equal force. In a good year the incremental admit costs almost nothing to serve — the auditorium is lit, the projector is running, the staff are rostered — so the ~₹126 retained from the ticket plus the ~₹110 gross margin on the popcorn drops nearly intact into operating profit. In a bad year the same machinery idles at full cost. There is no lever to pull. A cinema chain cannot furlough a lease.

06

Hostage to somebody else's release calendar

The dependency that makes this business genuinely hard to forecast.

Most companies control at least one side of their demand. A bank sets rates and chooses whom to lend to. A carmaker decides which vehicles to launch. A retailer picks its range. PVR INOX picks none of the products it sells. Its entire demand curve is produced, financed, cast, scheduled and marketed by third parties who owe it nothing — studios and producers in Mumbai, Hyderabad, Chennai and Los Angeles who decide, for their own reasons, how many big films to release and in which months.

The consequences run deeper than they first appear:

  • Forecasting is close to impossible beyond one quarter. Release dates move constantly — a film slips a month and takes an entire quarter's earnings with it. A strike, a production delay, a censorship dispute or a star's illness can move ₹100 Cr of quarterly EBITDA at a company with no ability to influence any of those events.
  • Quality cannot be hedged. A slate can be numerically full and commercially dead. Ten mediocre wide releases fill the calendar and empty the auditoriums. The industry's own hit rate — the share of releases that recover their cost — is low and unstable.
  • Clustering wastes capacity. Distributors crowd releases into a handful of prized windows: Diwali, Christmas, Eid, summer holidays, long weekends. Two giant films landing on the same date cannibalise each other while the following six weeks run near-empty — a schedule that maximises studio competition and minimises exhibitor utilisation.
  • Regional cinema has become the swing factor. Telugu, Tamil, Kannada and Malayalam films now regularly deliver national-scale box office, which broadens the slate and reduces dependence on Hindi output. PVR INOX's South Indian screen presence is therefore strategically important in a way it was not a decade ago.
PVR INOX owns the theatres. It does not own a single frame of what plays inside them.
The surprise: 1,750 screens, zero demand control

Here is the fact that unsettles most people who look at this company for the first time. PVR INOX has spent three decades and thousands of crores building the largest cinema estate in India — prime mall real estate, premium formats, a national loyalty base, a brand a billion people recognise. And it has approximately no influence over how many people want to use it in any given year. Compare that with almost any other capital-intensive Indian business: a cement plant can price to fill its kilns; a hotel can discount to fill its rooms; an airline can drop fares to fill seats. PVR INOX can cut prices all it likes — if there is no film worth watching this month, the seats stay empty at any price. Its capacity utilisation is set by an industry it does not belong to. That is not a risk factor buried in the annual report; it is the defining characteristic of the asset.

There is a partial defence, and the company has been building it: reduce the cost of a weak month rather than pretend it can be avoided. Alternative content — live sport, concert films, gaming events, corporate hires, regional re-releases, anime and event screenings — puts something profitable on screens that would otherwise run half-empty. Re-releases of beloved catalogue films have proven surprisingly effective, because the print costs nothing to acquire and the audience buys popcorn at the same margin as a first-run crowd. None of this replaces a blockbuster. All of it lifts the floor.

The corollary for anyone reading the quarterly print

Because the slate drives everything, PVR INOX's quarter-to-quarter results are close to meaningless as a trend signal. A blowout quarter usually means two big films landed in it; a terrible quarter usually means they did not. Q3 FY26 saw advertising revenue fall ~21% on "fewer marketable films"; Q4 FY26 swung to a ₹186 Cr profit with ATP up 22% on a premium-heavy slate. Neither told you anything durable about the company's competitive position. The signals worth extracting from a quarter are the ones that are not slate-driven: SPH, the trajectory of net debt, screen additions versus closures, and cost per screen. Those reflect management. Admits reflect the film industry.

The popcorn carries the picture

On these estimates, roughly 40% of PVR INOX's revenue produces more than half of its gross profit, and that 40% has nothing to do with cinema as an art form. The film is close to a pass-through: the company collects the ticket, hands about half of it to the distributor, and keeps the rest to cover its rent. The margin — the part that actually pays for the business — comes from a paper bucket of popcorn, a cup of aerated drink, and four minutes of brand messaging shown to a room that has nowhere else to look. This is not a cynical reading; it is the operating reality that every exhibitor in the world runs on, and it explains why so much management energy goes into menu engineering, kiosk deployment, combo pricing at checkout and loyalty-linked F&B offers. Each rupee of SPH is worth roughly three times a rupee of ATP to the bottom line.

Which is why these three levers get all the attention

  • Raise SPH. Broader menus (beyond popcorn into hot food, gourmet and beverages), point-of-sale combo attachment when the ticket is booked, in-seat ordering in premium formats, and self-service kiosks that reduce queue abandonment. FY26's SPH of ₹147 was a record; Q4's ₹165 showed what a premium-format-heavy quarter can do.
  • Raise ATP through format, not price. Selling the same film in a premium format — recliners, large-format screens, luxury tiers — lifts ATP without a headline price rise and without alienating the mass audience. Format mix has done more of the ATP work than raw price increases, which matters because raw price increases are politically fraught (see section 12).
  • Sell more advertising minutes at better rates. A scaled national chain can offer advertisers a genuinely national, targetable, premium-demographic reach — the one part of the business where the merger's scale directly creates pricing power. Ad revenue recovery is one of the clearest post-merger synergies available, and its softness in FY26 (a ~21% year-on-year fall in Q3) is one of the more fixable disappointments in the results.
08

Screens, malls, and the asset-light turn

Why the strategy shifted from "add screens" to "add the right screens, with someone else's money".

For twenty years the growth formula in Indian exhibition was straightforward: find a new mall, sign a long lease, spend ₹2–3 crore a screen on fit-out, open, repeat. FY24 and FY25 demonstrated the flaw in that formula — every new screen adds fixed cost immediately and revenue only if the slate cooperates. PVR INOX has visibly changed course.

Three shifts, all pointing the same way

  • Screen rationalisation. The company has been actively closing chronically underperforming screens — old properties, weak catchments, duplicated PVR/INOX locations in the same micro-market post-merger. Gross additions of roughly 100 screens in a year are partly offset by closures, so the net screen count has been broadly stable rather than climbing. That is a deliberate choice, and a reversal of two decades of habit: for the first time the chain is optimising for profit per screen rather than screen count.
  • Capital discipline. Capex per screen has been pushed down, expansion concentrated in high-return locations, and free cash flow directed at the balance sheet. Net debt fell to roughly ₹365 Cr by December 2025, down more than ₹1,000 Cr since the merger. For a business this operationally leveraged, low financial leverage is not conservatism — it is survival equipment for the next weak-slate year.
  • The asset-light / franchise model. Under franchise-owned, company-operated arrangements, a developer or partner funds the capital cost of building and fitting out the cinema; PVR INOX brings the brand, the programming, the F&B operation and the management, and shares the economics. It grows the estate — and the F&B and advertising base that rides on it — without consuming the company's own capital or adding a new owned-lease liability to the balance sheet.
The quiet significance of the franchise pivot

An asset-light screen changes the shape of the risk, not just the funding. In the traditional model, a screen that underperforms is a fifteen-year lease obligation the company must carry, service and eventually write down. In the franchised model, much of that capital risk sits with the partner who put up the money, while PVR INOX still captures the high-margin lines it cares most about — concessions, advertising and its share of the box office. It is the exhibition industry's version of the hotel sector's move from owning buildings to managing them, and it is the most consequential strategic change at the company since the merger itself. It also implicitly concedes something important: management no longer believes every incremental screen is worth owning outright.

The mall dependency

A multiplex cannot be built anywhere. It needs a large, column-free floor plate with the right ceiling heights, adequate parking, and — crucially — foot traffic. In practice that means a mall, which means PVR INOX's expansion is capped by India's rate of quality mall construction, a variable set by real-estate developers and the credit cycle. This is a genuine two-way relationship: the cinema is usually the anchor that pulls weekend crowds through the entire property, giving the chain real negotiating leverage on rent and fit-out contributions, especially for a developer trying to lease a new project. But it also means that in a year when few good malls complete, there are simply not many places to put a new screen, no matter how much capital is available.

Watch the closures as carefully as the openings. A press release announcing "100 new screens" is only half the story if 60 were shut in the same period. For PVR INOX in its current phase, net screen additions, and more importantly revenue and EBITDA per screen, tell you far more about management execution than gross openings do. A shrinking-but-more-profitable estate is a legitimate and arguably preferable outcome here.
09

The OTT question and the shrinking window

The structural threat — stated precisely, rather than as a slogan.

The obvious bear argument is that streaming kills cinema. The reality is more specific and, for an exhibitor, more uncomfortable than the slogan: streaming has not stopped people going to the movies for genuine events, but it has substantially raised the bar for what counts as an event — and it has done so by shortening the time a film stays exclusive to theatres.

The window

The theatrical window is the gap between a film's cinema release and its availability on a streaming service or television. Historically this ran to eight weeks or more in India; through and after the pandemic it compressed dramatically, with many films reaching streaming platforms within roughly four weeks and some sooner. The arithmetic for a consumer is brutally simple: if a film will be on a subscription service already paid for within a month, the decision is not "cinema or nothing" but "now, at ₹300 plus ₹150 of popcorn plus parking and travel — or free, at home, in four weeks". For a tentpole spectacle, most people still choose now. For an average mid-budget drama, an increasing number wait.

The version of this argument worth taking seriously

The weak form of the bear case — "nobody goes to cinemas any more" — is empirically wrong: 150 million admits in FY26, up 10%, with record ticket prices and record concession spend. The strong form is harder to dismiss. It says that streaming has not removed the audience so much as removed the middle of the release calendar: the routine, watchable, mid-budget films that used to keep screens ticking over between the giants. If cinema-going narrows permanently to a dozen event weekends a year, then a chain with 640,000 cost-committed screen-days is carrying capacity for a demand pattern that no longer exists — and the correct response is a smaller, more premium, more asset-light estate. That is precisely the strategy PVR INOX has adopted, which suggests management substantially agrees with the strong form of the argument.

The industry and the rivals

Dominant in organised exhibition, and competing against the sofa.

On screen count, PVR INOX has no true peer in India — the merger produced a chain several times the size of its nearest organised competitor. Its real competition comes from two very different directions: smaller chains nibbling at specific geographies, and home entertainment competing for the same evening.

What dominance does and does not buy

Scale gives PVR INOX three concrete advantages: distributors cannot realistically bypass it on a national release, mall developers court it as an anchor tenant, and advertisers can buy a genuinely national premium audience in one transaction. Those are real and defensible. What scale conspicuously fails to deliver is protection from the sector's central problem. Being the biggest exhibitor in a year with a thin release calendar means owning the most idle screens in the country — dominance amplifies the slate cycle rather than damping it. That is the unusual competitive position here: PVR INOX has effectively won its industry, and winning it does not insulate the P&L from a quiet quarter at the box office.

One further nuance worth holding: because the organised market is so concentrated, PVR INOX's numbers are frequently read as a proxy for the health of Indian theatrical film itself. That works both ways. When the company reports record admits, it is genuine evidence that the theatrical habit survived the pandemic and the streaming boom. When it reports a soft quarter, it is often measuring the film industry's output rather than its own execution — and the market does not always separate the two.

Financials & valuation

A real turnaround year — and an EBITDA figure that needs a warning label.

FY26 was the year the merged company finally worked: record revenue, a return to full-year profit, record ticket prices and record concession spend, and net debt at its lowest since the merger. Before drawing conclusions from the multiples, though, the lease-accounting caveat has to be dealt with, because it is unusually distorting for a cinema chain.

The Ind-AS 116 warning label

Under Ind-AS 116, long leases are capitalised: instead of a rent expense, the company recognises a right-of-use asset and a lease liability, then books depreciation on the asset and interest on the liability. For an ordinary manufacturer this is a technicality. For a cinema chain — whose largest single cost is mall rent on 15–20 year leases — it transforms the P&L. Rent disappears from above the EBITDA line and reappears below it, which inflates reported EBITDA enormously while pushing reported losses deeper in bad years.

Why the ~31% EBITDA margin is not what it looks like

FY26's reported EBITDA of roughly ₹2,095 Cr on ₹6,646 Cr of revenue works out to a ~31% margin — a number that would make PVR INOX look like a high-margin consumer business. It is not. Strip the lease effect back out and the pre-Ind-AS 116 EBITDA is far lower — in the region of ₹700–800 Cr, or roughly 11–12% (approx., estimated) — which is the figure that actually reflects the economics of running cinemas after paying the landlord. The company itself reports quarterly EBITDA "without the impact of Ind AS 116" for exactly this reason: Q4 FY26's ₹142 Cr on ₹1,547 Cr of revenue is about 9%. When comparing PVR INOX to anything else, or across time, use the pre-Ind-AS number — and when computing enterprise value, add the lease liabilities (several thousand crore) to the debt, or the multiple will be flattering nonsense.

Both calculations are defensible; only one of them is honest about what the company pays its landlords.

The number that settles it: admits, and the rupees each one leaves behind

Everything in this primer reduces to a single test — footfalls, and the spend per head that rides on them, showing up in the pre-Ind-AS 116 EBITDA margin. Not screen count, not revenue growth, not the reported 31% margin. Admits are the only variable large enough to move a 640,000-screen-day cost base, and SPH is the multiplier that decides how much profit each of those admits leaves behind. Watch three linked figures each quarter: admits per screen per day (is the estate being used?), SPH (is the high-margin line still compounding, or was FY26's ₹147 a slate-driven fluke?), and pre-Ind-AS 116 EBITDA margin (does the operating leverage actually reach the bottom line?). If admits hold near FY26 levels across a merely average release year while SPH keeps rising and the pre-lease margin holds double digits, the business has structurally re-based and the current price is undemanding. If FY26's admits prove to have been borrowed from an exceptional slate, the same fixed costs will reassert themselves within two quarters.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A repaired, consolidated, disciplined chain — still waiting on a calendar it does not write.

PVR INOX enters FY27 in genuinely better shape than at any point since the merger: profitable, deleveraging, disciplined on capex, closing bad screens, expanding through partners' capital rather than its own, and with record ATP and SPH. What has not changed, and cannot be changed by management, is the source of its demand.

The risks, stated without softening

  • Slate dependency — the defining risk. A weak release year is devastating and essentially unforecastable. FY25 is the proof: the same estate, the same team, a ₹281 Cr loss. There is no hedge, no forward book, and no management action that substitutes for a full calendar of films people want to see.
  • Structural OTT substitution and the shrinking window. If theatrical windows compress further, or the mid-budget theatrical film continues to disappear into streaming, the addressable number of cinema-going occasions per year shrinks permanently — and a fixed-cost estate sized for the old pattern becomes over-built.
  • Operating leverage in reverse, magnified by lease accounting. The same mechanics that produced a ₹614 Cr positive swing produce equally violent negative ones. Ind-AS 116 makes reported losses look worse in bad years, which affects sentiment, covenants and the optics of every downturn.
  • Regulation. Several states have imposed or debated ticket-price caps; GST on tickets and on in-cinema food has changed more than once and is a live policy variable; courts and consumer forums have repeatedly examined convenience fees and rules on carrying outside food and water into cinemas. Each of these directly attacks a high-margin line, and none of them is within the company's control.
  • Capex and mall supply. Growth in owned screens is gated by the pace at which quality malls actually get built. Fit-out capital is significant, payback periods are long, and a screen opened into a weak slate year begins its life underwater.
  • Competition and substitution. Cinepolis, Miraj and regional chains compete locally; single screens still anchor pricing in smaller towns; and the living room competes for every evening.
  • Valuation ambiguity. The multiple you compute depends on the EBITDA definition you accept and whether lease liabilities enter enterprise value. Two analysts can reach "cheap" and "expensive" from the same annual report without either making an arithmetic error.

The bull case

  • Unmatched scale in a consolidating market — ~1,750 screens against a nearest rival a fraction of the size, with the premium estate, the IMAX and large-format partnerships, and the only truly national cinema ad inventory.
  • The high-margin mix is compounding — record SPH of ₹147 (₹165 in Q4) and near-pure-margin advertising mean each admit is worth more than it used to be, independent of ticket prices.
  • Operating leverage cuts upward too — FY26 turned a ₹281 Cr loss into a ₹333 Cr profit on ~17% revenue growth. A second consecutive decent slate would drop through with the same force.
  • Balance sheet repaired — net debt down to ~₹365 Cr from over ₹1,400 Cr at the merger, giving the company the ability to survive a weak year without dilution.
  • Asset-light expansion — franchise-operated screens grow the F&B and advertising base using partners' capital, structurally improving return on capital employed.
  • Structural under-screening — India's screens-per-million remains a fraction of comparable markets, and single-screen closures keep converting demand toward organised multiplexes.

The bear case

  • No control over demand — the year is decided by a release calendar written by studios who owe the exhibitor nothing. Forecasting beyond a quarter is guesswork.
  • The window keeps shrinking — roughly four weeks to streaming for many films removes the urgency that fills weekday shows, and the mid-budget theatrical film is disappearing.
  • Fixed costs are contractual and long — 15–20 year mall leases plus payroll and power do not flex with a bad slate; Ind-AS 116 makes the resulting losses look larger still.
  • Regulatory attack on the profitable lines — ticket-price caps, GST changes, convenience-fee litigation and outside-food rules all target exactly the margins the company depends on.
  • Flattered reported margins — a ~31% headline EBITDA margin collapses to roughly 11–12% once rent is treated as the cost it is.
  • Low promoter holding — a combined ~27.5% promoter stake means less founder skin in the game than most Indian large-caps, and more exposure to external pressure.
  • FY26 may have been a slate peak — records in ATP, SPH and admits in the same year invite the question of what an ordinary year now looks like.
The bottom line

PVR INOX has now done everything an exhibitor can do to itself: it merged with its only real rival, closed the screens that did not work, cut its debt by more than a thousand crore, pushed ticket prices and concession spend to records, and started growing on other people's capital. The result was a record year and a return to profit. The part it cannot do anything about is the part that decides the outcome — whether, in the twelve months from April 2026, the Indian and international film industries put enough films worth leaving the house for into the calendar. So the checkable milestone is narrow and specific: through FY27, watch whether admits per screen per day hold near FY26's ~234 in a quarter without a marquee release, and whether SPH keeps climbing past ₹147 while pre-lease EBITDA margin stays in double digits. A company that can fill an ordinary Tuesday has re-based its economics. A company that only fills a blockbuster weekend has simply had a good year at the movies.
