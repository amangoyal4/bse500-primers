00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Aarti Industries Limited (AIL) is a Gujarat-based specialty chemicals maker that buys basic petrochemical feedstock — above all benzene — and converts it, through many demanding chemistry steps, into hundreds of higher-value derivatives that feed agrochemicals, pharmaceuticals, dyes and pigments, polymer additives and fuel additives. It is one of the few companies anywhere that operates at world or India scale in a handful of these specific chemistries, and its edge is depth: it makes its own intermediates rather than buying them, which is where the cost and reliability advantage lives.

Four facts frame everything:

  • The core molecule is benzene. Benzene accounts for roughly 60% of raw-material / revenue exposure — Aarti's economics are a benzene-derivatives story far more than a diversified-chemicals story. Almost everything downstream is a bolt-on to that six-carbon ring.
  • Two ways it earns. Commodity-style specialty sales that swing with global demand and Chinese competition; and prized multi-year, take-or-pay long-term contracts with global majors — the annuity-like crown jewel that most people miss.
  • Where it is in the cycle. A severe down-cycle — Chinese oversupply and dumping plus a weak global agrochemical market — pushed margins and returns to a trough (ROCE ~5.7%) and was bad enough that the company withdrew its FY25 guidance. The stock de-rated with it.
  • What it now is. After spinning off its pharma arm as Aarti Pharmalabs, AARTIIND is a purer-play specialty and agro-intermediates chemicals company — cleaner to read, but with one fewer shock absorber, and controlled throughout by the founding Gala family.
In plain terms

Aarti sits one rung above an oil refinery and several rungs below the shelf. A refiner splits crude into building-block molecules; Aarti takes one of those blocks — benzene — and does the patient, hazardous, capital-heavy chemistry that a Syngenta or a BASF would rather outsource. It does not sell you a product you recognise; it sells the ingredient inside the ingredient inside the thing you buy. And here is the catch that runs through the whole file: because Aarti buys benzene and sells benzene-derivatives, its profit is not the price of either — it is the spread between them, and that spread is the most volatile number in the business.

01

What Aarti actually makes

From a tanker of benzene to a shelf of derivatives — the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

Benzene is a colourless liquid that comes out of a refinery or a naphtha cracker. On its own it is a cheap, hazardous commodity. Its value is that it is a ring of six carbon atoms onto which a chemist can bolt other groups — a nitro group, a chlorine, an amine — one reaction at a time. Each bolt-on step raises the value and narrows the field of companies that can do it safely at scale. Aarti's business is that sequence of reactions, run at industrial scale, on the same molecule, for four decades.

Aarti runs benzene (plus toluene and nitric acid) through a chain of well-understood but demanding chemistries. Read the flow once and the company clicks into place.

The end of that line is a family of building blocks with unlovely names and large uses: nitrochlorobenzenes (NCB), nitrotoluenes, phenylenediamines (PDA), hydrogenated products and a long tail of downstream molecules. These are not consumer goods; they are the ingredients that agrochemical, pharma and dye companies buy by the tanker to make the next thing.

Why the names matter less than the position

You do not need to remember NCB from PDA. What matters is that in several of these specific molecules Aarti is one of the largest producers in India — and in a couple, among the largest anywhere. Scale in a narrow chemistry is the whole game: it lets you undercut smaller rivals on cost and out-supply them on reliability, and it is exactly the kind of scale a global buyer wants to lock up under a long-term contract. The danger of the chemistry is a feature, not a bug — hazardous, capital-heavy processes are precisely the ones a Western major would rather buy from a proven partner than run itself.

02

Two engines under one roof

Spot specialty sales vs multi-year contracts — conflating them is the commonest mistake made about Aarti.

Aarti earns in two very different ways, and conflating them is the most common mistake made about the company. One engine is loud and cyclical; the other is quiet and annuity-like. Both run off the same plants and the same chemistry, but they behave nothing alike — and the whole re-rating case rests on the mix shifting from the first to the second.

The two revenue engines, side by side
EngineHow it pricesWhat sets volumeBehaviour
Spot specialtyMarket spread over benzene, moves quarter to quarterGlobal demand + Chinese supplyCyclical; margin swings hard
Long-term contractsPre-agreed, often take-or-pay, formula-linked to feedstockCustomer's pre-committed volumeAnnuity-like, sticky, protected

The spot engine is what most people picture when they hear "chemicals company": Aarti makes a specialty intermediate, sells it into a global market, and the price it captures rises and falls with demand and with how much product Chinese producers are pushing out that year. In a good year, spreads are fat and the plants mint cash. In a bad one — like the current down-cycle — Chinese oversupply drags prices toward cost and the same plants earn a fraction as much.

The contract engine is the one that makes Aarti more than a commodity chemicals company. Global majors — the big agrochem and specialty players — sign multi-year, take-or-pay supply contracts: the customer pre-commits to buy a defined volume every year for five, ten, even twenty years, and sometimes funds a dedicated plant that Aarti builds specifically for them. In exchange the customer gets guaranteed supply from a low-cost, proven partner outside China. Aarti gets a floor under its volumes and a large chunk of its capex de-risked before a brick is laid.

In plain terms

The spot business is a fishing boat — some seasons the nets are full, some they come up light. The contract business is a landlord's rent roll — the tenant signed for ten years and paid to fit out the unit. A company that is only the boat trades like a commodity. A company adding more rent roll every year is trying to trade like something better. Aarti is trying to do the second while the market, in a trough, is judging it as the first.

The strategic question for the next several years is simply the mix: how fast the contracted, annuity-like layer grows relative to the volatile spot layer. Every dedicated plant that fills up shifts a little more of Aarti's earnings from "fishing boat" to "rent roll" — and that, more than any single quarter's spread, is what a durable re-rating would be built on.

03

The benzene spread is the whole story

The single most important idea in the entire investment case — and it is unique to this company.

Here is the single most useful lens on Aarti, and the one most easily missed: the company's fortunes do not hinge on what benzene costs, and they do not hinge on what a derivative sells for. They hinge on the gap between the two — the spread Aarti captures for converting one molecule into a more valuable one. Get this one idea and the volatility, the trough, and the valuation trap all fall into place.

This distinction is not academic. Because roughly 60% of Aarti's input cost is benzene, and because much of its output is priced with reference to benzene, a rise or fall in the benzene price itself largely washes through. When benzene goes up, input cost rises but so does the realisation on the derivative; when it falls, both drop. What Aarti actually keeps is the conversion spread — the value it adds through nitration, chlorination, hydrogenation and the rest. So the number to watch is not "benzene at $X a tonne" or "NCB at $Y a tonne." It is the difference — and how it moves when Chinese producers flood or starve the market.

The plant, headcount and tonnage barely change across the cycle, yet the money Aarti keeps roughly triples from trough to peak. That amplitude is why a specialty-chemicals stock can look cheap and expensive on the same earnings within eighteen months.

This is why the current trough is so instructive. Aarti's revenue in FY26 was ₹9,018 Cr — not a collapsed number. Volumes largely held; the plants ran. What cratered was the spread. Chinese producers, sitting on their own overbuilt capacity and a weak domestic economy, exported aggressively and compressed the gap that Aarti lives on. Same molecules, same tonnes, far less kept — and a ROCE that fell to roughly 5.7%.

The device that only fits Aarti: benzene in → spread kept → derivative out

Most companies you can track with one operating number — a price, a volume, a subscriber count. Aarti needs you to hold two prices and ignore both, watching only the distance between them. Because the spread is far more volatile than either volume or the benzene price, Aarti's earnings amplitude is much larger than its revenue amplitude: revenue can wobble by a tenth while profit halves and returns fall by two-thirds. Anyone valuing the company off a single trough year — in either direction — is valuing the spread on the day, not the business across the cycle. The order book of a normal company tells you demand; for Aarti, only the conversion spread tells you whether the demand was worth anything.

The non-obvious consequence: a chemical whose selling price and buying price move together looks like a hedge — and in the benzene level, it is. The exposure that is not hedged is the spread, and that is the one thing that swings hardest. Aarti is, in effect, long the conversion margin and roughly flat the commodity. That is a very different risk than "the price of chemicals," and it is why the stock trades on the cycle in spreads, not on the cycle in oil.
04

The Gala family, and the company that left

Promoter control, four decades of it — and the pharma demerger that reshaped what you're buying.

Aarti was founded in 1984 and has been run throughout by the Gala family, still the promoter group. It is a first-generation-built, family-operated industrial house of the kind that dominates Indian specialty chemistry — patient, engineering-led, and comfortable committing capital years ahead of the demand it is betting on. That temperament is the whole reason the company can spend heavily through a trough; it is also the reason to keep an eye on governance and succession, as with any promoter-controlled industrial.

The most important corporate event of recent years was structural, not operational: Aarti demerged its pharmaceuticals business into a separately listed company, Aarti Pharmalabs. What remains under the AARTIIND ticker is now a cleaner, purer-play specialty and agro-intermediates chemicals company. The pharma cash flows, the pharma capex and the pharma cycle all moved out from under this entity.

  • Why it matters for an investor. The company you are analysing today is not the diversified Aarti of a few years ago. Comparisons to older financials must be adjusted for the fact that a whole division walked out of the door. AARTIIND is now a more concentrated bet on the benzene-derivatives chain.
  • Cleaner, but more exposed. A pure specialty-chemicals company is easier to understand and value — and also has one fewer shock absorber. When the specialty cycle turns down, there is no pharma leg to lean on.
  • Family stewardship. The Gala family's long tenure cuts both ways: deep operating knowledge and long-horizon capital allocation, set against the usual governance and succession questions that attend any promoter-controlled industrial.
Read the demerger before you read the financials

A pure-play is a sharper instrument. Before the demerger, a soft specialty quarter could be cushioned by pharma, and vice versa; a reader could look at group numbers and never quite see either cycle cleanly. Post-demerger there is nowhere to hide — the AARTIIND you value today is the benzene chain, undiluted, in a specialty trough. That makes it a purer expression of the thesis in both directions: sharper on the way up, and with no second leg to steady it on the way down. Any comparison to Aarti's pre-2023 growth record is comparing two different companies.

05

The down-cycle that forced a company to stop guiding

China, agrochemicals, and a withdrawn forecast — the single most important context for reading Aarti today.

Specialty chemicals is a cyclical industry, but the last down-cycle was worse than a normal one, and it is the single most important context for reading Aarti today. Two forces hit at once, and they compounded.

  • Chinese oversupply and dumping. China built enormous chemical capacity in the prior up-cycle. When its own demand softened, that capacity did not disappear — it was exported. Chinese producers pushed product into global markets at prices that compressed spreads worldwide, and Aarti's spread-dependent economics felt it directly.
  • A weak agrochemical cycle. Aarti's largest end-market, crop protection, went through a sharp destocking and demand slump globally. Agrochem customers had over-ordered in the prior boom, then stopped buying to run down inventory — so even reliable volumes softened at the same time spreads were falling.

The combination was severe enough that Aarti did something companies do only reluctantly: it suspended and withdrew its FY25 guidance. For a business that had grown structurally for years, telling the market "we can no longer forecast the year" was an admission of how little visibility the dumping-driven air-pocket had left. Margins compressed, ROCE fell toward 5.7%, and the stock de-rated from the premium multiple it had long enjoyed.

A structural grower that withdraws guidance is not the same as a structurally broken business. It is a signal about the weather, not the climate — but the market, in a trough, tends to price the weather as if it were permanent.

By the fourth quarter of FY26 there were early signs the worst had passed: Q4 EBITDA margin recovered to roughly 15.6%, well above the ~13% blended level for the full year, suggesting the spread had begun to normalise off the bottom. Full-year EBITDA of ₹1,172 Cr was up around 15%, and PAT of ₹419 Cr up around 27% — but that PAT growth is off a genuinely weak base, and should be read as "off the floor," not "back to trend." The recovery is visible; it is not yet confirmed.

Weather vs climate — why the distinction is the trade

Everything in a trough looks terminal because a trough is, by construction, the point of maximum pessimism. The discipline this company demands is separating the cyclical from the structural: Chinese dumping and agrochem destocking are weather — painful, real, but historically self-correcting as excess capacity clears and inventories rebuild. What would make it climate is if the plants Aarti has built never fill because the demand simply is not there. The Q4 margin tick is the first data point on the weather side of the ledger. One quarter is not a season — but it is the difference between "the spread is recovering" and "the spread is gone."

06

The contract a customer walked away from — and paid to

A cancellation that made Aarti money — and revealed how the crown-jewel contracts actually behave under stress.

Here is a fact that tends to surprise even people who follow the sector: one of the most valuable episodes in Aarti's recent history was a customer cancelling a contract on it.

In 2020, a global customer walked away from a multi-year supply arrangement worth roughly ₹4,000 Cr over ten years — one of the largest long-term contracts Aarti had ever signed. On the face of it, losing a decade of committed volume sounds like a disaster. It was not, and the reason is exactly what makes the contract model powerful.

  • Aarti kept the compensation. Because these are take-or-pay style arrangements with a customer that had pre-committed and helped fund dedicated capacity, the cancellation triggered a payout. Aarti retained an estimated US$120–130 million (approx.) in compensation.
  • Aarti kept the plant. The dedicated capacity built for that contract did not vanish; it could be redeployed to other customers and products, so the asset kept working after the counterparty left.
  • The customer paid to leave. The structure meant the risk of the customer changing its mind sat substantially with the customer, not with Aarti — the opposite of how a commodity sale behaves.
Why this is the tell. The episode reveals the real economics of Aarti's crown-jewel contracts. When they run, they are an annuity. When they break, they can break in Aarti's favour, because the counterparty has pre-paid, co-funded, and taken the volume risk. A commodity chemicals company has none of that protection. This is the clearest single piece of evidence that the contract engine is genuinely different in kind from the spot engine — not just a steadier version of it.
The surprise, and what it does and doesn't prove

A cancelled contract that made money is the most vivid possible demonstration that Aarti's long-term deals carry structural protection a spot sale never does — the counterparty pre-committed, co-funded the plant, and absorbed the downside of its own change of mind. That is the good half. The other half, held for the risk section: a walked contract is a windfall on the way out but a lost annuity thereafter, and not every dispute will resolve so favourably. The 2020 episode proves the model has teeth; it does not prove every future dispute bites in Aarti's direction.

07

The capex cycle building the next volumes

Roughly ₹1,125 Cr a year, spent ahead of the demand — the bull case and the bear case in one sentence.

While the spread was collapsing, Aarti did not stop building. FY26 capex was around ₹1,125 Cr, and the multi-year capital cycle is the mechanism meant to drive the next volume leg once the cycle turns. This is the classic specialty-chemicals bet: spend heavily through the trough so the capacity is ready when demand returns — and wear depressed returns in the meantime for having done so.

The money is going into three broad buckets, each with a different return profile:

Where the capex goes, and when it pays
Capex bucketWhat it addsWhen it pays
New specialty capacityMore tonnage in existing, proven chemistriesWhen the spot cycle recovers and plants fill
New chemistry chainsEntirely new value chains / molecules Aarti did not make beforeAs new products qualify with customers — longer-dated
Dedicated long-contract plantsCapacity built for a specific pre-committed customerOn commissioning — largely de-risked before the build

The financial cost of this is visible in the balance sheet: net debt-to-equity of roughly 0.72. That is a real number — not alarming for an asset-heavy chemicals company mid-build, but not trivial either, and it is the price of spending through a trough. The debt was taken on to build capacity that is not yet earning its keep, which is precisely why returns look so poor right now: the denominator (capital employed) has grown while the numerator (profit) sat at the bottom of the cycle.

In plain terms — why ROCE is depressed for two reasons at once

Return on capital is depressed for two reasons at once, and only one of them is the cycle. The first is the thin spread. The second is arithmetic: Aarti has poured capital into plants that have not started earning. A ROCE of 5.7% is what you get when you divide trough profit by a capital base swollen with half-finished bets. As those plants commission and fill, the same profit recovery lands on a base that stops growing — which is why the ROCE recovery, when it comes, can be sharp rather than gradual. The capex that is crushing returns today is the same capex that could snap them back tomorrow.

The risk in this is equally plain: heavy capex only creates value when the plants fill. Capacity that commissions into a still-weak market earns nothing and still carries its depreciation and interest. The capex cycle is the bull case and the bear case in the same sentence — it is the source of the next volume leg, and the source of the returns drag if demand is slow to arrive.

Where all those derivatives end up

Five industries buy the benzene chain — and one structural tailwind sits over all of them.

Aarti's molecules disappear into other companies' products, which is why the brand is invisible to consumers and central to a surprising number of the things they use. Five end-markets absorb the benzene-derivative output, and the mix is part of what makes the company both defensive and cyclical at once.

  • Dyes & pigments — nitro and amine intermediates feed colourants for textiles, coatings and plastics. India is a global dye hub, and Aarti supplies the chemistry upstream of it.

Overlaying all five is the structural tailwind Aarti bulls lean on hardest: China+1 and friend-shoring. Global agrochem, pharma and specialty buyers have spent the last few years trying to reduce dependence on Chinese supply — for resilience, for ESG, and for geopolitics. A proven, low-cost, integrated Indian producer of exactly these chemistries is a natural beneficiary.

The irony sitting at the centre of the story

The same Chinese producers whose dumping caused the current trough are the ones China+1 is meant to route customers away from. So Aarti is simultaneously the victim of Chinese oversupply in the spot market today and the intended beneficiary of the world's wish to de-risk away from China over the next decade. Both forces are live at once, pulling in opposite directions, and which one dominates in any given year is much of the debate around the stock. The bear points at the dumping on the screen; the bull points at the contracts being signed to avoid the next round of it.

The numbers, and how to value a trough

FY26 results and the valuation problem — a premium multiple sitting on depressed earnings.

Read cold, Aarti's FY26 looks like a recovery year: revenue up double digits, EBITDA up ~15%, PAT up ~27%. Read in context, it is a business at the bottom of its returns cycle showing the first signs of a turn. Both readings are true, and the gap between them is the entire valuation question.

Note that capex (~₹1,125 Cr) is running at roughly the level of full-year EBITDA — the company is reinvesting almost the entire operating cash engine into the next volume leg while profit sits at the bottom.

The awkward feature of the stock is that it is a structural grower that de-rated inside a trough, which produces a genuinely confusing optics problem: a premium price-to-earnings multiple sitting on depressed earnings. The high multiple is not necessarily the market paying up for quality — it is partly the mechanical result of dividing a still-elevated valuation by a profit number scraping the bottom. On mid-cycle earnings, the same price would look far more ordinary; on peak earnings, cheap. On today's trough earnings, expensive.

This is the trap in valuing any deep cyclical on spot earnings, and it is acute here because — as the spread section showed — Aarti's earnings amplitude is enormous relative to its revenue. The company can look statistically expensive at the bottom and statistically cheap at the top while the underlying business barely changes. Neither snapshot is the truth; the truth is the earning power across a full cycle, which no single year reveals.

The metric that settles the thesis

Not the P/E, and not any one quarter's profit. The case for Aarti is proved or broken by the recovery in the benzene-to-derivative spread flowing through to ROCE — the climb off ~5.7% back toward the mid-teens — as the ~₹1,125 Cr/year of new plants commission and fill and the contracted layer grows. Volume growth plus spread normalisation plus rising asset-turns on that swollen capital base is the whole thesis in one chain. If ROCE grinds back to the mid-teens, the current multiple was cheap on trough earnings; if it stalls near the trough, the plants were built into a market that never showed up, and the premium was a mirage. Watch the spread per tonne and the ROCE line — everything else is downstream of those two.

Peers, and where Aarti stands

The Indian specialty-chemicals bench, plus the Chinese producers who actually set the spread.

Aarti competes in a crowded and generally high-quality Indian specialty-chemicals field, and — crucially — against Chinese producers whose behaviour sets the spread in Aarti's spot business more than any Indian rival does. Reading the peer set correctly means separating the companies Aarti is benchmarked against from the ones that actually move its economics.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Aarti's distinctive claim within this field is the combination of deep backward integration — it makes its own intermediates rather than buying them, which is where its cost advantage originates — and world/India scale concentrated in a few benzene chemistries. It is not the highest-return name on the bench; Vinati and Navin have historically earned more on capital, on narrower and more defensible niches. The trade-off is precise: Aarti's scale is concentrated in chemistries more exposed to Chinese competition and agrochem cyclicality than Vinati's boutique franchises. The real competitor that matters is not on any Indian screen — it is the Chinese capacity that sets the spread. Aarti wins the long game by being the proven non-Chinese supplier the world is trying to build; it loses the short game whenever that same Chinese capacity dumps.

11

The risks, stated plainly and once

What can keep the trough a trough — most of it observed, not imagined.

The honest summary is that most of Aarti's risks are not hypothetical — the last two years demonstrated several of them in real financial terms. That is useful: it means the downside is observed, not imagined, and can be sized against actual numbers rather than a worst-case story.

  • Specialty-chem cyclicality and Chinese dumping. Proved in the trough. If Chinese oversupply persists, the spread stays compressed and the ROCE recovery stalls — the single biggest external risk.
  • Customer and contract concentration. The crown-jewel contracts are also a concentration: a large customer or a large dispute matters a lot. The 2020 cancellation resolved in Aarti's favour; not every dispute will.
  • Benzene / crude spread volatility. The company's economics are the spread, and the spread is volatile. Sharp feedstock moves can whipsaw margins quarter to quarter even when volumes are stable.
  • Heavy-capex execution. The ~₹1,125 Cr/year build only creates value when the plants fill. Capacity commissioning into a weak market earns nothing while carrying depreciation and interest — and net debt/equity is already ~0.72.
  • Agrochemical weakness. Aarti's largest end-market has been in a demand and destocking slump. A slower-than-expected agrochem recovery directly delays the volume leg.
  • Environmental and regulatory. Nitration and chlorination are hazardous, effluent-heavy chemistries. Environmental compliance, plant incidents and tightening regulation are permanent operating risks for this kind of business.
  • Valuation. A structural grower de-rated inside a trough still trades at a premium multiple on depressed earnings — which means the recovery is, to a meaningful degree, already an expectation rather than a discount.
The through-line: every one of these risks routes back to the same variable — the spread, and whether the newly built capacity fills at a healthy one. Aarti does not have seven independent risks so much as one master risk (the spread staying thin) wearing seven costumes.
One master risk, seven costumes

This is what makes Aarti a cleaner bet to reason about than most cyclicals, even if not a safer one. The risks are not seven separate coin-flips; they are seven views of a single question — does the conversion spread recover to a level that lets the new plants earn their capital? Chinese dumping, agrochem demand, feedstock swings, capex timing, the multiple: each is just that one question seen from a different angle. You are not underwriting a scattered list of hazards. You are underwriting one variable, repeatedly.

12

Bull, bear, and the gate that decides it

What each side actually believes — and the single line item that will settle it.

Aarti enters the next leg as a structurally advantaged, deeply integrated producer sitting at the bottom of its returns cycle, mid-build, with a genuine annuity layer growing underneath a volatile spot one. The real debate is not whether the business is good — it is whether the spread recovers fast enough, and the new plants fill soon enough, to justify a premium multiple that never fully came down.

The bull case

  • Bought at the trough — a structurally advantaged, deeply integrated producer with ROCE at a visible ~5.7% low, priced for a cycle that historically self-corrects.
  • A real annuity engine — the contract layer is genuinely annuity-like; the 2020 cancellation proved the model protects Aarti even when a customer walks.
  • Capex loaded during the trough — ~₹1,125 Cr/year is building the next volume leg now, so recovery lands on capacity already built.
  • China+1 tailwind — friend-shoring structurally favours a proven non-Chinese supplier of exactly these chemistries over the coming decade.
  • Early turn visible — Q4 FY26's ~15.6% EBITDA margin is the first evidence the spread has begun to normalise off the bottom.

The bear case

  • China may not clear — Chinese oversupply can persist for years; the spread stays thin and the plants fill slowly.
  • Priced for recovery already — the premium multiple sits on depressed earnings, so the turn is an expectation, not a discount.
  • Leverage into idle capacity — net debt/equity ~0.72 into plants that aren't yet earning is a real drag if demand is late.
  • Concentrated exposure — agrochem-heavy, China-contested chemistries make Aarti more cyclical than higher-return peers like Vinati.
  • One fewer cushion — after the pharma spin-off, a pure specialty-chem company has no second leg to lean on in a downturn.

Both cases run through the same funnel, which is why this is a cleaner bet than most: there is one gate. The spread has to recover, and the newly built plants have to fill, so that ROCE climbs off ~5.7% back toward the mid-teens. Everything else — the multiple, the debt, the China+1 tailwind, the contract mix — is a downstream consequence of whether that one thing happens.

The one number that settles the thesis: spread per tonne → ROCE off ~5.7%

The milestone to watch is concrete and named: the commissioning and ramp of the new benzene-derivative value chains and dedicated long-contract plants funded by the current capex cycle, and ROCE crossing back above roughly 10% on its way to the mid-teens. That crossing is the gate. Below it, Aarti is a leveraged specialty-chemicals company that built into a Chinese air-pocket. Above it, the same plants and the same benzene chemistry become the earnings engine the premium multiple was always paying for — and the trough of FY26 becomes the entry point in hindsight rather than the warning it looks like today.

The company will tell you which one it is not through a slide or a slogan, but through a single line item, quarter after quarter: the spread it captures per tonne of benzene it converts, and where that lands on the ROCE line. Watch that, and the rest of the Aarti story writes itself.
