Privi Speciality Chemicals, the smell inside your soap and perfume

A complete walk through India's largest maker of aroma chemicals — the individual molecules that give perfumes, soaps, shampoos, detergents and air-fresheners their scent. Privi is a B2B ingredient supplier: it sells fragrance building-blocks to the handful of global "flavour & fragrance" houses — Givaudan, dsm-firmenich, IFF, Symrise — who blend them into the branded scents you actually buy. Much of its chemistry starts, improbably, from pine-tree turpentine recovered as a waste stream at paper mills.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're actually buying, before we go deep.

Privi Speciality Chemicals (formerly Privi Organics) is a Mumbai-headquartered aroma-chemicals manufacturer with two large plants at Mahad (Maharashtra) and Jhagadia (Gujarat). It does not make perfumes or consumer products. It makes the individual scent molecules — ingredients with names like Dihydro Myrcenol and Galaxolide — that go into fragrances. Its customers are the world's big flavour & fragrance (F&F) houses (Givaudan, dsm-firmenich, IFF, Symrise and others), who buy these molecules by the tonne and compound them into the branded scents sold to soap, detergent, shampoo, deodorant and perfume makers. It is export-heavy — roughly three-quarters of sales go abroad — and it is one of only a handful of firms worldwide with the chemistry to run this at scale.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Privi is an ingredient supplier two steps behind the shelf. The perfume on a counter is made by a brand (say, a designer label); that brand buys its scent from an F&F house like Givaudan; and Givaudan buys some of the raw molecules in that scent from Privi. Privi never touches the consumer — its whole business is being the reliable, qualified supplier of specific aroma molecules to the companies one rung above it.
  • The chemistry often starts at a pine forest, via a paper mill. A large slice of Privi's molecules are built from terpenes — compounds found in pine trees. The specific feedstock is crude sulphate turpentine (CST), a smelly by-product that kraft paper mills would otherwise treat as effluent. Privi buys an estimated a fifth to a quarter of the world's CST/turpentine supply and purifies it into fragrance-grade molecules. The scent in a global soap can, quite literally, begin as waste at a Canadian pulp mill.
  • The economics turn on one spread. Privi buys turpentine and other feedstocks and sells finished molecules; the gap between the two — the gross-margin spread — is where the money is, and it swings with feedstock prices, product mix and how full the plants are. FY26 was a strong year precisely because that spread widened and utilisation rose: revenue ~₹2,583 Cr, PAT ~₹327 Cr (up ~75%), EBITDA margin ~25.8%.
In plain terms

Open a bottle of perfume or a bar of soap and the smell you notice is not one thing — it is a blend of dozens of separate scent chemicals, each doing a job (fresh, floral, musky, woody). Privi is a factory that makes some of those individual chemicals and ships them, in drums and tankers, to the specialist companies that mix them into finished fragrances. You will never see Privi's name on anything you buy, because it sells to the mixers, not to you. The business is deeply unglamorous and deeply technical: purify the right molecules, at consistent quality, cheaper and more reliably than rivals, and get them written into the recipes the big fragrance houses use.

01

What an aroma chemical actually is — and why a soap maker needs one

The one idea you must understand before anything else here makes sense.

An aroma chemical is a single, defined molecule that has a smell. That's it. "Rose," "lemon," "fresh laundry," "sandalwood" — as your nose experiences them in a product — are usually not one substance but a constructed blend of many such molecules, some extracted from nature and many made in a chemical plant. There are roughly 3,000 aroma chemicals in commercial use; a modern fragrance might draw on dozens or hundreds of them. Privi makes around 75 of these molecules today (heading toward 95–100), and holds better than a 20% global share in about ten of them — meaning for a specific molecule, Privi can be one of the two or three plants on earth that supply it at scale.

Why can't a soap or perfume maker just use natural rose oil or real sandalwood? Three reasons, and they are the reason the entire synthetic-aroma industry exists:

  • Nature can't supply the volume, or the price. The world uses fragrance in billions of units of soap, detergent and shampoo a year. There is nowhere near enough rose petal, sandalwood tree or musk deer on the planet to scent all of it — and if there were, it would be wildly expensive. A synthesised molecule delivers the same scent note in industrial quantity at a fraction of the cost.
  • Consistency and stability. A natural oil varies crop to crop and degrades; a defined molecule is identical every batch and can be engineered to survive the harsh chemistry of a detergent or the heat of a hot wash. A fragrance house needs a scent that smells the same in Lagos and in London, this year and next.
  • Ethics, allergens and regulation. Synthetic musks replaced musk from animals; lab-made sandalwood molecules spare endangered trees; and a controlled molecule can be screened for allergen and safety rules (IFRA standards) far more precisely than a complex natural extract.
The single most important sentence in this primer

A finished fragrance is a recipe, and Privi supplies some of the ingredients. The company that writes the recipe — the F&F house — is not Privi; the brand that puts the recipe on a shelf is not Privi either. Privi occupies the layer underneath both: the molecule maker. That position is unglamorous, but it has a hidden strength, because once one of Privi's molecules is written into a recipe that ships in millions of units, switching it out is expensive and risky for everyone above it. The whole investment case rests on understanding that layer — so hold on to it.

A genuine surprise: the "smell of clean laundry" or the woody base note in a mass-market perfume is frequently a specific man-made molecule — and a meaningful share of those molecules trace back to pine trees. The pine connection isn't romantic marketing; it's literal industrial chemistry, and it is the foundation of Privi's cost position (section 04).

02

The four fragrance houses that basically are the market

Who Privi sells to — and why so few customers control so much demand.

The global flavour-and-fragrance industry is one of the most concentrated business-to-business markets in the world. A small group of firms — led by Givaudan (Switzerland), dsm-firmenich, IFF (International Flavors & Fragrances, US) and Symrise (Germany), followed by names like Takasago, MANE and Robertet — together control roughly two-thirds of the world fragrance market. These are the "compounders": they employ the perfumers, hold the recipes, and sell finished fragrances to the consumer brands. They are Privi's customers. Privi supplies all ten of the largest fragrance houses, plus FMCG majors such as P&G, Unilever, Henkel and Reckitt who also buy aroma chemicals directly.

Privi is Layer 2. It almost never sells to Layer 4 and never to the shopper. Its entire commercial relationship is with the concentrated set of Layer-3 compounders — which is both its moat and its single biggest risk.

This structure cuts two ways, and you must hold both halves at once. On one hand, having a tiny number of enormous, sophisticated, demanding customers is a concentration risk: lose or annoy one and a large chunk of demand can move. On the other hand, those same customers are sticky and rational: they do not casually re-qualify ingredients, they value supply security above all, and once they trust a supplier's quality and scale they tend to keep buying. The F&F majors also increasingly want to de-risk their own supply chains away from over-dependence on China — which has quietly favoured a large, reliable Indian supplier like Privi.

Why a concentrated customer base can be a strength, not just a risk

In most industries, "four customers are most of your market" is a red flag. In aroma chemicals it is closer to a structural feature — because the same concentration exists on the demand side for everyone, and the customers are the opposite of fickle. An F&F house re-formulating a fragrance that ships in tens of millions of units has to re-test stability, re-clear regulatory and allergen limits, and re-secure its own customers' approval — a slow, costly, risk-laden process. So the majors reward a supplier that is technically excellent and never fails to deliver, and they punish switching with their own internal friction. Privi's job is not to win a bidding war every quarter; it is to be so embedded and reliable that switching away is the customer's problem, not Privi's.

The clearest expression of that embeddedness is the PRIGIV joint venture — a company owned 51% by Privi and 49% by Givaudan — set up to make a portfolio of 40-plus proprietary molecules exclusively for Givaudan. When your largest customer co-invests in a dedicated plant with you, the relationship has moved well beyond a spot purchase order. PRIGIV is small today (revenue in the tens of crore) but is targeted to scale several-fold over the next few years, and it is the sharpest single evidence of Privi being "designed in" rather than merely "sold to."

03

Designed-in: the qualification moat

The most important — and most invisible — thing that protects this business.

Here is the mechanism that makes Privi more than a commodity chemical seller. When an F&F house builds a fragrance, it selects specific molecules from specific suppliers and locks that choice into a formula — and then that formula gets qualified: tested for stability, safety, allergen limits and performance, and approved by the brand and often by regulators, for a product that may sell for years in millions of units across dozens of countries. Once Privi's molecule is written into that qualified formula, it is "designed in." Swapping it out is not a purchasing decision; it is a re-engineering project.

That single fact reshapes the competitive dynamics:

  • Switching cost sits with the customer, not the supplier. A rival offering the same molecule 5% cheaper doesn't automatically win the business, because the customer would have to re-qualify, re-test and re-approve — often not worth it for a small saving on one ingredient in a large recipe.
  • Approval is slow to win and slow to lose. Getting a molecule qualified into a major's formulas can take years of sampling, auditing and trust-building. That barrier is painful going in — but once you are through it, it protects you on the way out.
  • New molecules compound the lock-in. Every additional molecule Privi gets designed into a customer's formulas deepens the relationship and raises the cost of ever leaving. This is why Privi's steady march from ~75 toward ~95–100 products matters strategically, not just for revenue.
The device that fits only Privi

Privi's real product is not a molecule; it is a qualified position inside a global fragrance recipe. A tonne of Dihydro Myrcenol is a commodity anyone with the plant can make — but a tonne of Dihydro Myrcenol that is already approved, audited and written into Givaudan's or IFF's formulas for products shipping in the millions is something a competitor cannot replicate by simply matching the price. The moat is not the chemistry alone; it is the accumulated stack of customer approvals sitting on top of the chemistry. That is why the sharpest question about Privi is never "can it make the molecule?" but "how many of its molecules are designed into how many of the majors' formulas — and is that number growing?"

The limit of the moat is worth stating plainly: this is not a patent monopoly. Most aroma chemicals are off-patent, and for any given molecule there are usually one or two other capable producers (often Chinese, sometimes European). So Privi's protection is real but relative — it comes from qualification, scale, backward integration and reliability stacked together, not from a legal exclusive. The moat is wide enough to earn good margins in good years; it is not so wide that a feedstock shock or a determined price war can't squeeze it.

04

From pine effluent to perfume — the terpene chemistry

The backward integration that is Privi's real cost advantage.

Most of Privi's flagship chemistry is terpene-based — built from compounds found in pine resin. The key raw material is crude sulphate turpentine (CST) and gum turpentine oil (GTO). CST is not a mined or farmed commodity; it is a by-product of paper making. When kraft pulp mills cook wood chips to make paper, they capture a foul-smelling, sulphurous turpentine stream that they would otherwise treat as waste. Privi buys this stream — sourcing from 60-plus kraft pulp mills across Canada, the US, Brazil and Scandinavia — and estimates put its purchases at roughly a fifth to a quarter of the entire global CST/GTO supply. It then purifies and transforms it into fragrance-grade aroma molecules.

The surprise, quantified: the crude turpentine Privi starts with can carry sulphur at around 20,000 parts per million and smells genuinely unpleasant. The fragrance-grade molecules Privi ships out are purified to under 1 part per million. The company's core technical feat is turning a paper-mill waste stream that reeks into an ultra-pure ingredient for luxury perfume — a roughly twenty-thousand-fold purification. Very few plants in the world can do this reliably at industrial scale, and that capability, not any single molecule, is the heart of the business.

Why does starting from CST matter so much? Because it is Privi's backward integration and therefore its cost moat. A competitor who buys finished intermediates on the open market is a price-taker; Privi, by processing the cheap raw turpentine stream itself, captures more of the value chain and can undercut on cost while still earning a margin. Being one of the world's largest single-location processors of CST (a facility handling on the order of 36,000 tonnes a year of it) gives Privi scale economics that a smaller rival cannot match.

Terpene chemistry is not the only route. Privi also runs petro-chemical / benzenoid and other feedstock chemistries (citral-based and phenol-based families among them), so the portfolio spans several distinct chemical trees rather than betting everything on pine. But the terpene/CST platform is the distinctive one — it is what makes Privi structurally different from a generic specialty-chemicals company and squarely an aroma specialist.

Why "we buy a fifth of the world's turpentine" is the whole cost story

Scale in feedstock buying is not a footnote here — it is the competitive position. Because Privi purchases such a large share of global CST/GTO from so many mills, it secures supply that a smaller entrant simply cannot get in the volume needed, and it processes that supply at a scale that spreads fixed costs thin. That combination — privileged access to a waste-stream feedstock plus the world's-largest-single-site processing scale — is what lets Privi make off-patent molecules profitably and defend share against cheaper-labour rivals. The flip side, covered in section 08, is that when turpentine prices spike or the molecule price falls, the spread Privi earns on that feedstock is exactly what gets squeezed.

05

The molecule portfolio — what Privi actually makes

The scent families, the flagship products, and the drive to add new ones.

Privi's ~75 molecules cluster into a handful of feedstock-defined chemistry families — broadly the pinene (terpene) family, the citral family, sandalwood-type woody molecules, and phenol-based chemistry. Across these it makes ingredients that deliver the fresh-citrus, floral, musky, amber and woody notes fragrance houses reach for constantly. Two strategic threads run through the portfolio. First, Galaxolide-class musks are a large-volume prize: Privi has built substantial musk capacity (on the order of several thousand tonnes), positioning it in one of the biggest single aroma-chemical categories by tonnage. Second, Privi is deliberately widening the range with new molecules and import-substitution products — items like maltol/ethyl maltol and other building blocks that India currently imports — so the company both deepens existing customer relationships and taps new demand pools. The march from ~75 to ~95–100 products is the growth engine that sits alongside raw capacity expansion.

Why new-molecule count is a real KPI, not marketing

Every new molecule Privi qualifies does three things at once: it opens a fresh revenue line, it deepens the lock-in with customers who now buy more of their recipe from one trusted supplier, and it shifts the mix toward higher-value chemistry. That is why the count of products — and especially the count of proprietary or hard-to-make molecules (the PRIGIV exclusives, the import-substitution items) — is one of the cleanest signals of whether Privi is climbing the value chain or just adding commodity tonnes. Watch new-molecule launches the way you'd watch new-store openings for a retailer.

06

The company, its history and who owns it

Privi Organics → Fairchem → Fairfax → Privi Speciality — and a promoter register worth reading carefully.

Privi's corporate history is unusually eventful for a chemicals company, and it explains an ownership structure that surprises people. The operating business was built as Privi Organics from the early 1990s by its founder-promoters — the Babani family (Mahesh Babani is Managing Director) alongside co-promoter D. B. Rao — starting with a couple of products and growing into an export-oriented aroma-chemicals maker. Along the way it took private-equity capital, and in 2016 the Canadian-listed Fairfax India (part of Prem Watsa's Fairfax group) invested for a large stake. A 2017 combination folded it into Fairchem Speciality, and a 2020 demerger separated the aroma-chemicals business out as the standalone listed entity now called Privi Speciality Chemicals Limited.

Why the ownership shape matters to a minority buyer

Two things follow from this register. First, the founders still run the business, so operational continuity and aroma-chemicals expertise sit with people who built it — a genuine positive for a technical, relationship-driven company. Second, a big chunk of the "promoter" holding belongs to a financial investor whose job is eventually to exit at a return, not to hold forever. That is neither good nor bad in itself, but it changes how you read share-sale news: a Fairfax sell-down is a portfolio decision, not necessarily a verdict on the business — while also being a real source of stock supply that can cap near-term upside. Read promoter-selling headlines here with that split lens.

07

Capacity, capex and the 72,000-tonne plan

The physical growth story — and why utilisation is the number to watch.

Aroma chemicals is a capital-intensive, plant-based business: growth comes from building more capacity and then filling it. Privi's total aroma-chemical capacity is around 48,000 tonnes a year (MTPA) across Mahad and Jhagadia, and the company is executing a multi-phase expansion toward roughly 72,000 MTPA by about 2028 — a roughly ₹1,200 Cr capex programme, largely self-funded from cash flow and modest debt. On top of raw tonnage, capex is going into new molecules, the PRIGIV Givaudan JV, and import-substitution products that carry better economics than commodity volume.

The strategic ambition management has articulated is sometimes shorthanded as a "5K : 1K" goal — roughly ₹5,000 Cr of revenue and ₹1,000+ Cr of EBITDA by around FY29–30. Against FY26's ~₹2,583 Cr revenue and ~₹665 Cr EBITDA, that implies close to a doubling of the business over the back half of the decade. Whether that target is hit turns almost entirely on one thing: not building the capacity (Privi has done that before), but filling it at a healthy spread.

The trap in every capacity story — and how it applies here

New capacity is a cost the day it commissions and a benefit only once it's running full and profitably. Between those two moments sits a danger window: fixed costs and depreciation land on the P&L while the new tonnes are still ramping, so margins and returns can dip before they climb. For Privi the specific risk is doubly linked — the new plants only pay off if (a) the F&F majors qualify Privi's molecules from those lines into their formulas, and (b) the feedstock-to-product spread holds while the volume fills. So the honest metric for this expansion is not "tonnes added" but incremental capacity utilisation at a sustained spread. A gleaming new plant running at 45% on a thin spread destroys value; the same plant at 85% on a healthy spread is the whole bull case.

08

The spread — where the profit actually lives

The single mechanism that turns Privi from a good year into a bad one, or back again.

Strip the business to its economic core and it is a conversion spread: Privi buys feedstock (turpentine/CST, petro intermediates, energy) at one price and sells finished molecules at another, and the gap between them — after processing cost — is the profit. That spread is not fixed. It moves with (1) the price of turpentine and other inputs, (2) the selling price of each molecule, which competition (often Chinese) can pressure, and (3) the mix of what Privi sells, since new and proprietary molecules earn more than commodity ones. The whole earnings pattern of the company is the story of that spread widening and narrowing.

The non-transferable calculation: the spread is the whole difference

Look at what actually happened at Privi across two years. Revenue grew a healthy but not spectacular ~47% (₹1,752 Cr → ₹2,583 Cr). Yet PAT went from roughly ₹95 Cr to ₹327 Cr — about 3.4×. The plants, the molecules and the customers were largely the same; the profit tripled because the spread and operating leverage moved together — a widening feedstock-to-product margin dropping onto a fixed-cost base. That asymmetry is the signature of this business: modest revenue moves translate into large profit swings, up and down. A generalist who values Privi on revenue growth alone will miss both the upside and the risk; the spread is the variable that matters, and it is set by turpentine prices, molecule pricing and mix — not by how many tonnes leave the gate.

The two-way nature of this is essential to internalise. The same operating leverage that turned a good FY26 into a great one will amplify a bad year if turpentine spikes, if Chinese competitors dump product to defend share, or if a demand air-pocket empties the plants. Privi's margin is a genuinely cyclical variable riding on a structurally decent business — not a smooth, defensible annuity. FY26's ~25.8% is a strong print; assuming it is the permanent baseline would be the classic error.

The one number that settles the thesis

For Privi it is the gross-margin spread — held at a healthy level while the new 48,000→72,000 MTPA capacity fills. Everything the bulls hope for (the "5K:1K" doubling) and everything the bears fear (a China-led price war, a turpentine spike) shows up in that one place: the margin Privi earns converting feedstock into molecules, multiplied across rising utilisation. If the spread holds near the mid-20s in EBITDA terms as the new plants ramp toward full, the profit compounds hard. If the spread compresses back toward the high-teens just as fresh depreciation and interest hit, the same expansion that was the bull case becomes a drag. Track the spread and utilisation together, quarter by quarter — that pair is the scoreboard. New-molecule launches and the PRIGIV ramp are the leading indicators that tell you which way it's heading.

09

The risk stack

What can go wrong — ranked by how much it actually matters here.

Privi is a fundamentally decent business with a specific, well-understood set of vulnerabilities. None is hidden or exotic; the discipline is in weighting them correctly and not being surprised when a good year is followed by a wobble.

How to hold the risk stack without over- or under-worrying

Rank matters. The top two rows — spread volatility and Chinese competition — are the same underlying force (pricing pressure on the conversion margin) and together they are 80% of the real risk. The rest are second-order: concentration is muted by the qualification moat, FX is half-hedged by the import bill, the balance sheet is comfortable, and the Fairfax overhang is a share-price weight rather than a business problem. So the disciplined worry is narrow and specific: is the feedstock-to-molecule spread durable, or is FY26's margin a cyclical peak? Almost every bear case for Privi, when you trace it back, is really a bet that the spread compresses. Almost every bull case is a bet that scale, integration and the qualification moat let Privi hold it while volume grows.

On valuation, the plain reading is that at roughly ₹14,100 Cr of market value against ~₹327 Cr of FY26 profit, Privi trades at a punchy earnings multiple (in the low-40s on trailing PAT) — the market is clearly paying for growth and margin durability, not for the current year's earnings alone. The valuation is not cheap, and it does not need to be for the investment to work — but it does mean the burden of proof sits on continued execution.

What actually decides whether today's price is justified

Do not anchor on the trailing multiple in isolation — anchor on the two variables feeding it. The multiple is only "reasonable" if EBITDA keeps compounding toward the ₹1,000 Cr goal, and that requires the spread to hold in the mid-20s while the 72,000-MTPA capacity fills. So the valuation question collapses into the same operational one from section 08: sustained spread × rising utilisation. If both hold, FY26 earnings are a way-station and the multiple compresses naturally as profit grows into it. If the spread mean-reverts while new depreciation lands, the market will re-rate the margin and the earnings at the same time. The stock is a claim on the durability of the spread, priced with optimism — measure that durability, not the P/E in the abstract.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A real global niche leader, riding a genuinely cyclical margin.

Privi is a legitimately world-class niche business: one of a small number of firms anywhere with the backward integration, scale and customer approvals to make aroma chemicals from pine turpentine at industrial quality, selling into the concentrated global fragrance oligopoly. The question is never whether the business is good — it is whether the strong FY26 margin is a new floor or a cyclical peak, and whether the big capacity build fills at a spread that justifies today's expectations.

The risks, laid out plainly

  • Spread cyclicality (the defining one). Earnings are a leveraged bet on the feedstock-to-molecule margin. FY26's ~25.8% EBITDA margin is a multi-year high; if turpentine spikes or molecule prices soften, profit falls faster than revenue — the same math that tripled PAT can halve it.
  • Chinese price competition. For off-patent molecules, Chinese capacity is the standing threat to pricing. Privi's cost position defends it, but not infinitely; a serious price war compresses the spread that is the whole earnings story.
  • Execution of the 72,000-MTPA build. The ₹1,200 Cr expansion must commission on time and, harder, be filled at a healthy spread. New tonnes that ramp slowly carry cost before they earn.
  • Customer concentration. A handful of F&F majors are most of demand; the qualification moat makes them sticky, but a share loss at one large customer would sting.
  • Fairfax overhang & valuation. A large financial holder likely to keep selling, layered on a rich multiple after a strong year, means the stock can wobble on stake sales or any hint of margin normalisation.

The bull case

  • Genuine global niche leadership — backward-integrated into a scarce waste-stream feedstock, buying a fifth-plus of world turpentine, with world's-largest single-site processing scale.
  • Designed-in with the majors — qualified molecules inside the F&F houses' formulas, deepened by the Givaudan PRIGIV JV; switching costs sit with the customer.
  • Structural tailwind — customers de-risking supply away from China favour a large, reliable Indian supplier.
  • Growth on two axes — more capacity (48k→72k MTPA) and more molecules (~75→~95–100), toward a "5K:1K" ambition.
  • Improving balance sheet — net debt and leverage falling even mid-expansion; FY26 cash flows fund much of the capex.

The bear case

  • The margin is cyclical — FY26's ~25.8% may be a peak; a spread reversion drags earnings and the multiple together.
  • Off-patent, not monopoly — no legal exclusivity; Chinese and other rivals can pressure prices on most molecules.
  • Operating leverage cuts both ways — the asymmetry that tripled PAT will amplify any down-cycle just as hard.
  • Expensive going in — a low-40s trailing multiple leaves little cushion if execution or margins disappoint.
  • Known seller on the register — Fairfax's ongoing stake reduction is a recurring weight on the stock.
The bottom line

Privi is the rare Indian small-cap that is a real, defensible global leader in something genuinely hard — turning pine-mill waste into the molecules that scent the world's soaps and perfumes, and getting those molecules written into the recipes of an oligopoly that does not switch suppliers lightly. That is a business worth owning at the right price. What it is not is a smooth compounder: its profit rides a cyclical feedstock-to-molecule spread, magnified by operating leverage, and FY26 caught that spread near its best. The next things to watch are concrete and checkable — the quarterly EBITDA margin as the new lines at Mahad and Jhagadia commission and ramp, the utilisation of the 72,000-MTPA capacity as it comes on, and the cadence of new-molecule launches and the PRIGIV ramp. If the spread holds in the mid-20s while those plants fill, the "5K:1K" ambition is credible and today's multiple grows into itself. If the spread slips back to the high-teens as fresh depreciation lands, the market will mark down the margin and the earnings at once. Own the durability of the spread, or don't own the stock.
