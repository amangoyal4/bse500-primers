Thomas Cook India, forex toll, travel engine

A complete walk through India's best-known travel-and-money house — the Fairfax-owned group behind Thomas Cook, SOTC, TC Forex and Sterling Holidays. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Thomas Cook (India) Limited is an integrated travel-and-financial-services group — one of the country's oldest and most recognised travel names, but with a second, quieter business that arguably matters more: it is India's leading non-bank foreign-exchange house. Under one roof it sells holidays and corporate trips (through the Thomas Cook and SOTC brands), converts and moves currency for travellers and companies (TC Forex, forex cards, remittances), runs a listed leisure-hospitality arm (Sterling Holiday Resorts), and even operates a global attractions-photography business (DEI / Digiphoto). It is controlled by Canada's Fairfax Financial — the investment group run by Prem Watsa — which owns roughly 64%.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It earns from the same traveller twice. When an Indian family books an overseas holiday and loads a forex card for the trip, Thomas Cook can collect on both — a margin on the package and a spread on the currency. The travel side is volume-driven and cyclical; the forex side is a high-margin, transaction-fee toll that keeps ringing whether or not the customer bought the trip from Thomas Cook. Two very different profit engines, one customer.
  • This is not the British Thomas Cook that went bust. The 178-year-old UK tour operator Thomas Cook Group plc collapsed in September 2019. The Indian company had already been bought out by Fairfax in 2012 and cut loose from that parent; it kept the brand licence, kept trading, and was entirely unaffected as a corporate entity. Same name, different owner, different balance sheet.
  • FY26 was a growth year that the profit line didn't reflect. Consolidated total income rose ~3.3% to ~₹8,558 Cr, yet reported net profit slipped ~15% to ~₹220 Cr — pressured by higher costs, a demerger in motion and continued investment in stores, digital and people. The stock trades on a full-looking multiple (low-to-mid 20s on earnings), pricing a recovery that is still working its way from the top line to the bottom.
In plain terms

Two engines sit inside one share. Engine one is travel: package holidays, corporate travel, MICE (meetings and events), visas — a business that lives and dies on how many Indians take a trip this season, and one where a single busy departure hall can swing the year. Engine two is money: swapping rupees for dollars and euros, loading prepaid forex cards, sending remittances abroad — a steadier, fee-based toll booth on the same wave of outbound travel. Fairfax bought the whole thing in 2012, well before the British namesake failed, and runs it as a debt-light, cash-generative franchise. The interesting tension is that the flashier engine (travel) is the cyclical one, while the dull engine (forex) is the reliable earner. FY26 showed the group can grow revenue in a choppy world; the unfinished job is turning that growth into profit.

01

Two businesses in one: travel and money

No jargon needed — just the two things the company actually does, and how they connect.

Most people meet Thomas Cook as a travel agent. That is only half of it. The company sits at the exact point where an Indian traveller needs two things at once — a trip and foreign currency to spend on it — and it sells both. Understanding the group means holding those two engines in your head separately, because they earn money in completely different ways and respond to different pressures.

The connection that makes the group more than the sum of its parts

The two engines share a customer and a wave. When outbound travel rises — more Indians flying abroad, more students going overseas, more companies sending staff to conferences — both the travel desk and the forex desk light up together, because the same trip generates a package sale and a currency need. That is the quiet logic of keeping travel and forex in one house: the group captures the traveller's wallet at two points on the same journey, and the forex toll keeps earning even from travellers who booked their flights elsewhere. The risk in the same coin is that a shock to outbound travel — a visa clampdown, a currency tax, a geopolitical scare — hits both engines at once.

02

Not the company that failed: the Fairfax story

The single most common confusion about this stock, cleared up — and why the owner matters.

Say "Thomas Cook" and many investors flinch, remembering the dramatic 2019 collapse of the British tour operator that stranded holidaymakers around the world. That was Thomas Cook Group plc of the United Kingdom — a separate, now-defunct company. Thomas Cook (India) Limited is a different corporate entity, listed in Mumbai, that had already been sold to a new owner seven years before the UK failure. The shared brand is a licence, not a lifeline to a dead parent.

How the two separated

The Indian business was originally part of the global Thomas Cook empire. In 2012, Canada's Fairfax Financial Holdings — the insurance-and-investment group led by Prem Watsa, often described as a value investor in the Buffett mould — acquired the controlling stake in the Indian company (through its subsidiary Fairbridge Capital) and delinked it from the struggling UK group. By the time the British parent went under in 2019, Thomas Cook India had spent years as a Fairfax company with its own board, balance sheet and strategy, and it retained the right to use the Thomas Cook brand in its territories. Nothing about the UK insolvency touched the Indian entity's operations or solvency.

Ownership & why it matters

  • Promoter: Fairfax Financial (through Fairbridge Capital (Mauritius) Ltd and affiliates) holds roughly 64% — a rock-stable, long-horizon controlling owner rather than a founder family or a rotating financial sponsor. (Confirm the exact figure in the latest shareholding pattern.)
  • What a Fairfax parent buys you: patient capital and a bias toward cash generation and conservative balance sheets. The group runs comparatively debt-light and treats free cash flow as the scorecard — a temperament that helped it survive COVID and that shapes how it invests today.
  • Group web: Fairfax's Indian interests connect Thomas Cook India to a wider ecosystem (Quess Corp historically, and other Fairfax-backed Indian names), which occasionally surfaces in related-party dealings and capital moves minority investors should watch.
The reputational discount worth understanding: the shared name with a famously failed operator is a genuine perception drag — retail investors conflate the two, and some overseas observers still assume the worst. For a clear-eyed reader that confusion is not a risk but a fact to price correctly: the Indian company is Fairfax-owned, independently financed, and was never part of the UK insolvency. The brand it licenses is an asset it has kept trading under for over a century in India.
03

The forex toll booth: the quiet engine that actually earns

The most under-appreciated idea in the investment case — and the one that separates Thomas Cook from a pure travel agent.

Ask what Thomas Cook does and most people say "sells holidays." The more interesting answer is that it runs a foreign-exchange toll booth: every time an Indian traveller, student or company needs dollars, euros or a prepaid travel card, Thomas Cook can take a small, reliable margin on that conversion. It is India's largest non-bank forex provider, and this business — capital-light, fee-based, and far steadier than travel — is where a disproportionate share of the group's quality earnings sits.

Here is the mechanism. When you buy $2,000 for a holiday, Thomas Cook sells it to you at a rate slightly worse than the wholesale rate at which it sourced the currency; that spread, plus fees on cards and remittances, is its revenue. The traveller barely notices a fraction of a percent on the exchange rate — but multiplied across billions of dollars of annual turnover, those slivers add up to a high-margin income stream that requires little fixed investment. Crucially, it keeps earning whether or not that customer booked their trip through Thomas Cook at all.

The device that only fits a travel-and-forex house: bill the same trip at two tolls

Picture a single Indian family's holiday to Europe. Thomas Cook can earn on it at two separate tolls on one journey: a margin when it sells the tour package (the travel engine), and a spread when the family loads €4,000 onto a Thomas Cook forex card for spending money (the forex engine). A pure online travel agent earns only at the first toll and hands the currency business to a bank. A bank earns only at the second and never sees the trip. Thomas Cook is one of the few players positioned to collect at both — and because the forex toll also catches travellers who booked their flights on someone else's app, the currency booth is the wider net. The same wave of outbound travel washes money into two of its tills at once.

Who needs the forex booth

  • Leisure travellers — the family buying holiday spending money and a prepaid card; the largest, most seasonal slice, riding the outbound-travel wave.
  • Students going abroad — a fast-growing, high-ticket segment; overseas education means large, recurring remittances for fees and living costs, often the single biggest forex transaction a family ever makes.
  • Corporates — travel money, cross-border payments and expense cards for companies sending staff overseas; sticky, relationship-based, and less price-sensitive than retail.
  • Inbound & institutional — currency services for foreign visitors and business flows, rounding out a two-way franchise.
Why the forex engine deserves the spotlight: in a group whose travel arm is cyclical and thin-margin, the forex business supplies the ballast — capital-light, high-margin, and levered to a structural rise in Indians moving money across borders. It is the reason Thomas Cook is not simply "an OTA that also fails when travel dips." If you only track the holiday business, you miss where a lot of the durable value lives.
04

The travel engine: SOTC, corporate travel and MICE

The bigger, louder, more cyclical half — and where operating leverage bites hardest.

The travel-and-related-services segment is the group's largest by reported revenue — ~₹6,703 Cr in FY26, up ~3.6% — spanning leisure holidays, corporate travel, MICE, and a stack of value-added services. It is a business of brands, distribution and volume: a network of stores, a growing digital front, and a portfolio of consumer names that between them cover the mass-premium Indian traveller.

The brands and what each does

  • Thomas Cook — the flagship consumer travel brand: outbound and domestic holidays, foreign exchange, visas and insurance, sold through owned stores, franchisees and online. The everyday face of the group.
  • SOTC Travel — a wholly-owned leisure-travel brand (the former Kuoni India business) strong in group tours and premium holidays; a second consumer marque that lets the group address different traveller segments without cannibalising Thomas Cook.
  • Corporate travel & MICE — managing business travel for companies and running meetings, incentives, conferences and events. A steadier, contract-based, relationship-led business than leisure — and a natural cross-sell into corporate forex.
  • TCI & destination management — Travel Corporation (India) and allied brands handle inbound tourism (foreigners visiting India, Nepal, Bhutan, Sri Lanka) and destination management across Asia — an earnings stream that moves on a different clock from Indian outbound.
  • Value-added services — visas, travel insurance, passport assistance and ancillary products that carry attractive margins and deepen the customer relationship around each trip.

Why two travel brands, not one

Running both Thomas Cook and SOTC looks redundant until you see the segmentation logic. A group tour buyer and an independent premium traveller want different products, price points and marketing voices; two brands let the company chase both without diluting either, and without a price war against itself. It also spreads distribution risk and preserves optionality — each brand keeps its own loyal channel. The cost is duplicated overhead, which is precisely why the group leans so hard on shared back-office, shared forex and digital consolidation to make the two-brand model pay. The brand portfolio is a revenue-coverage decision funded by relentless cost integration underneath.

05

Sterling and the photo booth: the two limbs nobody expects

A resort chain and a global theme-park imaging business — and why one is about to leave.

Two parts of Thomas Cook surprise almost everyone. The first is Sterling Holiday Resorts, a genuine leisure-hospitality chain the group turned around and grew. The second is DEI / Digiphoto, a business that photographs people on rollercoasters and at aquariums across the world. Neither is what you picture when you think "travel agent," and both change how you value the group.

Sterling Holiday Resorts — the hospitality asset

Sterling is a resort operator and vacation-ownership business: as of March 2026 it ran a network of about 78 resorts across ~61 destinations, earning like a hotel company from rooms, food-and-beverage and membership. In FY26 its revenue grew ~6.6% to ~₹534 Cr, leading the group's growth table as domestic leisure demand stayed resilient. Unlike the asset-light travel and forex arms, Sterling carries real property and fixed costs — so its economics turn on occupancy and average room rate, the classic hotel levers.

DEI / Digiphoto — the genuinely surprising limb

DEI (Digiphoto Entertainment Imaging) is a global attractions-photography operator: it runs the imaging concessions at theme parks, aquariums, observation decks and rides — the souvenir photo of you mid-scream on a rollercoaster, or your family at an aquarium — at partner venues across the Middle East, Asia and beyond, including marquee names among its clients. In FY26 it contributed ~₹836 Cr of revenue (roughly flat, −0.8%). It is easily the group's most international and least "Indian travel agent" business, and it gives Thomas Cook a foothold in global experiential tourism spend that has nothing to do with whether Indians are flying this quarter.

The genuine surprise: a listed Indian travel group owns a worldwide rollercoaster-photo business

It is easy to assume a company called Thomas Cook (India) just books holidays and sells dollars. In fact one of its four reporting limbs — worth over ₹800 Cr of revenue — is a business photographing tourists at theme parks and attractions on multiple continents, earning a cut of imaging spend at venues most of its Indian customers will never visit. That is not a rounding error bolted on; it is a deliberate diversification into global experiential-tourism revenue, decoupled from Indian outbound cycles and from currency policy. Whether it belongs in the group long term is a fair debate — but its existence is the clearest sign that this is a more unusual, more international company than its name suggests.

06

The outbound-India wave: the structural tailwind

The one force that lifts every engine at once — and the real reason to own the story.

Strip away the segments and one structural current runs beneath the whole group: more Indians are travelling internationally, spending abroad, and moving money across borders — and the trend is early. Rising incomes, a young population, cheaper connectivity, more passports, and a cultural shift toward experiences over possessions are pushing outbound travel and cross-border spend up for years, not quarters. Thomas Cook sells picks-and-shovels to exactly that migration of the wallet.

The under-penetration is the crux. Only a modest fraction of Indians hold a passport, and international travel remains a small share of household spending compared with richer economies — yet the number flying abroad, studying overseas and shopping across borders is climbing quickly. Every one of those journeys is a potential package sale, a forex load, a visa, an insurance policy, and sometimes a resort stay. Thomas Cook does not need to win a bigger share of a static market; it can grow simply by riding a market that is itself expanding — and its forex arm captures the money-movement half of the wave even when the trip is booked on a phone app.

The nuance a first-timer should hold

A structural tailwind is not a guarantee of a straight line. Outbound travel is real and rising, but it is also the most cyclical, event-sensitive spending a household does — the first thing cut in a downturn, the first thing hit by a visa freeze, a currency scare or a conflict that closes a corridor. So the wave lifts Thomas Cook over years while roughing it up over quarters. The investment question is not whether the wave is real — it plainly is — but whether the group can convert a rising, choppy flow of travellers and dollars into steadily rising profit, which brings us to operating leverage.

07

Operating leverage: the asset-light recovery machine

Why the same revenue growth can produce very different profit — and why FY26 profit lagged.

Both of Thomas Cook's main engines share a defining trait: a large chunk of their cost base is fixed — stores, staff, technology, brand — while incremental revenue carries very high margins. That is the definition of operating leverage: once the fixed base is covered, each additional package sold or dollar exchanged drops a big slice to profit. It is the mechanism that can turn modest revenue growth into outsized earnings growth — and, in reverse, turn a soft quarter into a sharp profit dip.

This cuts both ways, and FY26 showed the awkward side. Consolidated revenue grew ~3.3%, but reported profit fell ~15% — because the group kept investing ahead of the curve: adding stores and feet-on-street, spending on digital, absorbing higher operating costs and carrying the frictions of the Sterling demerger. In an operating-leverage business, spending up while revenue grows only modestly means costs temporarily outrun the top line, and margins compress before they expand. The bet is that today's fixed-cost build becomes tomorrow's flow-through when volumes step up.

The lever, stated plainly

Imagine a Thomas Cook store that costs a fixed amount to run whether it processes 100 forex transactions a month or 400. At 100 it barely washes its face; at 400 the extra 300 are almost pure margin because the rent, the staff and the software are already paid for. Multiply that across a store network and a digital platform, and you see why volume is everything here and why the group is willing to accept a lean profit year to widen the base. The uncomfortable truth is that operating leverage is a promise, not a fact: it pays off gloriously if volumes climb into the fixed base, and it hurts if they don't. FY26 was a year of paying for the leverage; the return on that spend is what FY27–FY28 must demonstrate.

The forex arm is where the leverage is sweetest, because its incremental cost per transaction — especially a digital one — is tiny. That is why the rising digital-adoption number matters beyond convenience: each customer who loads a card online instead of at a counter lifts the margin on the group's highest-quality earnings stream. Push volumes through a capital-light, increasingly digital forex platform, and the profit response is far steeper than the revenue response.

08

The 20% toll on travel: the levy sitting in the model

A tax rule, not a market force, is now one of the biggest swing factors on the outbound business.

Here is a risk unique to an Indian outbound travel-and-forex house, and one a first-timer never anticipates: the government has placed a large upfront tax collection on the very transactions Thomas Cook sells. Under the Tax Collected at Source (TCS) regime on foreign remittances and overseas tour packages, big-ticket outbound spend now carries a 20% TCS above a ₹7 lakh annual threshold (with lower slabs below it). It is a levy on the customer, not on Thomas Cook — but it lands squarely on the group's demand.

The mechanism hurts even though the tax is ultimately creditable against the traveller's income tax. A family booking an expensive overseas holiday, or a parent remitting fees for a child studying abroad, must now front 20% extra cash at the point of purchase and reclaim it later. That upfront cash sting can defer, shrink or reroute a booking — nudging travellers toward cheaper trips, toward keeping spends under the threshold, or toward channels that (rightly or wrongly) feel less exposed. For a business whose best customers are precisely the high-ticket outbound holiday and overseas-education flows, a 20% upfront friction is a material demand headwind on the richest slice of the wave.

Normally you would not spend a whole section on a tax provision. A cut in the rate or a lift in the threshold would be a direct, dateable tailwind to high-ticket bookings and forex; a further tightening would bite. It is the clearest example of a broader truth about this company: several of the forces that decide its year — a currency tax, RBI's remittance rules, a visa policy abroad — are set by governments, not by how well Thomas Cook runs its stores. The company's job is to be the best-run operator when those winds turn favourable.

09

The industry and the rivals

A fragmented market where Thomas Cook's edge is being two things at once.

Indian travel and forex is a crowded, fragmenting market, and Thomas Cook faces different competitors on each of its engines. No single rival attacks the whole group — which is both its defence (few can match the integrated span) and its challenge (it fights specialists on every front). The key is to see who competes for what.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, Thomas Cook is not trying to out-OTA MakeMyTrip at pure online booking — a fight of app scale and cash-burn it would likely lose. Its edge is integration and physical-plus-digital reach: the ability to serve a traveller's package, currency, visa and stay together, and to hold high-touch corporate and premium-leisure relationships that a thin app cannot. Second, the forex engine is where it enjoys a genuine leadership position among non-banks — its most defensible ground, contested mainly by banks and a few specialists rather than by the travel apps. The bear worry is real, though: OTAs keep eating the simple, high-frequency travel bookings, and if forex spreads compress under digital competition, two of the group's moats erode at once.

10

Financials & valuation

Revenue recovered; the question priced into the stock is when profit follows.

FY26 is best read as a growth year with a soft profit line: total income up ~3.3% to ~₹8,558 Cr, but reported PAT down ~15% to ~₹220 Cr as the group invested through the year and carried demerger and cost frictions. The stock does not trade like a distressed recovery — it carries a full-looking multiple (earnings multiple in the low-to-mid 20s on consolidated profit), which means the market is already paying for the next leg of earnings, not just this year's. The debate is entirely about the pace at which operating leverage converts a recovered top line into a recovered bottom line.

Two features distinguish the balance sheet from a typical leveraged travel company. First, thanks to its Fairfax temperament, the group runs relatively debt-light and holds meaningful liquidity, including customer float on forex cards — a very different risk profile from the debt-laden Cox & Kings that failed. Second, working capital swings with travel seasonality and forex float, so cash flow and reported profit can diverge quarter to quarter; judge the business over a full year, not a single peak or lean quarter.

The one number that settles the thesis: the group's EBIT margin as volumes scale

At a low-20s multiple on depressed FY26 profit, the case does not rest on revenue — the top line has already recovered. It rests on operating margin (group EBIT as a share of income) climbing as travel and forex volumes flow through the fixed-cost base. That is the number to track, quarter by quarter: is each rupee of incremental revenue dropping more to operating profit, or is cost-investment still eating the flow-through? Read it alongside two companions — the forex segment's contribution and turnover (the high-margin engine that leverages hardest) and the Sterling demerger outcome (what value the hospitality asset crystallises and what the residual travel-plus-forex group then earns). If margins expand toward the levels operating leverage promises while forex keeps compounding, the full multiple is justified by rising profit. If revenue keeps growing but margins stay stuck where FY26 left them, the stock is paying up front for a flow-through that isn't arriving.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A recovered, well-owned travel-and-forex franchise, asked to turn growth into profit.

Thomas Cook India enters FY27 with its top line rebuilt, a leading forex franchise, a Fairfax balance sheet behind it, a resort chain about to be spun off, and a structural outbound wave at its back. What it has not yet proven this cycle is that all of that converts into rising profit rather than merely rising revenue. That single gap — between a recovered income line and a still-soft earnings line — frames both cases.

The bull case

  • Two-toll model on a rising wave — earns on both the trip and the currency of a structurally growing Indian outbound market few can bill twice.
  • Forex leadership — the largest non-bank forex house, a capital-light, high-margin engine that leverages hardest as digital adoption climbs.
  • Operating leverage ahead — FY26's fixed-cost investment sets up outsized profit flow-through if volumes step up into the base.
  • Fairfax ownership — a patient, cash-focused, debt-averse controlling owner; a fundamentally different risk profile from the leveraged operators that failed.
  • Sterling demerger catalyst — crystallises the hospitality asset's value and sharpens the residual group into a cleaner travel-plus-forex story.

The bear case

  • Profit lags revenue — FY26 PAT fell as costs ran ahead; the flow-through the valuation assumes has yet to show up.
  • TCS & policy drag — 20% upfront tax on premium outbound and overseas-education spend directly dampens the group's richest demand.
  • Event sensitivity — travel is the first spend cut in a downturn and the first hit by a visa freeze, conflict or currency scare; both engines fall together.
  • OTA & forex competition — MakeMyTrip and lean OTAs keep taking simple bookings; banks and specialists press forex spreads and card economics.
  • Full multiple on soft earnings — a low-20s P/E on depressed FY26 profit leaves little cushion if margin recovery slips.
The bottom line

Thomas Cook India is a genuinely unusual franchise — a century-old travel brand fused to India's leading non-bank forex toll booth, wrapped around a resort chain and a global photo business, and owned by a patient Canadian value investor with no connection to the British firm that failed. The strategic logic is sound: bill the same outbound traveller at two tolls, ride a structural wave, and let operating leverage do the rest. The catch is that FY26 delivered the growth without the profit — revenue up, earnings down — while the stock already prices the recovery. The next test is concrete and dateable: whether group operating margin turns up through the FY27 travel seasons as forex compounds and the Sterling demerger completes around Q1 FY28. Convert the recovered revenue into expanding margin and the full multiple earns its keep; keep spending into a flat-margin top line, and the market will have paid in advance for a flow-through that never left the station.
