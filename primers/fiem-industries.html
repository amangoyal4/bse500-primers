Fiem Industries, the light on India's two-wheelers

A complete walk through one of India's leading makers of two-wheeler lamps, indicators and mirrors — how a headlight, tail-lamp or rear-view mirror is designed, moulded and bolted to a scooter next door to the customer's own line, why the shift from halogen bulbs to LEDs quietly multiplies what Fiem earns per bike, and why the same customer that made this company can also unmake it.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Fiem Industries makes the lights and mirrors you see on an Indian scooter or motorcycle — the headlamp, the tail-lamp, the winking orange indicators, the rear-view mirrors — plus the plastic and sheet-metal parts around them. It is one of the country's largest automotive-lighting specialists focused on two-wheelers, and it is unusually good at exactly one thing: designing and mass-producing lamps and mirrors, in enormous volume, for the handful of companies that build most of India's bikes. Its biggest customers are Honda's Indian arm (HMSI) and TVS Motor, with Yamaha, Suzuki, Royal Enfield, Hero and a growing list of electric-scooter makers behind them.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a focused specialist, not a diversified major. Roughly 99% of revenue is automotive, and the overwhelming majority of that is two-wheeler lighting and signalling. Where a Uno Minda or a Varroc spreads across dozens of parts and into cars, Fiem has deliberately gone deep on one product family for one vehicle type — and become a top-two supplier of lamps to the OEMs that matter. FY26 revenue was ~₹2,791 Cr, PAT ~₹254 Cr, on a well-run, essentially debt-free balance sheet.
  • Its content per bike keeps rising even when bike volumes don't. The two-wheeler market is shifting halogen → LED and entry-level → premium, and every step up puts more lighting value on the same vehicle. A basic halogen headlamp is a commodity; an LED headlamp with daytime-running lights, a signature light-bar and a fancier tail-lamp cluster is worth multiples of it. Fiem sells the more expensive part, so its revenue can grow faster than the number of scooters sold. LED already accounts for ~63% of its automotive-lighting revenue.
  • Its greatest strength is also its single biggest risk: Honda. HMSI and TVS together are around 70% of Fiem's automotive revenue, and HMSI alone has historically been ~40%. Deep, decades-old, co-located relationships are exactly why Fiem is so profitable — and exactly why one customer's model decisions, insourcing choices or supplier rebalancing can move the whole company. The stock trades near 22× earnings: a fair-to-full multiple for a high-return, single-customer-heavy ancillary in a genuine content up-cycle.
In plain terms

Fiem is a precision lamp-and-mirror factory bolted to India's scooter industry. When Honda or TVS designs a new bike, Fiem's engineers help design its lights, tool up the moulds, and then supply the finished lamps — often from a plant built right next to the customer's assembly line — for the entire life of that model. The money is made not by inventing a breakthrough but by being embedded: reliable, co-located, quality-approved, and hard to replace mid-model. The upside is that as bikes get brighter and smarter, each one carries more Fiem content. The catch is that a supplier this concentrated lives and dies by a small number of customer relationships — the very thing that makes it steady in good years makes it fragile if one of those relationships cools.

01

Automotive lighting 101: why a headlamp is a real business

No engineering degree needed — just why the humble bike lamp is worth caring about.

A vehicle lamp looks trivial and isn't. It is a small assembly of optics, electronics, plastics and precision moulding that has to be bright enough to see by, legally compliant, waterproof, vibration-proof for years on Indian roads, and cheap enough to fit a scooter's bill of materials. Making millions of them, to an OEM's exact spec, on time, without a single field failure that could trigger a recall — that is the actual skill. Fiem sits on the supply side of this: it designs and manufactures the lighting and signalling gear that bike-makers bolt on. A few terms unlock the whole business.

The handful of terms that run this industry
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
OEM"Original Equipment Manufacturer" — the vehicle maker (Honda, TVS, Yamaha…). Fiem sells to OEMs, who fit its lamps on the production line. Winning a "platform" (a bike model) means supplying it for that model's whole life.
Lighting & signallingHead-lamps and tail-lamps (you see by them) plus indicators, blinkers, DRLs and licence lamps (you signal with them). Fiem's core product family — designed, moulded and assembled in-house.
Halogen vs LEDThe old bulb (halogen) vs the solid-state light (LED). LED lamps are brighter, last longer, use less power and allow slim, styled "signature" lighting — and they cost and are worth much more per lamp. The whole growth story is this switch.
Content per vehicleThe rupee value of parts one supplier puts on a single bike. A bike moving from halogen to a full LED cluster, or from a basic to a premium model, lifts Fiem's content per vehicle — the number that lets it out-grow bike volumes.
Wallet shareThe share of a customer's spend on a given part that goes to one supplier. Fiem's wallet share at HMSI and TVS runs very high on some parts (up to 100% on mirrors) — a measure of how embedded it is.
The economics that decide everything: content, not units

An auto-lamp maker has two ways to grow. One is more vehicles — but India's two-wheeler volumes are cyclical and, over a decade, only modestly higher. The other, far more powerful for Fiem, is more value on each vehicle. Every time a scooter swaps a halogen headlamp for an LED one, adds a daytime-running-light bar, upgrades to a three-dimensional tail-lamp, or steps up from a commuter to a premium trim, the lighting bill of materials rises — and Fiem, if it holds the platform, captures that increase without selling a single extra unit. That is why a supplier tied to a flat-ish end market can still compound: it is riding premiumisation of the part, not growth in the number of bikes.

Who buys two-wheeler lamps

  • Mass two-wheeler OEMs — HMSI (Honda's scooter and motorcycle arm), TVS, Hero, Bajaj, Yamaha, Suzuki, Royal Enfield. The bulk buyers; a spot on their approved-vendor list is the whole game.
  • EV two-wheeler makers — Ola Electric, Ather, Hero Vida, River, Ampere and others. A newer, fast-growing class of customer whose vehicles are more electronics-heavy and LED-intensive from day one.
  • The aftermarket & exports — replacement lamps and mirrors sold after the sale, plus lighting supplied to global OEMs. Smaller today, but a useful diversifier away from pure OEM dependence.
02

What Fiem actually sells: one bike, several parts

The product families — read this once and the revenue mix makes sense.

Fiem's pitch to a bike-maker is that it can supply an integrated bundle of the visible bits: the lamps, the mirrors and the plastic body parts around them, all designed, tooled and made under one roof and delivered to the assembly line. That single relationship spreads across four product lines with quite different economics — and one of them dwarfs the rest.

The four product lines, decoded

  • Automotive lighting & signalling — by far the largest line: head-lamps, tail-lamps, indicators, DRLs, licence-plate lamps. This is the heart of Fiem, and within it the story is the halogen → LED shift (§05) that keeps lifting the value of every lamp.
  • Rear-view mirrors & signalling assemblies — prismatic and plastic mirrors and related fittings. Small-looking, but Fiem's wallet share here is exceptionally high (up to 100% at HMSI), which tells you how trusted and embedded it is on the visible exterior parts.
  • Plastic-moulded & sheet-metal parts — the housings, brackets and body components that go with the lamps. Lower-margin, more commoditised, but it deepens the relationship: an OEM that already buys Fiem's lamps can hand it the surrounding plastics too.
  • LED luminaires (non-auto) — a separate diversification into general LED lighting: bulbs, tube-lights, street-lights, flood-lights and panel lights for homes, industry, government and even railway display systems. Still a small slice of revenue, but a hedge that uses the same core LED competence outside autos (see §08).

Why the "visible parts" bundle matters

A bike-maker fitting a new scooter wants the exterior — the "face" of the vehicle that a buyer sees in the showroom — to look sharp, work flawlessly, and arrive on time. By supplying the lamps, mirrors and surrounding plastics together, Fiem becomes the single vendor for that whole visible front-and-rear package. That is convenient for the OEM and sticky for Fiem: once its parts define how a model looks and its tooling is amortising over a multi-year run, replacing it means re-styling, re-tooling and re-validating — a cost few OEMs will bear unless something has gone badly wrong. The flip side, as always with deep integration into one customer, appears in §04.

03

The company, the founders, and who owns it

A promoter-built, Sonipat-headquartered specialist — 35 years in one trade.

Fiem is a first-generation, family-promoted business, founded in 1989 by J.K. Jain and Seema Jain and still run by the family. It has never been a conglomerate dabbling in auto parts; it has spent its entire life making lamps and mirrors for two-wheelers, which is a large part of why it is trusted on OEM approved-vendor lists that take years to earn a place on.

Timeline

  • 1990s–2000s

  • 2006

  • 2010s
    Adds an LED luminaires business (general indoor/outdoor lighting) and secures railway display approvals (RDSO IPIS) — a diversification off the same LED competence.
  • ~2016 onward
    The halogen → LED transition in two-wheeler lighting begins in earnest, steadily lifting content per vehicle and mix.
  • 2021–2024
    Onboards EV two-wheeler customers (Ola, Ather, Ampere and others); promoter stake is trimmed via market sales, widening the float.
  • FY25–FY26
    Record years: FY26 revenue ~₹2,791 Cr (+16%), PAT ~₹254 Cr (+24%), EBITDA margin ~14%; nearly debt-free; ROCE ~29%. LED at ~63% of auto-lighting sales; four-wheeler entry begun.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Jain family (founder J.K. Jain as Chairman & Managing Director; son Rahul Jain in a senior operating role) hold roughly ~55% — down from ~66% a couple of years earlier after the promoters pared their stake in the market. That reduction widened the public float and improved liquidity, but the family retains clear control.
  • A single-segment company: Fiem operates essentially as one auto-components business rather than a sprawl of subsidiaries. That simplicity is a feature — there is no holding-company overhang, no complex group map to untangle, and the balance sheet is clean and nearly debt-free, funding its own capex (~₹100 Cr guided for FY27) from internal cash.
  • Returns over scale: the family has historically prioritised quality of business — high return ratios (ROCE ~29%, ROE ~23%), steady dividends (yield ~1.8%) and conservative gearing — over chasing revenue for its own sake. That temperament is central to how the stock is valued (§10).
Why the founder's focus is the asset: approved-supplier status at a Japanese-parented OEM like HMSI is not bought with a low bid — it is earned over years of zero-defect delivery, and it is guarded jealously once won. A 35-year, family-run specialist that has never diluted its attention across a dozen unrelated products is precisely the kind of vendor those OEMs keep. The concentration in §04 is the cost of that same focus.
04

The Honda question: strength and single point of failure

The one idea that decides how you should think about this company.

Every analysis of Fiem eventually collides with the same fact: an enormous share of its revenue comes from a very small number of customers, and one of them — Honda's HMSI — towers over the rest. This is simultaneously the reason Fiem is so profitable and the reason it can never be a low-risk holding. You cannot understand the company without sitting with both truths at once.

The distinction that resolves the paradox: dependence vs entrenchment

Dependence and entrenchment look similar on a pie chart but behave oppositely. Dependence is Fiem needing HMSI more than HMSI needs Fiem — the danger case, where the customer can dictate price, dual-source, or insource and Fiem simply absorbs it. Entrenchment is the reverse pull: when Fiem owns 100% of a customer's mirrors and 76% of its tail-lamps across live models, replacing Fiem means re-tooling, re-validating and risking line stoppages on bikes already in production — a cost and risk the OEM would rather not take. The real question for an investor is which force dominates. The high wallet-share and multi-decade tenure argue that Fiem is entrenched, not merely dependent — but entrenchment is defended platform-by-platform, and it can erode quietly if Fiem loses the next model rather than the current one.

The concentration risk in plain words: if HMSI decided to bring lighting in-house, hand a new platform to a rival, or simply lost share in the scooter market to TVS and others, the effect on Fiem would be large and hard to offset quickly. Diversifying the customer base — deeper into TVS, EV makers, exports and four-wheelers — is not a growth luxury for Fiem; it is risk management. Watch the trend in HMSI's share of revenue as closely as you watch revenue itself.
A genuine surprise: the thing that would most damage Fiem is not a recession or a raw-material spike — it is a design decision inside one customer. If HMSI restyles a best-selling scooter and awards the new lamp to someone else, Fiem can lose a chunk of revenue while India's two-wheeler market is still growing. The company's fate is unusually hostage to product-planning meetings it doesn't sit in.
05

Halogen → LED: how the same bike pays Fiem more

The structural tailwind that lets a supplier out-grow its end market.

If concentration is the risk, the LED transition is the reward — and it is the single most important growth mechanism in the business. India's two-wheelers are steadily swapping cheap halogen bulbs for LEDs, and buyers are trading up from bare-bones commuters to better-equipped, better-looking bikes. Both trends do the same thing to Fiem: they raise the lighting value bolted onto each vehicle. This is how a company tied to a cyclical, only-modestly-growing end market can still compound revenue.

LED has plateaued near 63% for several quarters, which is exactly why management flags a further push toward ~70% over the next two-plus years. Each point of LED mix is worth more than a point of volume, because an LED lamp carries far higher content — and margin — than the halogen part it replaces.

Here is the mechanism, made concrete. A conventional halogen head-lamp is a low-value, near-commodity assembly. Replace it with an LED head-lamp, and you are now selling optics, an LED board, thermal management and often a styled daytime-running-light (DRL) signature — a part worth a multiple of the old one. Add a three-dimensional LED tail-lamp cluster and sequential indicators, and the lighting bill of materials on a single scooter can rise substantially. Fiem doesn't need Indians to buy more bikes to grow; it needs each bike to carry a brighter, smarter, more expensive set of lights — which is precisely the direction the market is moving.

The non-transferable arithmetic: content, not units, is the engine

Put numbers on it. India's two-wheeler industry sells on the order of ~20 million units a year, a figure that swings with the cycle but grows slowly across cycles. Fiem's revenue, by contrast, has compounded at a mid-teens rate — because the value of lighting per bike is rising faster than the number of bikes. A halogen-to-LED head-lamp swap alone can multiply the content on that lamp; layer on premiumisation and EV-specific lighting, and you get a supplier whose top line can grow double-digits in a year the two-wheeler industry itself barely moves. The clean test of the thesis is therefore not "did bike sales rise?" but "did Fiem's revenue per vehicle rise?" — content growth is the whole game, and LED mix is its most visible dial.

06

The EV two-wheeler bet: more electronics, more light

Why electrification is a content opportunity, not a threat, for a lamp maker.

Electrification terrifies plenty of auto-component makers — anyone selling exhausts, fuel systems or engine parts watches EVs erase their market. For a lighting specialist, the opposite is true. An electric scooter still needs head-lamps, tail-lamps, indicators and mirrors — and it tends to want more of them, and fancier ones, because EV brands lean on distinctive lighting signatures to look modern and premium. Fiem's product is agnostic to what turns the wheels; the switch to electric is, if anything, a tailwind.

Electric two-wheelers now make up roughly 9% of the market and climbing. Because EV models are LED-intensive and electronics-rich from launch, each incremental EV tends to carry more Fiem-type lighting content than the halogen commuter it displaces — turning electrification into a mix-up, not a mix-down.

Crucially, Fiem got onto the EV bandwagon early rather than as a defensive afterthought. It supplies a spread of electric two-wheeler makers — Ola Electric, Ather, Hero Vida, River, Ampere and others — and has been winning fresh models: lighting for Ather's newer platforms, River's electric scooter, Royal Enfield's electric line and expanding volumes at Hero's Vida. That breadth matters for two reasons. First, it is genuine customer diversification away from the HMSI/TVS concentration of §04. Second, EV startups value a supplier that can co-develop distinctive LED lighting quickly — exactly Fiem's design-plus-tooling strength.

Why "EVs are safe for Fiem" isn't complacency

The reassurance has a real basis, but it carries a caveat worth naming. The good news is structural: lamps and mirrors are needed whatever the powertrain, and EV design conventions favour more, richer LED lighting — so Fiem's addressable content per vehicle goes up as bikes electrify. The caveat is that the EV two-wheeler market is young, crowded and financially fragile: several EV startups are loss-making and some may not survive, so a lamp supplier must manage credit risk and avoid over-committing tooling to a brand that could fold. Fiem's edge is that it can serve both the incumbents electrifying (Honda, TVS, Bajaj going electric) and the pure-play startups — hedging which camp ultimately wins.

07

The plant-next-door model: geography as a moat

A structural quirk that quietly explains both the stickiness and the margins.

Here is something a casual reader would never guess about a lamp company: much of Fiem's competitive advantage is physical location. Its factories are deliberately scattered across India's auto hubs so that a plant sits close to — sometimes effectively next door to — the customer's own assembly line. This co-location is not incidental; it is the operating model, and it shapes everything from stickiness to working capital.

Fiem runs a spread of plants across the two-wheeler manufacturing belt — around Kundli and Rai in Sonipat (Haryana), Hosur (Tamil Nadu), Mysore (Karnataka), Nalagarh (Himachal Pradesh), Tapukara (Rajasthan) and Ahmedabad (Gujarat). The pattern is not random: these are precisely the regions where HMSI, TVS, Hero and others build bikes. A plant near HMSI, a cluster near TVS at Hosur — the map of Fiem's factories is a shadow of the map of its customers' factories.

Why building next to the customer changes the economics

Lamps are bulky, fragile and low-value-density — expensive and risky to truck across the country. Delivering them just-in-time to an OEM line hundreds of kilometres away means high freight, breakage and inventory buffers. Build the plant next door, and those costs collapse: parts move a short distance, arrive in sequence on the line, and both supplier and customer hold less inventory. But the deeper effect is lock-in. A supplier that has invested in a dedicated plant beside a customer's factory is woven into that customer's logistics — and a rival bidding for the business would have to replicate not just the tooling but the geography. Co-location turns a commercial relationship into an industrial one that is genuinely hard to unpick.

The quirk worth remembering: Fiem's capex plans are best read as a map of where its customers are growing. Management has flagged that a large share of FY27's ~₹100 Cr capex is going toward Hosur in the south — which is exactly where EV and TVS-linked demand is expanding. When Fiem builds a plant, it is placing a bet on a specific customer, in a specific place, for years — so its factory footprint is a leading indicator of which relationships it expects to grow. The cost of that model is its mirror image: a plant built for a customer that stumbles is capacity you cannot easily redeploy.
08

Luminaires and four wheels: the diversification legs

Two attempts to widen the business beyond two-wheeler lamps — one older, one new.

A company as concentrated as Fiem has an obvious strategic imperative: widen the base. Two efforts stand out — an older diversification into general LED lighting, and a newer push into four-wheeler (passenger-vehicle) components. Neither is large today; both matter for how the story could broaden over the next five years, and both come with real caveats.

LED luminaires — the same competence, a different customer

Years ago Fiem leveraged its LED know-how to enter general lighting: bulbs, tube-lights, panel lights, street-lights, flood-lights and bay-lights for homes, factories, government and outdoor use — well over a hundred products. It even secured railway approvals (RDSO) to supply LED passenger-information display systems for trains and buses. The logic is sound: the core skill (LED optics and electronics, made at scale) travels beyond autos. In practice, though, the luminaires business remains a small slice of revenue and a lower-margin, more competitive market than automotive lighting, so it functions more as a modest hedge and capability-showcase than as a second engine.

Four-wheelers — the new, still-unproven leg

The more consequential push is into passenger-vehicle (four-wheeler) components — a far larger addressable market than two-wheelers, where Fiem has historically been minor. Management has been building toward this, but candidly: four-wheeler revenue is still only around 2.5% of the total, and the ramp has been pushed out — guidance now points to meaningful revenue spilling into FY28, because winning and converting car-maker programmes takes longer than two-wheeler ones. It is a real optionality, not a near-term driver.

How to weigh the diversification honestly

Treat both legs as call-value on the base business, not as the reason to own the stock today. The luminaires arm proves Fiem's LED capability extends beyond scooters but has not scaled into a major profit centre in a decade of trying. The four-wheeler push is the one that could genuinely change the size of the company — cars carry far more lighting content than bikes — but it is early, slower than hoped, and unproven at scale. The right posture is to value Fiem on its two-wheeler lighting franchise as it exists, and regard a successful four-wheeler ramp as upside to be underwritten only once the revenue actually shows up.

09

The industry and the rivals

A field of larger, more diversified lighting groups — and Fiem's deliberate narrowness.

Automotive lighting in India is contested by several scaled players, most of them bigger and broader than Fiem. The interesting thing is that Fiem competes not by matching their breadth but by out-focusing them — being the deepest, most embedded specialist in two-wheeler lamps rather than a generalist across autos. Understanding the rivals clarifies exactly what Fiem is and isn't.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, Fiem is the odd one out by choice: where the peer set has diversified across parts and vehicle types, Fiem has stayed narrow and gone deep — which is why its return ratios are strong and its customer relationships unusually sticky, but also why it is more exposed to a handful of accounts than any of them. Second, the competition that should worry a Fiem shareholder is less a rival winning share and more a customer changing behaviour — insourcing lighting, dual-sourcing a platform, or awarding a new model elsewhere. In two-wheeler lamps, Fiem's entrenchment is real; the risk is concentrated not across the industry but inside two customer relationships.

10

Financials & valuation

A high-return, debt-light compounder — priced fairly-to-fully for steady growth.

Fiem's numbers describe a quietly excellent small-cap industrial: mid-teens revenue growth, faster profit growth, strong and improving return ratios, and almost no debt. The market knows this — the stock trades around 22× earnings, a full-but-not-extreme multiple. The debate is not whether the business is good (it plainly is) but whether growth and mix keep improving fast enough to justify the price against the concentration risk.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A quietly excellent specialist — asked to prove it can widen without losing its edge.

Fiem enters the next few years in strong shape: a high-return, debt-light franchise riding a real content up-cycle in two-wheeler lighting, with EV wins and a four-wheeler entry as optionality. The genuine debate is narrow and specific — can it keep lifting content per bike and broaden its customer base fast enough to earn its multiple, without a stumble at HMSI undoing the story?

The bull case

  • Content-per-vehicle tailwind — halogen → LED and premiumisation lift lighting value per bike; revenue can out-grow two-wheeler volumes for years.
  • Entrenched, high wallet-share position — up to 100% of some parts at HMSI/TVS; validated tooling and co-located plants make it hard to displace.
  • EV is a mix-up, not a threat — electric scooters need more, richer LED lighting; Fiem already supplies Ola, Ather, Vida, River and more.
  • Quality financials — ~29% ROCE, ~23% ROE, nearly debt-free, self-funding capex, steady dividends — a genuine compounder.
  • Optionality — four-wheeler entry and LED luminaires could widen the base and the addressable content per vehicle over time.

The bear case

  • Customer concentration — HMSI + TVS ~70% of revenue; a lost platform, insourcing or share loss at one customer would hit hard.
  • LED mix plateau — the LED share has stalled near 63% for several quarters; if it doesn't push toward 70%, the content story slows.
  • Cyclical, competitive end market — two-wheeler demand swings with the economy; larger, diversified rivals (Uno Minda, Lumax, Varroc) have scale.
  • Cost pressure — minimum-wage hikes (notably in Haryana) and raw-material inflation squeeze the ~14% margin near term.
  • Diversification unproven — four-wheeler ramp pushed to FY28; luminaires still tiny after years — the widen-the-base bet is slow.
The bottom line

Fiem is one of those small industrials that does an unglamorous thing exceptionally well: it lights India's scooters, and it earns high returns doing so because it is embedded in its customers to a degree few suppliers achieve. The next chapter is not about survival — the franchise is sound and the balance sheet clean — but about shape: whether it can keep pushing LED content up each bike while quietly reducing how much of its fate sits inside one customer's product-planning room. Two numbers will tell you how it's going, printed every quarter: the LED share of auto-lighting revenue climbing back above its 63% plateau toward ~70%, and HMSI's share of total revenue drifting down as EV, export and four-wheeler wins fill in. Watch those two together — rising content, broadening base — and you are watching a specialist turn its greatest vulnerability into just another line on a longer customer list.
