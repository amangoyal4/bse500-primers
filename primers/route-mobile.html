00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Route Mobile Limited is a cloud-communications company — in the jargon, a CPaaS provider (Communications-Platform-as-a-Service). In plain English, it is the delivery pipe and switchboard that businesses rent to send you messages: the one-time password (OTP) from your bank, the "your order is out for delivery" text from an online retailer, the WhatsApp booking confirmation from an airline, the marketing blast from a brand. Route Mobile plugs its software into thousands of enterprises on one side and into hundreds of mobile networks on the other, and moves the traffic in between. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) it carried 174.9 billion billable transactions for more than 3,100 active clients.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The headline revenue is mostly other people's money passing through. When Route bills an enterprise for an SMS, most of that charge is a fee it must hand straight to the mobile operator that actually delivers the message. So of ~₹4,408 Cr of FY26 revenue, only ~₹1,007 Cr — the gross profit — is money Route keeps before its own costs. The real size of the business is that ₹1,007 Cr, not the ₹4,408 Cr top line. Revenue in CPaaS flatters.
  • The margins are thin by nature. After paying the operators, Route keeps a gross margin of only ~22.9%, and after its own people and platform costs, an adjusted EBITDA margin of ~11.9% and net profit of ~₹257 Cr. This is a high-volume, low-margin logistics business dressed in software clothing — closer to a courier than to a typical SaaS company.
  • It is now controlled by a Belgian telecom group, and its future is a merger question. In 2024, Proximus (Belgium's incumbent telco) took majority control of Route Mobile and has since folded it — alongside its US identity firm Telesign and wholesale carrier BICS — into a single global entity called Proximus Global. Route Mobile stays listed in India for now, but the big overhang hanging over the stock is how this India-listed piece fits into the parent's global consolidation.
In plain terms

Picture a national courier that promises next-second delivery of a letter to any phone on earth. Businesses pay it per letter. But the courier does not own the last mile — the mobile operators do — so it pays them a toll on every single letter it hands over, and that toll eats most of what it collected. What is left over, a few paise per message, is the whole business. Route Mobile is that courier for text: it makes its money on gigantic volume at a wafer-thin margin, and it lives or dies on two things — keeping the toll it pays the operators lower than the price it charges enterprises, and moving customers up from plain SMS to richer, better-paid channels like WhatsApp and RCS before old-fashioned texting fades. Sitting on top of all of that is a new owner, Proximus, that is reshaping the whole group around it.

01

CPaaS 101: what Route Mobile actually does

No engineering background needed — just who plugs into whom, and why.

Every time a business needs to reach you on your phone at scale — reliably, in bulk, across every network and country — it faces a messy problem. There are hundreds of mobile operators worldwide, each with its own connection, pricing, spam rules and technical quirks. No single company wants to wire itself into all of them. So it rents a middleman that has already done the wiring. That middleman is a CPaaS provider, and Route Mobile is one of the larger ones. To follow the company you need five terms.

The only CPaaS terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
CPaaSCommunications-Platform-as-a-Service. Software (APIs) that lets a business drop messaging, voice or verification into its own app without building the plumbing. Route sells this platform plus the connectivity behind it.
A2P SMS"Application-to-Person" text — a message sent by a computer system to a human (an OTP, an alert, a promo), as opposed to person-to-person chat. This is Route's core, highest-volume product, and the mature part of the market.
MNO / operatorMobile Network Operator — Airtel, Jio, Vodafone, and hundreds abroad. They own the last mile to the handset and charge Route a per-message termination fee to deliver. Route has 280+ direct connects and reach to 900+ operators.
OmnichannelSending across many channels from one platform — SMS, WhatsApp, RCS, email, voice, Viber. The strategic growth direction, because richer channels carry richer margins than plain SMS.
Billable transactionOne chargeable message or interaction. Route counts these in the billions (174.9 bn in FY26). Volume × price-per-message × the margin left after operator fees = the whole model.
The one dependency that shapes the sector: Route does not own the last mile

This is the fact that explains almost everything about CPaaS economics. Route Mobile can write brilliant software, sign thousands of enterprises, and route traffic across the planet — but the actual handset is reached only by the mobile operator, and the operator charges for that. So a CPaaS company is structurally an aggregator sitting on top of infrastructure it rents. It buys message delivery wholesale from operators and sells it, bundled with software and reliability, to enterprises. Its profit is the gap between the two, and that gap is thin because operators know exactly how valuable their last mile is. This is why CPaaS gross margins sit in the low-to-mid 20s while pure software companies enjoy 70–80% — most of the money never belonged to the aggregator in the first place.

Who plugs in, and why they pay

  • Enterprises & OTT apps — banks, e-commerce, fintech, logistics, travel, social apps. They need OTPs, alerts, notifications and marketing delivered reliably and compliantly. They pay Route per message and value uptime, deliverability and one integration for many channels.
  • Mobile operators (MNOs) — on the other side, Route also sells operators a firewall product that filters and monetises the A2P traffic flowing into their networks, and shares revenue with them. So an operator can be both a supplier (of delivery) and a customer (of Route's firewall).
  • Developers — Route's APIs let a developer add a "send OTP" or "send WhatsApp confirmation" function in a few lines of code. The easier the integration, the stickier the platform, because ripping it out and re-wiring is painful.
02

Gross versus net revenue: the number that fools first-timers

The single most important structural fact about this company — understand it and the rest follows.

If you glance at Route Mobile and see "₹4,408 crore of revenue," you will badly misjudge the business. In CPaaS, the top line is largely a pass-through — money that lands in the company's revenue only to be paid straight back out to mobile operators. The number that tells you how big Route really is, and how much it can ever earn, is not revenue. It is gross profit — what remains after the operator fees are stripped out. In FY26 that was ~₹1,007 Cr, less than a quarter of the headline.

Think about a single A2P SMS. An enterprise pays Route, say, 25 paise to send it. To actually deliver it, Route must pay the recipient's mobile operator a termination fee — often the large majority of that 25 paise. Route pockets the difference, out of which it must still pay for its platform, its people, its network and its overhead. What finally drops to the bottom is a sliver measured in fractions of a paisa. Multiply that sliver by 174.9 billion messages and you have the company.

This is why revenue and profit can move in opposite directions here. In FY26 Route's revenue actually fell ~3.7% (from ₹4,576 Cr to ₹4,408 Cr) as it deliberately shed low-margin, high-volume SMS traffic and as some enterprises shifted channels — yet its gross profit rose to a record ₹1,007 Cr and gross margin climbed from 20.8% to 22.9%. Less revenue, more money kept. For a pass-through business, that is not a contradiction; it is the whole point. Chasing top-line volume at razor margins can destroy value; walking away from it can create value.

The mental switch a first-timer must make: watch gross profit, not revenue

Retrain your eye. For a grocer or a carmaker, revenue is a fair proxy for size. For a CPaaS aggregator it is not — it is inflated by the operator toll that flows through it. So when you read Route Mobile, mentally cross out the top line and look at gross profit as "revenue," and read gross margin as the sign of whether the mix is getting richer or poorer. A rising gross margin means Route is selling more of the good stuff (international traffic, WhatsApp, RCS, firewall, software) and less commodity domestic SMS. A falling one means the opposite. FY26 crossing ₹1,000 Cr of gross profit for the first time, on lower revenue, is the single cleanest signal in the results — and it is invisible if you only look at the headline.

03

What Route Mobile actually sells

One core product doing the heavy lifting, and a stable of richer ones being grown around it.

Route is fundamentally a messaging company with a widening product edge. The overwhelming bulk of its traffic and revenue is still A2P SMS, but management has spent years building higher-value products around that core — because SMS is a mature, low-margin, commoditising business, and the future spread has to come from somewhere richer. The revenue splits across a handful of lines with very different economics.

The product lines

  • A2P SMS (the core) — enterprise text: OTPs, alerts, notifications, promotions. Huge volume, thin margin, and the most commoditised. Domestic (India) SMS is the lowest-margin slice; international / cross-border A2P carries far better economics, so mix within SMS matters as much as SMS overall.
  • Omnichannel & new products — WhatsApp Business, RCS (the richer successor to SMS), Viber, IP messaging, email and voice, sold through one platform. In FY26 new-product revenue grew ~11.3% to ~₹353 Cr. Smaller today, but the intended engine of future margin and the answer to SMS decline.
  • Firewall & MNO monetisation — software sold to operators to filter, secure and monetise the A2P traffic hitting their networks. A different, stickier, revenue-share business that also deepens Route's operator relationships.
  • Digital identity & Network APIs — verification, anti-fraud "silent authentication," and emerging telco Network APIs (Route is a pioneer of these in India). This is the strategic overlap with parent Proximus's Telesign identity business, and a stated growth vector.

Why the mix shift is the smartest thing management controls

Route cannot set the operator toll and it cannot stop SMS from slowly commoditising — those are the market's to decide. What it can control is what it sells. Every rupee of revenue it can move from plain domestic SMS toward international A2P, WhatsApp, RCS, firewall or identity is a rupee earning a fatter margin. That is precisely why FY26's record gross profit came on falling revenue: management pruned the thinnest business and leaned into the richer lines. It won't happen in a straight line, and the new channels have their own toll-payers (Google for RCS, Meta for WhatsApp). But mix is the one dial Route turns itself, and it is turning it deliberately.

04

The founder, and the Belgian telco that now owns it

A bootstrapped Mumbai success story that sold control to a European incumbent.

Route Mobile has one of the more dramatic ownership arcs in the Indian mid-cap market: a business built from nothing by a first-generation entrepreneur, taken public, and then sold into the control of a foreign state-linked telecom group that is now reorganising the entire company around its own global ambitions. Understanding that arc is essential, because the identity of the owner now matters more to the stock than almost any operating metric.

Timeline

  • 2004
    Route Mobile founded in Mumbai by Rajdipkumar (Rajdip) Gupta, bootstrapped — no outside capital. It grows into a global A2P messaging aggregator over the following years.
  • 2020
    IPO on NSE & BSE (BSE code 543228). Lists as a fast-growing, founder-led CPaaS play and becomes a serial acquirer, buying messaging and platform businesses across geographies.
  • 2021–2023
    Acquisition spree — messaging, campaign and voice assets across India, the Middle East and beyond — to add scale, geographies and product. The "serial acquirer" phase.
  • Jul 2023
    Belgium's Proximus Group agrees to acquire a majority stake from the Gupta promoters — a landmark, control of an Indian listed tech company passing to a European telecom incumbent.
  • May 2024
    Proximus (via Proximus Opal) completes the acquisition: ~57.56% bought for ~₹5,922 Cr (~€643 m), and after a mandatory open offer its stake reaches 82.70%. Route Mobile now has a foreign telco promoter.
  • Sep 2024
    Proximus sells down below 75% to meet SEBI's minimum-public-shareholding rule — the sell-down draws strong institutional demand but leaves promoter holding right at the ~75% ceiling.
  • Jan 2025
    Proximus combines Route Mobile + Telesign (US digital identity) + BICS (wholesale carrier) into a single global business, Proximus Global, targeting €2 bn+ of international revenue within a couple of years.
  • FY26
    Record gross profit (~₹1,007 Cr, first time above ₹1,000 Cr); revenue eases to ~₹4,408 Cr; PAT ~₹257 Cr; dividend raised to ₹16.5/share. Leadership transitions at the Proximus level create noise (see section 07).

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter: Proximus (through Proximus Opal), holding roughly 74.8% as of mid-2026 — right at the regulatory ceiling for a listed Indian company. Proximus is majority-owned by the Belgian state, so Route Mobile's ultimate controller is a European government-linked telecom group. Confirm the exact promoter percentage in the latest shareholding filing.
  • The founders' new home: the Gupta family's Clear Bridge Ventures reinvested part of its proceeds into a stake in the combined Proximus Global entity — so the founders retained economic exposure to the wider group rather than only to the India-listed shell. Rajdip Gupta has continued in a senior leadership role.
  • What this means for the listed stock: a minority public shareholder in Route Mobile India now owns ~25% of a subsidiary that is being managed as one arm of a global consolidation. The listed entity's strategy, capital allocation and even its long-term independence are set at the Proximus level, not by an independent Indian board answerable primarily to public floaters.
Why the ownership change reframes the whole stock: before 2024, Route Mobile was a founder-led growth company whose fate was its own. Now it is a ~75%-controlled subsidiary of a foreign telecom group that has explicitly merged it into a global brand. That brings real benefits — access to Proximus's 900+ operator relationships, BICS's carrier scale, and Telesign's identity products to cross-sell. But it also means the minority's return depends on how fairly the parent treats the listed piece: related-party traffic pricing, the allocation of group opportunities, and, above all, the terms of any eventual merger or buy-in. The operating business and the shareholder-structure question are now two separate investments stapled together.

The per-message spread: where the profit actually lives

The single most important idea in the operating case — and it is measured in fractions of a paisa.

Most people track a messaging company by its revenue or its client count. Both mislead. The truest lens on Route Mobile is astonishingly granular: how much it keeps on each of the billions of messages it carries. Break the FY26 accounts down to the level of a single billable transaction and the entire business snaps into focus — and you see just how thin the margin per message is, and therefore how much the model depends on sheer scale.

Now sit with how small those numbers are. Route keeps roughly 3 paise of operating profit per message. For that to add up to ~₹526 Cr of EBITDA, it needs to carry billions upon billions of them — which is exactly why "174.9 billion transactions" is the headline operating metric, not revenue. A CPaaS aggregator is a scale machine: the fixed cost of the platform is spread across an enormous message count, so volume and mix, not price, drive profit. Lose a big block of volume and the platform still costs the same; win richer volume and almost all of the extra margin drops through.

The device that fits a CPaaS aggregator: (price per message) − (operator toll) − (platform cost), times billions

Forget "revenue grew" or "revenue fell." The number that runs Route Mobile is the spread it keeps per message, multiplied by the number of messages. Both halves move for different reasons. The spread widens when the mix tilts to international, WhatsApp, RCS, firewall and identity, and narrows when domestic commodity SMS dominates or operators raise their tolls. The volume grows with digital adoption and new clients but is under long-term threat as SMS gives way to app-based channels. FY26's story — record gross profit on lower revenue — is that story exactly: fewer, richer messages beat more, cheaper ones. The scoreboard is spread × volume, and a company can improve the first even while the second wobbles.

The per-message spread, tracked quarter by quarter against the transaction count, is the cleanest read on whether the business is getting better or merely bigger.

06

From SMS to omnichannel: the channel shift that decides the future

The structural force reshaping every CPaaS company — richer channels replacing the plain text.

The A2P SMS that built Route Mobile is a mature, slowly commoditising product. In several markets its volume is flat or falling as businesses and consumers move to app-based channels. That is simultaneously the biggest threat to a messaging aggregator and its biggest opportunity — because the channels replacing SMS (WhatsApp, RCS) are richer, more interactive, and generally carry a better spread. The company that manages the migration well grows margin as the old product fades; the one that clings to SMS shrinks with it.

Two shifts make this timely. First, RCS — Rich Communication Services, effectively an upgraded, branded, interactive form of SMS built into the phone's native messaging — has gathered momentum now that Apple supports it alongside Android, giving businesses a richer default channel than plain text. Second, WhatsApp Business has become a mainstream way for enterprises in India and beyond to reach customers with two-way, media-rich conversations. Route is building on both, plus conversational commerce and AI-assisted messaging, and pioneering telco Network APIs in India (programmable access to operator capabilities like verification and location).

The catch every CPaaS bull must weigh: new channels bring new toll-collectors

It is tempting to assume that moving "up the ladder" automatically fixes margins. It helps — but each richer channel comes with its own gatekeeper taking a cut. On SMS, Route pays the mobile operator. On WhatsApp it pays Meta's conversation fees; on RCS, Google sits in the path; on identity, the value is more software-like but the market is more competitive. So the migration is not a free upgrade from thin to fat — it is a change of landlord. The genuine prize is that these channels let Route sell orchestration, software and outcomes (campaigns, chatbots, verification success) rather than just carriage, and that is where a durable, above-commodity margin can live. But the company still does not own the pipe — it never has — and each new channel re-poses the same old question of how much the owner of the last mile will keep.

The migration also reframes the volume threat. If SMS transaction counts eventually decline, Route's headline "billions of transactions" could fall even as the business improves — because a single rich WhatsApp conversation or a verified-identity call is worth many plain texts. That is why, over time, gross profit and the per-message spread will tell the story more faithfully than raw transaction volume. The number that looks like the business today (message count) may quietly become the wrong one to watch.

07

The merger overhang: the question the accounts can't answer

The defining uncertainty for a minority shareholder — and it sits above the P&L entirely.

Everything so far has been about the operating business. But for someone buying the listed shares today, the largest single variable is not the per-message spread or the channel mix — it is the structural fate of the listed entity itself. Route Mobile is ~75% owned by Proximus and has been publicly branded and organised as part of a single global business, Proximus Global. What happens to the 25% that trades in Mumbai is an open question, and it hangs over the stock.

The facts, straight: Proximus took majority control in 2024, briefly held 82.7%, then sold down to ~75% only because SEBI requires at least 25% public float — not, it appears, out of any wish to keep a large independent minority. It has since folded Route Mobile, Telesign and BICS into one operating model with a unified global leadership, targeting €2 bn+ of combined international revenue. Company management has, at various points, publicly said Route Mobile will remain listed and pushed back on delisting speculation. But a business run as one arm of a global group, with a parent that owns three-quarters of it, structurally invites the eventual questions of delisting, buy-in, or a merger of the Indian assets into the group — on terms the controlling shareholder will heavily influence.

Adding to the noise, the group has seen leadership churn at the Proximus level — senior figures associated with the Route Mobile board and the global business departing through 2025–26 — which markets have read as a source of uncertainty even when the operating numbers held up. A stock's price can wobble on parent-level personnel news that has nothing to do with how many messages Route carried last quarter.

How to hold the overhang honestly, without over- or under-weighting it

Treat this straight: it is neither a guaranteed windfall nor a certain trap. A controlling parent that has merged the business into a global brand may one day offer to buy out the minority — sometimes at a premium — or it may leave the stock listed and simply run it for group ends, in which case the minority rides along at the parent's discretion. Neither outcome is promised, and the timing is unknowable. What a careful reader does is separate the two investments stapled together in this share: an operating CPaaS business with improving gross profit and a real channel-migration challenge, and a corporate-structure bet on how a Belgian state-linked parent will eventually treat the 25% it does not own. The first can be analysed from the accounts. The second cannot — and it may matter more.

08

The industry and the rivals

A global, consolidating aggregation market where scale and mix decide the winners.

CPaaS is a global business, and Route Mobile competes far beyond India. It is a mid-sized player in a market led by much larger Western names, with a strong home position in India and a growing international footprint. The competitive logic is the same everywhere: you win by being large enough to command good operator rates, and by selling a richer mix than plain SMS.

The pecking order, by approximate recent-year revenue, runs from Twilio (US, ~$4.5 bn) and Sinch (Sweden, ~$3.0 bn) at the top, down through Infobip (Croatia, ~$1.7 bn) to a cluster that includes Route Mobile (~$0.5 bn) and India-listed Tanla Platforms (~$0.45 bn). Remember that in a pass-through business those revenue figures overstate true size — but they do sketch the shape of the market: two large Western aggregators, then a chasing pack. The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, this is a scale-and-mix game, not a branding game: enterprises buy on price, deliverability and coverage, and the aggregator that carries the most volume gets the best operator rates and the widest spread on the same message. That is why the industry keeps consolidating — size is a genuine advantage in a pass-through business. Second, notice the pattern of who is buying whom: Kaleyra into Tata Communications, Vonage into Ericsson, and Route Mobile into Proximus. Standalone mid-sized CPaaS players are increasingly being absorbed into larger telecom or platform groups that can fund the scale and cross-sell the software. Route's tie-up with Proximus is not an oddity; it is the direction of the whole sector — which is both a validation of the model and the source of the overhang on its own listed shares.

09

Financials & valuation

Record gross profit on softer revenue, a big dividend, and a low multiple shadowed by the parent.

FY26 was a study in the gross-vs-net lesson: revenue eased ~3.7% while gross profit hit a record and margins improved, even though reported PAT fell — largely on a higher tax charge and one-offs rather than an operating collapse. The stock trades on an undemanding multiple, which reflects both the thin-margin nature of the business and the discount markets tend to apply to a tightly-controlled subsidiary with a merger question hanging over it.

The one number that settles the operating thesis: gross profit, read with the per-message spread

At single-digit-to-low-teens earnings, Route Mobile does not need a re-rating to work on the operating case — it needs gross profit to keep grinding higher. So the metric that proves or breaks it is not revenue and not even PAT (which tax and one-offs distort), but gross profit and gross margin, tracked quarter by quarter, read against the transaction count and the growth of new-product revenue. If gross profit keeps compounding as the mix tilts to international, WhatsApp, RCS and identity — the way FY26's record ₹1,007 Cr on lower revenue suggests — the cheap multiple looks like a mispricing. If SMS erosion outruns the new channels and gross profit stalls, the multiple is telling the truth. Gross profit times durability, not headline revenue, is the operating scoreboard — with the parent's intentions layered on top of everything.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cash-generative aggregator improving its mix — under the shadow of the owner that controls it.

Route Mobile enters FY27 with record gross profit, strong cash flow, a rising dividend, a richer product mix, and the backing of a global parent's scale — and it enters with a share price that has fallen, a maturing core product, and an unresolved question about what its controlling shareholder ultimately intends. Management guides to mid-to-high-single-digit revenue growth and an EBITDA margin around 12% for FY27: steady, not spectacular. Both the bull and bear cases start from the same accounts and diverge mostly on the two things above the accounts — the channel migration and the parent.

The bull case

  • Record gross profit, improving mix — ₹1,007 Cr gross profit on lower revenue shows the pivot to richer traffic is working; margin, not just volume, is rising.
  • Cheap with a real yield — ~9–12× earnings and a 3%+ dividend for a scaled, cash-generative CPaaS business; little optimism is priced in.
  • Cash-backed earnings — operating cash flow ~110% of EBITDA and a raised dividend point to genuine, disciplined cash generation.
  • Proximus scale & cross-sell — access to 900+ operator relationships, BICS's carrier reach and Telesign's identity products to sell into Route's enterprise base.
  • Omnichannel & identity optionality — WhatsApp, RCS, conversational commerce and Network APIs give a path to software-like margin if the climb up the channel ladder succeeds.

The bear case

  • Structurally thin margins — a pass-through model where ~77% of revenue is operator toll; gross margin in the low-20s caps how profitable it can ever be.
  • SMS erosion — the high-volume core is maturing; if app channels replace texts faster than Route can climb the ladder, gross profit stalls.
  • New-channel gatekeepers — WhatsApp (Meta) and RCS (Google) bring their own tolls; the migration is a change of landlord, not a free margin upgrade.
  • The merger overhang — a ~75% parent running the business as one arm of Proximus Global; delisting, buy-in or merger terms are set by the controller, and the minority is a price-taker.
  • Parent-level & governance risk — leadership churn at Proximus, related-party traffic pricing, and dividends that conveniently route cash to the majority owner all need scrutiny.
The bottom line

Route Mobile is a well-run, cash-generative aggregator having quietly improved the quality of its earnings — pruning cheap volume, lifting gross margin to a record, and paying out real cash — while the stock has drifted and the market has focused on what it cannot see. The operating challenge is concrete and datable: whether gross profit keeps compounding as SMS matures and the omnichannel and identity products scale up through FY27. If it does, a ~9–12× multiple on a business with rising gross profit and a 3%+ yield will look too cheap. But stapled to that operating bet is a second, un-analysable one: how a Belgian, state-linked telecom parent that owns three-quarters of the company and has merged it into a global brand will eventually treat the quarter it does not own. The first question the accounts can answer. The second they cannot — and for this particular stock, that second question may end up mattering most of all.
