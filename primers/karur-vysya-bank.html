00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

The Karur Vysya Bank (KVB) is a 109-year-old private-sector bank from the temple-and-textile town of Karur in Tamil Nadu. It lends mostly to small businesses (MSMEs), retail borrowers, farmers and traders across South India, and it is one of a distinct breed called "old private-sector banks" — long-established, regionally rooted, professionally-run banks with no single controlling owner, quite different from the newer giants like HDFC or ICICI. As of FY26 (year ended March 2026) it runs ~901 branches and about ₹2.14 lakh crore of total business (deposits plus loans).

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • This is a turnaround, and the turnaround is the whole story. Around 2019–20, KVB was a troubled bank: gross bad loans peaked at 8.68% of its book (March 2020) and profits had collapsed. Under a new professional CEO, it cleaned house. By FY26, gross NPAs are just 0.75%, net NPAs 0.19%, and net profit hit a record ₹2,510 crore, up 29%. That healing — not any single product — is why the stock re-rated.
  • It now earns like a quality bank. Return on assets (RoA) — the master gauge of a bank's efficiency — reached roughly 1.7–1.9% in FY26, and return on equity (RoE) climbed into the high-teens (~18%). Those are top-tier numbers for an old private bank, backed by a fortress 18.8% capital ratio and a lucrative gold-loan book.
  • The debate is "can it last?" KVB is concentrated in South India, leans on cyclical MSME and gold lending, and is now earning at what may be a peak in the credit cycle — bad-loan costs this low rarely stay this low forever. The stock re-rated from below book value to about 2× book; bulls see a permanently better bank, bears see peak earnings meeting a full valuation.
In plain terms

Picture a century-old neighbourhood bank in small-town Tamil Nadu that grew up lending to jewellers, textile units, rice mills and farmers. For years it did fine, then a wave of bad corporate and business loans nearly sank its profits. A new, SBI-trained boss arrived, stopped chasing risky big-ticket loans, shifted the book toward small, well-secured loans (a lot of them backed by household gold), digitised the plumbing, and slowly recovered the old bad debts. Five years later it is one of the healthiest small banks in India. The open question is simply whether a bank this small and this regional can keep earning like a big-league one once the good times in the credit cycle fade.

01

What is an "old private-sector bank"?

The category KVB belongs to — and why it's a species of its own.

Indian banks come in a few families, and knowing which one KVB sits in explains most of its character. There are the huge public-sector (government-owned) banks like SBI and PNB; the glossy new private banks licensed after 1993 — HDFC Bank, ICICI, Axis, Kotak; the small finance banks (SFBs) and payments banks of the 2010s; and then the oldest private survivors: the "old private-sector banks."

Old private banks are institutions that were already private and running before bank nationalisation in 1969, and simply never got taken over by the state. They tend to share a distinctive DNA:

  • Deep regional roots. Most were founded a century ago to serve a specific community or region — KVB for the traders and farmers of western Tamil Nadu; Federal Bank in Kerala; City Union Bank in Kumbakonam; South Indian Bank in Kerala. Their branch density and customer trust are concentrated where they were born.
  • No single promoter — professionally and widely held. Unlike the new private banks (which grew from a founding institution) or PSU banks (owned by government), the classic old private bank has no controlling shareholder. Ownership is spread across the public, mutual funds and foreign investors, and the bank is run by a professional board and management. KVB's "promoter" holding is barely ~2%.
  • SME, trade and gold heritage. They grew by financing local businesses, agriculture and gold-backed loans — sticky, granular, relationship-driven lending rather than flashy corporate deals.
Why the distinction matters to an investor

The old private banks are the "middle child" of Indian banking. They lack the enormous scale, brand and cheap deposits of an HDFC or SBI, so their cost of funds is higher and they can't win the biggest corporate loans. But they are more nimble, better-run and more profitable than most PSU banks, and their regional trust is a real moat in their home turf. The whole investment question for any of them is the same: can a small, regional, professionally-run bank grow faster than the giants while keeping bad loans low and returns high? KVB, post-turnaround, has become the poster child for "yes, for now" — which is exactly why it's worth understanding.

02

How a bank actually makes money

No finance degree required — five ideas explain almost everything a bank does.

A bank is, at heart, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (deposits) and lends it out more expensively (loans), and lives on the difference — minus the loans that go bad. Master five terms and you can read any bank's results, KVB included.

The banker's five-word vocabulary
TermWhat it means (and KVB's FY26 number)
NII & NIMNet Interest Income = interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits. Expressed against assets, it's the Net Interest Margin (NIM) — the bank's core "gross margin." KVB's NIM was ~3.97% in FY26. Higher = more profitable lending.
CASACurrent Account + Savings Account deposits — money that sits in cheque and savings accounts paying little or no interest. The more CASA, the cheaper a bank's funding. KVB's CASA ratio is ~27% — modest, and one of its weaker spots.
Fee incomeNon-interest earnings: transaction charges, processing fees, insurance/investment commissions, forex, card fees. It boosts profits without using up capital. A steady fee stream is a mark of quality.
Asset qualityHow many loans are going bad. Gross NPA (non-performing assets) is the % of loans in default; Net NPA is what's left after provisions; PCR (provision coverage) is how much of the bad loans the bank has already set aside money for; credit cost is the yearly hit to profit from bad loans; slippage is fresh loans turning bad. KVB: GNPA 0.75%, NNPA 0.19%, PCR ~96%.
Capital (CRAR)The bank's own cushion of shareholder money against losses, as a % of risk-weighted loans. Regulators demand a minimum (~11.5%); more is safer but can dilute returns. KVB's CRAR is 18.8% — very well capitalised.
The one equation to remember

A bank's profit is roughly: (interest earned − interest paid) + fees − operating costs − loan losses − tax. Two levers dominate. First, the spread (NIM), which is helped enormously by cheap CASA deposits and by lending to higher-yielding segments like small businesses and gold loans. Second, loan losses (credit cost): a bank can look wildly profitable for years and then hand it all back in one bad-loan cycle. That is precisely what happened to KVB around 2018–20 — and precisely what it fixed. So when you judge a bank, you're really asking two things: is the spread healthy and durable? and are the loan losses low and honest?

Why RoA is the master gauge

Because banks are so leveraged (a bank holds a slim sliver of its own capital against a mountain of deposits and loans), analysts reduce all of the above to one headline number: Return on Assets (RoA) — profit as a percentage of the bank's total assets. It nets out spread, fees, costs and loan losses into a single efficiency score. In Indian banking, an RoA above ~1.5% is considered strong; the very best (HDFC, Kotak, in their prime) run ~1.8–2%. KVB reaching roughly 1.7–1.9% in FY26 is the whole turnaround expressed in one figure — and it's why the market started treating it as a quality bank rather than a laggard.

03

The company and who controls it

From a 1916 trader's bank in Karur to a professionally-run, widely-held lender.

KVB was founded on 25 July 1916 in Karur, a textile and trading town in western Tamil Nadu, by two businessmen — M. A. Venkatarama Chettiar and Athi Krishna Chettiar — to meet the banking needs of the local trading and agricultural community. It has paid a dividend every year since inception, weathered the Depression and multiple cycles, and acquired several small banks along the way. Its centenary in 2016 was marked in Chennai in the presence of the then President of India.

Ownership & management

  • Widely held, no promoter. KVB has no controlling promoter — the promoter/founder-family holding is barely ~2%. The rest is spread across mutual funds (~32%), retail investors (~39%), foreign investors (FIIs ~19%) and domestic institutions. This professional, diffuse ownership is the classic old-private-bank structure: the board and management, not a dominant family, run the bank.
  • The CEO who turned it around. B. Ramesh Babu, MD & CEO since 2020, came from a long career at State Bank of India, where he was Deputy Managing Director & Chief Operating Officer. He brought big-bank risk discipline, retail/technology focus and a clear de-risking playbook — and in 2026 secured RBI approval for a third term, signalling continuity.
  • Balance-sheet strength. The bank is very well capitalised (CRAR 18.8%), comfortably above regulatory minimums, giving it room to keep growing loans without raising fresh equity.
04

The turnaround — the heart of the story

How a troubled bank became one of the best-run old private banks in five years.

If you understand one thing about KVB, understand this section. In FY2020 KVB was a problem bank: gross bad loans of 8.68% meant nearly one rupee in twelve of its loan book was in default, fresh slippages were running at ~4% a year, and profits had been gutted by the provisioning needed to cover it. Five years later it reports GNPA of 0.75% and a record profit. That journey — from stress to quality — is the re-rating.

What actually drove it — four levers

  • De-risking the book toward small, secured loans. KVB deliberately shrank chunky corporate exposure (down to ~14% of loans) and pivoted to "RAM" — Retail, Agriculture and MSME — granular, well-collateralised lending where a single default can't blow a hole in the book. A lot of the new lending is backed by physical gold (more on that next section), which is about as safe as secured lending gets.
  • Tighter underwriting & digitisation. Under an SBI-trained management, KVB rebuilt credit processes, added data-driven and digital underwriting, and centralised risk — so fewer loans go bad in the first place (slippages collapsed from ~4% to under 1%).
  • Aggressive recovery of legacy bad loans. The bank chased down and recovered or wrote off its old stressed assets while raising provision coverage, so the historic mess was cleaned rather than left to fester.
  • Steady, profitable growth on top. With the book de-risked, loans still grew ~15–17% a year, NIM stayed healthy, and fee income rose — so the bank grew and got safer at the same time. That combination is what produced the record profits.

05

The loan book — RAM, MSME and the gold engine

What KVB actually lends against — and why gold is central.

Post-turnaround, KVB's loan book is deliberately granular and secured, organised around the "RAM" strategy — Retail, Agriculture and MSME (small businesses) — with corporate lending cut to a supporting role (~14% of loans). Total advances reached about ₹98,750 crore in FY26, growing ~17% a year. The single most distinctive feature is how much of it is backed by gold.

Why gold loans are the quiet hero

A striking feature of KVB is that gold (jewel) loans make up roughly 30% of the entire loan book — the bank runs an internal cap of ~35%. In FY26, retail jewel loans grew ~61% and gold-backed lending is the backbone of the agriculture book (jewel loans are ~91% of agri advances). Here's why that matters:

  • It's about the safest lending there is. The borrower pledges physical gold jewellery worth substantially more than the loan (typically a ~75% loan-to-value). If they default, the bank sells the gold. Losses are minimal — which is exactly why gold loans helped drive KVB's NPAs so low.
  • It's high-yielding and fast. Gold loans carry attractive interest rates and can be disbursed in minutes against a physical pledge — good for both NIM and customer stickiness in KVB's semi-urban and rural heartland (over half its branches are in semi-urban/rural areas).
  • It fits the culture. Lending against household gold is deeply embedded in South Indian finance — KVB has done it for a century. It's a natural, trusted product for its customer base of traders, farmers and small households.

The trade-off inside the strategy

Small, secured, high-yield lending is what makes KVB safe and profitable — but it comes with two catches. MSME lending is cyclical: small businesses are the first to wobble when the economy slows, so this book can generate bad loans faster in a downturn. And a 30%-of-book gold concentration ties KVB to the gold price: if gold falls sharply, loan-to-value ratios spike, collateral cushions thin, and demand for fresh gold loans can swing. So far the design has worked beautifully; but "granular and secured" is a fair-weather superpower that gets tested in a real downturn.

06

Deposits, CASA and the margin

The funding side — where KVB is decent, not dominant.

A bank's profitability starts with how cheaply it can raise deposits. KVB's total deposits reached about ₹1,15,666 crore in FY26, growing ~13–15% a year. But its CASA ratio — the share of low-cost current and savings deposits — is ~27%, which is modest. This is the honest soft spot in an otherwise strong story.

Rates are illustrative. The more of a bank's deposits are CASA (near-free), the lower its overall cost of funds and the wider its NIM. KVB's ~27% CASA is below the best large private banks (~40%+), so it relies more on pricier term deposits.

The NIM picture

  • NIM ~3.97% in FY26 — a healthy core margin, helped by the higher-yielding RAM and gold book on the asset side. This is comfortably above most PSU banks and respectable versus the best private banks.
  • But margins are under mild pressure. As interest rates move and deposit competition stays fierce, KVB's NIM has drifted down modestly (Q1 FY26 NIM was ~3.86%, down year-on-year). With only ~27% CASA, KVB is more exposed than deposit-rich rivals to rising deposit costs — a key thing to watch as rate cycles turn.
  • Fee income supplements the spread. Beyond interest, KVB earns from transaction fees, processing charges, third-party product commissions and treasury — helping RoA without consuming capital.
The CASA challenge: raising low-cost deposits is the hardest thing for a small regional bank — it's exactly where the giants (HDFC, SBI) dominate with scale, brand and salary-account relationships. KVB's ability to lift CASA and hold NIM as rates fall is one of the two or three swing factors for whether its high RoA proves durable.

Why these numbers are genuinely good — with an asterisk

A GNPA of 0.75%, net NPA of 0.19% and 96% provision coverage together mean KVB has almost no unrecognised bad-loan risk on its books — a remarkable place for a bank that was at 8.68% five years ago. How to read the valuation

For a bank, the primary valuation lens is price-to-book (P/B) — the market value versus the accounting net worth — read alongside RoE, because a bank that earns a high RoE deserves a higher multiple of book. As of mid-2026 KVB traded around ₹294 a share, ~₹28,400 crore market cap, ~2× book value and ~11× earnings.

  • The re-rating. Through the troubled years KVB traded below book value (P/B under 1×) — the market's verdict on a bank with too many bad loans and weak returns. As RoA/RoE recovered, the multiple expanded to ~2× book. That P/B re-rating, layered on top of profit growth, is what delivered the strong share-price return.
  • The debate now. At ~2× book and ~11× earnings on peak-of-cycle return ratios, KVB is no longer cheap for an old private bank. Bulls argue a durably better bank earning ~1.8% RoA deserves it (and it's still cheaper than the new private giants). Bears argue you're paying up for return ratios that likely normalise down as credit costs rise from today's lows — leaving limited margin for error.
The valuation question in one line

Is FY26's ~1.8% RoA the new normal for a structurally better bank (in which case ~2× book is fair-to-cheap), or a cyclical peak that fades as loan losses normalise (in which case the multiple has re-rated ahead of reality)? Everything about KVB's near-term investment case comes down to that single distinction.

The industry and the rivals

A crowded field — bigger private banks above, SFBs below, and a pack of old private peers alongside.

KVB competes on several fronts at once. It is small enough that the giants can out-scale and out-fund it, yet it occupies the same MSME/retail/gold turf that small finance banks and other old private banks fight over. Understanding the competitive set clarifies both its edge and its ceiling.

Where KVB actually wins — and where it can't

KVB's edge is local trust, relationships and speed in its South-Indian MSME and gold heartland — a century-old brand a customer in Karur or Coimbatore knows and trusts, with fast, flexible small-ticket lending the giants find uneconomic to chase. Its ceiling is scale and funding cost: it will never have HDFC's cheap deposits or an SBI's balance sheet, so it competes by being better run and more profitable per rupee, not bigger. Among old private banks specifically, the turnaround has moved KVB from the middle of the pack to near the front — the strategic question is whether it can stay there as rivals (City Union, Federal, CSB) also sharpen up and gold-loan NBFCs press on its most profitable product.

10

The risks — read this before the bull case

A high-quality turnaround, but four honest question-marks.

KVB is a genuinely improved bank, but the same features that make it attractive carry their own risks. Four stand out — and together they frame the whole "can-it-last" debate.

1 · Regional concentration

KVB is heavily concentrated in South India, especially Tamil Nadu, where the bulk of its branches, deposits and loans sit. That concentration is a strength (deep local trust and knowledge) but also a risk: a regional economic shock, a bad monsoon hitting its agri book, or intensifying local competition would hit KVB harder than a geographically diversified bank. Its growth beyond the South is real but gradual.

2 · Cyclicality of MSME and gold

The RAM strategy leans on small businesses (cyclical, first to stress in a slowdown) and gold loans (~30% of the book, tied to the gold price). A sharp economic downturn would raise MSME slippages; a sharp fall in gold prices would thin collateral cushions and dent gold-loan demand. The book is secured, which limits losses — but it is not immune to the cycle.

3 · Margin pressure and the CASA gap

With CASA only ~27%, KVB relies more on pricier term deposits than deposit-rich rivals. As interest rates move and deposit competition stays intense, its NIM is under mild, persistent pressure. Sustaining a healthy spread while growing deposits is a permanent uphill task for a small regional bank.

4 · Can it sustain peak returns?

This is the big one. FY26's ~1.8% RoA and 0.75% GNPA are near-cyclical-best readings. Credit costs this low don't stay this low across a full cycle — when they normalise upward, RoA will drift down, even if management does everything right. The stock's re-rating to ~2× book already prices in a durably high-return bank; if returns instead prove cyclical, the valuation has little cushion.

The honest framing: KVB is best described as a high-quality turnaround with a regional-concentration and can-it-sustain-peak-returns overhang. The turnaround is real and the balance sheet is strong. But you are buying trough-cycle credit costs at a re-rated multiple in a small, geographically concentrated bank. That's a very different risk profile from "cheap and improving" — it's "good and fully-priced."
11

The future, and the bull/bear

A well-run small bank at a crossroads between "durably better" and "cyclically peaked."

KVB has done the hard part — it fixed a broken bank and now earns like a good one. The next chapter is less dramatic and more demanding: proving that the quality is structural, not cyclical, while growing beyond its South-Indian base without letting bad loans or margins slip.

The bull case

  • Structural turnaround — better underwriting, digitisation and a de-risked RAM book suggest the improvement is durable, not luck.
  • Top-tier returns — ~1.8% RoA and ~18% RoE put KVB near the front of the old-private-bank pack, with 96% PCR and 18.8% capital behind it.
  • Secured, high-yield niche — gold and MSME lending gives healthy NIM with low losses; a genuine regional moat of trust and speed.
  • Still cheaper than the giants — ~2× book / ~11× earnings is a discount to new private banks for comparable return ratios.
  • Continuity & capital — a proven CEO re-appointed for a third term, self-funding growth from a fortress balance sheet.

The bear case

  • Peak-cycle earnings — 0.75% GNPA and sub-1% credit cost will likely normalise upward, dragging RoA down from here.
  • Full valuation — the re-rating from below book to ~2× book already prices in durability; little margin for error.
  • Regional concentration — outsized exposure to Tamil Nadu / South India and to the monsoon-sensitive agri book.
  • Gold & MSME sensitivity — ~30% gold book ties it to gold prices; MSME is first to stress in a slowdown.
  • Margin & CASA gap — only ~27% CASA leaves NIM exposed to deposit competition and rate moves; scale disadvantage vs giants.
The bottom line

KVB is one of the best examples in Indian banking of a genuine, well-executed turnaround — a 109-year-old regional lender that took its bad loans from 8.7% to under 1% and turned a ~₹235 crore profit into ₹2,510 crore. The business quality is no longer in doubt. What is in doubt is durability and price: you are buying trough-cycle credit costs and peak return ratios in a small, South-concentrated bank that has already re-rated to ~2× book. Watch three numbers each quarter — credit cost (is asset quality holding as the cycle normalises?), NIM and CASA (is the spread durable?), and growth beyond the South (is the franchise widening?). Get those right and KVB is a quality compounder at a fair price; stumble, and a re-rated multiple on peak earnings has room to give back.
