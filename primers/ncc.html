NCC Limited, the builder whose cash is in the treasury

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

NCC Limited — for its first three decades, Nagarjuna Construction Company — is a Hyderabad-headquartered construction and EPC contractor and one of the largest privately-owned ones in India. It does not own toll roads, power plants or apartments to sell. It builds things for other people: commercial towers and government complexes, drinking-water pipelines and sewage-treatment plants, highways and flyovers, electrical substations and distribution networks, and — a segment most readers will not expect — contract mining, stripping overburden off coal seams for state mining companies. Its customers are overwhelmingly state and central government bodies and their agencies.

Three facts frame the whole company:

  • The order book has never been bigger. NCC closed FY26 (year to March 2026) with a consolidated backlog of ₹83,004 Cr — very close to four years of revenue already signed — after winning ₹31,884 Cr of fresh orders during the year. Ten years ago that book was about ₹19,300 Cr.
  • And revenue went down. FY26 revenue fell to ₹20,944 Cr from ₹22,355 Cr in FY25; net profit fell to ₹675 Cr from ₹820 Cr. A contractor with a record backlog shrank. That is not a demand problem — it is a payment problem, and NCC's management said so plainly.
  • The money is stuck at the client end. Working capital ran at roughly 33% of turnover during FY26 — about ₹5,406 Cr of the company's own money parked in unpaid bills, retention deposits and work done but not yet certified. That is roughly eight times the entire year's net profit. Net debt sat near ₹3,949 Cr (Q3 FY26), borrowed largely to fund exactly that gap.
In plain terms

NCC is a company that spends its own cash — concrete, steel, diesel, wages, subcontractors — to build assets that a state government has ordered, then waits for that government to inspect the work, certify it, and release the money. Everything between "we finished the pipeline" and "the payment landed" is financed by NCC's balance sheet. When the states pay on schedule, NCC is a fast-growing, capital-light builder with a four-year runway of work. When two or three states go slow, NCC's profit stays on paper, its borrowings rise, and — as happened in FY26 — the company will deliberately slow down work it has already won rather than lend more to a client who isn't paying.

01

What an EPC contractor actually does

The bid-build-bill-collect loop, and the five words that decide whether it works.

"EPC" stands for Engineering, Procurement and Construction. A client — usually a government department, a state water board, a municipal corporation, a public-sector power utility, sometimes a private developer — decides it wants a thing built: a 400-bed hospital, 900 km of village water pipeline, a 220 kV substation, a stretch of six-lane highway. It publishes a tender. Contractors like NCC study the drawings, price the job, and bid. In the vast majority of Indian government tenders, the winner is L1 — the lowest technically-qualified bid. NCC then designs the detail, buys the cement and steel and pumps, mobilises men and machines, and builds it over two to four years.

The revenue mechanics are where beginners go wrong. NCC does not get paid at the end. It gets paid in slices, and each slice has to be earned twice:

Why "percentage of completion" flatters a contractor

Accounting rules let a contractor book revenue and profit as work progresses, not when it is paid for. Pour a third of the concrete, book a third of the contract value and its margin. This is entirely correct and entirely standard — and it means a construction company's profit-and-loss statement can look healthy for years while its bank balance does not. It also means the single most useful test of an EPC company is not the P&L at all. It is whether operating cash flow keeps pace with EBITDA. If a contractor earns ₹1,836 Cr of EBITDA and collects a fraction of it, the profit is genuine but it is currently sitting in a government department's ledger rather than in the company's account.

The five divisions: what NCC actually builds

From office towers to sewage networks to coal overburden — a genuinely unusual spread.

Most listed Indian construction companies are recognisably one thing: a roads specialist, a transmission-line specialist, a marine specialist. NCC is deliberately not. It runs several substantially separate businesses under one balance sheet, each with its own tendering universe, its own client type and its own margin profile. The order-book mix below is the clearest single picture of the company.

1 · Buildings — the biggest single block

Roughly 31% of the backlog. This is NCC's oldest and deepest capability: commercial office towers and IT campuses, government secretariats and administrative complexes, mass housing for state housing boards, medical colleges and hospitals, university and institutional campuses, stadiums and convention centres. The work is labour- and subcontractor-heavy, relatively light on owned equipment, and the client list is a mix of private developers and — increasingly — government departments building public infrastructure. Buildings work is generally faster-cycle than heavy civil, which is why a big buildings share supports revenue velocity.

2 · Transportation — roads, highways, flyovers

About 22%. Highway packages for the national highways authority and state road agencies, urban flyovers and elevated corridors, bridges. This is the most crowded, most competitively-bid part of Indian construction: dozens of qualified bidders, thin spreads, and heavy dependence on a single agency's award calendar. Importantly, NCC bids these largely as a pure EPC contractor — it takes construction risk for a fee rather than building and owning toll roads on its own balance sheet, which is what wrecked several of its peers in the last cycle.

3 · Electrical (transmission & distribution) — bigger than you'd guess

Around 18% of the book, and at some points in FY26 above 20%. Substations, distribution networks, rural electrification, feeder separation, and smart-metering programmes for state discoms. On the backlog alone this makes NCC's electrical arm a meaningful power-infrastructure contractor in its own right — it competes for exactly the kind of work that specialists such as KEC International and Kalpataru chase. Very few investors associate NCC with T&D, which is precisely why the segment is worth naming.

4 · Mining — the division nobody expects

About 13%, and rising sharply over the last two years. NCC does contract mining: it is hired by coal companies to remove overburden — the hundreds of millions of cubic metres of soil and rock sitting above a coal seam — and to extract and haul coal. It is not a mine owner; it is a paid excavator at enormous scale. The economics are completely different from civil construction: heavy owned equipment (excavators, dumpers, dozers), long-tenure contracts often running 10–20 years, volumes measured in cubic metres rather than kilometres, and revenue that is far more annuity-like and repeatable than a one-off building. It also brings a different risk: fuel prices, machine availability and the client coal company's production plan.

5 · Water & environment — the segment that caused FY26

Around 10% of the book combined with railways, but far more consequential than that share suggests. This is drinking-water supply — intake works, treatment plants, transmission mains, village distribution networks — plus sewerage and sewage treatment. Much of it flows from centrally-sponsored, state-executed rural drinking-water schemes of the Jal Jeevan type, where the money is shared between the central and state governments and paid out by the state. That payment structure is the single reason NCC's FY26 looks the way it does, and it gets its own section below.

Plus: railways (station redevelopment, track and infrastructure work), irrigation (canals, lift-irrigation schemes — historically a large Telangana/Andhra business), and a modest international presence, principally in the Middle East.

The point of being this diversified

A single-vertical contractor lives on one client's budget cycle. When the highways authority slows its awards, a roads specialist has nowhere to go. NCC's spread means an air-pocket in one department can be filled by another: in FY26 the water division was throttled by non-payment, and the company kept the machine running on buildings, mining and electrical work. The cost of that flexibility is real, though — five different tendering universes means five different sets of qualification credentials, five different equipment pools and management attention split five ways. NCC is not the best-in-class specialist in any one of its verticals. It is the contractor that can credibly bid in all of them, which is a different and less glamorous kind of moat.

03

The company, the family, and who actually owns it

A Hyderabad construction house — with a surprisingly small promoter stake.

NCC began life in 1978 in Hyderabad as Nagarjuna Construction Company, founded by A.V.S. Raju, and grew through the 1980s and 1990s from a regional Andhra Pradesh civil contractor into a national multi-division builder. The name shortened to NCC Limited as the business outgrew its regional identity (approx. late-2000s). The founding family — through AVSR Holdings Private Limited and associated entities — still controls the board and management, with A.V.S. Raju's sons long involved in running the company.

A 22.81% promoter stake is the quiet oddity here

For a family-founded Indian industrial company, this is a strikingly small controlling holding — most comparable promoter families sit somewhere between 40% and 75%. Two consequences follow. First, NCC has historically been willing to issue equity to fund itself, and a large institutional register means the company is more exposed to outside pressure on capital allocation than a tightly-held peer would be. Second, at roughly a fifth of the capital, the promoter group holds effective rather than absolute control — a structure that has attracted periodic market speculation about strategic interest in the company. Set against that, the FY26 declaration that promoter holding carries zero encumbrance is a genuinely reassuring detail: pledged promoter shares have been the classic early-warning signal in Indian construction, and NCC does not have that problem.

Structure note: NCC's reported numbers are consolidated and include subsidiaries and joint ventures, some of which sit outside the core EPC business. When comparing NCC with peers, check whether you are looking at standalone or consolidated figures — the gap matters, and the order book quoted publicly is the consolidated one.

The ₹83,004 Cr order book — and what it does not tell you

Four years of work in hand. Also: the most over-quoted number in Indian construction.

NCC's backlog is the headline every broker note leads with, and its trajectory is genuinely impressive. It stood at roughly ₹19,323 Cr a decade ago. It passed ₹71,957 Cr by the September 2025 quarter, ₹79,571 Cr by December 2025 — up about 43% year-on-year at that point — and closed the year at ₹83,004 Cr. Fresh orders in FY26 alone came to ₹31,884 Cr, a book-to-bill of roughly 1.5×: for every rupee of revenue executed, one-and-a-half rupees of new work walked in the door.

Look at that chart for a moment. The equity market values the entire company at about 12 paise for every rupee of signed backlog. That is not, by itself, a bargain signal — a rupee of order book converts into roughly 8–9 paise of EBITDA and 3 paise of net profit, spread over several years, and the market is discounting execution risk, margin risk and, above all, collection risk. But it does frame the debate correctly: nobody is paying for the backlog here.

Three things a backlog does not guarantee

  • It does not guarantee timing. An order converts at whatever pace land, approvals, right-of-way, client decisions and — critically — client payments allow. A ₹5,000 Cr contract with a stalled client is a number in a slide, not a revenue stream. FY26 proved this precisely.
  • It does not guarantee margin. The price was fixed on the day of bidding, sometimes years before the concrete is poured. Where the contract lacks an escalation clause, every rupee of cement, steel or wage inflation between bid and build comes out of NCC's margin.
  • It does not guarantee it survives. Government orders can be re-scoped, deferred, or frozen — as happened in 2026 when central scrutiny of the rural water programme put a reported ~₹7,000 Cr of NCC's water backlog under a question mark (reported figure; treat as approx. and verify against company disclosure). Backlog is a claim on future work, not a certificate.
The right way to read an order book

Two derived numbers do more work than the headline. The first is book-to-bill — inflow divided by revenue — which tells you whether the runway is lengthening or shortening; NCC's ~1.5× in FY26 says lengthening, comfortably. The second is order-book-to-revenue, currently about 4.0×. Above roughly 3× a contractor has enough visibility that its constraint is execution capacity, not demand. NCC crossed that line some time ago. Which means, unusually, the interesting question about this company is no longer "can it win work?" — it plainly can — but "can it convert the work it has already won into money in the bank?"

05

The 8-to-1 problem: profit on paper, cash in the treasury

The crux. If you read one section of this primer, read this one.

Here is NCC's defining arithmetic. In FY26 the company earned ₹675 Cr of net profit. In the same year it had roughly ₹5,406 Cr of its own money locked up in working capital — around 33% of turnover at the September 2025 quarter, up from about 30% three months earlier. That is roughly eight rupees tied up for every one rupee of annual profit. To earn one incremental rupee of profit, NCC must first lend about eight to its customers, and its customers are state governments.

The order book measures what NCC has been promised. Working capital measures what NCC has advanced. The gap between those two numbers is the company.

That working capital is made of four things, and each behaves differently:

Note the fourth line carefully, because it is the trap. For a contractor with this working-capital intensity, growth consumes cash. Every additional ₹100 of revenue requires roughly ₹30 of incremental funding before the first rupee comes back. A contractor that grows revenue 25% a year while running at 30%-plus working capital either raises equity, borrows, or stops growing. NCC chose the third option in FY26 — which is the most revealing decision the management has made in years.

The contractor as an unwilling lender

Strip away the concrete and NCC's balance sheet has an odd resemblance to a lending business. It advances several thousand crore of capital to counterparties (state governments), earns a spread on that capital (the construction margin), takes duration risk (the payment cycle), and funds itself partly with borrowed money on which it pays real interest. The difference from an actual lender is that NCC never chose the exposure, cannot price it, is not compensated for it, and cannot refuse to extend it once the contract is signed. That is the structural inequity at the heart of Indian government contracting: the contractor carries the state's cash-flow timing risk for free.

Why state governments are the specific problem

Central-government agencies and public-sector undertakings — the national highways authority, the railways, central power utilities — generally pay to a schedule. State governments and their agencies are a different animal. A state's payment to a contractor competes, inside a finite treasury, with salaries, pensions, subsidies, welfare transfers and debt service. When a state's finances tighten or its political priorities shift, contractor bills are among the easiest items to defer, because deferring them causes no immediate visible harm to anyone except the contractor. Election cycles amplify this: spending accelerates before a poll and can freeze afterwards while a new administration reviews inherited projects.

NCC has lived this repeatedly. Its home region — the successor states of undivided Andhra Pradesh — has produced some of the sharpest examples, with large irrigation and civil dues stretching across administrations. In FY26 the pressure point was elsewhere: Uttar Pradesh, where the company's receivables ran to about ₹1,700 Cr before collections brought them down toward ₹1,200 Cr. One state. One programme. Roughly a fifth of the company's entire market capitalisation, outstanding.

The metric that settles this thesis: not the order book, not PAT, not the P/E. It is net working capital as a percentage of turnover — disclosed by the company each quarter, ~33% in FY26 — read together with receivable days (~73 at the FY26 close) and operating cash flow as a share of EBITDA. If working capital walks down toward the mid-20s while revenue grows, everything else in this business works. If it stays above 30%, NCC will keep funding its clients with borrowed money and the return on capital will stay mediocre no matter how large the backlog gets.
06

Jal Jeevan: the year NCC deliberately built less

The most instructive twelve months in the company's recent history — and the genuine surprise.

Rural drinking-water schemes of the Jal Jeevan Mission type work like this: the central government and a state government share the funding, the state's water department awards and supervises the contracts, and the state pays the contractor. For NCC's water division it was a large, welcome, multi-year opportunity — a backlog of roughly ₹7,000 Cr, around a tenth of the total order book, laying pipelines and building treatment capacity across several states.

Then the money stopped arriving on time. NCC had planned to execute roughly ₹4,000–5,000 Cr of Jal Jeevan work in FY26. Through the first nine months it executed roughly ₹1,300–1,500 Cr — somewhere around a third of plan. Not because pipe was unavailable, or crews were short, or the sites were inaccessible. Because the client was not paying, and NCC made the deliberate decision to slow down rather than pour more of its own capital into a project whose bills were not being settled.

The surprise worth sitting with

Almost every listed contractor's bad year is a story of not enough work, or work it could not win. NCC's FY26 was the opposite: it won more work than in any previous year in its history — ₹31,884 Cr — and then chose to build less of it. Revenue fell 6% and profit fell 18% in a year when the backlog grew 43%. That is a company voluntarily declining revenue it had already secured, because taking the revenue would have meant lending more money to a customer who had not repaid the last tranche. Read as an accounting outcome it looks like failure. Read as a capital-allocation decision it is arguably the most disciplined thing NCC has done in a decade — and it is the clearest evidence available that management now optimises for cash rather than for the top line.

There was a second, sharper development on the same programme. During 2026, central scrutiny of the rural water mission's implementation raised the prospect of order freezes and re-scoping across the contractors working on it, with press reports putting NCC's exposure at roughly ₹7,000 Cr of backlog alongside other listed infrastructure names (reported; not independently confirmed here). Separately, press reports in 2026 described a debarment action by the national highways authority affecting NCC, which knocked the shares to a 52-week low (reported; the scope, duration and status of any such order should be checked against the company's own stock-exchange filings before relying on it). Both items belong in the risk column with an asterisk rather than in the financial model.

What "recovery" looks like from here

  • Collections came first. Uttar Pradesh receivables fell from about ₹1,700 Cr toward ₹1,200 Cr as payments resumed — the first hard evidence that the freeze was a timing problem rather than a permanent write-off.
  • Then receivable days. Trade-receivable days improved to roughly 73 at the FY26 close from about 87 a quarter earlier, with receivables at ~₹3,336 Cr. Two consecutive quarters of that trend would matter more than any single order announcement.
  • Execution follows cash, with a lag. If the water division's payment cycle normalises, a large slug of already-won, already-mobilised work can be executed quickly — the sites exist, the design is done, the crews know the job. That is the mechanical source of the revenue rebound the market is being asked to underwrite.
07

Margins, and the discipline of the bidding table

Why 8.8% is a good number in this industry, and what pushes it around.

NCC earned ₹1,836 Cr of EBITDA on ₹20,944 Cr of revenue in FY26 — an operating margin of about 8.8%. To a reader coming from consumer goods or software this looks thin. In competitively-bid Indian EPC it is broadly normal-to-decent: the sector's private contractors typically operate in a 9–12% band, and the ones reporting much more are usually either specialists with genuine technical scarcity value or companies taking risks that will show up later.

Three forces move that 8.8% line, and they pull in different directions:

Competitive intensity — the L1 discipline

Because most government contracts go to the lowest qualified bidder, margin is set on the day of the tender by whoever is hungriest. In crowded segments — roads especially — a regional contractor with lower overheads and a desperate need for volume can bid a price that a national player cannot profitably match. NCC's defence is selectivity: bidding where its pre-qualification credentials, equipment base and balance sheet actually narrow the field, and walking away from the rest. That discipline shows up as slower order growth in the short run and better margins in the long run, and it is impossible to verify from outside except by watching the margin line over several years.

Commodity and wage inflation on multi-year, fixed-price work

A three-year contract priced at today's cement and steel costs is a bet that those costs behave. Where the contract carries a price-variation clause indexed to published commodity and wage indices, the client absorbs most of the swing. Where it does not, NCC does. The proportion of the backlog covered by escalation clauses is therefore one of the most important things about NCC that is not routinely disclosed in detail — and one of the reasons the margin band is what it is rather than higher.

Mix — and the quiet upgrade underneath

Not all of NCC's rupees are equal. Contract mining, with its owned heavy equipment and long tenures, and electrical T&D work, with its narrower field of qualified bidders, generally carry different — and often better — economics than commodity roads work. Mining has climbed from roughly 6% to about 13% of the backlog in the space of a year or so. If that shift continues, blended margin should drift upward even without any improvement in the individual segments. That is a slow, unglamorous, entirely mechanical source of improvement, and it is more reliable than hoping the bidding environment gets kinder.

The margin that isn't in the margin

An EPC company has a second, hidden margin: interest cost. Because NCC borrows to fund the working-capital gap, every extra month a client takes to pay is a month of interest that comes straight out of net profit. A contractor operating at 25% working-capital intensity and one at 33% can report identical EBITDA margins and materially different net margins — the difference is entirely financing cost on money the client is holding. This is why the working-capital number is not a housekeeping detail for NCC. It is a profit-and-loss item wearing a balance-sheet costume.

08

Debt, interest and the return on capital

A moderately-levered balance sheet doing an immoderate amount of client financing.

NCC's net debt stood at around ₹3,949 Cr at the December 2025 quarter, having risen through FY26 as receivables stretched. Set against roughly ₹1,836 Cr of EBITDA that is a net-debt-to-EBITDA ratio of about 2.2× — not distressed, comfortably serviceable, but not the fortress balance sheet of a net-cash contractor either. Crucially, almost none of that borrowing funds capacity. It funds the receivable.

There is an important distinction between NCC's debt and the debt that destroyed a generation of Indian infrastructure companies in 2012–2016. That earlier debt was asset debt: borrowed to build toll roads, power plants and real estate the company then owned, with repayment depending on traffic, tariffs or property sales that never materialised. NCC largely exited those exposures in its 2016–2020 clean-up. Its borrowing today is working-capital debt: short-tenure, secured against receivables from government counterparties, and self-liquidating when the client pays. It is a far better kind of debt to have. It is still debt, and it still costs money every day the client delays.

Why return on capital is the honest scoreboard

Take the two halves of NCC together — a business earning ~3.2% net margin, deploying roughly ₹5,400 Cr of working capital plus its equipment base to do so — and the resulting return on capital employed lands in the low-to-mid teens in a good year and lower in a year like FY26. That is the real ceiling on what this company can be worth. An EPC contractor cannot escape it with scale; a bigger version of the same business needs proportionally more capital tied up. The only routes to a structurally better return are shortening the cash cycle, shifting mix toward segments with better economics, or both. Which is precisely what management has said it is trying to do.

Two balance sheets, one company

It helps to picture NCC as two businesses stapled together. The first is a genuinely good one: an asset-light, capable, diversified builder with a four-year order book, credible pre-qualification credentials in five different verticals, and a management team that has kept it out of the concession-ownership trap. The second is an involuntary finance company with ₹5,400 Cr of exposure to state-government payment behaviour, funded with ₹3,900 Cr of borrowings, earning nothing on the spread. The equity market's valuation of NCC is essentially a running estimate of how much the second business detracts from the first.

The rivals: who NCC actually bids against

Different opponents in every vertical — which is both the strength and the difficulty.

There is no single "NCC competitor". Because it runs five businesses, it meets a different set of bidders at every tender table. Understanding that spread explains both why NCC rarely wins on technical scarcity, and why it is rarely shut out of the market entirely.

The thing that actually keeps competitors out

The real barrier in this industry is not skill — pouring concrete is not a secret. It is the combination of pre-qualification credentials and balance-sheet capacity. To bid a ₹2,000 Cr water package a contractor must usually prove it has completed similar work of a similar size, and must be able to post bank guarantees and fund months of unpaid execution. That double filter eliminates almost everyone. It is why the same dozen names appear on every large Indian tender, and why NCC's five sets of credentials — built over four decades and impossible to acquire quickly — are the asset that does not appear anywhere on its balance sheet.

One further competitive note is worth making. Several of NCC's peers are public-sector or PSU-linked contractors that receive work through nomination rather than open competition on certain government projects. NCC has no such channel. Every rupee in that ₹83,004 Cr backlog was won in an open bid against other bidders — which is a harder way to build a book, and a cleaner one.

The stock has traded between roughly ₹131 and ₹240 over the preceding twelve months — a 45% peak-to-trough range on a company whose revenue moved 6%. That volatility is itself informative: the market is not repricing NCC's construction ability. It is repricing, quarter by quarter, its confidence about collections, and about headline risks such as the water-programme review and the reported highways debarment.

At roughly 15× trailing earnings — a discount of about a third to the median multiple of its listed infrastructure peers — NCC is not expensive on any conventional screen. But a low multiple on an EPC contractor is not automatically a signal. The market has historically been right to discount contractors whose earnings do not convert to cash, and NCC's own history includes long stretches where a cheap-looking multiple stayed cheap because the working-capital cycle never tightened. The discount is a statement about cash conversion, not about the P/E being wrong.

The one number that settles it

Every other metric in this primer is downstream of one line: net working capital as a percentage of turnover, which NCC reports each quarter and which ran at about 33% in FY26. Read it alongside receivable days (~73 at year-end) and the ratio of operating cash flow to EBITDA. If that percentage grinds toward the mid-20s while the ₹83,004 Cr backlog converts at a normal pace, then the same 8.8% EBITDA margin drops far more cash to the bottom line, interest cost falls, return on capital rises into the high teens, and the current multiple will look like it was measuring the wrong thing. If it holds above 30%, then NCC will keep borrowing to finance state treasuries, growth will keep consuming the cash it generates, and no order-book announcement will change the outcome. Order inflow and EBITDA margin are the supporting cast. Working capital is the plot.

11

Future, risks, and the two-sided case

What could go right, what could go wrong, and the specific things to check next.

NCC enters FY27 with more signed work than at any point in its history, a receivable line that has just started to improve, and a management team that has demonstrated it will trade revenue for cash discipline when forced to choose. It also enters with two open regulatory questions hanging over parts of its book, a leveraged working-capital position, and complete dependence on the payment behaviour of governments it cannot influence.

The risk list, stated plainly

  • Receivable and state-payment risk — the central one. A handful of state governments hold several thousand crore of NCC's money. A fiscal squeeze, an election, or a change of administration in any of them can strand those sums for quarters. This is not a tail risk for NCC; it is the normal operating environment, and FY26 showed what happens when it worsens.
  • Programme and regulatory risk. Central scrutiny of the rural water mission put a reported ~₹7,000 Cr of backlog under review, and press reports in 2026 described a highways-authority debarment action affecting the company. Both remain to be resolved and both should be checked in NCC's own exchange filings rather than taken from secondary reporting.
  • Fixed-price margin risk. Multi-year contracts priced years before execution are exposed to cement, steel, fuel and wage inflation wherever escalation clauses are absent or partial. An 8.8% EBITDA margin does not have much cushion for a serious commodity shock.
  • Order-inflow dependence on the government capex cycle. Roughly the whole book comes from public spending. A general slowdown in central or state capital expenditure — or a diversion of budgets toward welfare — hits award volumes across every one of NCC's five verticals at once. Diversification across departments is not diversification away from the government.
  • Leverage and interest cost. ~₹3,949 Cr of net debt at roughly 2.2× EBITDA is manageable, but it exists only because clients are slow. If working capital expands rather than contracts, debt and interest rise together, and net profit — already only 3.2% of revenue — absorbs the difference.
  • Competition. L&T sets the ceiling, specialists like Afcons and KEC take the technically-scarce work, and regional bidders compress prices from below. NCC bids in open competition without any nomination channel, in every segment.
  • Execution and safety. Five verticals, hundreds of sites, tens of thousands of workers and subcontractors. Site incidents, delays and disputes are chronic operational realities in this industry, and arbitration claims can take years to resolve.

The constructive case

  • Four years of work in hand. ₹83,004 Cr of backlog on ₹20,944 Cr of revenue means the growth constraint is capital and cash, not demand — a much more solvable problem than an empty order book.
  • Collections have already turned. Receivable days improved from ~87 to ~73 and Uttar Pradesh dues fell from ~₹1,700 Cr toward ~₹1,200 Cr. The FY26 squeeze looks like timing, not impairment.
  • Suppressed base. FY26 revenue fell because execution was voluntarily throttled. If payments normalise, a large volume of already-mobilised work can be executed quickly — the sites, designs and crews already exist.
  • Mix is quietly improving. Contract mining has roughly doubled its share of the backlog to ~13%, and electrical T&D sits near a fifth — both structurally better than commodity roads work.
  • Asset-light, and staying that way. No toll-road or power concessions on the balance sheet, no pledged promoter shares, and borrowing that is short-tenure and self-liquidating rather than project-locked.
  • Valuation reflects the problem, not the franchise. ~15× earnings and ~7.7× EV/EBITDA for a top-tier private EPC platform with five sets of pre-qualification credentials.

The sceptical case

  • The cash gap is structural, not cyclical. Working capital at ~33% of turnover is not a one-year accident — Indian state contracting has produced this pattern for decades, and no contractor has permanently solved it.
  • Growth eats cash. At this intensity, every ₹100 of incremental revenue needs ~₹30 of funding first. Converting the backlog quickly would increase borrowing before it increases free cash.
  • Two live regulatory overhangs. A reported water-programme review touching ~₹7,000 Cr of book and a reported highways debarment are unresolved, and either could remove work or restrict bidding.
  • Thin margin, thick sensitivity. At 3.2% net margin, a modest cost overrun, a commodity spike or an extra ₹500 Cr of stuck receivables is the difference between a good year and a poor one.
  • One customer, many names. Diversifying across buildings, water, roads, electrical and mining does not diversify away from the fact that the payer is almost always a government.
  • Cheap can persist. Contractors have re-rated durably only when cash conversion improved, and NCC has been through cycles where the multiple stayed low for years because it did not.

What to check next, specifically

  • Net working capital as a % of turnover, disclosed each quarter. The trend from ~33% is the single most important line in the results deck.
  • Receivable days — whether the improvement to ~73 days holds for two more quarters or reverses.
  • Operating cash flow against EBITDA in the annual and half-yearly statements. This is where the profit-versus-cash question is answered without interpretation.
  • Jal Jeevan execution run-rate — whether quarterly water-division execution returns toward the ₹1,000 Cr-plus pace originally planned, which would confirm state payments have normalised.
  • Net debt at each quarter-end, from the ~₹3,949 Cr Q3 FY26 level. Rising debt with rising revenue means the cycle has not been fixed; rising revenue with flat debt means it has.
  • Resolution and scope of the reported water-programme review and highways debarment, in NCC's own stock-exchange filings.
The bottom line

NCC has spent four decades becoming the contractor that can build almost anything a state government wants — a tower, a pipeline, a substation, a highway, a coal face — and it has never had more of that work booked than it does today. What it has not solved, and what no amount of order-book growth addresses, is that it must finance its customers to earn its living. FY26 made the tension unmistakable: a record ₹31,884 Cr of new awards, and a company that chose to build less because the bills were not being paid. The next chapter is not written in tender announcements. It is written in the working-capital line of the quarterly deck, and in whether the water division's execution run-rate climbs back toward the ₹1,000 Cr-a-quarter pace it was designed for. Watch those two figures together; they will tell you whether NCC's ₹83,004 Cr of promises is a business or a balance sheet.
