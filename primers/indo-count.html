Indo Count Industries, the world's bed-linen powerhouse

A complete walk through India's largest home-textiles exporter — how it turns bales of raw cotton into the bedsheets stacked on American shelves, why it earns in dollars but pays its bills in rupees, how it is climbing out of anonymous private-label sheeting into owned and licensed brands, and why one tariff decision in Washington sliced its profit roughly in half in a single year.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Indo Count Industries makes the soft, folded things on a bed you never think about — bedsheets, pillowcases, duvet covers, comforters and quilts — and it makes them at a scale few companies on earth match. It is widely described as the world's largest producer and exporter of bed linen, running the bulk of its manufacturing out of a single large complex in Kolhapur, Maharashtra, plus an acquired plant in Gujarat. What makes it unusual for an Indian consumer-facing name is that it barely sells to Indians at all: something like 90%-plus of its revenue is exported, overwhelmingly to the United States, where its sheets end up on the shelves of the big retailers — the Walmarts, Targets and department stores of American middle-class home-goods aisles.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • It is a dollar business run on a rupee cost base. Indo Count books almost all of its sales in US dollars but pays for cotton, labour, power and freight largely in rupees. That single mismatch — plus the price of cotton — swings its margin far more than volume does. In FY26, revenue barely moved (roughly ₹4,191 Cr to ₹4,211 Cr of total income), yet net profit almost halved, from ~₹250 Cr to ~₹127 Cr. Nothing about the quantity of sheets sold explains that; the squeeze came from price, cost and tariff.
  • It is trying to stop being a commodity. For decades Indo Count was a superb private-label factory — it wove and stitched sheets that went out under someone else's label. The whole strategy now is to move up the value chain into owned and licensed brands (it bought the storied American name Wamsutta, and licenses Fieldcrest, Waverly, Beautyrest and others) and into higher-margin utility bedding (comforters, pillows, mattress pads). That branded/new-business bucket grew from about $33 million to $90 million in a year.
  • FY26 was the tariff year. US "reciprocal" tariffs on Indian goods ratcheted from 10% up to a punishing 50% during the year before easing back to ~10%. Rather than lose its shelf space, Indo Count absorbed a large chunk of that cost itself — protecting customers and market share at the direct expense of its own margin. That decision is the reason a flat-revenue year produced a halved profit, and it is the backdrop to a stock still trading near 50× earnings on the hope that margins normalise.
In plain terms

Indo Count is a giant contract bedsheet factory that is trying to become a bedding brand. Today the profitable core is unglamorous: buy cotton, spin/weave/print/stitch it into sheets, ship containers to American retailers who put their own name on them. That business is world-class but it is a price-taker — its fortunes rise and fall with cotton, the rupee-dollar rate, and whatever Washington does to tariffs. The bet management is making is that owning brands (Wamsutta) and selling stickier products (comforters, pillows, US-warehoused utility bedding) will slowly turn a commodity exporter into something with pricing power. FY26 showed both sides at once: the commodity core got hammered by tariffs, while the new branded engine quietly tripled.

01

Home textiles 101: the sheet on the shelf

No industry knowledge assumed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

"Home textiles" is the trade's name for the soft furnishings of a home: bed linen, bath towels, rugs, curtains, kitchen textiles. Indo Count is a specialist — it lives almost entirely in the bedding corner: sheets and everything that goes with them. To follow the company, you need a small vocabulary and one economic idea.

The vocabulary you actually need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Bed linenFlat sheets, fitted sheets, pillowcases, duvet/quilt covers — woven cotton (or blends) that is dyed, printed and stitched. Indo Count's core product; it is measured in metres of fabric processed.
Thread countThreads per square inch of fabric — a rough proxy for how fine and premium a sheet feels. Higher counts and finer cottons (e.g. long-staple) command higher prices, which is the lever a maker pulls to trade up out of commodity sheeting.
Private labelGoods made to a retailer's spec and sold under the retailer's brand (a store's own bedsheet range). Reliable volume, thin margin, and no consumer knows your name. This was Indo Count's whole business for years.
Licensed / owned brandSelling under a recognised consumer name — one you own (Wamsutta, Boutique Living) or license (Fieldcrest, Beautyrest). Higher price, higher margin, stickier shelf space. The direction of travel.
Utility beddingThe "filled" bedding around the sheet — comforters, duvet inserts, mattress pads, pillows, protectors. Bulkier, often made close to the customer, higher-value than flat sheeting. Indo Count's newest growth leg.
ConverterA textile firm that buys yarn and converts it into finished fabric/product, rather than spinning its own yarn from raw cotton. Indo Count is primarily a converter — which is why yarn and cotton prices flow almost straight through its cost line.
The one economic idea: the same sheet can carry three price tags

Take one physical bedsheet off the line. Sold as anonymous private label, it earns the factory a thin conversion margin. Put a licensed brand on it — a name a US shopper trusts — and the same sheet sells for more, with more of that premium sticking to the maker. Sell it under a brand Indo Count owns, direct to the consumer online, and it captures the retail margin too. The product hardly changes; the label and the channel change everything about the economics. That ladder — private label → licensed → owned/direct — is the single strategic idea behind almost everything Indo Count is doing, because the bottom rung is a price-taker's game and the top rungs are where pricing power lives.

Why India makes the world's bedsheets

India is a natural home for this business: it grows its own cotton, has deep, low-cost textile labour, and decades of processing know-how. Home textiles is one of the few manufacturing categories where India genuinely leads global export share rather than trailing China. The customer, though, sits on the other side of the world — the American retail shelf is where the money is — so the whole industry is built around long ocean supply chains, container freight, and the eternal tension between an Indian cost base and a US selling price. That geography is Indo Count's greatest strength and its central vulnerability at once.

02

What Indo Count actually sells: one factory, a widening product ladder

From plain sheets to filled bedding to branded direct-to-consumer — read this once and the company clicks into place.

At its heart Indo Count does one thing supremely well: take cotton and turn it into finished bedding at enormous scale, to a quality that the world's most demanding retailers will put on their shelves. But "finished bedding" now spans a widening ladder of products, and the higher rungs matter far more to profit than to volume.

The product lines, decoded

  • Core bed sheets (fashion & basic bedding) — the bread-and-butter: flat and fitted sheets, pillowcases, duvet covers and coordinated "fashion bedding" sets, made mostly for retailers' own labels and for licensed brands. This is the volume engine and the deepest expertise, but the thinnest margin per metre.
  • Utility bedding — the "filled" category: comforters, down-alternative duvets, mattress pads, pillows and protectors. Bulkier and higher-value than flat sheeting, and increasingly made in the US close to the customer (via acquired American firms). One of the two fastest-growing pieces.
  • Institutional bedding — bedding sold to hospitality and institutions (hotels, healthcare) rather than to households. Steadier, contract-driven demand that diversifies the retail-heavy core.
  • Branded & licensed portfolio — the up-market push: owned brands such as Wamsutta, Boutique Living, Pure Earth, Heirlooms of India and The Pure Collection, plus a stable of licensed American names — Fieldcrest, Waverly, Gaiam and Beautyrest. This is where Indo Count is trying to convert manufacturing muscle into consumer pricing power.

Why the product mix is the whole margin question

A commodity bedsheet competes on price against every other low-cost supplier on earth, so the margin is whatever the market allows — often not much. A comforter made in a US facility and sold under a name a shopper recognises competes on brand, service and proximity, so it can hold a richer margin even when cotton or freight moves against it. That is why Indo Count keeps pouring effort into utility bedding and brands even though core sheeting still pays most of the bills: each rupee of revenue that shifts up the ladder is worth more than a rupee that stays at the bottom. The mix, not the metres, is what will decide whether this company ever escapes commodity economics.

03

The company, the family, and who controls it

A Kolhapur cotton-spinner that became a global bedding house — still promoter-run.

Indo Count is a promoter-driven family business built by the Jain family. What began in 1988 as a small, export-oriented cotton-spinning unit in Kolhapur was steadily reinvented into a downstream bed-linen manufacturer selling to the world's biggest retailers — a rare Indian textile story of moving up the value chain rather than staying stuck in yarn.

Timeline

  • 1988
    Indo Count set up by Anil Kumar Jain as an export-oriented cotton spinning unit in Kolhapur, Maharashtra — the humble, commodity-yarn beginning.
  • 2000s
    A decisive pivot downstream: from selling yarn to manufacturing finished bed linen for export, chasing the far larger, higher-value home-textiles market in the US and Europe.
  • 2010s
    Scales into one of the world's largest bed-linen exporters; wins long-run private-label programmes with major US retailers and a 4-star export house standing.
  • 2022
    Acquires the home-textiles business of GHCL (~₹610 Cr), adding ~45 million metres of capacity at Vapi (Gujarat) and lifting total bedding capacity toward ~153 million metres.
  • 2024
    Buys the iconic American brand Wamsutta and US utility-bedding firms (Fluvitex, Modern Home Textiles) via Indo Count Global — the leap into owned brands and US-made filled bedding.
  • 2025
    Relaunches Wamsutta with a direct-to-consumer strategy; builds out US utility-bedding manufacturing footprint across multiple states.
  • FY26
    Total income ~₹4,211 Cr (flat); PAT ~₹127 Cr (down ~49%) as US tariffs are absorbed; new-business revenue jumps from ~$33m to ~$90m. Guides ~₹5,500 Cr for FY27.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Jain family, led by executive chairman Anil Kumar Jain (with the next generation active in the business), holds roughly 59% — a clear controlling stake that keeps this a founder-led company rather than a professionally-diffused one.
  • Public float: the balance is held by institutions and retail — FIIs around 10%, domestic institutions around 6%, and the wider public the rest. A liquid but promoter-anchored register.
  • Group map: the listed entity is Indo Count Industries Ltd (ICIL). Its US-facing acquisitions — the Wamsutta brand and the utility-bedding businesses — sit under overseas subsidiaries (Indo Count Global Inc. and related entities), which is how an Indian manufacturer comes to own American brands and run American factories.
A genuine surprise: Indo Count started life as a spinner — the most commoditised rung of textiles — and deliberately walked away from it. Rather than defend yarn, it moved downstream into finished bedding, and today buys much of its yarn as an input. It is now circling back to add some backward-integration spinning capacity, but the strategic identity was forged by abandoning the business it was born in. Few textile founders let go of the spindle; this one did, and the whole company exists because of that choice.
04

Dollars in, rupees out: the cotton-and-currency sandwich

The mechanism that swings this company's profit more than any sales number does.

To understand Indo Count you have to hold two facts together: it sells in US dollars and it spends in Indian rupees, and its single largest cost — cotton — has a price all its own. Volume is almost a sideshow. The profit each year is really decided in the gap between a dollar selling price set months earlier and a rupee cost base that keeps moving.

The three levers that actually move the margin

  • Cotton price. As a converter, Indo Count's biggest input is cotton/yarn. When cotton rises faster than it can re-price its sheets, margin compresses; when cotton is soft, margin breathes. A relief valve arrived in FY26 when India waived customs duty on cotton imports for part of the year, letting mills buy cheaper fibre.
  • The rupee-dollar rate. Because revenue is in dollars and costs in rupees, a weaker rupee is generally helpful — the same dollar sale converts to more rupees. But the company hedges forward, so the benefit shows up with a lag and is partly locked in advance.
  • Freight & the selling price. Ocean freight and the price the retailer will accept sit at the two ends of the chain. A retailer squeezing price, or a freight spike, lands straight on the margin because a commodity supplier has limited power to push either back.

The device that only fits Indo Count: read the margin, not the metres

For most manufacturers, sales volume is the headline number — sell more units, make more money. Indo Count breaks that intuition. In FY26 it processed fewer metres of fabric (Q4 volumes fell around 20% year-on-year) yet total income was essentially flat, because a richer product mix and a favourable currency lifted the price per metre. And profit still halved — not because it sold too little, but because tariffs and costs crushed the spread between dollar price and rupee cost. So the number that tells you how Indo Count is really doing is not "how many sheets" but EBITDA margin — the cents of profit kept on each dollar of sheet. When that margin sits in the mid-teens, the machine is healthy; when it slips toward ~11%, as in FY26, something in the cotton-currency-tariff sandwich has gone wrong, whatever the top line says.

05

Climbing the brand ladder

The strategic heart of the story — turning a private-label factory into a brand owner.

Every commodity manufacturer dreams of pricing power, and almost none of them get it. Indo Count is making a serious, capital-backed attempt: to stop being the anonymous factory behind other people's labels and to own the brands and the customer relationship itself. This is the most important thing the company is doing that a casual observer would miss, because it barely shows up in the headline revenue yet.

How the climb works

  • License first. Indo Count licenses well-known American home names — Fieldcrest, Waverly, Gaiam, Beautyrest — putting recognised brands on its bedding without having to build consumer awareness from scratch. Higher price, better shelf position, still asset-light.
  • Then own. In 2024 it acquired Wamsutta, a genuinely iconic American bedding brand with deep heritage and consumer recall, and in 2025 relaunched it direct-to-consumer — selling online under its own name and capturing the retail margin, not just the manufacturing slice.
  • Build a portfolio. Around these sit owned brands like Boutique Living, Pure Earth, Heirlooms of India and The Pure Collection, giving Indo Count a spread of price points and positions rather than a single bet.

The branded and utility-bedding "new businesses" nearly tripled in a year — from ~$33m to ~$90m. It is still small against a ~₹4,200 Cr group, but it is growing at a pace the commodity core never will, and it carries a fundamentally better margin profile.
Why owning a brand changes the maths

When Indo Count makes a sheet for a retailer's own label, the retailer keeps the relationship with the shopper and most of the markup; Indo Count is one interchangeable supplier among several. When Indo Count sells the same sheet under Wamsutta, direct to the consumer, it captures the brand premium and the retail margin, and — crucially — the customer is loyal to Wamsutta, not to whichever factory is cheapest this season. That is the difference between renting shelf space and owning demand. The risk is equally clear: building and defending a consumer brand is a marketing game Indo Count has never had to play, a different discipline from running an efficient factory, and it costs money up front before it pays.

The factory can make anyone's sheet. The strategy is to make sure at least some of those sheets say a name Indo Count owns.
06

Bedding & beyond — inside America

Utility bedding, US near-shoring, and an Indian exporter that builds factories in North Carolina.

The second growth leg runs alongside the brand climb: pushing beyond flat sheeting into utility bedding — the filled, bulky products (comforters, duvets, pillows, mattress pads) — and, strikingly, making a growing share of it inside the United States, close to the customer. This is where Indo Count stops looking like a conventional Indian exporter.

The logic of "bedding and beyond"

  • Higher value per unit. A comforter or a filled pillow carries more material and more assembly than a flat sheet, so it sells for more and, done well, keeps more margin. It also deepens the relationship — a retailer buying sheets can now buy the whole top-of-bed from one supplier.
  • Made near the customer. Filled bedding is bulky and expensive to ship across an ocean, and speed-to-shelf matters. So Indo Count acquired US utility-bedding firms (Fluvitex, Modern Home Textiles) and is building greenfield US manufacturing — reportedly a third American facility, in North Carolina, spanning East Coast, West Coast and Midwest.
  • A tariff hedge, by accident and design. Bedding made in America is not an import, so it sidesteps the very US tariffs that hammered the export core in FY26. A strategy begun for logistics quietly doubles as insulation from trade policy.

The counter-intuitive move worth sitting with

The instinctive picture of an Indian textile exporter is cheap labour in India shipping to expensive markets abroad. Indo Count is doing something the opposite of that intuition: an Indian company building factories in the United States to make bedding for Americans. It does this because filled bedding is too bulky to ship economically, because retailers want fast replenishment, and — increasingly — because goods made on US soil dodge US tariffs. It is a reminder that the "cheap-India-to-rich-America" arrow can run both ways once a manufacturer moves up into products where proximity and brand beat pure labour cost. The near-shore facilities are still ramping (utilisation in the low-to-mid 60s), so this is a bet in progress, not a finished advantage.

07

The tariff year: how a flat top line halved the profit

The single event that defined FY26 — and the clearest window into how this business really works.

FY26 will be remembered as the year US trade policy reached into Indo Count's P&L. Washington's "reciprocal" tariffs on Indian goods climbed in steps — beginning around 10%, escalating to 25%, and spiking to a punitive 50% before easing back toward ~10% later in the year. For a company that sends most of its output to America, that was not a background risk; it was a direct tax on its core business.

Faced with it, Indo Count made a deliberate choice: rather than pass the full tariff to customers and risk losing shelf space, it absorbed a large share of the cost itself. It protected its relationships with the big US retailers and defended market share — and it paid for that decision out of its own margin. Lower volumes meant fixed costs were spread over less output (under-absorption), compounding the squeeze.

The one line that captures the whole year

Indo Count's FY26 total income barely moved — around ₹4,191 Cr to ₹4,211 Cr — yet its net profit fell from roughly ₹250 Cr to ₹127 Cr. Almost the entire ~₹123 Cr that vanished is the price of one policy decision made in Washington, absorbed by an Indian factory to keep American shelf space. There is no clearer illustration of what it means to be a price-taker: the company sold about as much, made its product just as well, and still lost half its profit because someone else changed the rules of the border it sells across. That is the risk that sits under every rosy growth number in the home-textiles export story.

Two things soften the blow. First, the tariff eased back toward ~10% within the year, so the worst was a spike rather than a permanent setting. Second, some relief came on the cost side — India temporarily waived customs duty on imported cotton, cheapening the biggest input. But the episode is a permanent lesson about the model: a business this concentrated on one export market is only ever one trade decision away from a bad year, however well it is run.

08

The big-box dependency

The customer base that is both Indo Count's greatest asset and its sharpest concentration risk.

Indo Count's customers are, in the main, the largest retailers in the United States — the big-box chains and department stores whose home-goods aisles carry mountains of bedding. Being a trusted, at-scale, compliance-ready supplier to that channel is a genuine moat: these retailers vet suppliers ferociously on quality, ethics, reliability and volume, and a new entrant cannot simply undercut its way onto the shelf. But the same relationship is a concentration few consumer companies would choose.

Why concentration is the quiet second risk

The tariff shock of FY26 was the loud risk; customer and market concentration is the quiet one that made the tariff hurt so much. Because so much of Indo Count's business flows to a few big American buyers, it had little choice but to absorb the tariff — walking away from those customers was never a real option. A more diversified customer base, spread across geographies and channels, would have given it room to push back on price. This is precisely why the two strategic pushes — into Europe/UK on one axis and into owned brands and direct-to-consumer on the other — matter beyond their growth: each one buys the company a little more independence from the handful of retail giants that currently hold the whip.

09

The industry and the rivals

Where Indo Count sits — and why it is a different animal from Welspun and from spinning-yarn textile plays.

Home-textiles export is a scale game dominated by a few big Indian houses competing with lower-cost Asian rivals for space on Western shelves. The name most often set beside Indo Count is Welspun Living — but the two are less alike than they look, and Indo Count is emphatically not the same kind of business as the spinning and yarn textile companies it is sometimes lumped with.

The distinction that matters most: focus vs breadth

The cleanest way to separate Indo Count from Welspun Living is this: Welspun is a broad home-textiles conglomerate — towels, bath, bedding, flooring — so a weak year in one category can be offset by another, and its brands span the home. Indo Count is a deliberate specialist — it does bedding, and it aims to be the best and biggest in the world at exactly that. Focus is a double-edged sword: it means Indo Count has no towel business to lean on when bedsheets get tariffed, but it also means every rupee of management attention and capital compounds inside one category rather than being spread thin. An investor choosing between them is really choosing between diversified home textiles and concentrated bedding leadership — not between two versions of the same company.

Sitting over the whole industry are the free-trade agreements. Deals that make Indian textiles duty-free into the UK and Europe are a structural tailwind for every Indian exporter — and a particular prize for Indo Count as it works to grow its European book and dilute its American concentration. Trade policy giveth (FTAs into Europe) even as it taketh away (tariffs into America); the net of those two forces is the industry's swing factor for years to come.

Financials & valuation

A flat, tariff-hit year — priced as if the margin recovery is already in the bag.

FY26 was, on the surface, uneventful — revenue essentially flat — and, underneath, painful, with profit almost halving on absorbed tariffs and softer margins. The stock, however, trades at a multiple that assumes the margin damage was a one-off and the growth engines fire from here. The tension between those two facts is the entire valuation debate.

A few balance-sheet notes matter. Return ratios softened with profit — ROE fell to the mid-single digits in FY26 (from a healthier double-digit run-rate), and ROCE sat around 8% — both depressed by the margin hit and by capital deployed into US assets and inventory that has not yet earned its keep. The company still pays a modest dividend (a final dividend was declared for FY26 with a payout around a fifth of earnings), signalling that management views the profit dip as temporary rather than a reason to conserve cash.

The one number that settles the thesis: EBITDA margin back toward the mid-teens

At roughly 50× depressed earnings, Indo Count is not priced as a battered cyclical — it is priced as a company whose margin will heal and whose new engines will scale. So the figure that proves or breaks the case is the EBITDA margin trajectory: does it climb from ~11% back toward the ~14–16% the company earned before the tariff shock? Read it alongside two supporting numbers — the branded/new-business share of revenue (is the $90m base compounding toward a real slice of the mix?) and volume recovery (are the metres coming back as tariffs normalise?). If margin re-rates toward the mid-teens while the branded engine keeps tripling off its small base, the multiple is defensible and FY27's ~30% growth guide is credible. If margin stays stuck near 11% because the retail channel keeps the pricing power, then a 50× multiple on a price-taker's earnings is the whole risk. Everything else — cotton, currency, freight — is the weather; the margin is the climate.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A world-class factory reaching for pricing power — while the border it sells across keeps moving.

Indo Count enters FY27 as the largest bed-linen maker on the planet, with two credible growth engines (brands and utility bedding), a European diversification underway, and a temporary tariff shock hopefully behind it. The unresolved question is whether a business born as a commodity exporter can genuinely lift its margins — or whether the retail channel and trade policy will keep capping them no matter how well it executes.

The bull case

  • Global scale leadership — the world's largest bed-linen maker, a vetted, trusted supplier to the biggest US retailers; that position is hard to replicate.
  • Two real growth legs — branded/licensed (Wamsutta, Fieldcrest) and utility bedding nearly tripled to ~$90m; better margins than the commodity core.
  • US near-shoring — building bedding capacity inside America hedges tariffs and shortens supply to the customer.
  • Diversification underway — a growing Europe/UK book, aided by duty-free FTAs, dilutes the heavy US concentration over time.
  • Cyclical, not structural — FY26's profit dip was tariff-and-cotton-driven; as those normalise, margin and EPS can snap back sharply.

The bear case

  • Price-taker economics — a commodity bedsheet supplier has limited pricing power; the retail channel and cotton/currency set the margin, as FY26 brutally showed.
  • Tariff & policy risk — ~70–75% US concentration means one trade decision can halve profit again; the FY26 spike may not be the last.
  • Rich valuation on depressed earnings — ~50× on a halved profit assumes a clean margin recovery that the channel may not permit.
  • Brand-building is a new game — owning and marketing consumer brands is a different, cash-hungry discipline from running a factory; execution is unproven at scale.
  • Rising leverage & soft returns — debt up, ROE in the mid-single digits; US assets and inventory must start earning to justify the capital.
The bottom line

Indo Count has spent thirty-five years turning a small Kolhapur spinning unit into the largest bed-linen exporter on earth — a genuinely world-class factory that the biggest American retailers depend on. FY26 exposed the limit of that achievement: being the best commodity supplier in the world still leaves you at the mercy of a tariff schedule and a cotton price, which together halved the profit in a flat year. The next chapter is whether Wamsutta, licensed brands, US-made utility bedding and a European book can lift Indo Count off the bottom rung of the ladder into something with real pricing power. The scoreboard is a single quarterly figure — whether EBITDA margin climbs back toward the mid-teens while the branded engine keeps compounding. Watch that print each quarter; it will tell you whether Indo Count is becoming a brand or merely remaining the world's best-run bedsheet factory.
