00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Bank of Maharashtra ("MahaBank") is a Pune-headquartered, government-controlled public-sector bank — one of the smaller of the state-owned lenders by size, but on nearly every quality-and-growth metric that matters, arguably the best-run of them all. Founded in 1935 and nationalised in 1969, it takes deposits from savers nationwide and lends to households, farmers, small businesses and corporates. It is the dominant bank in its home state, with the largest PSU-bank branch network in Maharashtra. In FY26 it did ~₹6.43 lakh crore of total business (deposits + advances) — modest next to a State Bank or a Bank of Baroda, but growing faster, and earning more per rupee, than any of them.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It runs the fastest, cleanest and most profitable book in the PSU pack. This is the whole differentiator. In FY26 Bank of Maharashtra grew advances ~22% — the fastest of any PSU bank — while simultaneously holding the group's best asset quality: gross NPAs of just 1.45%, a near-invisible net NPA of 0.13%, and a provision coverage ratio of ~98.6%. That combination of speed and cleanliness is genuinely rare. Layer on a ~53% CASA ratio (among the highest of any PSU bank) and a ~3.9% net interest margin (near the top of the pack), and it earns a ~1.86% return on assets and ~23% return on equity — figures that stand with the best private banks, not the average state one.
  • Strong, consistent, self-funded profit growth. FY26 net profit rose 27% to a record ₹7,019 Cr, capping a five-year run in which profit compounded at roughly 65% a year off a low post-crisis base. Net interest income grew 17% to ₹13,664 Cr. It is comfortably capitalised (CRAR 18.36%, CET-1 14.59%) and pays a rising dividend (₹2.20/share for FY26).
  • It is ~73.6% owned by the Government of India — a very high stake. Even after a December-2025 share sale that finally took it below 75%, the government still owns ~73.6%, one of the highest promoter stakes among listed PSU banks. That means a small public float (a thin ~26% of shares actually trade), sovereign safety on its deposits, and — the key nuance — a standing need to keep selling down to satisfy the minimum-public-shareholding rule, which is the dilution overhang the stock lives with.
In plain terms

Think of Bank of Maharashtra as a ₹3.5-lakh-crore deposit machine wired to a ₹2.9-lakh-crore loan book — smaller than the giants, but run with unusual discipline. It gathers an exceptionally cheap pool of deposits (over half sits in near-free current and savings accounts), lends it out fast to a clean, granular set of borrowers, loses almost nothing to bad loans, and so keeps far more of the spread than a typical state bank. The result is that a small PSU bank punches like a good private one — fastest growth, top-tier margins, best-in-class bad-loan numbers, best-of-PSU returns. The catches: the government owns so much of it that its shares are thinly floated and periodically diluted, and its margins — however good — will feel the same RBI rate-cut squeeze as everyone else.

01

Bank economics 101: how a bank actually earns

No finance degree required — just the numbers that drive everything.

A bank is, at its core, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors) and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap between what it earns on loans and pays on deposits — scaled to its assets — is the engine. A handful of terms tell you almost the whole story, and they matter doubly for Bank of Maharashtra, whose entire investment case is that it does each of these things a notch better than every PSU peer.

The only banking units you need (Bank of Maharashtra, FY26)

NIINet Interest Income — interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings. The core revenue line. MahaBank: ₹13,664 Cr in FY26 (+17.1%).
NIMNet Interest Margin — NII as a % of interest-earning assets. The "spread", standardised. MahaBank: ~3.91% domestic (Q4 ~3.95%) — among the highest of any PSU bank, a direct result of ultra-cheap funding.
CASACurrent + Savings Account deposits — money in everyday accounts that pays little or no interest. The cheaper your funding, the fatter your margin. MahaBank: ~53% — one of the highest CASA ratios in the entire banking system, PSU or private.

CAR / CET-1Capital Adequacy — the equity cushion versus loans, the buffer against losses. MahaBank: CRAR 18.36%, CET-1 14.59% — comfortably above the regulatory floor.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. So a bank gets richer four ways — lend more (grow advances), fund cheaper (more CASA, lower deposit cost), lose less (low NPAs and low credit cost), and earn fees (cards, insurance, forex). What makes Bank of Maharashtra the standout is that it does exceptionally well on all four levers at once — and it grows the fastest while doing so. Cheap CASA funding → wide margin; clean lending → tiny loan losses; both together → a ~1.86% ROA that most PSU banks (nearer ~1.0%) can only envy. Everything else in this primer is a deeper look at how those levers connect.

Why "loan losses" are the swing factor for a PSU bank

  • Provisioning is the hidden lever. When a loan turns bad, the bank must set aside money to cover the expected loss — a provision, charged straight against profit. In the FY16–18 crisis, PSU banks were provisioning tens of thousands of crores; as books cleaned up, that charge collapsed and profit surged. Bank of Maharashtra went further than most: it kept provisioning even after loans were covered, pushing its PCR to ~98.6% — meaning almost nothing is left to surprise it. That conservatism is the foundation of its earnings quality.
  • Slippages are the leading indicator. A "slippage" is a loan freshly turning bad. Bank of Maharashtra runs slippages below 1% of the book and guides to keep them there — the early-warning gauge that a clean book stays clean even while it grows fast. Watch this each quarter.
  • The rate cycle sets the margin. Because roughly half of a bank's loans are linked to external benchmarks (the RBI repo rate), they reprice quickly when the RBI cuts — but deposits reprice more slowly. So a rate-cut cycle squeezes NIM first: loan yields fall before funding costs do. That is the one headwind even a well-run bank like MahaBank cannot dodge, and why it guides FY27 NIM down to ~3.75% from ~3.9%.
02

How the money flows: deposits → loans → margin

Read this once and the whole bank clicks into place.

A bank is a conveyor belt for money. Savers' deposits come in one end; loans go out the other; the spread between the two is harvested as net interest income. Two things govern how much profit drips out: how cheap the deposits are (the CASA mix and deposit cost) and how much more the loans yield than the deposits cost (the margin). Bank of Maharashtra runs this belt better than any PSU peer because the funding end is unusually cheap — more than half its deposits sit in near-free CASA accounts — and that is the single biggest reason its margin and returns lead the pack.

Why the CASA mix is the whole game

A current account pays 0% and a savings account ~3%; a fixed deposit pays 6.5–7.5%. The more of your funding that sits in low-cost CASA, the cheaper your money — and the wider your margin on the same loan. Bank of Maharashtra's CASA of ~53% (₹1.84 lakh crore of the ₹3.5 lakh crore deposit base) is genuinely exceptional: most large PSU banks sit around 30–40%, and even the best private banks rarely top the mid-40s. This is not an accident — it is the product of being the dominant everyday bank across Maharashtra, with millions of salary, pension and government accounts that park money at near-zero cost. That cheap, sticky funding is the quiet engine behind everything else the bank does well.

Why a great deposit franchise beats a great loan book

Anyone can lend money; the hard, valuable part of banking is gathering deposits cheaply and reliably. A bank with a sticky, low-cost deposit base can lend profitably through any cycle; one that has to bid up for deposits at high rates is always squeezed. Bank of Maharashtra's edge is precisely here: a deep, largely retail Maharashtra deposit base, over half of it in near-free CASA. That is why — even with the same RBI rate cuts hitting everyone — its margin (~3.9%) sits at the top of the PSU pack, well above a Canara (~2.5%) or a PNB. The whole "best-run PSU bank" thesis starts with this one fact: the cheapest funding in the group, feeding the fastest, cleanest loan book in the group.

03

The company and who controls it

A 90-year-old Pune institution, deeply rooted in its home state, majority-owned by the government.

Bank of Maharashtra was founded on 16 September 1935 in Poona (now Pune), established under the aegis of the Mahratta Chamber of Commerce to serve the small traders, farmers and businesses of the region during India's cooperative-banking movement. It was nationalised in July 1969 alongside 13 other major banks. Ninety years on it remains a Pune-headquartered public-sector bank — the flagship bank of Maharashtra, with the largest PSU branch network in the state — whose controlling shareholder is the Government of India (~73.6%). Its deep home-state roots are not incidental: they are the source of the low-cost deposit franchise that drives everything.

Ownership & management

  • Government of India ~73.6%. The state is the promoter and controlling shareholder. Even after the December-2025 share sale, this is one of the highest government stakes among listed PSU banks (versus ~74% at Indian Bank, ~64% at Bank of Baroda, lower at SBI). It anchors the stock's "PSU bank" identity, underpins its ultra-safe deposits, and — the live nuance — leaves the float thin and the dilution question open (see §08).
  • Public & institutional float ~26%. Domestic institutions (~14%, led by LIC and mutual funds), foreign institutions (~5.5%) and retail (~7%) hold the rest. Because the government owns so much, even routine sell-downs to meet float rules are meaningful supply for a relatively small pool of freely-traded shares.
  • Board-driven, government-appointed leadership. Like all PSU banks, top management (MD & CEO, executive directors) is appointed through the government's selection process with fixed tenures. Bank of Maharashtra's execution under this model has been unusually consistent — long-tenured, strategy-continuous leadership is part of why the turnaround stuck.
  • A focused, plain-vanilla bank. Bank of Maharashtra is fundamentally a lender-and-deposit-taker with no large listed subsidiaries to unlock (unlike a Canara or SBI). Its edge is operational quality, not financial engineering — so the "best-run PSU bank" thesis is entirely a story about how well it runs the core bank.
04

The best-run PSU bank thesis — how a small bank became the standout

The single feature that defines Bank of Maharashtra — it is arguably the best-run of the entire public-sector pack, leading on growth, margin, funding, asset quality AND returns at once.

Here is what makes Bank of Maharashtra genuinely different from Baroda, PNB, Canara, Indian Bank or Union Bank. It is not size — it is one of the smallest of the group. It is not a subsidiary IPO or a splashy merger. It is that on the metrics that separate a good bank from a merely large one — growth, funding cost, margin, asset quality and returns — Bank of Maharashtra ranks at or near the top of the entire public-sector group, and does so on all of them simultaneously. Most banks are good at one or two of these levers and average on the rest. MahaBank leads on nearly all five. An analyst covering it doesn't lead with "cheap small PSU bank"; they lead with "the PSU bank that behaves like a great private bank — only faster-growing."

How the levers compound into best-of-PSU returns

The magic is that these strengths are not independent — they reinforce each other. Start with the ~53% CASA: it hands MahaBank the cheapest funding in the group, which produces the ~3.9% margin — the widest spread in the pack — on the very same loans a rival would make. Now grow that book at ~22% — the fastest rate around — and each new loan is being funded by that same cheap deposit base and earning that same fat spread. Keep the book clean (0.13% net NPA, ~1% credit cost), so almost none of that spread leaks away to loan losses. Stack cheap funding × wide margin × fast growth × tiny losses and the arithmetic produces a ~1.86% ROA and ~23% ROE — best-of-PSU returns, sustained. That is why a small bank out-earns much larger peers per rupee, and why it has quietly outperformed them.

Why this is the section that matters most

Every other force in this primer — the funding engine, the clean-book combination, the RAM shift, the government stake — ultimately feeds this one question: is Bank of Maharashtra a generic small PSU bank that had a good few years, or a genuinely superior bank the market should treat as an exception? The evidence (fastest growth + highest CASA + top-tier NIM + lowest net NPA + highest PCR + best-of-PSU ROA/ROE, all held together for several years) points firmly to the latter. The whole bull case is that this quality is durable and structural, not a flash — so the numbers to watch each quarter are the same five levers: growth, CASA, NIM, net NPA and ROA. As long as they stay best-in-class, the thesis holds.

05

The high-CASA, high-NIM engine — the source of the edge

The distinctive strength: a low-cost, sticky deposit franchise that drives everything downstream.

If the "best-run PSU bank" is the conclusion, the deposit franchise is the cause. Bank of Maharashtra's single most distinctive feature — the thing that separates it from every large peer — is an unusually high CASA ratio of ~53%, one of the highest of any bank in India. Over half of its ₹3.5-lakh-crore deposit base sits in current and savings accounts that pay little or no interest. That is the low-cost fuel that produces its top-of-pack net interest margin and, downstream, its best-of-PSU returns. Understanding why the CASA is so high, and how it converts into margin, is the heart of the story.

Every percentage point of CASA is a slice of near-free funding. At ~53%, MahaBank funds itself far more cheaply than peers — the structural reason its margin leads the group even though it lends to a similar mix of borrowers.

Why the CASA is so high — and so sticky

  • Home-state dominance. Bank of Maharashtra is the government's flagship bank in Maharashtra and runs the largest PSU-bank branch network in the state (~2,600+ branches). That density makes it the default everyday bank for millions of households, salary-earners, pensioners and small businesses — the exact accounts that hold low-cost CASA balances.
  • Government & institutional accounts. As a state-owned bank in its home region, it handles a large volume of government-department, treasury, salary and pension accounts — sticky current-account and savings balances that don't chase the highest FD rate.
  • Granular, retail-heavy deposits. Its deposit base is spread across many small savers rather than a few large wholesale depositors. Granular deposits are stickier and cheaper than bulk deposits, which flee to whoever pays most — so MahaBank's funding is both low-cost and reliable.
The mechanism: how cheap CASA becomes a fat margin

Picture two banks making the same 9% loan. Bank A funds it with deposits costing 6%; Bank B funds it with deposits costing 4.5%. Same loan, same risk — but Bank B keeps a 4.5% spread versus Bank A's 3%. That is precisely Bank of Maharashtra's advantage: because ~53% of its funding is near-free CASA, its blended cost of deposits is among the lowest in the pack, so it harvests a wider margin (~3.9% NIM) on the very same lending. And because that funding is sticky, the advantage doesn't evaporate when competition heats up. Cheap, durable funding is the hardest edge in banking to build and the most durable once you have it — and it is the engine under everything MahaBank does well. The one caveat: as the RBI cuts rates, savings-account rates barely fall (they're already low) while loan yields drop, so even a high-CASA bank sees its margin compress in a rate-cut cycle — hence the FY27 guide to ~3.75%.

06

Clean book + fast growth — the combination that shouldn't exist

The rare quality: growing the loan book faster than any PSU peer while keeping the lowest bad loans and highest provisioning.

Here is the tension at the centre of Bank of Maharashtra's story, and why it is so unusual. In banking, fast growth and clean books are natural enemies. Lend aggressively and you usually reach for riskier borrowers, and the bad loans show up two or three years later. The graveyard of Indian banking is full of lenders that grew 25% a year and then blew up. Bank of Maharashtra has done the opposite: it grew advances ~22% in FY26 — the fastest of any PSU bank — while simultaneously running the group's cleanest book: gross NPAs of 1.45%, net NPAs of just 0.13%, and provision coverage of ~98.6%. Speed and cleanliness, together, is the combination that shouldn't exist — and it is exactly what marks the bank as genuinely well-run rather than merely lucky.

How it grows fast without wrecking the book

  • Growth is granular, not lumpy. The fastest-growing part of the book is retail (up ~32% in FY26) and the broader RAM segment (retail + agri + MSME, up ~21%) — many small, spread-out, well-secured loans (home loans dominate retail). Granular lending diversifies risk far better than a handful of jumbo corporate loans that can each sink a quarter.
  • Conservative provisioning as a habit. The bank keeps its PCR near 98.6% — it sets aside money against bad loans well beyond what's strictly required. That habit, born in the PCA years, means even the loans that do go bad are already paid for, so credit costs stay low and predictable (guided ~1%).

07

The RAM shift and the shape of the loan book

A deliberate tilt toward granular retail, agri and small-business lending — the safer, higher-yielding, stickier engine.

Bank of Maharashtra's loan book is deliberately structured around RAM — Retail, Agriculture and MSME — the granular, well-distributed lending that now makes up roughly 60% of advances, with corporate loans the other ~40%. This RAM tilt is central to both the fast growth and the clean book: RAM loans are smaller, better secured, spread across many borrowers, and higher-yielding than wholesale corporate lending. Growing here is how the bank compounds fast without concentration risk.

Within RAM, retail is the star — up ~32% to ~₹86,000 Cr, led by home loans. Management guides to hold a ~60:40 RAM:corporate mix, so growth stays granular rather than chasing a few big corporate deals.

Why the RAM tilt matters

  • Higher yield. Retail and MSME loans (home, vehicle, personal, small-business) typically earn more than large corporate loans, which are priced razor-thin because big companies can shop between banks. A RAM-heavy book lifts the average loan yield — reinforcing that top-of-pack margin.
  • Lower concentration risk. A book of hundreds of thousands of small loans is far safer than one built on a handful of jumbo corporate exposures. When one small borrower defaults, it barely registers; when one ₹5,000-crore corporate loan sours, it can wipe out a quarter's profit. RAM is what keeps the book clean while it grows fast.
  • Cross-sell & stickiness. Retail and MSME customers hold their savings account, salary account and loan all with the same bank — deepening the CASA franchise and the fee income, and making the relationship harder to poach. The RAM engine and the CASA engine feed each other.
  • Home loans as the anchor. Housing dominates the retail book — long-tenure, well-secured against property, and low-loss. It is the ballast that lets the bank grow retail aggressively without adding much risk.
Why RAM is the right growth engine for this bank

A small PSU bank chasing 20%+ growth has two choices: pile into a few big corporate loans (fast, but risky and low-margin), or grow a granular retail/MSME/agri book (harder work, but safer and higher-yielding). Bank of Maharashtra chose the second — and it is the right choice for this bank precisely because it already has the deposit franchise to fund it and the branch network to originate it. Every home loan or MSME loan it makes is funded by cheap CASA, earns a healthy spread, spreads the risk, and often brings the customer's everyday banking along too. That is the virtuous circle: RAM lending and CASA gathering reinforce each other, and together they explain how a small bank grows the fastest while staying the cleanest.

08

The very high government stake, the thin float, and the dilution question

The distinctive structural nuance — the government owns ~73.6%, one of the highest stakes among listed PSU banks, so the public float is small and periodic dilution is a permanent feature.

Bank of Maharashtra carries an unusual structural feature that every holder must understand: the Government of India owns ~73.6% of it — and until December 2025, it owned ~79.6%, one of the very highest promoter stakes of any listed PSU bank. That has two consequences that pull in opposite directions. On one hand, near-total sovereign ownership makes the bank's deposits ultra-safe and cheap. On the other, it leaves a very small public float — only about a quarter of the shares actually trade — and creates a standing obligation for the government to keep selling down to satisfy the minimum-public-shareholding rule. That sell-down is the dilution overhang the stock lives with.

The minimum-public-shareholding (MPS) rule — and why it forces sell-downs

SEBI requires every listed company to have at least 25% of its shares in public hands (i.e. the promoter can own at most 75%). For years, most PSU banks — including Bank of Maharashtra — breached this, with the government owning far more than 75%. SEBI has repeatedly extended forbearance for state-owned entities (now to August 2026), but the direction is one-way: the government must bring its stake below 75%. Bank of Maharashtra crossed that line in December 2025.

What the high stake means for the stock — the honest nuance

A very high government stake is a double-edged sword for a shareholder. The upside: the deposits are effectively sovereign-backed, funding is cheap, and the government has every incentive to keep the bank well-capitalised. The downsides are two. First, a thin float: with only ~26% of shares trading, the stock can be more volatile, less liquid, and harder for large institutions to build positions in — a structural cap on how much the market will pay. Second, a dilution overhang: to meet MPS (and to fund its fast growth), the bank periodically issues new shares — the ₹3,500 Cr QIP in 2024, the Dec-2025 OFS, and a board-approved enabling resolution for up to ₹7,500 Cr more equity. Fresh share issuance can dilute existing holders and caps per-share book-value growth even when total profit is racing ahead. The paradox: the bank's fast growth needs capital, and its high government stake forces equity sales — so dilution is a semi-permanent feature, not a one-off.

The float nuance to remember: because the government owns ~73.6%, the "market cap" of ~₹68,000 Cr overstates what actually trades — the free-float is a fraction of it. Watch each capital-raise (OFS/QIP/FPO) for two things: the price (a raise below book value is dilutive to book per share) and the size of new supply hitting a small float. Over time, further government sell-downs to fund growth are likely — good for liquidity and index weight, but a recurring supply headwind for the share price.

Financials & valuation

Record profits, best-of-PSU ratios — and, unusually for a PSU bank, a premium price-to-book.

Bank of Maharashtra's financial record is the picture of a turnaround that became a compounding machine: profit up from a small post-crisis base to a record ₹7,019 Cr, sector-leading margins and returns, and a clean, fast-growing book. The debate isn't the business quality — it's whether a small PSU bank can keep leading, and whether its already-premium valuation leaves room to re-rate further.

How to read the valuation — the P/B is the key lens

At ~₹68,000 Cr market cap on ~₹7,000 Cr of FY26 profit, Bank of Maharashtra trades at roughly ~9.7× trailing earnings and ~2.0× book value. For a bank, price-to-book is the sharper lens than P/E, because a bank's book value is its equity capital — the raw material it lends against. Here is the striking part: at ~2.0× book, Bank of Maharashtra trades at a premium to almost every other PSU bank (Indian Bank ~1.4×, most peers ~1.0–1.3×) — the market already pays up for its superior ROE. That's the flip side of quality: MahaBank is not the "cheap PSU bank" — it's the one the market has already partly re-rated. The bull says a ~23% ROE justifies an even higher multiple (private banks with similar returns fetch 2.5–3×); the bear says a small, government-controlled, thinly-floated bank facing NIM compression and dilution shouldn't be the priciest book in the pack. The debate is entirely about how much more the market will pay for best-of-PSU quality (see §10).

10

The re-rating debate — how much is best-of-PSU quality worth?

The valuation question that defines the stock — already re-rated, or more to come?

Most PSU-bank primers end on the same question: will the market ever pay a premium for a state-owned bank? Bank of Maharashtra is the interesting case because, uniquely, it already has — it trades at ~2× book, a premium to the pack. So the debate is subtler: is that premium fair, is there more re-rating to come, or has the market got ahead of a small bank facing headwinds?

The bull re-rating argument

  • ROE justifies a higher multiple. A bank earning a sustainable ~23% ROE is, on the standard math, worth well over 2× book — private banks with comparable returns trade at 2.5–3×. If MahaBank sustains best-of-PSU returns, its ~2× is not stretched; it's still a discount to what the returns imply.
  • Quality is being recognised, not just hoped for. The premium to peers shows the market is already treating MahaBank as an exception to the PSU discount — a re-rating that could deepen as the record lengthens.
  • Improving liquidity. Each government sell-down raises the free float, which over time lifts institutional ownership and index weight — a technical tailwind for the multiple.

The bear counter

  • It's already the priciest PSU book. Buying at ~2× book means the easy re-rating (from cheap to fair) has happened. From here you're paying up-front for continued out-execution — with little margin for a stumble.
  • NIM compression is coming. Management itself guides margins down to ~3.75%; the RBI rate-cut cycle squeezes the very spread the whole thesis rests on. Slower margin = slower profit growth = a harder multiple to defend.
  • Structural PSU limits. Government control, mandated priority-sector lending, appointment-driven management, a thin float and recurring dilution are real constraints a private bank doesn't carry — reasons the multiple may stay capped below private-bank levels regardless of ROE.
The crux of the re-rating debate

Strip it down and the debate is one question: is Bank of Maharashtra's best-of-PSU quality durable enough to earn a private-bank-style multiple, or is it a small state bank whose premium is already full? The bull owns the fundamentals — the returns genuinely rival private banks and, on ROE math, could support a higher multiple. The bear owns the structure — a small, government-controlled, thinly-floated bank with margin headwinds and dilution ahead has natural ceilings. The tell will be durability: if MahaBank holds ~1.8%+ ROA, ~20%+ ROE and sub-1% slippages while growing 18–22% for another few years, the premium proves justified and can extend. If margins compress and growth cools, a ~2× multiple on a PSU bank has room to fall. Watch the return ratios, not the headlines.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely excellent PSU bank — the question is durability and price, not quality.

Bank of Maharashtra is one of the clearest quality stories in the entire PSU-bank space: the fastest-growing, cleanest, most profitable and best-funded of the state-owned pack, run with a discipline that survived the crisis years and compounded through them. The investment debate is almost entirely about durability and valuation — can it keep leading, and is the premium price fair — not about whether the business is good.

The bull case

  • Best-of-PSU on every lever — fastest growth (+22%), highest CASA (~53%), top-tier NIM (~3.9%), lowest net NPA (0.13%), best ROA/ROE (~1.86% / ~23%), all at once.
  • Structural low-cost funding — a ~53% CASA franchise anchored in home-state dominance; the hardest, most durable edge in banking.
  • Clean-book-plus-fast-growth — grows the fastest while staying the cleanest; ~98.6% PCR and sub-1% slippages leave little to surprise.
  • Long runway — a small bank with room to keep gaining share; 18–22% credit-growth guidance and RAM-led granular lending.
  • Well capitalised & sovereign-backed — CRAR 18.4%, rising dividend, ultra-safe deposits.

The bear case

  • Already re-rated — at ~2× book it's the priciest PSU book; the cheap-to-fair re-rating is done, so little margin for error.
  • NIM compression — management guides margins down to ~3.75%; the RBI rate-cut cycle squeezes the core spread.
  • Growth outpacing deposits — advances (+22%) growing faster than deposits (+14%) strains the loan-to-deposit ratio and funding cost over time.
  • High govt stake, thin float & dilution — ~73.6% state-owned; recurring OFS/QIP supply and equity raises dilute holders and cap book-per-share growth.
  • PSU structural limits & unseasoned book — priority-sector mandates, appointment-driven management, and a fast-grown loan book yet to be tested through a full downturn.
The bottom line

Bank of Maharashtra is the standout of India's public-sector banks — a small lender that, on nearly every metric that matters, out-performs peers many times its size, powered by an exceptional low-cost deposit franchise that feeds fast, clean, high-return growth. The business risk is genuinely low for a PSU bank. The catch is that the market already knows it: unlike its cheap peers, MahaBank trades at a premium to book, so the valuation risk is real and the easy re-rating is behind it. The story over the coming years will be written in a handful of numbers each quarter — CASA and NIM (is the funding edge holding as rates fall?), slippages and net NPA (is the clean book surviving the fast growth?), and ROA/ROE (are the best-of-PSU returns durable?). Keep those best-in-class and the premium is earned; let them slip and a re-rated PSU bank has room to fall.
