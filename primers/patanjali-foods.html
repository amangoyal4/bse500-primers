00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Patanjali Foods Limited is the listed food-and-FMCG company of the Patanjali group — the yoga-guru-turned-business-empire built by Baba Ramdev and Acharya Balkrishna. But it did not start life as a Patanjali company. It is the erstwhile Ruchi Soya Industries, once one of India's largest edible-oil processors, which collapsed under ~₹12,000 crore of debt, went through bankruptcy court, and was bought by Patanjali Ayurved in 2019 for ₹4,350 crore. Patanjali then used this already-listed shell as the vehicle to inject its own consumer brands and rebranded it Patanjali Foods in 2022.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is really two businesses stapled together. A giant, low-margin edible-oil business (soya, palm, mustard, sunflower — brands like Ruchi Gold, Mahakosh, Nutrela) that did ~₹29,300 Cr of FY26 revenue at a razor-thin ~2–3% margin; and a smaller but far more profitable FMCG / food business (ghee, biscuits, honey, atta, spices, nutraceuticals) plus a newly-acquired home-&-personal-care (HPC) portfolio, doing ~₹11,200 Cr at ~11% margins.
  • The whole investment story is the mix shift. FMCG is only ~27.6% of FY26 revenue but ~61% of EBITDA. The market is paying an FMCG-style multiple (~25× earnings) on the bet that Patanjali keeps growing the profitable branded business and shrinking commodity oil's dominance — turning a trading company into a consumer-brands company.
  • FY26 was a record year. Revenue crossed ₹40,000 Cr for the first time (₹40,170 Cr, +19%) and net profit jumped 39.5% to ₹1,814 Cr. But most of that scale is still cyclical, price-driven edible-oil turnover — impressive top-line, thin and volatile margins underneath.
In plain terms

Imagine a company that is 70%+ a commodity oil-trading-and-refining machine and 30% a consumer-brands company — and the stock market mostly cares about the smaller, tastier 30%. Patanjali Foods sells enormous volumes of cooking oil, on which it earns pennies, and a growing pile of branded ghee, biscuits, honey, toothpaste and soap, on which it earns real money. Baba Ramdev's Patanjali took over a bankrupt oil giant and is now trying to convert it into a proper FMCG business — the same shelf-space game as Dabur, Marico or Hindustan Unilever. Whether that conversion is truly happening, fast enough to justify the price, is the entire debate.

01

The Ruchi Soya backstory: bankruptcy, rescue, and a name change

You cannot understand this company without understanding the corporate resurrection that created it.

Patanjali Foods is one of the most unusual origin stories on the Indian market: a listed company that died and was reborn under a completely different owner. The old Ruchi Soya Industries was a soya and edible-oil heavyweight — but aggressive expansion and commodity bets left it with roughly ₹12,000 crore of debt, and in December 2017 lenders dragged it into India's bankruptcy court (the NCLT) under the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC).

How the rescue worked

  • Insolvency (2017). The Mumbai NCLT admitted Ruchi Soya into the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process on 15 December 2017. A resolution professional took control, and creditors invited bids for the whole company.
  • Patanjali wins the bid (2019). Patanjali Ayurved's resolution plan — a ₹4,350 crore package — was approved by the NCLT on 24 July 2019 and implemented from 18 December 2019. Lenders took a large haircut; Patanjali got a ready-made national edible-oil business plus a stock-exchange listing.
  • The renaming (2022). On 24 June 2022 the company formally changed its name from Ruchi Soya Industries to Patanjali Foods Limited — signalling that this listed shell was now the group's consumer-goods flagship, not just an oil refiner.
Why an insolvency deal is such a good starting hand

Buying a company out of bankruptcy is often a bargain: creditors write off huge sums, so the acquirer inherits real factories, brands and market share at a fraction of build cost, frequently with a cleaned-up balance sheet and even accumulated tax losses to shelter future profits. That is exactly what happened here — Patanjali got Ruchi Soya's refineries, soya-protein plants, oil-palm rights and distribution for ₹4,350 crore, then loaded its own brands on top. The catch, which the next sections explore, is that the core business it inherited is structurally low-margin commodity oil — so the group has spent the years since trying to graft a higher-value brands business onto a thin-margin trading chassis.

The near-disaster: the FPO and the free-float overhang

The rescue created a peculiar and dangerous problem. Under Indian rules (SCRR Rule 19A), a listed company must have at least 25% of its shares in public hands ("minimum public shareholding", or MPS). After the takeover, Patanjali owned so much of Ruchi Soya that the public float was tiny — well below 25%. The company had to sell more shares to outsiders to fix this.

  • The FPO (March 2022). Patanjali Foods launched a ₹4,300 crore Follow-on Public Offer, issuing ~66 million new shares at ₹648 each to widen the float. The offer was nearly derailed by a temporary market-manipulation freeze on the shares before it went ahead.
  • Still short. Even after the FPO, public shareholding sat at only ~19% — still below the 25% floor — leaving a permanent free-float overhang: the promoter had to keep selling down.
  • SEBI/exchange intervention. When deadlines lapsed, the exchanges (BSE/NSE) froze the promoters' shares for the MPS breach — a public embarrassment. The promoter then sold stock via the Offer-for-Sale route (dropping the holding to ~73.8%, then lower), a supply overhang that has weighed on the share for years.
Why the overhang matters to a buyer of the stock: whenever a promoter must sell shares to satisfy a rule, that forced selling caps the price — it's a steady supply of stock hitting the market regardless of fundamentals. Patanjali's promoter holding of ~68% today is still above the level at which the MPS question fully closes, so watch for further sell-downs; each one is a technical headwind even if the business is doing well.
02

The two-engine model: commodity oil vs branded FMCG

One company, two utterly different economics — read this and the whole thesis clicks.

Patanjali Foods reports as a single company, but financially it runs on two engines with opposite characteristics. Engine one is edible oil: enormous volume, wafer-thin margins, hostage to global commodity prices and import duties. Engine two is FMCG / food + HPC: smaller revenue, but several times the margin, driven by brand and distribution rather than crush spreads. The gap between them is the single most important thing to grasp.

Why the market wants the mix to shift

Commodity oil and branded FMCG are valued completely differently. A commodity processor earns thin, volatile margins on price you don't control (global soya/palm prices, import duties, the rupee) — the market pays a low multiple for that, because the profit can vanish in a bad cycle. A branded FMCG business earns fat, stable margins because the customer pays for the brand, not the raw material — the market pays a high multiple for that predictability. So every rupee of profit that moves from "oil" to "FMCG" is worth more as a valuation, even before it grows. That's why "FMCG-isation" — lifting the branded share of profit — is the lever bulls obsess over, and why the stock trades far above what a pure oil refiner would fetch.

03

Edible-oil economics: the thin-margin core

The engine that makes most of the revenue and almost none of the multiple.

The edible-oil business is what Patanjali inherited from Ruchi Soya, and it remains the company's revenue backbone — ~₹29,300 Cr in FY26, roughly three-quarters of the top line. But it is a fundamentally commodity business, and understanding why its margins are so thin (and so volatile) explains half of everything about the stock.

How an oil refiner actually earns

India does not grow nearly enough cooking oil for its own kitchens — it imports well over half its edible oil (crude palm oil from Indonesia/Malaysia, soya and sunflower from the Americas and Black Sea). A processor like Patanjali buys crude oil or oilseeds, refines/crushes them, and sells refined oil. Its profit is the "crush spread" — the gap between what it pays for the raw input and what it gets for the finished oil, minus processing cost. That spread is:

  • Small. Edible-oil EBITDA margins run in the low single digits — around 2–3%. On ₹100 of oil sold, a couple of rupees of operating profit is a good day.
  • Volatile. The spread swings with global prices, freight, the rupee, and inventory timing. If you bought crude palm oil high and prices fall before you sell, you can post an inventory loss; if prices rise, a windfall gain. Quarter-to-quarter profits can lurch on price moves you did not cause.
  • Policy-driven. The single biggest swing factor is government import duty. India raises or cuts the duty on crude vs refined palm oil to protect farmers or tame food inflation; each change reshuffles who makes money — domestic refiners vs importers — overnight.

The one bright spot in oil — branding it

Not all cooking oil is equal. Bulk/loose oil is pure commodity; branded, packaged oil (Patanjali, Nutrela, Ruchi Gold, Mahakosh) commands a small premium and a bit more margin stability, because the shopper is choosing a name. Management has pushed the branded share of oil sales to roughly 85% — a genuine, if modest, up-margining of even the commodity engine. It also gives Patanjali pricing points on the shelf next to Fortune (Adani Wilmar) and Saffola (Marico). But make no mistake: branded or not, edible oil is still governed by global commodity prices, and its margins will never look like biscuits or toothpaste.

Why FY26 revenue "jumped" without much margin help: a lot of the FY26 top-line growth was edible-oil volume and price — the segment's revenue rose over 20% partly because oil prices and volumes were up. That flatters the headline revenue number (₹40,170 Cr) but does little for the profit pool, because the extra sales came at ~2–3% margins. Always separate Patanjali's revenue growth (oil-heavy) from its profit growth (FMCG-heavy) — they tell different stories.
04

The FMCG engine: where the profit and the multiple live

Smaller, richer, faster — the reason this is a "foods" company at all.

The FMCG / food segment is the smaller engine by revenue (~₹11,200 Cr, ~28% of FY26 sales) but the dominant one by profit — it delivered roughly 61% of FY26 EBITDA at margins around 11%, several times the edible-oil segment. This is where Patanjali's brand equity, distribution and pricing power actually pay off, and it is what earns the company its FMCG label and valuation.

What's inside the FMCG basket

  • Food staples & foods — cow ghee, atta (wheat flour), spices, pulses, honey, gram flour, dry fruits, kesar, madhuram (jaggery/sweeteners). The high-frequency, kitchen-staple core, sold heavily under the Patanjali and Nutrela brands.
  • Biscuits, cookies, rusks, cereals & noodles — the packaged-snacks push, built partly from acquired businesses (Patanjali Natural Biscuits, noodle & cereal lines). This is direct combat with Britannia, Parle and Nestlé for shelf space.
  • Nutraceuticals & soya foods — textured soya protein (Nutrela is the market leader here), health supplements and wellness products — a natural adjacency to the "Ayurveda / natural" brand positioning and among the higher-margin lines.
  • Home & Personal Care (HPC) — dental care, skin care, hair care and home care (toothpaste, soaps, shampoos, detergents), acquired from Patanjali Ayurved in 2024 for ₹1,100 Cr. This is the newest, and arguably most strategically important, leg — it takes Patanjali Foods beyond food into the everyday personal-care aisle where FMCG margins are best.
Why FMCG margins are ~4× oil margins

A biscuit or a tube of toothpaste is sold on brand, taste and habit, not on the price of wheat or palm derivatives. The customer isn't tracking the commodity, so the company keeps a fat, stable gross margin, spends some of it on advertising and distribution, and still nets far more than an oil refiner. That's why FMCG delivers ~11% operating margins vs ~2–3% for oil — and why every rupee of FMCG growth does disproportionate good to both profit and valuation. The HPC acquisition matters precisely because personal-care products (toothpaste, soap) sit at the top of the FMCG margin ladder — richer even than most packaged foods.

05

The oil-palm plantation: backward integration & a policy tailwind

The under-appreciated upstream asset that could shrink India's import bill — and Patanjali's.

Patanjali Foods is the country's largest player in a business almost no one talks about: growing oil palm on Indian soil. Because India imports the majority of its edible oil, the government has made domestic oil-palm cultivation a strategic priority (the National Mission on Edible Oils – Oil Palm). Patanjali sits right in the middle of that push, with ~71,000 hectares under cultivation and allotted land-bank access to roughly 628,000 hectares across 12 states.

Why owning the plantation is strategic

  • Backward integration. If Patanjali grows its own palm, it captures the raw material margin instead of importing crude palm oil at global prices — reducing its exposure to the very commodity swings that make the oil business so thin and volatile.
  • Import-substitution tailwind. The government wants domestic palm to displace imports, and offers price support and subsidies to farmers and processors under the oil-palm mission. Patanjali is a prime beneficiary of that policy direction.
  • A long-dated option. Oil palm takes ~4 years from planting to first fruit and years more to mature, so this is a slow-burn asset — but as those 71,000 hectares (and future plantings) come into full yield, they feed the refineries with cheaper, home-grown feedstock.
In plain terms — the DOP (Domestic Oil-Palm) opportunity

Right now India is dangerously dependent on imported cooking oil, which is both a national-security worry and the reason Patanjali's oil margins bounce around. If Patanjali can grow enough palm at home, it turns a chunk of its most volatile cost into an owned asset — smoothing margins and riding a government subsidy wave at the same time. It won't transform the numbers overnight (trees are patient), but over a decade the plantation business is the closest thing edible oil has to a moat: land, government allotments and a multi-year head-start that a rival can't quickly replicate.

The honest caveat: 71,000 hectares is meaningful but still small against a 628,000-hectare land bank and against India's total oil-palm ambition — and oil-palm cultivation carries environmental and land-use scrutiny. The plantation is a real long-term asset and policy tailwind, but it is not yet a needle-mover for near-term profits.
06

The owners and the brand behind them

A listed company whose value rests on a name it does not fully own.

Patanjali Foods is controlled by the Patanjali group — the sprawling business empire of yoga guru Baba Ramdev and his associate Acharya Balkrishna, who runs the group's flagship, unlisted Patanjali Ayurved Limited. Promoters hold roughly 68% of Patanjali Foods. The listed company's greatest asset — and its central dependency — is the Patanjali brand itself, which it licenses from the promoter group rather than owns outright.

The Patanjali empire, in brief

  • Patanjali Ayurved (unlisted) — the original flagship: Ayurvedic medicines, personal care, and the brand's spiritual home. It is a promoter of Patanjali Foods and the counterparty to the brand-licensing and business-transfer deals.
  • Patanjali Foods (this company, listed) — the group's designated vehicle for food, edible oil and FMCG, built on the Ruchi Soya chassis.
  • The brand promise — "swadeshi" (indigenous), natural, Ayurvedic, value-for-money. This positioning, and Ramdev's mass following, gave Patanjali extraordinary distribution reach and consumer trust very quickly — a genuine competitive edge against multinational FMCG rivals.

Why "leases the brand" is the crux

Here's the subtlety that separates Patanjali Foods from a normal FMCG company: it doesn't own the Patanjali trademark outright — it pays the promoter (Patanjali Ayurved) a royalty to use it. The food-business transfer carried a ~1% turnover royalty; the HPC deal carries a 20-year licence at ~3% of the relevant turnover (minimum ~₹83 Cr/year). So the very brand that justifies the company's premium valuation is rented from the controlling shareholder. That's a durable arrangement, but it means a slice of the value the listed company creates flows back to the promoter — a structural tension buyers of the stock have to accept.

07

Related-party deals, royalties & brand baggage

The governance questions a careful investor cannot skip.

Because the same promoter controls both the listed company (Patanjali Foods) and the private flagship (Patanjali Ayurved), a large part of Patanjali Foods' growth has come through related-party transactions — buying businesses from, and paying royalties to, its own controlling shareholder. None of this is illegal, and each deal cleared regulatory approval, but it creates a recurring governance question: are these deals priced fairly for the listed company's minority shareholders, or do they favour the promoter?

The brand-baggage problem: the Supreme Court ads case

Patanjali Foods' fortunes are tied to the reputation of a brand it does not control. That became uncomfortably clear in the Supreme Court misleading-advertisements case against Patanjali Ayurved — the sister company, not the listed one. Beginning in 2022, the Indian Medical Association accused Patanjali Ayurved of running ads that made unproven medical claims and disparaged modern medicine. The case escalated into contempt proceedings against Baba Ramdev and Acharya Balkrishna in 2023–24, with public court scoldings and an apology, before the contempt charges were dropped in August 2024.

Why a case against the sister company matters here: Patanjali Foods didn't run those ads — but it shares the brand. Any dent to "Patanjali" as a trusted, wholesome name is a dent to the asset the listed company's valuation rests on. Reputational risk at the promoter level is, in effect, a risk to Patanjali Foods' shareholders — a form of brand baggage that a company owning its own trademark wouldn't carry.
How to weigh the governance angle

Related-party growth is a double-edged sword. On one hand it let Patanjali Foods bulk up into FMCG fast, using a ready-made, trusted brand — a genuine shortcut a from-scratch rival couldn't take. On the other, it means minority shareholders are along for the ride on deals set between the promoter's two pockets, and they permanently share the group's reputational and royalty burden. The market's willingness to pay an FMCG multiple depends on trusting that these deals are fair and that the FMCG-isation is real value creation, not just intra-group shuffling. That trust is the soft variable under the hard valuation.

08

Distribution reach and the competitive battlefield

Two different fights: a commodity war in oil and a brand war in FMCG.

Patanjali Foods competes on two fronts at once. In edible oil it fights a scale-and-cost war against other giant processors; in branded FMCG it fights a brand-and-shelf-space war against the biggest names in Indian consumer goods. Its weapon in both is an enormous distribution network — reportedly reaching over 2 million retail outlets, expanded by 0.2–0.25 million outlets in the last year.

The clearest mirror for Patanjali Foods is Adani Wilmar (now AWL Agri Business) — another edible-oil-plus-FMCG giant with the same two-engine structure and the same "shift to food" thesis. Against the pure FMCG majors — HUL, Dabur, Marico, Nestlé — Patanjali is the disruptive value challenger, strong on price and natural positioning but still building the distribution depth and category leadership those incumbents took decades to build.

How to think about the ~25× multiple

At roughly ₹45,000 Cr market cap on ~₹1,814 Cr of FY26 profit, Patanjali Foods trades near 25× trailing earnings — a full FMCG-style multiple. Here's the tension: a pure edible-oil refiner would trade at maybe 10–15× because its earnings are thin and cyclical. Patanjali gets the higher multiple only because the market believes the FMCG/HPC engine — already 61% of profit — keeps growing and lifting the whole company's margin and return profile toward true consumer-goods levels. Bulls say a trusted mass brand with 2m+ outlets and a plantation option deserves the re-rating. Bears say you're paying an FMCG price for a company whose revenue is still 72% low-margin commodity oil, whose ROE (~12%) lags real FMCG peers, and whose profit jump was partly a one-off tax reversal. The multiple only holds if FMCG-isation is genuinely accelerating.

Read the profit growth carefully: FY26 PAT rose 39.5%, but part of the Q4 jump came from a tax reversal / exceptional item, and much of the revenue growth was oil price/volume. Underlying operating profit growth is real but more modest than the headline PAT suggests — always look at segment EBITDA (especially FMCG) rather than the bottom line alone.
10

The FMCG-isation thesis — is it actually working?

The bespoke question that decides whether the stock deserves its price.

Everything about Patanjali Foods' valuation rests on one thesis: that a commodity-oil company is genuinely transforming into a branded-FMCG company. So the honest question is — is it working? The evidence is mixed-but-improving, and this is the number to track above all others.

The evidence FOR (the thesis is delivering)

  • FMCG is now the profit engine. From a standing start, FMCG reached ~28% of revenue but ~61% of EBITDA in FY26 — the profit mix has decisively shifted toward brands.
  • FMCG is growing ~20%/yr — far faster than the low-single-digit growth of the oil business in real terms, so the mix keeps improving on its own.
  • The HPC acquisition adds the highest-margin FMCG category (personal care), and the brand + 2m-outlet distribution give it a real runway.
  • Branded oil (~85% of oil sales) shows even the commodity engine is being up-margined where possible.

The evidence AGAINST (the thesis is unproven)

  • Revenue is still ~72% commodity oil. The company remains, by turnover, an oil business — the "FMCG company" framing flatters a still-commodity reality.
  • Blended margins are still ~4.8% — a fraction of a Dabur or Marico — because oil drags the average down. The re-rating assumes a mix shift that will take years.
  • Returns lag. ROE ~12% and ROCE ~15% are below the 20%+ that genuine FMCG leaders earn — so the market is paying up for a future return profile, not today's.
  • Growth is partly acquired, not organic. Much of the FMCG build came from buying businesses off the promoter — the true organic brand-building pace is harder to isolate.
The bet isn't whether Patanjali Foods is a good oil company — it is. The bet is whether it becomes a good FMCG company fast enough to have deserved an FMCG price all along.
The single metric that settles it

Watch the FMCG share of EBITDA and its margin, quarter after quarter. If FMCG keeps compounding ~20% and its share of profit climbs from ~61% toward, say, 70–75% while its margin holds or expands, the FMCG-isation is real and the multiple is defensible — the company slowly becomes the thing the market is paying for. If FMCG growth stalls, or its margin gets competed away in the brutal personal-care aisle, then you're left holding a low-margin oil company at an FMCG price, and the stock has a long way to de-rate. Two things can therefore both be true: it's a genuinely improving business and a fully-priced stock.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A real transformation underway — priced as if it's already complete.

Patanjali Foods is a genuinely improving business: a bankrupt oil giant rebuilt, debt sharply reduced, and a growing, profitable branded engine bolted on. The investment debate is almost entirely about whether the FMCG-isation justifies the FMCG valuation, and about the governance and commodity risks that come with the package.

The bull case

  • Mix shift is real — FMCG already ~61% of EBITDA and growing ~20%/yr; every quarter tilts the profit pool toward high-margin brands.
  • A powerful, trusted brand — "swadeshi/Ayurveda" positioning plus Ramdev's mass following, giving pricing power multinationals must fight for.
  • Deep distribution — 2m+ outlets, still expanding — a genuine FMCG asset.
  • HPC + nutraceuticals add the richest FMCG categories; oil-palm plantation is a long-dated policy-backed option.
  • Record FY26 — ₹40,000 Cr revenue, ₹1,814 Cr PAT; scale and cash flow to self-fund the transition.

The bear case

  • Still 72% commodity oil — thin (~2–3%) margins, hostage to global prices, import duty and the rupee; blended margin only ~4.8%.
  • Full FMCG valuation (~25× P/E) on sub-FMCG returns (ROE ~12%) — little room for error.
  • Related-party dependence — brand is rented from the promoter (royalties leak value); growth partly bought from the promoter's own pockets.
  • Brand baggage — the Supreme Court misleading-ads case against sister-company Patanjali Ayurved is reputational risk shared by the listed name.
  • Free-float overhang — MPS not fully resolved; further promoter sell-downs are a technical drag. FY26 PAT flattered by a tax reversal.
The bottom line

Patanjali Foods is a fascinating transformation story — a bankrupt commodity giant being reshaped into a consumer-brands company on the back of one of India's most powerful mass brands. The direction of travel is genuine: FMCG is the profit engine, it's growing fast, and the distribution and brand are real. But the stock is priced as if the transformation is already done, while the reality is a company that still earns most of its revenue from thin-margin oil, generates FMCG-lagging returns, and shares its value and reputation with a controlling promoter. The story over the next few years will be written in one number each quarter: the FMCG segment's growth and margin. Keep tilting the profit pool toward brands and the premium is earned; stall, and an FMCG multiple on an oil company has a long way to fall.
