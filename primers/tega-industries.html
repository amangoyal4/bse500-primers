00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Tega Industries makes the parts that wear out inside a mine's grinding mill. When a copper, gold or iron-ore mine digs up rock, it doesn't sell boulders — it has to crush and grind that rock into a fine powder to free the metal. The grinding happens inside enormous rotating steel drums called mills, and the inside of every mill is lined with replaceable liners that take a relentless pounding from tumbling rock and steel and slowly get destroyed. Tega is a world leader in rubber and composite mill liners, and it wraps around them a whole basket of other mining consumables and wear parts — screens, hydrocyclones, mill accessories, chute liners. It is not a machine-maker; it is a consumables-maker, and that single distinction is the heart of the business.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The product is designed to be destroyed. A mill liner is not equipment you buy once — it is a wear part with a life measured in months. It grinds away, the mill is stopped, the old liner is stripped out, and a fresh one goes in. That means Tega earns recurring, aftermarket-style replacement revenue tied to how much ore a mine keeps grinding — its throughput — rather than to whether the mine builds anything new. Consumables were ~84% of FY26 revenue; equipment (the mills and plant) was the smaller ~16%.
  • It is a genuinely global niche leader run from Kolkata. Tega sells into mines across Africa, Australia, Latin America and beyond, and manufactures on three continents — India (Dahej, Kalyani, Samali), plus Chile, South Africa and Australia — right next to the world's big ore bodies. FY26 was steady: standalone revenue ~₹1,774 Cr (up ~5%), adjusted EBITDA ~₹397 Cr (~22% margin), PAT ~₹227 Cr (up ~13%).
  • It has just made a bet several times its own size. In a consortium with Apollo funds, Tega agreed to acquire Molycop — a global maker of grinding media, the steel and high-chrome balls that do the actual grinding inside those same mills — at an enterprise value of roughly US$1.48 bn (approx.). It is a debt-funded deal that takes a lightly-geared company to consolidated leverage of ~4–4.5× net-debt/EBITDA at the FY27 peak. That transaction, not the quiet FY26 numbers, is now the centre of the story.
In plain terms

Every mine that grinds ore is quietly eating a stream of rubber, steel and composite parts every single month, and someone has to keep supplying the replacements. Tega is one of the world's specialists at supplying that stream — above all the liners inside the grinding mill. Because the parts are consumed and swapped on a clock, its demand tracks tonnes of rock ground, which is far steadier than the on-again-off-again cycle of building new mines. The fresh twist is Molycop: Tega is buying the company that makes the grinding balls that tumble against its liners — moving from lining the mill to owning both halves of what the mill destroys — and it has borrowed heavily to do it.

01

Grinding mills 101: how a mine eats wear parts

No mining degree needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

A metal mine has a problem you rarely picture: the valuable metal is locked inside rock at tiny concentrations. A good copper ore might be under 1% copper; the rest is worthless stone. To get the metal out, the mine must first break the rock down from boulders to a fine sand so the metal grains can be separated chemically. That size-reduction — comminution, in the trade — is one of the most energy- and equipment-intensive activities in all of heavy industry, and it is where Tega lives.

The last and most punishing stage of that grinding happens inside a mill: a huge steel cylinder, sometimes 10–12 metres across, that rotates slowly on its axis. Inside, the crushed rock tumbles together with a charge of hard steel balls (the grinding media). As the mill turns, rock and balls cascade and smash into each other and against the mill walls, pulverising the ore. Two things get relentlessly abraded in that cascade: the liners bolted to the inside wall, and the grinding balls themselves. Both are sacrificial. Both must be replaced. Learn five terms and the rest of this primer reads easily.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Mill (SAG / ball)The rotating drum that grinds ore to powder. A SAG mill uses the rock itself plus some balls; a ball mill uses mostly steel balls. Their insides wear out — that lining is Tega's core market.
Mill linerThe replaceable plates and lifter bars bolted to the inside of the mill that protect the shell and lift the charge. Made of steel, rubber, or a rubber-metal composite. A wear part — Tega's flagship product.
Grinding mediaThe steel or high-chrome balls that tumble inside the mill and actually do the grinding. Consumed as they wear to nothing — this is Molycop's business, which Tega is acquiring.
ThroughputThe tonnes of ore a mine grinds per year. This — not the metal price and not mine capex — is what drives how fast liners and media wear out and need replacing. The single demand driver to hold onto.
Re-lineThe scheduled shutdown to strip out worn liners and bolt in fresh ones. It happens on a recurring clock (often every few months). Every re-line is a Tega sale — the heartbeat of the annuity.
The economics that decide everything: the re-line clock

Here is the mechanism that makes this business tick. A mill cannot run with worn-out liners — a liner that fails or wears through can let tumbling rock and steel damage the mill shell itself, which is a catastrophic, production-halting event. So a mine re-lines on a disciplined schedule, whether the copper price is high or low, because the alternative is an unplanned mill stoppage that costs it far more than a set of liners ever could. That is why Tega's demand is anchored not to a mine's mood or its expansion plans but to a physical fact: the mill keeps turning, the liner keeps wearing, and the clock keeps striking. The more ore the world grinds, the faster that clock runs.

Why a wear part is a better business than a machine

  • It recurs by physics. A machine sells once and then sits there for a decade. A liner sells, wears out, and sells again — a self-renewing order every few months for the life of the mill.
  • It rides throughput, not capex. When mining is booming and new mines are built, equipment-makers feast; when it slumps, their orders vanish. Consumables are steadier: even a mine that has frozen all expansion still has to keep grinding the ore it already mines, and so keeps buying liners and media.
  • It is small-ticket and non-negotiable. A liner set is a rounding error against a mine's revenue, but running out of them stops the mill. Miners rarely squeeze the supplier of a mission-critical consumable the way they squeeze a one-off equipment vendor.
02

What Tega actually sells: a basket of things that wear out

One customer, one grinding circuit, many consumable lines — read this once and the model clicks.

Tega's pitch to a mine is that it can supply almost the entire suite of wear parts in the mineral-processing circuit — the flow that turns dug-up rock into a saleable metal concentrate. Rather than sell a single product, it sits alongside the mill and supplies the parts that erode at every stage. The bulk of that is consumables; a smaller slice is equipment (the mills, crushers and plant themselves).

The revenue lines, decoded

  • Mill liners (the flagship consumable) — the plates and lifter bars that line the grinding mill. Tega's speciality is rubber and rubber-metal composite liners rather than pure cast metal, and its premium composite product, DynaPrime, is the share-gain weapon (Section 04). Liners are the core of the ~84% consumables book.
  • Other mining consumables — a long tail of wear products: screens and screening media (to sort rock by size), hydrocyclones (to separate particles by size in a slurry), chute and pipe liners, and assorted abrasion-resistant parts. All of them wear and re-order on their own clocks, broadening the basket Tega sells into each mine.
  • Equipment & plant (the minority) — Tega also sells some of the machines: mills, crushers and mineral-beneficiation equipment, partly through capabilities picked up in earlier acquisitions such as McNally Sayaji. This is the lumpier, project-shaped ~16% of revenue — and notably it grew ~25% in FY26, faster than consumables that year, even as consumables remained the ballast.
Why selling the whole basket matters

A mine's processing plant is a headache to keep supplied: dozens of wear parts, each from a different vendor, each on its own re-order clock, each capable of stopping production if it runs out. A supplier that can cover liners and screens and hydrocyclones — and now, via Molycop, the grinding media too — becomes a single, trusted partner for the whole grinding circuit rather than one item on a purchasing list. The more of the basket Tega supplies, the deeper it is embedded in the mine's operations and the harder it is to displace. Breadth here is not diversification for its own sake; it is a route to becoming indispensable.

03

The replacement annuity: paid in worn-away steel and rubber

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — and it is specific to this company.

Most industrial suppliers to mining live and die by the capex cycle: when miners are flush and building, orders pour in; when commodity prices crack and expansion stops, orders evaporate. Tega's core business is wired to a different, steadier driver. Its liners and media are not bought to build a mine — they are bought to keep an already-built mine running, and they are consumed continuously. Its revenue therefore tracks how much ore the world grinds, not how much money miners spend on new projects.

In a downturn, miners stop building (capex collapses) but keep grinding the ore they already mine, because idle mines earn nothing. Liners and grinding media keep wearing and keep being re-ordered — so consumables demand decouples from the capex cycle that whipsaws equipment vendors.

This is the entire reason a niche mining supplier can command a premium rating. A liner that lasts a few months and then must be replaced is, in financial terms, a subscription the customer cannot cancel without stopping production. Multiply that across a global fleet of mills and you get a large, self-renewing order stream that grows as the world grinds more copper, gold, iron and other ores — driven by decades-long demand for the metals of electrification and construction, not by any single miner's budget decision.

The device that only fits Tega: count the grams worn away, not the dollars of capex

Picture the wrong way to value Tega — as an equipment supplier whose fortunes swing with mining investment. Now picture the right way. Weigh what physically disappears inside the world's mills each year: the millimetres abraded off every liner, the steel balls ground down to nothing. Tega gets paid, in effect, by the gram of wear — and that wear is a near-constant function of tonnes ground, which barely flinches in a downturn. Its two flagship products, liners and (via Molycop) grinding media, are the two things being consumed in the very same tumbling action inside the very same drum. The company's revenue is not a bet on where the copper price goes next; it is a claim on how much rock keeps tumbling. That reframing — wear consumed, not capital spent — is the whole thesis, and it is one you could not paste onto an equipment-maker's page.

The steadiness is real, but it is not perfect insulation, and it is worth saying so plainly. If ore throughput itself falls — a mine closes, a grade declines so much that milling is cut, a region's production is curtailed — then even a consumable's demand shrinks with it. Tega's annuity is anchored to volume ground, which is far stickier than capex, but it is not immune to a genuine, sustained contraction in how much the world mines. The thesis is "stickier than a cyclical," not "not cyclical at all."

04

Metal → polymer: the DynaPrime share shift

The product story that lets Tega take share from far bigger incumbents.

For most of mining history, mill liners were made of cast metal — heavy steel plates, supplied by large metallurgical firms. Tega built its position on a different material: rubber and rubber-metal composite liners. Its premium composite product, DynaPrime, is the tip of the spear — and the reason a mid-sized Indian company can win business from mining giants that historically bought metal.

A composite liner is far lighter than a cast-metal one, so it is quicker and safer to install during a re-line — which means less mill downtime, the single most expensive thing in a mine. That operational edge, not just the part's price, is what wins the conversion.

The economics of a switch turn on downtime. When a mill is stopped for a re-line, the mine is not producing — every hour is lost metal. A composite liner is dramatically lighter than a metal one, so it is faster to lift into place and safer for the crew handling it, which shortens the shutdown. Add competitive wear life and the total value proposition can beat metal even where the sticker price is similar. Every mill that Tega converts from metal to composite is not a one-time win — it is a recurring customer captured, because that mill will now re-order composite liners on its re-line clock for years.

Why the conversion is a structural, not cyclical, tailwind

Set aside the mining cycle for a moment. Even in a flat market for ore, there is a slow, one-way migration happening under the surface: mills that have always run metal liners switching to composite, driven by safety rules, downtime maths and an ageing mine workforce that increasingly cannot manhandle heavy metal plates. That conversion is a share-shift growth engine independent of commodity prices — Tega can grow simply by taking metal-liner mills and turning them composite, one mill at a time, against incumbents whose whole product line is the thing being displaced. It is the closest thing this business has to a structural compounder buried inside a cyclical end-market.

05

Why a mill liner is so sticky: spec'd-in, service-led

The moat that a first-timer under-rates — and it is worth spelling out.

On paper, a liner is a lump of rubber and steel — surely easy to copy and price-shop. In practice it is one of the harder things in a mine to switch suppliers on, for a reason that has nothing to do with the part's materials and everything to do with what happens if it fails.

A mill liner is engineered to a specific mill and a specific ore. Its exact profile — the shape of the lifter bars, the thickness, the wear pattern — is designed so the mill grinds efficiently and the charge cascades correctly. Get that geometry wrong and grinding performance drops or the liner fails early; a failure can let the tumbling charge damage the mill shell, an event that can idle a mill for weeks. Because the downside of a bad liner is so severe and so expensive, a mine that has a liner design working well and proven over several re-line cycles is extremely reluctant to change it. The incumbent supplier has the wear data, the design history and the trust. A challenger has to persuade the mine to risk its most critical asset on an unproven part to save a small fraction of its costs — a trade few operations managers will take.

The moat in one sentence

Once Tega's liner is designed-in and performing in a mine's mill, switching away is not a purchasing decision — it is a risk decision, and the risk is a stopped mill. That is why this looks less like selling a commodity part and more like an embedded, service-led relationship: Tega monitors wear, tweaks the next design, and re-supplies on the clock. The consumable is sticky not because it is patented but because being wrong about it is catastrophic for the customer, and no one wants to find out the hard way.

Layer the geography on top and the moat deepens. Because a re-line cannot wait for a part on a slow boat, being able to design, manufacture and service near the mine — in Chile for Latin American copper, in South Africa and Australia for their ore bodies — is itself a barrier. A supplier with local plants, local engineers and a proven design is entrenched in a way a distant, cheaper bidder simply cannot dislodge on price alone.

06

A Kolkata company on four continents

The genuinely surprising shape of a niche global exporter.

Here is the fact that tends to catch people off guard: a company headquartered in Kolkata — not a city one associates with global mining technology — is a leading supplier of wear parts to copper mines in Chile, gold and platinum mines in Africa, and iron-ore operations in Australia. Tega earns the majority of its revenue outside India, selling into a concentrated set of the world's large miners, and it does so by putting its factories and people where the ore is.

In a business where a re-line cannot wait and where liner designs must be tuned to local ores, local presence is the product as much as the rubber is. It also, unavoidably, makes Tega a currency and geopolitics story: it bills in dollars, rands, pesos and Australian dollars, so exchange-rate swings move reported results, and its fortunes are tied to mining regions with their own political and regulatory weather.

A genuine surprise worth sitting with: the profit driver here is not a machine sold to a mine — it is a set of parts that get physically destroyed inside the mine's grinding mills and thrown away, again and again. Demand tracks how much rock the world grinds, not how much miners invest. And the company doing this at global scale, supplying wear parts to mines on four continents, is run out of Kolkata. Both facts tend to surprise even people who follow industrials closely.
07

The company, the family, and who controls it

A founder-built exporter, listed only in 2021, still firmly promoter-controlled.

Tega Industries is a Mohanka-family business, built over four decades from a Kolkata base into a global mining-consumables specialist, and taken public only recently. The founder, Madan Mohan Mohanka, started the company in the late 1970s; his son Mehul Mohanka runs it today as Managing Director and Vice Chairman. It remains, in the truest sense, a family-controlled compounder that happens to sell to the world.

Why a family firm ended up global before it was public

The sequence is unusual and revealing. Tega spent decades building plants in Chile, South Africa and Australia — planting itself next to the world's ore bodies — before it ever tapped public markets in 2021. That order of operations tells you the international footprint was earned the hard way, mine by mine and re-line by re-line, as a private, founder-run exporter rather than assembled with IPO cash. It also means the discipline that built a global niche leader on a modest balance sheet is now being tested against the very different demands of a large, debt-funded, transformational acquisition.

The listing context matters: Tega came to market only in December 2021, so its public track record is short. Investors are underwriting a business with a long private operating history but only a few years of listed disclosure — and now judging it through the lens of the largest, most transformative transaction of its life, executed early in that public life.
08

The Molycop bet: a minnow swallows a whale

The move that now dominates the story — and rewrites the balance sheet.

In late 2025 Tega agreed, in a consortium with Apollo funds, to acquire Molycop — one of the world's leading makers of grinding media, the steel and high-chrome balls consumed inside the very mills Tega already lines — at an enterprise value of roughly US$1.48 bn (approx.). For a company whose own standalone revenue was around ₹1,774 Cr, this is not a bolt-on. It is a bet several times Tega's own size, and it deserves to be understood on its own terms.

Strategically, the logic is elegant. Grinding media and mill liners are the two things destroyed in the same tumbling action inside the same drum. A mine's grinding department buys both, on the same throughput-driven clock, from what have historically been different suppliers. By adding Molycop, Tega goes from lining the mill to owning both of the mill's biggest consumables — deepening exactly the wear-part annuity described in Section 03, and cross-selling into a shared customer base of the world's large miners. If the consumables-annuity thesis is right, doubling down on it by acquiring the other half of the mill's wear stream is the natural, if audacious, move.

Financially, this is where the story stops being a quiet compounder and becomes a leveraged transformation. Tega is funding its portion through a combination of an equity raise of ~₹1,713 Cr and a new ~₹1,500 Cr term loan (with structuring alongside its consortium partner). The result: a company that entered this period lightly geared will carry materially higher consolidated debt, peaking around 4–4.5× net-debt/EBITDA at the FY27 high-water mark. Credit-rating agencies responded — ratings were placed on watch and revised around the deal — precisely because leverage of this order changes the risk profile of what used to be a conservative balance sheet.

The surprise — and the tension — in one place

The counter-intuitive fact at the centre of Tega today: this careful, family-run, cash-generative Kolkata consumables maker has taken on debt of a scale that would have been unthinkable a year earlier, to buy a company much larger than itself. It is the same wear-part thesis pushed to its logical extreme — owning both the liners and the media a mill eats — but executed with borrowed money and integration risk on a global scale. The prize is a far bigger, deeper annuity on world grinding throughput. The price is that the balance sheet, once the quiet strength of the story, is now the thing to watch. This is no longer only a "sticky consumable" story; from here it is a deleveraging-plus-integration story bolted onto a consumable one.

Watch this closely: the returns from Molycop depend on (1) integrating a much larger business without disrupting either company's customer relationships, (2) grinding-media volumes holding up with global ore throughput, and (3) the group paying debt down on the promised schedule. A deep or prolonged mining-throughput downturn would hurt far more with 4–4.5× leverage on the books than it would have on the old, lightly-geared Tega. Integration and deleveraging are now core to the thesis, not footnotes to it.
09

The industry and the rivals

A concentrated global market of mining-technology heavyweights.

Mining consumables and processing equipment is not a fragmented cottage market — it is contested by a handful of large, global mining-technology firms, because supplying mission-critical wear parts to the world's miners demands engineering depth, local presence and decades of proven performance. Tega competes as the focused specialist against much bigger, broader groups.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, Tega is the focused David among industrial Goliaths — Metso, FLSmidth and Weir are multiples of its size and can bundle equipment with consumables. Tega's answer is specialisation: be the best at composite liners and the deepest at service near the mine, and convert metal-liner mills one at a time. Second, the Molycop deal reshapes the map — in grinding media, Tega moves from bystander to a scaled global player overnight, going head-to-head with the likes of ME Elecmetal on the balls that tumble against everyone's liners. The competitive question is whether a specialist can keep out-servicing giants while simultaneously digesting a transformational acquisition.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A sticky consumables annuity, now leveraged into a much bigger one.

Tega enters this chapter as a proven, high-margin, globally-embedded mining-consumables leader that has just made the boldest move of its life. The debate is no longer only about how sticky a mill liner is — it is about whether a specialist can integrate a company larger than itself, pay down the debt it took on to buy it, and keep converting metal-liner mills to composite while the world keeps grinding ore. Give it the fair, unvarnished treatment.

The bull case

  • Throughput-linked annuity — ~84% consumables, consumed on a re-line clock tied to tonnes ground, not mining capex; far stickier than an equipment vendor.
  • Composite conversion runway — DynaPrime takes share from metal-liner incumbents, a structural growth engine that runs even in a flat commodity market.
  • Spec'd-in, service-led moat — a proven liner is a risk decision to switch, not a price decision; local plants near the ore deepen the lock-in.
  • Molycop scale & cross-sell — owning both the liners and the grinding media a mill eats deepens the annuity and creates a much larger, global consumables platform.
  • Founder alignment — a ~75% Mohanka stake and a long private operating history behind a niche the world's miners depend on.

The bear case

  • Leverage & integration risk — ~4–4.5× peak net-debt/EBITDA to digest a business larger than itself; deleveraging and integration must go to plan.
  • Mining-throughput exposure — stickier than capex, but a deep or prolonged fall in ore ground (closures, grade decline, curtailment) still cuts consumable demand — and bites harder with debt on the books.
  • Customer & geographic concentration + FX — a global exporter to a concentrated set of large miners, billing in multiple currencies exposed to swings.
  • Heavyweight competition — Metso, FLSmidth, Weir, ME Elecmetal/Bradken and Trelleborg are large and can bundle; composite expertise is contested, not unique.
  • Premium valuation, short public record — richly rated on standalone earnings, listed only since 2021, now judged through its largest-ever deal.
The bottom line

Tega spent four decades quietly perfecting the least glamorous thing in mining — the parts that get destroyed inside a grinding mill and thrown away — and turned it into a globally embedded, high-margin annuity on how much rock the world grinds. Then, early in its listed life, it pushed that same idea to its limit: borrowing heavily to buy Molycop and claim the grinding media that tumbles against its own liners, doubling down on the wear-part thesis with a bet larger than the company itself. Whether that was visionary or over-reach will not be settled by the elegance of the strategy but by a plain, checkable cadence — how fast consolidated net-debt/EBITDA falls from its ~4–4.5× peak toward the sub-2.5× goal, quarter after quarter, as consumables volumes feed the EBITDA that retires the debt. Watch that deleveraging print each period; it tells you whether the whale Tega swallowed becomes an annuity it owns, or a weight it carries.
