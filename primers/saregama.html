00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Saregama India is a music company that owns copyrights — not a manufacturer, not a broadcaster, not a cinema chain. Its core asset is a catalogue: the legal ownership of a very large body of recorded Indian music, reportedly more than 150,000 songs (approx.) across Hindi film music, regional-language repertoire, devotional, folk and classical, built up since the company made India's first commercial recordings at the start of the twentieth century. Whenever one of those recordings is played on a streaming app, set to a reel, used in a film scene, spun on radio, performed at an event or sold as a caller tune, somebody has to pay Saregama for permission. That permission fee is a royalty, and royalties on songs whose production costs were settled decades ago are close to pure profit.

Four facts frame the whole company:

  • The music business is the company; everything else is an appendage. In FY26 the music vertical did ~₹814 Cr of revenue (+17% YoY) and ~₹517 Cr of EBITDA (+22%) — an operating margin north of 60%. Consolidated revenue was ~₹984.6 Cr. That is not a coincidence of accounting; it is the shape of an intellectual-property business bolted onto some much weaker adjacent ventures.
  • Consolidated revenue fell in FY26 while the music business grew sharply. Group revenue slipped from ~₹1,171 Cr in FY25 to ~₹984.6 Cr — a ~16% decline — because the films-and-series slate was far smaller. Consolidated PAT was almost exactly flat at ~₹206.2 Cr against ~₹204.2 Cr. Reading only the headline, you would conclude a bad year. Reading the segment, you would conclude a very good one. Both numbers are true, and the gap between them is the first thing to understand about this stock.
  • It is controlled by the RP-Sanjiv Goenka Group. Promoter entities (principally Composure Services and Quest Capital Markets) hold roughly 59–61% (approx.). Saregama is the fifth RPG-family company in this library, alongside CEAT, PCBL, Firstsource and Zensar — the same house that sells tyres, carbon black, BPO services and IT consulting also owns the master recordings of a large slice of Indian film music.
  • It is spending heavily to buy the future. Management has guided to a content-investment budget of ~₹300–350 Cr for FY27 — money spent acquiring or commissioning new songs — and in January 2026 completed a ₹325 Cr minority investment in Bhansali Productions to lock in pre-agreed access to marquee Hindi film music. The stock trades near 44× earnings on a market value of ~₹8,930 Cr. Whether that spending earns its keep is the argument.
In plain terms

Two businesses live inside one ticker. The first is a rent-collecting estate: a library of old recordings, already paid for, that yields money every year without needing much done to it, and whose yield has been rising as India moved from pirated MP3s to paid apps. The second is an acquisition programme: a team going out each year and buying fresh songs — new film soundtracks, regional hits, independent releases — at today's competitive prices, because a catalogue that stops adding music slowly ages out of the playlists people actually stream. The first business is unambiguously excellent. The second is unproven at scale, costs ₹300 Cr-plus a year, and is where every rupee of incremental risk sits. Judging Saregama means judging the second while the first quietly pays the bills.

01

How a song actually earns money

Music-copyright economics in plain English — the five minutes that make the rest of this page obvious.

Most people assume a music label's job is to sell music. It hasn't been, for a long time. A modern label's job is to own rights and license them. Nobody buys a CD any more; instead, a dozen different businesses need permission to use a recording, and each of them pays for that permission. Understanding which permissions exist — and who else has a claim on the money — is the whole of music-industry economics.

Every song contains two separate copyrights

This trips up almost every first-time reader. A single song is legally two things stacked on top of each other:

  • The sound recording (the "master"). The specific recorded performance — that particular voice, orchestra and mix, frozen onto tape or a file. Labels like Saregama typically own masters outright, often having paid for the studio session in the first place. This is the asset that gets streamed.
  • The underlying composition (the "publishing" side). The tune and the lyric as abstract works, authored by the composer and lyricist. This can be owned separately from the master, and in India the Copyright (Amendment) Act, 2012 gave lyricists and composers a non-assignable right to share in royalties from many non-film uses — a genuine, permanent cost line that did not exist for recordings made before it.

So when a streaming platform pays out for a play, the money is split between whoever owns the master, whoever owns the composition, and the authors who carry statutory rights. Saregama's strength is that on a large part of its legacy repertoire it holds the master and acquired the rights under old, sweeping contracts — meaning far less of each rupee leaks away than on a song bought in 2026.

Why the cost structure is the point

A song recorded in 1974 has a fully sunk cost. The studio was paid, the musicians were paid, the tape was bought — half a century ago, in rupees that no longer matter. Everything that recording earns in 2026 arrives with almost no matching expense: no factory, no inventory, no delivery, barely any incremental staff. The only meaningful ongoing costs are digitisation, catalogue management, marketing to keep the songs discoverable, and whatever statutory royalties are payable. That is why the music vertical clears a ~63% EBITDA margin while the group as a whole clears roughly 41%. The library is not a good business because Saregama runs it cleverly. It is a good business because it was paid for in a currency and an era that will never bill again.

The one number that changed everything: monetisation per listener

For roughly two decades, India was a paradox for music owners — colossal listenership, negligible revenue. Cassettes were pirated, CDs were counterfeited, and the early internet turned Indian film music into free MP3s. A catalogue could be culturally dominant and financially near-worthless at the same time. What flipped it was not new music; it was the arrival of licensed, ad-funded and subscription streaming, which finally attached a payment rail to a play. The same catalogue that earned very little in 2010 earns materially more per year today because listening moved onto platforms that count plays and remit money.

The uncomfortable half of that story

India's recorded-music market remains, by global standards, tiny relative to its audience. Hundreds of millions of Indians stream music; only a small single-digit percentage (approx.) pay a subscription, against roughly half or more in mature Western markets, and Indian advertising rates per stream are a fraction of American ones. India sits well outside the world's top ten recorded-music markets despite being near the top by sheer listening volume. For Saregama, this cuts both ways: it is the reason growth can continue for years without any new listener being added — monetisation simply catching up — and it is the reason today's revenue base is small enough that a change in one platform's payout terms is felt immediately.

02

The catalogue: what Saregama actually owns

The asset that doesn't appear at anything like its worth on the balance sheet.

Saregama's library was assembled over more than a century, largely by being the only serious record company in India for the first several decades of the industry's existence. As The Gramophone Company of India — trading under the His Master's Voice (HMV) label, with its record-pressing plant at Dum Dum on the edge of Kolkata — it recorded the singers, film soundtracks and classical performances of an era when there was effectively nowhere else to record. The company has publicly described its holding as covering a very large share of Indian recorded music; a widely repeated figure is around half of all music ever recorded in India (approx., company-associated claim — treat with care), spanning 25-plus languages.

Why an old catalogue keeps earning

  • Familiarity compounds. A song that a hundred million people already know needs no marketing to be played. It shows up in nostalgia playlists, in wedding sets, in film flashbacks, in remixes, in reels. Streaming platforms discovered that back catalogue — music older than about eighteen months — accounts for the majority of all listening worldwide. India, with its extraordinarily deep film-music tradition, skews further that way than most markets.
  • Re-use begets re-use. Every time a producer recuts an old Saregama song for a new film, or a creator turns a hook into a viral audio, the original recording gets discovered by an audience that was not born when it was made. The asset is not just durable; it is periodically re-launched, for free, by third parties who pay for the privilege.
  • Regional depth is a hedge. Hindi is the biggest single pool, but the fastest growth in Indian streaming has come from regional-language listening. A catalogue that already contains decades of Bengali, Tamil, Telugu, Marathi and Punjabi recordings gets to participate in that shift without buying anything.
The quiet accounting oddity worth knowing

The legacy catalogue is barely visible on Saregama's balance sheet. Rights acquired decades ago at historical cost, and long since amortised, carry almost no book value — while the songs bought in the last five years sit there at full price as intangible assets being written down. So the balance sheet shows you the new content clearly and the old content hardly at all, which is precisely backwards from where the earnings come from. Any return-on-capital calculation for Saregama is therefore flattered by an asset the accounts have already forgotten. This is a general feature of catalogue businesses, and it is why the market values them off earnings and cash flow rather than off book value.

A structural detail most readers miss: copyright does not last forever. In India, protection for a sound recording runs for a fixed term — 60 years from publication (approx., per the Copyright Act) — after which the recording enters the public domain and anyone may use it without paying. Saregama's very oldest masters, from the shellac era, have therefore already aged out of protection, and each passing year retires another vintage. In practice this matters far less than it sounds, because commercial listening is heavily concentrated in music from the 1960s onward and expiry proceeds one year at a time — but it is a real, permanent leak at the back of the library that no amount of streaming growth reverses.
03

The company, the group, and who controls it

An RPG house asset, run out of Kolkata since before independence.

Saregama India Limited is headquartered at 33 Jessore Road, Dum Dum, Kolkata — the same address as the record factory the company built more than a century ago. It is controlled by the RP-Sanjiv Goenka Group (RPG), the Kolkata industrial house led by Sanjiv Goenka, which acquired the business from EMI in the mid-1980s. The label was renamed Saregama in 2000, after the first four notes of the Indian solfège — sa, re, ga, ma — retiring the HMV brand in India.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: RPG-affiliated entities, principally Composure Services Private Limited and Quest Capital Markets Limited, hold roughly 59–61% (approx.) of the equity. The remainder is public float, with institutional ownership including domestic mutual funds and foreign investors. A promoter block of this size means control is not in question and the free float is correspondingly modest — which contributes to how sharply the stock can move on earnings.
  • The RPG connection matters. This is the group behind CEAT (tyres), PCBL (carbon black), Firstsource (business-process services), Zensar (IT services) and CESC (power distribution). Saregama is by far the most unusual asset in that portfolio — a copyright estate sitting in a house otherwise built on manufacturing, utilities and services. It is also why the company's capital allocation reads more like a conservative industrial group's than a media start-up's: heavy net cash, dividends paid, acquisitions negotiated rather than auctioned wherever possible.
  • Balance sheet: Saregama runs effectively debt-free, in a net-cash position (approx.), funding its content purchases from internal accruals and its cash pile. That is unusual for a media company and is the single biggest reason it can keep buying music through a downturn while smaller labels cannot.
  • Subsidiaries & investments: Yoodlee Films (in-house film production), Pocket Aces (digital content, majority-acquired in 2022), Saregama Inc./international arms, and since January 2026 a minority stake in Bhansali Productions.
What "oldest music label in India" really buys you

Age is not sentiment here; it is a legal moat. Saregama's advantage is not that it is skilled at recording music — plenty of firms are — but that for the first several decades of Indian recorded sound, if you wanted to make a record, you very largely came to The Gramophone Company. That accident of history handed one company the masters to an era of music that can never be re-created, because the singers, orchestras and film composers who made it are gone. A competitor with unlimited capital can outbid Saregama for a 2026 soundtrack. It cannot manufacture a 1961 one. Everything durable about this business flows from that single asymmetry.

04

Five formats, one set of masters

The genuinely surprising thing about this company — and the best argument for the asset's durability.

Saregama began life pressing shellac discs for wind-up gramophones. It has since sold the same underlying recordings on vinyl, on cassette, on compact disc, as digital downloads and ringtones, and now as streams. Five complete technology generations have come and gone. Every one of them destroyed the businesses built around the physical format — the pressing plants, the distributors, the record shops. Not one of them destroyed the masters. The tape was re-transferred each time and sold again into a new machine.

The surprise, stated plainly

A company founded to sell wind-up gramophone records now makes most of its money from data centres streaming those same recordings to smartphones. The intervening 120 years wiped out shellac, vinyl, cassettes, CDs and downloads — five distinct industries, each of which looked permanent at the time — while the asset underneath them was never once obsoleted. That is the strongest evidence available that a music catalogue is a genuinely format-independent store of value: the delivery mechanism is disposable, the recording is not. It also reframes the technology risk investors usually worry about. The threat to Saregama is not the next format. It is whoever controls the pipe in that format, and what they choose to pay.

The four businesses, and how unequal they are

One profit engine, one volatile studio, one shrinking gadget, one small stage.

Saregama presents itself as a diversified entertainment company. Financially it is not: the music vertical produces essentially all of the group's profit, and the other three lines currently range from break-even to loss-making. Understanding what each one is for — and what it costs — is how you avoid mistaking a bad quarter at the film studio for a problem with the catalogue.

All four routes ultimately sell the same underlying thing — access to music Saregama controls. Only the first does so at catalogue margins.

1. Music — the licensing annuity

This is the business. It comprises catalogue licensing (the royalty streams described in section 01) plus the revenue Saregama earns from music it has newly acquired. In FY26 the vertical delivered ~₹814 Cr of revenue, up ~17%, and ~₹517 Cr of EBITDA, up ~22% — margin expanding as revenue grew, which is the signature of an asset with fixed costs and variable income. In the March 2026 quarter alone, music revenue was ~₹200.4 Cr, up ~19.2% YoY. Growth here comes from three places at once: more Indians streaming, better monetisation per stream, and more songs in the library.

2. Films & Series — the volatile studio

Yoodlee Films, started in 2017, makes deliberately low-budget, script-first films and sells them primarily to streaming platforms rather than releasing them theatrically. Pocket Aces — acquired majority in 2022, home to digital brands including Dice Media and FilterCopy — produces short-form and youth-facing content, and doubles as a promotional machine for Saregama's music. Management stated that Pocket Aces reached breakeven in FY26, completing a turnaround. But the segment's revenue is slate-dependent, and FY26 saw a much thinner slate than FY25: non-music revenue fell from roughly ₹475 Cr to ~₹171 Cr (both approx., derived from reported totals). That single swing explains almost the entire decline in consolidated revenue.

3. Carvaan — nostalgia, sold as an object

Launched in 2017, Carvaan is a portable player styled like a vintage radio, preloaded with thousands of legacy songs and requiring no internet connection or app. Its target buyer was explicit and clever: the older Indian listener who knew every song in Saregama's catalogue but had no intention of learning to use a streaming service. For several years it was a genuine hit — and a rare case of a rights owner monetising its own library offline, capturing the full retail price rather than a fraction of a stream. It is now in structural decline: ~55,000 units in Q4 FY26 against ~73,000 a year earlier, a continuation of a multi-year fall as smartphones reach the demographic Carvaan was built to serve. Management plans to extend the brand into live music — "Carvaan Live" sit-down concerts for older audiences — from FY27.

4. Live events & artist management

The smallest leg: representing artists, and staging events that monetise the catalogue on stage. It is strategically sensible — live music in India is growing quickly and artist management feeds the new-music pipeline — but it is not yet financially material.

The number that reorganises how you read this company

FY26 music EBITDA of ~₹517 Cr exceeded the group's entire adjusted EBITDA of ~₹405 Cr. Read that again: the music business earned roughly ₹112 Cr more than the whole company did. Everything outside music — the films, the series, the events, unallocated corporate cost — consumed that difference. The catalogue is not just Saregama's best business; it is currently subsidising every other thing the company does. That is not automatically damning, because Pocket Aces feeds music promotion and the film arm can generate soundtrack rights. But it does mean an investor buying Saregama is buying a superb annuity plus a portfolio of ventures that, on FY26 numbers, are a net drag — and paying one blended multiple for both.

06

The old catalogue versus the new content

The central financial question in this company — and it cannot be asked of any other stock in this library.

Saregama's earnings come from two pools of songs that behave completely differently. The legacy pool was acquired decades ago; its cost is gone, its royalty obligations are light, and every rupee it earns drops through. The new pool is being bought right now, at 2026 prices, in competition with the largest and best-funded music companies in India, and it carries continuing artist and composer royalties on every play. The company reports them together. The market values them together. They are not the same asset, and the entire investment case turns on the spread between them.

The free-carry, and what it hides

Here is the mechanic. Because the legacy catalogue costs nothing to keep and earns more every year as streaming monetisation improves, it generates a rising stream of profit with no capital attached — a free carry. That free carry is large enough to absorb a great deal of mediocre new investment before anyone notices. If Saregama spends ₹325 Cr on new songs that turn out to earn a poor return, the reported music line can still show growth, because the old library is growing underneath it. The damage would show up not as a decline, but as growth that is slower than the money spent to buy it.

The device: the content-payback ratio (spend ÷ incremental music revenue)

This single ratio is the most useful lens on Saregama, and it belongs to no other company. Divide the year's content investment by the year's increase in music-vertical revenue. In FY26 that is roughly ₹325 Cr ÷ ₹118 Cr ≈ 2.8× — meaning it takes something like three years of the incremental revenue to return the cash outlay, before the costs of servicing it, and before stripping out the portion of that increase which the old catalogue would have delivered anyway. Now hold the ratio against the alternative use of the money: the legacy library requires zero investment and yields a ~63% margin forever. New content must clear that bar, not merely be profitable. If the ratio falls year after year — more incremental revenue per rupee spent — Saregama is compounding its catalogue and deserves its multiple. If the ratio drifts upward, the company is renting growth at a rising price while the free carry conceals it. Everything else in the story is downstream of this one comparison.

Why Saregama spends anyway — the case for buying new music

  • Playlists decay. Streaming discovery favours current music. A catalogue that adds nothing steadily loses share of plays even if its absolute streams hold, and share of plays is precisely how platform pools are divided.
  • New listeners arrive with new tastes. The Indians coming online now were born long after most of Saregama's library was recorded. Owning some of what they actually listen to is the only way to be in their rotation at all.
  • Today's hit is tomorrow's catalogue. A song bought in 2026 is expensive because it is current; in 2046 it will be a cheap legacy asset with sunk costs. Buying new music is, in effect, planting the free carry of the future.
  • Regional bolt-ons are cheap. Acquisitions such as the Haryanvi catalogue from NAV Records buy whole regional repertoires at prices far below a marquee Bollywood soundtrack, and plug into the fastest-growing part of Indian streaming.

The Bhansali move: buying access instead of bidding for it

The ₹325 Cr minority investment in Bhansali Productions, completed in January 2026, is the clearest statement of Saregama's answer to the cost problem. Rather than competing at auction each time a major Hindi film's music comes to market — the mechanism that has driven soundtrack prices up — Saregama has taken a stake in a producer of exactly that kind of film, with pre-agreed access to the music that results. It is an attempt to convert a variable, competitively-bid cost into a fixed, negotiated one. Whether it works depends on things no spreadsheet settles in advance: how many films get made, whether their music connects, and what the pre-agreed terms actually are.

Watch the royalty line, not just the revenue line: Saregama's royalty expense in Q4 FY26 was around ₹34 Cr and rising. This is the cleanest visible fingerprint of the old-versus-new spread. Legacy recordings, acquired under sweeping old contracts, generate comparatively little outgoing royalty. Newly acquired songs come with continuing obligations to artists, composers and lyricists — so as the new pool grows as a share of plays, royalty expense grows faster than revenue. A rising royalty-to-music-revenue ratio is exactly what a shift toward lower-margin new content looks like in the accounts, quarter by quarter, before it ever shows up in the margin.
07

The platforms that pay — and how much leverage they have

Saregama's customers are a handful of very large technology companies. That is the structural risk.

A catalogue is only worth what someone will pay to license it, and in India the people paying are few and enormous. Spotify, JioSaavn (inside the Reliance-Disney media orbit), YouTube/Google, Apple Music, Amazon Music and Gaana together represent the overwhelming majority of licensed digital consumption. Add the broadcasters and telcos and you still have a customer list you could fit on one page. Saregama negotiates with each of them, periodically, over rates and minimum guarantees.

Where the leverage actually sits

Music licensing looks like a negotiation between equals, and mostly it isn't. A platform needs a catalogue, but no single Indian label's absence would empty its service — listeners would shift to whatever else is available. A label, by contrast, cannot be missing from the two or three services where most listening happens without losing a large share of its income immediately. That asymmetry is why Indian per-stream payouts sit far below Western levels and why consolidation among platforms is bad news for rights holders even when total listening rises. Saregama's counterweight is the irreplaceability of specific songs: a platform can survive without a label, but a film director who wants one particular 1970s number has exactly one place to go. That is why sync licensing carries better economics than bulk streaming, and why a catalogue with genuinely iconic recordings is worth more than one merely large.

The upside hiding in the same fact

Low Indian monetisation is the bear case and the bull case simultaneously. Because the revenue per listener is so far below global norms, Saregama does not need a single new listener to grow — it needs the existing ones to be worth slightly more. Paid-subscription conversion moving from low single digits toward even the low teens, or Indian digital advertising rates continuing to rise, would lift the entire pool that gets split among rights holders, with no additional cost to Saregama whatsoever. That is the mechanism behind the music vertical compounding at high-teens growth with expanding margins. It is also entirely outside the company's control — it depends on Indian consumers deciding to pay for music, on platforms deciding to charge them, and on advertisers deciding what an Indian listener is worth.

The industry and the rivals

A concentrated label market where one competitor is very much larger.

Indian recorded music is not a fragmented cottage industry; a handful of labels control most commercially valuable repertoire. Saregama's position is distinctive — it has the deepest historic library, but it is not the largest label by current market share, and the gap in new music is wide.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two separate contests are running at once, and Saregama's standing in them is opposite. In the market for old music it has no real rival — nobody else can supply a 1958 recording, and there is nothing to compete for because the rights were settled generations ago. In the market for new music it is a challenger, bidding against T-Series' scale in Bollywood and against two global majors with worldwide balance sheets. This is why Saregama's competitive advantage is unusually easy to describe and unusually hard to extend: it is unassailable in the past tense and contested in the present tense. The Bhansali investment, and the pattern of buying regional catalogues rather than marquee Hindi ones, are both attempts to compete where the bidding is less crowded.

Financials & valuation

A high-margin annuity, an unproven investment programme, and a multiple that assumes the first pays for the second.

At roughly ₹8,930 Cr of market value against ~₹206 Cr of FY26 profit, Saregama trades near 44× trailing earnings, with a modest dividend yield of about 1% and a balance sheet carrying effectively no debt. On adjusted EBITDA of ~₹404.7 Cr that is roughly 22× EV/EBITDA (approx., before adjusting for net cash). This is a premium rating in absolute terms, and defenders of it point at the margin structure rather than at the growth rate.

The one number that settles the thesis: the content-payback ratio

At ~44× earnings, Saregama is priced as a compounding intellectual-property annuity, not as a media company with a lumpy film slate. So the figure to track is not revenue, not PAT, and certainly not the consolidated top line that fell 16% in a year the business improved. It is content investment divided by the increase in music-vertical revenue — the FY26 reading of roughly ₹325 Cr against ~₹118 Cr, or about 2.8× — watched alongside two supporting gauges: the music EBITDA margin (currently ~63%; new content is structurally lower-margin, so a slide here is the first warning that the mix is shifting the wrong way) and the royalty expense as a share of music revenue (~₹34 Cr in Q4 FY26 and rising). If music revenue keeps compounding in the high teens while the payback ratio compresses and the margin holds near 60%, Saregama is buying growth cheaply and the multiple is defensible arithmetic. If music growth decelerates toward ten percent while the content budget stays at ₹300–350 Cr, the company is paying more each year for less, and the legacy free carry will hide it for exactly as long as it takes for the margin to start slipping.

Two further points on valuation. First, the comparison most often made is with Tips Music, a smaller listed pure-play whose margins are higher because it carries almost none of Saregama's non-music baggage — which means the premium the market awards catalogue purity is directly observable, and it is the discount Saregama pays for owning a film studio. Second, the reported P/E understates the cash economics somewhat: amortisation of acquired music rights is a real cost but a non-cash one in the year it is charged, so cash generation runs ahead of accounting profit. That argues for looking at operating cash flow after content spend — free cash flow properly defined for a catalogue business — rather than at the earnings multiple alone.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

An irreplaceable asset, a rising bill for the next one, and a market rate set by other people.

Saregama enters FY27 with the strongest catalogue in Indian music, a music vertical compounding in the high teens at sixty-plus percent margins, no debt, and a record fourth quarter behind it. It also enters with a ₹300–350 Cr annual cheque to write for content it does not yet own, in a market where the biggest bidder is not listed and does not have to explain its margins to anyone. The honest tension in this stock is not whether the library is valuable — it plainly is — but whether the company can keep replenishing it at a price that beats simply harvesting what it already has.

The bull case

  • An asset that cannot be rebuilt — a century of masters, including music whose performers are long gone. No amount of capital re-creates it.
  • Annuity economics — ~63% music EBITDA margin on revenue that recurs without reinvestment; costs sunk decades ago, income indexed to India going online.
  • Monetisation runway — Indian paid-subscription conversion and ad rates remain far below global levels; the pool being split can grow for years with no new listeners.
  • Mix is improving — music rose from ~59% to ~83% of consolidated revenue in FY26 as the loss-making film slate shrank; Pocket Aces reached breakeven.
  • Balance-sheet freedom — net cash and promoter backing let Saregama buy catalogues when weaker labels are forced sellers, and negotiate access deals like Bhansali rather than bid at auction.

The bear case

  • Return on new content unproven — ~₹325 Cr a year against ~₹118 Cr of incremental music revenue; if new songs underperform, the legacy free carry masks the destruction for years.
  • Platform concentration — a handful of counterparties (YouTube, JioSaavn, Spotify) set the rate; Indian per-stream payouts are low and platform consolidation weakens label leverage further.
  • Competition for new music — T-Series' dominance in current Bollywood, plus Sony and Universal, bids up acquisition costs Saregama must match to stay current.
  • Non-music drag — films & series is slate-driven and consumed ~₹112 Cr of group EBITDA in FY26; Carvaan is in structural decline (55k units in Q4 FY26 vs 73k).
  • Copyright, piracy & regulation — the 60-year term retires the oldest masters, the 2012 Act's statutory author royalties are a permanent cost, and enforcement against unlicensed use is never complete.
  • Rich multiple on flat PAT — ~44× earnings against consolidated profit that did not grow in FY26, with a modest free float that amplifies moves in both directions.
What to watch through FY27

Four checkable things, in order of importance. One: whether music-vertical revenue clears roughly ₹950 Cr — high-teens growth off the ₹814 Cr base — because that is the pace the multiple assumes. Two: whether the music EBITDA margin holds near 60% as newly-bought, royalty-bearing content grows as a share of plays. Three: whether the ₹300–350 Cr content budget is actually spent, and what it buys — regional catalogues at sensible prices, or marquee soundtracks at auction prices. Four: the first Bhansali-linked soundtracks reaching the catalogue, which is the only way to find out whether ₹325 Cr bought genuine access or an expensive option on somebody else's release schedule.

The bottom line

Saregama collects rent on a hundred years of Indian music, and that rent is rising because the country finally built a way to pay for songs it was already listening to. Nothing about the legacy library is in doubt: it is the closest thing in Indian equities to a piece of cultural infrastructure, and it earns like one. The whole argument sits in the second half of the business — a company that spends ₹325 Cr a year to add songs it does not have, into a market where the largest buyer answers to no public shareholders. The way to keep score is not the headline revenue line, which fell 16% in a year the company got better, but the ratio of that content cheque to the extra music revenue it produces. Saregama has already proved it owns the past. FY27's music-vertical print, set against the money spent to extend the catalogue, is where it starts proving it can afford the future.
