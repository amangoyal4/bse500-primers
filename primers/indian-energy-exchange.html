Indian Energy Exchange, a toll booth the regulator is redesigning

A complete walk through India's dominant power exchange — how electricity gets auctioned by the day and by the quarter-hour, how a company that owns no plants and no wires earns roughly four paise on every unit it matches, why that produces ~86% EBITDA margins, and why a single draft regulation called market coupling can dissolve a 90% market share without anyone building a competing exchange.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Indian Energy Exchange runs the electronic marketplace where Indian electricity is bought and sold on short notice. It is not a power producer. It owns no generating station, no transmission line, no meter. What it owns is an auction engine: every day, thousands of sellers (generators with spare output) and buyers (state distribution companies, factories, data centres, traders) submit price-and-quantity bids into IEX's system, the system matches them, and a single market-clearing price pops out for each 15-minute block of the coming day. IEX charges a tiny fee on each unit matched — currently around two paise per kilowatt-hour from each side — and that fee, multiplied by an enormous volume, is essentially the whole business.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • It is a volume toll with extraordinary economics. In FY26 IEX matched a record 141.1 billion units (BU) of electricity, up ~17% on the year, and turned that into consolidated revenue of about ₹747 Cr and PAT of about ₹492.9 Cr, at an EBITDA margin of roughly 85.7%. The platform cost is largely fixed; an extra billion units of trade costs almost nothing to process, so incremental volume drops nearly whole to the bottom line. The company carries no meaningful debt and pays most of its profit out (a ₹2 final dividend for FY26, on a ₹1 face value).
  • Its dominance is built on liquidity, not licences. Three power exchanges are licensed in India. IEX has historically held ~84–90% of exchange-traded electricity volume (about 83% in 9MFY26, and ~98.8% in the flagship Day-Ahead Market) for one self-reinforcing reason: traders go where the other traders are, because a deeper order book gives you a better fill and a fairer price. Nobody has out-competed that.
  • A draft regulation attacks exactly that. The Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC) has moved to couple the exchanges: pool every exchange's bids into one common order book, compute one national clearing price centrally (with Grid India designated Market Coupling Operator), then hand the matched trades back to each exchange. If bids are pooled, the liquidity advantage stops being an advantage — a bid placed on a small rival clears at the same price against the same national book. The Draft CERC (Power Market) (Second Amendment) Regulations, 2026 put DAM and RTM first in line. The stock has de-rated hard on it: roughly ₹121 in July 2026, down about 40% over a year, at a P/E in the low-20s for a business earning ~86% operating margins.
In plain terms

Picture a toll plaza on the busiest road into the city. IEX built it, everyone uses it, and it takes a couple of paise per vehicle-kilometre without owning a single truck or metre of tarmac — which is why almost nine rupees in ten of its revenue survives to operating profit. The peculiarity of this particular plaza is that its monopoly does not come from the tarmac. It comes from habit: drivers use it because other drivers use it. The government has now proposed a scheme in which every toll plaza in the country pools its queue into one national queue, and the price is set centrally. The plaza still exists, the traffic still flows, but the reason everybody chose this plaza has been legislated out of existence.

01

How India actually sells electricity

The one page of background that makes the entire business legible.

Electricity is a strange commodity. It cannot be stored cheaply at scale, and supply must match demand second by second or the grid's frequency drifts and things break. So the trade in electricity is really a trade in promises to deliver power at a specific time. India organises those promises in two very different ways.

The long-term world: PPAs

Most Indian electricity — the large majority of units consumed — moves under Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs): long-term contracts, often 20 or 25 years, between a generator and a state distribution company (DISCOM), at a tariff fixed or formula-linked in advance. A DISCOM is the utility that actually sells power to your home; it is usually state-owned and, notoriously, often financially stretched. PPAs give both sides certainty: the generator gets bankable revenue to finance the plant, the DISCOM gets guaranteed supply.

PPAs also make the system rigid. A DISCOM contracted for a fixed block of coal power has to pay fixed charges whether or not it takes the electricity. When demand is soft, or when a monsoon afternoon floods the system with cheap solar, that contracted power can be more expensive than what someone else is willing to sell right now. And when demand spikes — a heatwave, a festival evening — the DISCOM may be short and need power it never contracted for.

The short-term world: the market

That is what the short-term market is for. It is the residual, flexible slice: power bought and sold for tomorrow, or for the next hour, or for the next 15 minutes. It has three channels — bilateral deals brokered by traders, deviation settlement with the grid, and the power exchanges. The exchanges are the transparent, anonymous, auction-based channel, and IEX is by far the largest of them.

Why a spot market for power exists at all

Two plants can produce the identical kilowatt-hour at wildly different costs — an old coal unit burning imported fuel versus a hydro station with a full reservoir versus a solar farm at noon whose marginal cost is roughly zero. Without a market, each DISCOM would be locked into whatever it contracted for years ago, and cheap surplus power in Karnataka would sit idle while Bihar burned expensive fuel. The exchange lets the cheapest available electron in the country find the buyer who values it most, in a 15-minute window, at a price everyone can see. The efficiency gain is real and measurable — and IEX's fee is a small share of that gain.

02

What IEX actually is

An auction engine, a clearing guarantee, and a rulebook — nothing else.

Strip IEX to its parts and there are three. A matching engine that runs closed double-sided auctions. A settlement layer that takes money from buyers and pays sellers, with margin collected up front so no participant carries the other's credit risk. And a membership network — over 8,000 registered participants across generators, DISCOMs, open-access consumers and traders, spread across every state. That is the entire asset base, and none of it is physical.

Note what IEX never does: it never buys the electricity, never takes a position, never owns a wire. It has no inventory risk and no fuel cost — which is why its margin looks like a software company's and its balance sheet looks like a cash box.

The four things IEX sells, in order of importance

  • Price discovery. A transparent, auditable national reference price for electricity, published block by block. Even parties who never trade on IEX use its price as a benchmark.
  • Counterparty safety. A seller on IEX does not have to assess whether a particular DISCOM will pay. IEX collects margin and guarantees settlement — a meaningful service in a sector where receivables from distribution utilities have historically been a chronic problem.
  • Immediacy. With the Real-Time Market, a buyer can source power an hour before delivery. That is impossible bilaterally at any scale, and it is the fastest-growing thing IEX does.
  • Anonymity and standardisation. No negotiation, no relationship, no bespoke contract. A standard product, a standard clearing process, a standard fee.
The clearing guarantee is the underrated bit

A generator selling into the exchange gets paid on a fixed cycle regardless of which DISCOM happened to be on the other side of the trade. India's distribution utilities collectively owe generators very large sums under long-term contracts; on the exchange, that problem largely does not arise, because payment is pre-secured. For a merchant generator, "I will definitely be paid, on day X" is worth real money relative to a bilateral contract with a stressed state utility. This is one reason exchange volumes have kept growing even in years when market prices fell.

03

The products, decoded

DAM, RTM, TAM, Green, RECs — five names, one underlying trade.

All of IEX's products do the same thing — match a seller to a buyer for a defined delivery window — and differ only in how far ahead that window sits and what kind of electricity is being traded. Get the ladder of time horizons straight and the segment mix reads itself.

Why the Real-Time Market grew fastest

Solar output collapses when a cloud passes; wind dies at dusk. A grid that used to be scheduled by slow, predictable coal now has tens of gigawatts of weather-dependent supply, and every forecasting error becomes a short-notice imbalance somebody has to cover. The Real-Time Market is the settlement valve for that error. So RTM's 41% growth in FY26 is not really a product success story — it is the measurable financial shadow of India's renewable build-out. The more variable the generation mix becomes, the more the country has to trade near delivery, whether or not anyone intends to.

Volume up, prices down — and that's fine: the DAM clearing price fell to ~₹3.86/unit in FY26 from ₹4.47, and RTM to ~₹3.59 from ₹4.28. For most commodity businesses a 14% price fall would be a disaster. For IEX it is close to irrelevant at the revenue line and helpful at the volume line: the fee is charged per unit, not as a percentage of value, and cheaper market power pulls more DISCOMs off expensive contracted supply and onto the exchange. IEX is short the electricity price in sentiment and neutral to it in economics.
04

The paise-per-unit toll: doing the arithmetic

Where an 85% margin actually comes from — and how small the take really is.

The single most clarifying exercise for this company is to divide its revenue by its volume. FY26 standalone revenue of roughly ₹608 Cr against 141.1 BU — that is 141.1 billion kilowatt-hours — works out to about 4.3 paise of revenue per unit traded. That is the whole toll: two paise from the buyer, two from the seller, a rounding of other fees on top.

Now set that against the value of the thing being tolled. In FY26 the average DAM clearing price was about ₹3.86 per unit. So IEX's take is roughly 1.1% of the value of the electricity that crosses its platform — and that is the gross take, before any cost. On a ₹100 basket of power, IEX keeps a bit more than a rupee and hands over the rest.

Where operating leverage comes from

The cost side is the mirror image. IEX's expenses are, in substance: a technology platform and its maintenance, a few hundred employees, compliance and regulatory costs, and marketing to bring new members on. Almost all of it is fixed. Matching one more gigawatt-hour requires no additional server rack of consequence, no additional salesperson, no additional rupee of working capital. So the incremental EBITDA margin on new volume is close to 100%, and the reported margin has ratcheted up as volume has grown — 85.7% in FY26 against 85.3% in FY25.

The uncomfortable flip side of operating leverage

Fixed costs cut both ways, and IEX's investors do not usually say the second half out loud. If market coupling or a fee revision took, say, a third off revenue, almost none of the cost base would go away with it — the platform, the compliance team and the licence obligations all still have to be paid for. A third off the top line would therefore take considerably more than a third off profit. The same structure that makes every extra billion units so lucrative makes every lost billion units disproportionately painful. The leverage does not care which direction the volume moves.

05

The company, the group, and who owns it

A rare Indian large-cap with no promoter at all.

IEX was set up in 2008 as India's first power exchange, backed initially by Financial Technologies and PTC India, and began trading in June of that year. It listed in October 2017 at ₹1,500 a share on the BSE. What is unusual is where the ownership ended up: the original sponsors sold down over the years and IEX today is a professionally managed company with essentially no promoter holding — the entire register is public shareholders, institutions and retail (approx., verify against the latest shareholding filing). There is no controlling family, no parent group, and no promoter pledge to worry about.

The group beyond electricity

  • Indian Gas Exchange (IGX) — a subsidiary doing the same thing for natural gas. FY26 volume of 76.8 million MMBtu, up 28%, with PAT of ₹41.9 Cr, up 35%. Small in absolute terms but growing faster than the parent, and structurally exposed to India's gas-market liberalisation rather than to power-market rules.
  • International Carbon Exchange (ICX) — the newest venture, trading renewable-energy certificates internationally. FY26 revenue up 126% off a tiny base, with about 179 lakh I-RECs issued. Optionality, not earnings.
  • Indian Coal Exchange — a wholly-owned subsidiary incorporated on 1 June 2026 to build an exchange for coal. Too new to assess; it signals the strategic response to coupling: replicate the platform playbook in commodities where no one is proposing to pool the order book.
What promoter-less ownership actually changes

Two things, in opposite directions. On the good side, there is no controlling shareholder extracting value, no related-party web, no succession question — capital allocation has been unusually shareholder-friendly, with steady dividends and buybacks rather than empire-building. On the awkward side, when a regulator proposes something existential, there is no deep-pocketed parent to absorb the blow or fund a pivot, and no promoter with the standing to lobby as an owner. IEX faces its regulator as a widely-held, professionally-run licensee — which is exactly the position from which a rule change is hardest to resist.

06

The liquidity moat: why 90% share was never really contested

How exchange dominance builds itself — and why it cannot be attacked head-on.

India has licensed three power exchanges. Two of them — PXIL (Power Exchange India, the older rival) and HPX (Hindustan Power Exchange, launched 2022 with PTC, BSE and ICICI Bank behind it) — have the same regulatory permission, the same technology available, the same access to the same grid. Neither has been able to take meaningful share. IEX has run at roughly 84–90% of exchange-traded electricity volume for years, and about 98.8% in the Day-Ahead Market specifically. That gap is not explained by better software.

The mechanism, step by step

  • A deeper book gives a better price. If you are a DISCOM buying 200 MW for tomorrow evening, you want the exchange where the most sellers are bidding, because more competing sellers means a lower clearing price and a higher chance your full quantity fills.
  • A seller wants the same thing in reverse. A generator with 100 MW of surplus wants the venue with the most buyers. Both sides of the trade are pulled toward the same, largest venue.
  • Which makes the largest venue larger. Every trader who joins IEX because it is deepest makes it deeper still, and makes the alternative shallower by comparison. This is a positive feedback loop, and it does not reverse on its own.
  • Splitting your order is costly. A participant who bids half on IEX and half on a rival risks clearing at two different prices, or clearing on one venue and not the other. So people do not split; they consolidate on the deepest book.
  • The fee is too small to be the deciding factor. At two paise a unit, undercutting IEX by even half a paise saves a buyer a fraction of a percent of the power bill — nowhere near enough to compensate for a worse fill on a thin book.

The genuine surprise

This is a near-monopoly whose moat is not an asset. A cement company's moat is a limestone lease; a railway's is a right of way; a bank's is a deposit franchise. You cannot legislate any of those out of existence — you would have to buy them or build alongside them. IEX's moat is the collective behaviour of its participants, which exists only because the rules permit each exchange to run its own auction. Change that one rule and the moat does not shrink, decay or get out-competed. It simply stops applying, on the day the rule takes effect, with no capital destroyed and no competitor having built anything. There are very few businesses of this quality anywhere whose entire competitive position sits inside a regulation that can be amended by a commission.

07

Market coupling: the rule that dissolves the moat

The single most important thing to understand about this stock. Read this section twice.

Today, each of India's three exchanges runs its own auction on its own order book and arrives at its own clearing price. Market coupling replaces that with one national auction. Every exchange collects bids as before — but instead of matching them internally, it forwards them to a central Market Coupling Operator (MCO), which pools all bids from all exchanges into a single order book, computes one uniform market-clearing price for the country, allocates the matched trades back to each exchange, and lets each exchange settle its own members. The Draft CERC (Power Market) (Second Amendment) Regulations, 2026 designate Grid Controller of India (Grid India) — the national system operator — as that MCO.

The critical consequence sits in step 2: once the book is national, a bid placed on the smallest exchange clears against exactly the same liquidity, at exactly the same price, as a bid placed on the largest. The reason to choose IEX evaporates.

Read that table carefully, because it explains why the debate is genuinely two-sided. Coupling does not abolish IEX or confiscate its business. Members still have to be onboarded, margined, settled and served by some exchange, and IEX has 8,000-plus of them, eighteen years of operating record, and a technology stack the others are still catching up with. What coupling removes is the reason a new member would pick IEX over a rival. That converts a network-effect monopoly into an ordinary services business competing on price and service — and price competition, in a business whose entire revenue is a two-paise fee, is not a comfortable place to be.

Why the regulator wants this

Coupling is not a punishment aimed at IEX; it is standard practice in Europe, where national power markets were coupled precisely to stop electricity being mispriced across fragmented venues. The case for it is real: one national price is economically more efficient than three, it eliminates arbitrage between exchanges, it lets the system operator manage transmission congestion against the full national supply-demand picture rather than one exchange's slice, and it removes the awkwardness of a single private company effectively setting the reference price for a nationally strategic commodity. From CERC's chair, the fact that coupling also dismantles a private near-monopoly is a feature of the design, not a side effect of it.

Where the process actually stands

  • The draft is out. CERC issued the Draft (Power Market) (Second Amendment) Regulations, 2026, and invited stakeholder comments to 16 May 2026. This followed an earlier, deferred attempt at the same reform a couple of years before, which had drawn substantial industry pushback.
  • Grid India is the designated MCO — the state-owned national load despatch operator, not a private or rotating operator.
  • Sequencing: coupling is to be implemented in DAM first, then RTM, with the Commission retaining flexibility on rollout timing for each. Those two segments are roughly 73% of IEX's FY26 volume, and DAM alone is the segment where its share is near-total.
  • Timing: Grid India is to formulate the Power Market Coupling Procedure within six months of the amendments being notified. So the operative clock has not started until final notification — the date to watch is notification, not the draft.
  • The sell-side view, for what it is worth: published estimates have suggested IEX's share could fall from around 85% today toward roughly 50% by FY28 (analyst projection — treat as an assumption, not a fact). Nobody knows, because nothing comparable has been done here before.
The honest framing: this is not a headline risk that will pass. It is a live regulatory process with a named operator, a named sequence and a defined procedural clock, aimed squarely at the mechanism that produces IEX's market share. The reasonable disagreement is about magnitude and speed — how fast members actually migrate once execution is identical everywhere, and whether fee competition follows immediately or takes years — not about whether the mechanism works as described.
The counter-argument worth taking seriously

Europe's experience with coupled power markets is that the incumbent exchange usually keeps most of its volume for years afterwards. Switching an exchange membership is not free: a member must re-paper contracts, re-establish margin arrangements, connect to a different system, and satisfy itself about a smaller counterparty's settlement robustness. Meanwhile the incumbent's costs are already sunk while a challenger must spend to win each account, and at four paise of gross revenue per unit there is not much of a purse to fight over. It is entirely possible that coupling arrives, the price advantage disappears, and IEX still holds a large majority of volume five years later — because inertia, service and settlement quality turn out to matter more than anyone modelled. That is the bull case in a single paragraph, and it is not a silly one.

08

The other regulated variable: the fee ceiling

Coupling attacks the volume share. This attacks the price.

IEX does not set its own price. CERC caps what a power exchange may charge, and the current ceiling is around 2 paise per unit per side — roughly 4 paise all-in on a completed trade. That cap has been the effective price for years. Separately from coupling, the regulator has been reported to be considering a move to a fixed fee of around 1.5 paise per unit, with term-ahead contracts possibly lower still at around 1.25 paise (reported proposals — treat as approx. until formally notified).

Because transaction fees are the overwhelming majority of IEX's revenue, the arithmetic is unforgiving and simple. A move from 2 paise to 1.5 paise is a 25% cut to the revenue line on unchanged volume. Against a cost base that barely moves, that flows through to profit at more than one-for-one. Reported estimates of a roughly quarter-sized hit to earnings from such a change are, structurally, about right.

Nothing about the company changes across these three bars — same volume, same members, same platform, same costs. The entire difference is a number written in a regulation.
Two threats, one root

It is tempting to treat coupling and the fee ceiling as separate risks, but they are the same risk expressed twice. IEX's revenue is volume share × regulated price, and CERC holds the pen on both terms. Coupling puts the first term into play by removing the reason members concentrate on one venue; a fee revision puts the second into play directly. A company can hedge a commodity, diversify a customer, or refinance a debt. There is no hedge against your regulator holding both variables in your revenue equation — which is why the stock's multiple has compressed even while its volumes were setting records.

09

The volume runway: the part that is genuinely growing

Whatever happens to share and fee, the pie itself is small and expanding.

Set the regulatory question aside for a moment and look at the market IEX operates in. India generates on the order of 1,800–1,900 billion units of electricity a year. Total exchange-traded volume, across all three exchanges and all products, runs at a small fraction of that — IEX's own 141.1 BU is somewhere under a tenth of national consumption (approx.). In mature electricity markets abroad, the share of consumption that changes hands on short-term and spot markets is very substantially higher. That gap is the runway, and several forces are widening it.

  • Demand growth itself. Indian power demand grows at a mid-single-digit pace in a normal year and faster in a hot one. Even at a constant market share, a larger pie means more units to trade — and the short-term market is the residual, so it flexes more than proportionally when demand surprises.
  • The structural shift away from PPAs. DISCOMs increasingly find that buying on the exchange at ₹3.86 beats paying fixed charges on old, expensive contracted coal. Every megawatt that moves from a long-term contract to the market is new addressable volume — and the migration is sticky once a utility's procurement team learns to use it.
  • Renewables force short-term trade. A grid with tens of gigawatts of solar and wind is a grid that cannot be scheduled accurately a year in advance. That variability has to be resolved somewhere, and the day-ahead and real-time markets are where. RTM's 41% growth in FY26 is the visible edge of this.
  • Open-access C&I buyers. Factories, cement and steel plants, commercial complexes and — increasingly — data centres buying power directly rather than from their DISCOM. Each new open-access consumer is a permanent new exchange member, and this is the class that grows fastest as green-power mandates tighten.
  • Green obligations. Renewable purchase obligations force obligated entities to buy either green power or RECs. IEX runs the venue for both. FY26 saw record REC trade of 187.2 lakh certificates and green-market volume of 10.78 BU (+23%).
  • New commodities. Gas through IGX (76.8 mn MMBtu, +28% in FY26), carbon and I-RECs through ICX, and now the newly incorporated Indian Coal Exchange. None is material to profit yet; collectively they are the platform being pointed at markets where nobody has proposed pooling the order book.

A distinction worth holding onto

Coupling threatens IEX's slice. It does nothing to the pie — arguably it enlarges it, since a single national price is a cleaner, more trustworthy signal and should pull more volume onto exchanges generally. So the two forces run in opposite directions and on different clocks: the market's growth is a slow, demographic, decade-long compounding, while a share loss would be a step change concentrated in a few years after notification. An investor is really being asked whether a growing pie multiplied by a shrinking, uncertain slice compounds or shrinks. That is not a question anyone can answer from a spreadsheet today; it will be answered by monthly volume disclosures after coupling goes live.

10

Rivals, and the market-infrastructure family

Who competes, and what IEX looks like next to India's other listed plumbing.

IEX's direct competitors are small and, until now, structurally disadvantaged. Its closest analogues, however, are not power companies at all — they are the listed market-infrastructure businesses that toll India's financial system.

What IEX shares with CDSL, CAMS and the stock exchanges

The family resemblance is strong: asset-light, regulator-licensed, tiny per-transaction fees, extreme operating leverage, net cash, high payouts, and returns on capital that look implausible until you notice there is almost no capital employed. A depository like CDSL clips a few rupees per transaction on securities it does not own; a registrar like CAMS earns basis points on assets it does not manage; IEX earns paise on electricity it never touches. All of them are, in essence, taxes on activity in a regulated system.

The difference is the concentration of regulatory exposure. A depository's economics rest on a broad framework covering many products and many participants; a stock exchange's franchise is diversified across cash equities, derivatives, listings, data and clearing. IEX's economics rest on one commodity, one regulator, two variables (share and fee), and a mechanism that regulator has explicitly proposed to change. When comparing IEX's multiple to CDSL's or BSE's, that is the difference being priced.

The comparison that actually matters

Ask which of India's market-infrastructure monopolies could be neutralised by a single notification, with no competitor spending a rupee on capital expenditure and no customer changing their behaviour voluntarily. For a depository, a registrar or a securities exchange, the answer is that it would take a sweeping structural reform touching many market participants — the sort of thing that takes a decade and dies in consultation. For IEX, the answer is a two-page amendment naming Grid India as coupling operator. Same business model, radically different fragility. That is the whole reason IEX trades where it does relative to its cousins.

Financials & valuation

A pristine set of numbers, priced as if they will not last.

FY26 was, on the operating numbers, the best year IEX has ever had. Consolidated revenue of about ₹747 Cr was up 13.6%; consolidated PAT of about ₹492.9 Cr was up 14.9%; standalone PAT of about ₹473.7 Cr was up 14.2%. EBITDA margin improved to roughly 85.69% from 85.29%. Volume set a record at 141.1 BU, and Q4 alone did 39.4 BU with PAT of about ₹130 Cr, up 11%. The board recommended a final dividend of ₹2 per share (200% of the ₹1 face value).

What the multiple is saying

A business with an 86% operating margin, a net-cash balance sheet, a ~30%-plus return on equity, 17% volume growth and a record year, trading at roughly 22 times earnings, is not being valued on its historical economics. It is being valued on a probability-weighted view of what its economics look like after CERC finishes writing. The de-rating from the 40-times-plus multiple of a year ago is almost entirely a regulatory re-pricing, not an operating disappointment — the operations were, if anything, better than expected throughout.

Which means the valuation debate is unusually clean, and unusually binary in structure. It is not a debate about growth rates or margin trajectories or capital allocation. Those are all settled and all good. It is a debate about a single forthcoming set of rules and how the market behaves after they land.

The one metric that settles this

Not revenue, not margin, not PAT. Watch IEX's monthly share of exchange-traded electricity volume in the first four to six quarters after coupling is notified and goes live — and read it alongside realised fee per unit (revenue ÷ BU, which you can compute yourself each quarter). Volume growth alone will look fine for a while regardless, because the market is expanding and DISCOMs keep migrating off PPAs. The share number is the only figure that distinguishes "the moat was liquidity and the liquidity is now national" from "the moat was actually service, settlement and switching costs all along." If share holds in the seventies with realised fee near four paise a unit through the first couple of years of coupling, the bear case is empirically dead. If it slides toward the fifties while realised fee slips, no amount of volume growth compensates, because both terms of the revenue equation are moving the same way.

12

Future, risks, bull & bear

The full ledger, with the regulatory item where it belongs — at the top.

The risks, ranked

  • Market coupling (the dominant risk). The draft 2026 amendment names Grid India as coupling operator and puts DAM and RTM — about 73% of FY26 volume — first in line. If a bid on a small exchange clears identically to a bid on IEX, the liquidity advantage stops operating. Design details, sequencing and the pace of member migration are all unknown. This is not a risk that resolves quietly; it resolves in a notification.
  • Regulated fee compression. CERC caps the transaction fee and has reportedly weighed cutting the ceiling from ~2 paise per side toward ~1.5 paise (and ~1.25 for term-ahead). A 25% revenue cut lands on a near-fixed cost base and takes more than 25% of profit with it. Coupling makes fee competition likelier still, since price becomes the main remaining differentiator.
  • Volume dependence on policy and weather. Revenue is volume × fee, and volume is driven by things IEX does not control: the pace of DISCOM migration off PPAs, open-access rules that vary by state, price caps that can suppress trading when they bind, and simply how hot the summer is. A mild year is a bad quarter.
  • Competition, newly enabled. PXIL and HPX are small today precisely because of the liquidity loop. Coupling would hand them an equal order book overnight; HPX in particular has substantial backing and every incentive to buy share on price.
  • Single-market concentration. Effectively all profit comes from one commodity in one country under one regulator. IGX, ICX and the new coal exchange diversify the story but not yet the earnings.
  • DISCOM credit and sector health. IEX's settlement design insulates it well, but the buyers on its platform are mostly state utilities whose finances are structurally weak. A broad deterioration in DISCOM ability to pay would eventually show up in volumes.

The case for

  • The pie keeps growing. 141.1 BU is under a tenth of Indian consumption. Demand growth, PPA migration, renewables and open-access C&I buyers all push more volume onto exchanges regardless of who runs them.
  • Coupling may cost less than feared. European precedent suggests incumbents retain most volume for years. Switching an exchange membership means re-papering contracts, re-establishing margin and trusting a smaller settler — friction that four paise of fee does not easily overcome.
  • The economics are exceptional if they survive. ~86% EBITDA margin, net cash, negligible capital needs, high payout, ~30%-plus ROE. There are very few businesses like this listed anywhere.
  • Optionality outside power. IGX is growing faster than the parent with a 35% PAT increase; ICX and the new coal exchange point the same platform at markets nobody is proposing to couple.
  • The price already assumes damage. At ~22× trailing earnings for a record-setting, debt-free near-monopoly, a good deal of share loss and fee compression is embedded in the number.

The case against

  • The moat is a rule, and the rule is being rewritten. There is no capital expenditure, no acquisition and no product launch that can rebuild a network effect the regulator has dissolved.
  • Both revenue variables sit with CERC. Share is exposed to coupling; fee is exposed to the ceiling. Management controls neither.
  • Operating leverage runs in reverse. The cost base is fixed, so any revenue loss is amplified on the way down exactly as volume gains were amplified on the way up.
  • DAM is disproportionately exposed. IEX's ~98.8% share sits in precisely the segment coupling targets first, and DAM plus RTM is roughly three-quarters of volume.
  • The clock has not started. The coupling procedure is due within six months of notification — so the disruptive period is still ahead, not behind, and the stock must carry that uncertainty until real post-coupling share data exists.

The bottom line

Where this actually lands

IEX is one of the highest-quality business models in the Indian market attached to one of its most fragile competitive positions. It matched 141.1 billion units of electricity in FY26 at about four paise a unit, kept eighty-six of every hundred rupees as operating profit, borrowed nothing to do it, and paid the surplus out. Every operating fact about the year was excellent. And none of that is what the share price is about.

The specific milestone to diarise is not a plant, a product or a quarter: it is the day CERC notifies the Power Market (Second Amendment) Regulations, which starts a six-month clock for Grid India to publish the Power Market Coupling Procedure. From that publication you learn the design — which segments, what sequencing, how allocation across exchanges actually works. And roughly a year after DAM goes live coupled, the monthly volume releases will show, in one percentage figure, whether eighteen years of liquidity built something members stay for or something they were merely routed through.
