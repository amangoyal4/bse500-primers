00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Ion Exchange (India) is the country's oldest and most complete water-and-environment company — a single business that treats water three different ways. It builds treatment plants (the Engineering segment: desalination, effluent recycling, zero-liquid-discharge, municipal and industrial water systems, on an EPC/BOT basis). It makes the chemistry those plants and thousands of others run on (the Chemicals segment: ion-exchange resins — a globally competitive product exported to dozens of countries — plus specialty water-treatment chemicals). And it sells purifiers into homes and institutions (the Consumer segment: the "Zero B" and INDION brands, RO/UV). One water problem, monetised three ways — each with a completely different cash-flow shape.

Three facts frame the whole company:

  • The engineering is the biggest line; the chemicals is the best line. Engineering is roughly half of revenue but it is lumpy, working-capital-heavy project work. Chemicals — barely a third of revenue — is where the durable, high-margin profit sits, because a resin bed exhausts and must be refilled, so it sells again and again. Understanding which segment does what to the P&L is the whole game.
  • Revenue rose, profit fell — on purpose, sort of. FY26 consolidated revenue grew ~6.5% to ~₹2,915 Cr, but net profit dropped to ~₹143 Cr from ~₹208 Cr. The cause was not a demand collapse — it was the start-up drag of a brand-new ₹450 Cr resin plant at Roha plus a spike in raw-material costs (styrene, oleum). The company spent this year's margin to buy next decade's capacity.
  • It has no promoter — and never really did. Since 1985 Ion Exchange has been an employee-and-trust-owned company with no controlling family. Promoter holding is only ~25%. This 62-year-old industrial is, unusually, run by managers and owned by its own people — a structure almost unique on the Indian market. The stock trades near 49× earnings, pricing in a margin recovery it has yet to deliver.
In plain terms

Picture the whole water problem of a factory, a city or a home, and Ion Exchange sells to every layer of it. It will build you the plant that cleans your effluent; it will supply the resin beads that plant needs to keep working and that wear out on a schedule; and it will put a purifier under your sink at home. The engineering wins the headlines and the order book; the resin quietly pays the bills, because it is the consumable — the thing every treatment plant on earth has to replace whether Ion Exchange built the plant or not. The current test is simple: the company just spent heavily on a new resin factory, and profit dipped while that factory warms up. The bet is that once it runs full, the most profitable part of the business gets much bigger.

01

The business of water: how treating it makes money

No chemistry degree needed — just the few ideas that drive the whole company.

Water is never simply "clean" or "dirty" — it is clean enough for a purpose. A power station needs water so pure it won't scale a boiler; a pharma plant needs water pure enough to injure nothing; a city needs water safe to drink; and a factory must clean its wastewater enough to discharge — or increasingly, to reuse. Every one of those is a treatment job, and treatment costs money that someone will pay. Ion Exchange sits across the whole chain. Five ideas unlock the business.

The handful of terms that run the story
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Ion-exchange resinTiny plastic beads that grab unwanted ions out of water (hardness, salts, metals) and swap in harmless ones. The core chemistry the company is named for. A resin bed slowly exhausts and must be regenerated or replaced — which is exactly why it is a repeat-purchase consumable, not a one-time sale.
EPC / BOT"Engineering, Procurement & Construction" — design and build a treatment plant for a fee. "Build-Operate-Transfer" — build it, run it for years, then hand it over. This is the lumpy, project-based Engineering segment.
ZLD"Zero Liquid Discharge" — treat industrial wastewater so thoroughly that nothing leaves the gate as liquid; the water is recovered and reused, the residue crystallised. A demanding, high-value process India increasingly mandates. A core Ion Exchange capability.
DesalinationTurning seawater or brackish water into usable water, usually via reverse-osmosis membranes. Water-scarce coasts and industries buy it; Ion Exchange builds these plants at home and abroad (e.g. North Africa).
Reuse / recycleInstead of drawing fresh water and dumping waste, treat and re-circulate the same water. As water gets scarce and regulated, reuse shifts from "nice to have" to "must do" — and reuse plants are more complex, so they are worth more per project.
The one economic idea to hold onto: the razor and the blade

A treatment plant is a one-time capital sale — build it, book it, move on. But the resin inside it is a consumable that wears out and must be re-bought, year after year, for the life of the plant. That is the difference between the two big segments in a sentence: Engineering sells the razor; Chemicals sells the blades. And crucially, the blade business is bigger than Ion Exchange's own razors — its resins go into plants built by rivals and by customers worldwide. So the least glamorous segment, a bag of beads, is the one with the annuity underneath it. Keep that picture; the rest of the primer is variations on it.

Who pays Ion Exchange

  • Industry — power, refining, steel, pharma, chemicals, textiles, food and, lately, solar-cell and semiconductor fabs that need ultra-pure water. These buyers want treated water, ZLD compliance and reliable resin supply.
  • Municipalities & government — city water and sewage schemes, and programmes such as the drinking-water push under Jal Jeevan; typically large EPC/BOT contracts, often slower-paying.
  • Homes & institutions — households buying a purifier, plus offices, hospitals and hotels; the Consumer segment's customers.
  • The world — resin buyers across the Americas, Europe, Africa, the Middle East and Asia. Exports make Ion Exchange's chemistry a genuinely global product, not a domestic one.

Three companies in one share: the segment map

Read this once and the entire P&L stops being confusing.

Ion Exchange reports three segments, and they behave so differently that it helps to treat them as three businesses sharing a balance sheet. One is a project contractor, one is a specialty-chemicals maker, and one is a consumer-durables brand. They serve the same broad theme — water — but their revenue rhythm, margins and cash cycles have almost nothing in common.

The trap to avoid when reading this company

Because Engineering is the biggest revenue line, it is tempting to value Ion Exchange as an EPC contractor and watch the order book like you would for a Wabag or an L&T. That misreads it. A pure EPC contractor lives and dies on execution and working capital; Ion Exchange's quality lives in the chemicals it sells, which throw off far higher and steadier margins on far less capital. Value it as an EPC firm and you undervalue the resin annuity; value it as a chemicals firm and you ignore a genuinely large engineering franchise. It is both — and the two segments deserve different multiples in your head.

03

The 62-year-old with no promoter

An ownership story you will not find at most Indian industrials.

Almost every listed Indian company has a controlling family or founder — a "promoter" whose name and stake dominate the shareholding table. Ion Exchange is one of the rare exceptions. It began in 1964 as the Indian arm of a British water company, Permutit, which held 60%. When Permutit exited in 1985, the shares did not pass to a new tycoon — they were absorbed into employee welfare trusts, a pioneering move for India at the time. The company became, in effect, owned by its own people and run by professional managers. It has stayed that way.

Timeline

  • 1964
    Incorporated as the Indian subsidiary of Britain's Permutit (60% held), bringing world-standard ion-exchange water treatment to a young Indian industrial economy.
  • 1979
    Sets up its own ion-exchange resin plant at Ankleshwar (Gujarat) — the start of India-made resins competing with global chemistry.
  • 1982
    Adds an engineering & fabrication facility at Hosur — building the capability to make treatment equipment, not just chemicals.
  • 1985
    Indianisation. Permutit divests; the holding is placed with employee welfare trusts — a management-and-employee-owned structure highly unusual for the era. No single promoter family emerges.
  • 1990s
    Launches the consumer water-purifier business — the Zero B ("Zero Bacteria") brand — taking water treatment from factories into homes.
  • 2000s–2010s
    Scales resin exports to the Americas, Europe, the Middle East and Asia; wins large EPC and desalination work at home and overseas; the INDION brand becomes a global resin name.
  • 2024–25
    Breaks ground and begins phased commissioning of a large greenfield resin plant at Roha (Maharashtra), ~₹450 Cr capex — a step-change in chemicals capacity.
  • FY26
    Revenue ~₹2,915 Cr; profit dips to ~₹143 Cr as Roha start-up costs and input inflation bite; engineering order book ~₹2,640 Cr with a ~₹9,500 Cr bid pipeline.

Ownership & structure

  • No promoter family. "Promoter" holding sits near ~25% and is largely the employee/associate trusts and group entities, not an individual dynasty. The remaining ~75% is public — institutions, funds and retail. Control rests with the board and professional management rather than a founder.
  • A famous public holder. The late investor Rakesh Jhunjhunwala was for years a large public shareholder in Ion Exchange — a well-known vote of confidence in the water theme, and a reminder that the free float, not a promoter, drives this share.
  • Balance sheet: modestly geared, with total borrowings around ~₹480 Cr against net worth of roughly ~₹1,340 Cr — debt that stepped up to fund the Roha build. Return ratios are solid but not spectacular: ROE ~12%, ROCE ~14% in FY26, both dented this year by the plant's start-up drag.
A genuine surprise: a company this old, this asset-heavy and this respected has no controlling promoter — it was effectively handed to its employees four decades ago and has run on professional management ever since. That cuts both ways. It removes the classic Indian-promoter risks (pledging, related-party dealings, succession drama), but it also means no deep-pocketed family to backstop a bad year or fund an aggressive expansion — the company must self-finance from profits and the market. The Roha plant is exactly that kind of bet made without a promoter's balance sheet behind it.
04

Resins: the crown jewel most people miss

The smallest-sounding segment carries the most durable profit — here is why.

If you remember one thing about Ion Exchange, make it this: its chemicals segment — principally ion-exchange resins — is the profit engine. It is only about a third of revenue, but it earns a far higher margin than engineering and it recurs, because resin is a consumable. And unlike the engineering business, which competes mostly at home, Ion Exchange's resins compete globally, against the handful of world-scale makers, and win enough to export across continents. India has very few companies that can say that about any specialty chemical.

Why resins are such good economics comes down to the razor-and-blade point, made concrete. Every water-treatment plant that uses ion-exchange chemistry — for softening, demineralisation, metal recovery, or ultra-pure water — packs its vessels with resin beads. Those beads slowly lose capacity and must be regenerated and eventually replaced. So each installed plant, whether Ion Exchange built it or a rival did, becomes a standing order for resin refills for years. The company that makes the beads sells into an installed base far larger than its own project book — including its competitors' plants.

The device that fits only Ion Exchange: it sells blades for its rivals' razors

A pure water-EPC contractor is only as good as its next order; when the order book empties, so does the plant. Ion Exchange has a second engine bolted on that a pure contractor can't have: because it manufactures the consumable resin, it earns from the entire installed base of ion-exchange plants across the world — its own and those built by Wabag, Thermax, or a customer's in-house team. The engineering wins a plant once; the chemistry can feed that plant, and thousands like it, for a decade. That is the structural reason this company deserves a better multiple than a project contractor — and the reason a resin factory, not an order win, is the most important thing it built this year.

The Roha plant: this year's whole story in one asset

Ion Exchange is commissioning a large greenfield resin plant at Roha, Maharashtra, at roughly ₹450 Cr of capex — a major addition to resin capacity, phased in from late 2025. This single project is the reason FY26 looks the way it does. New chemical plants don't switch on profitable: they carry heavy start-up costs, low initial utilisation, and a learning curve before yields and volumes normalise. Management has guided that Roha reaches only about 25% utilisation in FY27, with a payback of four to five years. So the plant is a drag on today's margin and an addition to tomorrow's capacity — the classic shape of a capacity bet.

Watch the input costs, not just the volumes: resin is made from petrochemical feedstocks — styrene and oleum among them — so the chemicals margin swings with those prices as well as with utilisation. 05

Engineering: the big top line and the lumpy order book

Where the revenue is largest — and the working capital is hardest.

The Engineering segment is what most people picture as Ion Exchange's core: it designs and builds water and wastewater plants — demineralisation, effluent and sewage treatment, recycle-and-reuse systems, ZLD, and desalination — for industry, municipalities and government, at home and overseas. It is the largest revenue line, and it is project revenue: won in tenders, recognised as work progresses, and paid on milestones that can stretch out. That makes it both the growth story and the balance-sheet strain.

The booked backlog of ~₹2,640 Cr is a bit over a year of engineering revenue; the ~₹9,500 Cr bid pipeline is what could refill it. A big pipeline is opportunity, not certainty — conversion and pricing discipline decide whether it helps.

What the segment builds has been shifting toward higher-value, harder jobs. Recent order flow has leaned into ZLD and ultra-pure-water systems for the solar-manufacturing boom — Ion Exchange won contracts (for example from names in the solar-PV supply chain) to supply the demanding water systems that cell and module fabs need — plus desalination work, including projects in North Africa. These are exactly the complex, reuse-and-recovery jobs that carry better margins than plain build-and-hand-over water plants.

Why the order book is a weaker guide here than you'd expect

For a pure EPC contractor, backlog-to-revenue is the master gauge. For Ion Exchange it is only half the picture, for two reasons. First, engineering is the lower-margin, capital-hungry segment — booking more of it can add revenue while diluting group returns if the work is priced thin. Second, the real quality of this company sits in Chemicals, which the order book doesn't capture at all. So a swelling engineering backlog is genuinely good news for scale and for pulling resin and chemical sales alongside the plants — but it is not, on its own, the number that decides whether Ion Exchange is a good business. The margin the backlog earns, and the resin it drags behind it, matter more than its size.

06

Consumer & Zero B: the brand bet that hasn't paid yet

The smallest segment, the longest game, and — for now — a drag.

The third segment takes Ion Exchange's water expertise into the home and the institution: water purifiers under the Zero B and INDION brands, plus RO/UV systems and point-of-use products for offices, hospitals and hotels. It is the smallest of the three by revenue and, at present, it loses money — the segment ran an operating loss in FY26. That combination, small and loss-making, is easy to dismiss; it deserves a closer look.

The reason it loses money is the reason any consumer-brand build costs money up front: distribution, marketing, service networks and brand-building are expensed today, while the pay-off — a large installed base of purifiers that generate repeat filter-and-service revenue — comes later. A purifier, like a treatment plant, has its own razor-and-blade: the filters and servicing recur. If Ion Exchange can get enough units into enough kitchens, the segment can flip from a cost centre to a small, sticky annuity. The FY26 numbers show revenue growing briskly (the consumer line grew strongly year on year) even as the segment still ran a loss — the shape of a business being scaled, not one being wound down.

The honest read on Consumer

India's household water-purifier market is large, growing and fiercely contested — Kent, Eureka Forbes (Aquaguard), Livpure, HUL's Pureit and others spend heavily. Ion Exchange is a smaller player fighting for shelf space and mind-share, and there is no guarantee the segment reaches the scale where its economics turn. For an investor, the pragmatic stance is to value Consumer at roughly zero today — neither give the company credit for a turnaround that hasn't happened, nor penalise it much for a small loss — and treat any move to breakeven as upside rather than base case. The engineering and chemicals segments are what you are really buying.

07

Why water is a structural tailwind

The backdrop that gives all three segments a decade of demand.

Ion Exchange's three businesses share one thing that keeps growing: water is getting scarcer, dirtier to ignore, and more heavily regulated — and every one of those forces is a reason to spend on treatment. This is not a fashion; it is the slow, compounding consequence of industrialisation, urbanisation and a stressed water table in a country of 1.4 billion people. The tailwind shows up differently in each segment, but it blows on all of them.

The insight that separates water from a hype theme

Plenty of "megatrends" depend on a subsidy or a fashion that can vanish. Water demand doesn't. A factory that must recycle its effluent to keep its consent to operate will keep spending on treatment through booms and busts, because the alternative is being shut down. A city with a stressed aquifer cannot un-need clean water. That regulatory-and-physical compulsion is why water treatment is a defensive growth theme rather than a cyclical bet — and why a company spanning engineering, chemicals and consumer across that theme has genuinely long-dated demand behind it. The catch is that being in a good theme is not the same as earning good returns within it, which is where execution and mix come back in.

08

The margin paradox: revenue up, profit down

FY26's central puzzle — and how to read it correctly.

FY26 delivered a jarring pair of numbers: consolidated revenue grew to ~₹2,915 Cr, but net profit fell to ~₹143 Cr from ~₹208 Cr, and the operating margin roughly halved from about 11% to about 7%. On the surface that looks like a business losing its grip. Under the surface it is mostly the accounting signature of a company mid-investment — but "mostly" is not "entirely", and the distinction is the whole debate.

Revenue rose ~6.5% while profit fell ~31%. The gap is the cost of growth landing before its benefit: a new plant's start-up expenses and higher input prices hit the P&L now; the extra capacity earns later.

Three things pulled the margin down at once, and it is worth separating them because they heal on different clocks:

  • Roha start-up costs (structural, self-inflicted, temporary). A new resin plant carries fixed costs — depreciation, staffing, commissioning — before it makes much product. This drag reverses as utilisation climbs, and management's own ramp guidance says that is a multi-year process.
  • Raw-material inflation (cyclical). Higher styrene and oleum prices squeezed the chemicals margin directly. This is a price cycle, not a permanent impairment — it can ease, or worsen, independent of anything Ion Exchange does.
  • Consumer losses and engineering mix (ongoing). The loss-making consumer build and the share of lower-margin engineering in the mix both weighed on the blended margin.
Reading a "bad" year the right way

The bull and bear split precisely here. Read charitably, FY26 is a company deliberately trading a soft year of profit to roughly step-change its most valuable capacity — the resin plant that, once full, thickens the highest-margin segment for a decade. Read sceptically, a company already priced at a rich multiple just posted a sharp profit fall, and the promised recovery rests on a slow ramp and on input costs it doesn't control. Both descriptions are of the same year. Which one is right gets settled not by narrative but by a single line item over the next several quarters: the chemicals-segment margin as Roha fills.

09

The rivals — and why Ion Exchange doesn't fit any single box

It competes with different companies in each of its three segments.

Because Ion Exchange is three businesses, it has no single competitor. In engineering it is up against water-EPC specialists; in chemicals it takes on the global resin majors; in consumer it fights the purifier brands. Its distinctiveness is precisely that no rival competes with it across all three — which is both a moat (integration, cross-selling, six decades of process credibility) and a curse (it is sub-scale versus the pure-play leader in each arena).

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Set Ion Exchange next to VA Tech Wabag and the contrast is the clearest way to understand it. Wabag is the bigger, cleaner water-engineering bet — if you want pure exposure to India's water-project capex, it is the more direct instrument. Ion Exchange is the hedged version: a smaller engineering book, but bolted to a high-margin, globally-traded resin business and a consumer optionality Wabag simply doesn't have. Whether that integration is worth the complexity is the investor's judgement call. The resin franchise is the reason to prefer Ion Exchange; the sub-scale engineering and the loss-making consumer arm are the reasons someone might not.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A durable water franchise in an investment year — asked to prove the investment pays.

Ion Exchange enters FY27 as a rare thing: a genuinely integrated, six-decade water-and-environment company riding a structural tailwind, with a globally competitive resin business and no promoter overhang. It also enters it fresh off a profit dip, a half-utilised new plant, and a valuation that assumes the recovery arrives on schedule. The debate is not whether water is a good place to be — it is whether this company earns good returns from being there while the resin plant warms up.

The bull case

  • Structural water demand — scarcity, reuse mandates, ZLD and ultra-pure-water needs give all three segments long-dated, regulation-backed growth.
  • The resin annuity — a high-margin, globally-traded consumable that earns off the whole installed base of ion-exchange plants, not just Ion Exchange's own projects.
  • Roha optionality — a ₹450 Cr capacity step-up in the best segment, dragging today but built to thicken profit for a decade as it fills.
  • Clean ownership — no promoter pledging, related-party or succession risk; professional management and a broad float.
  • New-economy orders — solar, semiconductor and desalination demand widening the engineering opportunity beyond traditional industry.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation on soft earnings — ~49× trailing P/E on a depressed FY26 profit prices in a recovery that hasn't yet shown up in the numbers.
  • Ramp & input risk — the whole thesis leans on Roha filling and on styrene/oleum easing; the first is slow, the second is out of the company's hands.
  • Lumpy, WC-heavy engineering — the biggest revenue line ties up working capital and can pay slowly, especially on government projects.
  • Sub-scale in each arena — smaller than Wabag in EPC, than the majors in resins, than the leaders in purifiers; a jack of three trades.
  • Consumer keeps losing money — the brand build may not reach the scale where its economics turn.
The bottom line

Ion Exchange is the most complete way to own India's water problem in a single share — the firm that builds the plant, makes the consumable it runs on, and puts the purifier in the kitchen, backed by six decades of process credibility and an ownership structure refreshingly free of promoter games. But FY26 was the bill for growth arriving before the benefit: a new resin plant that costs now and pays later, in a business already valued as if it pays already. The next several quarters resolve it in one checkable line — the chemicals-segment margin climbing as Roha's utilisation rises past 25%. If that print turns up, the investment year was exactly that; if it stalls, the market re-prices a plant it paid for in advance. Follow the resin margin quarter by quarter — it, not the order book, is where this company's next chapter is decided.
