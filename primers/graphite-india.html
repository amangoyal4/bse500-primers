Graphite India, the rod-maker whose profit is a price bet

A complete walk through one of the world's larger makers of graphite electrodes — the giant carbon rods that carry the current to melt scrap steel. How the rods are made, why they get burned up and constantly replaced, why a single once-in-a-generation price spike in 2018 left this company sitting on a cash pile worth a third of its own market value, and why almost everything about the earnings comes down to one moving number: the gap between what an electrode sells for and what its raw material costs.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Graphite India makes graphite electrodes — enormous, precisely-machined carbon rods, some as thick as a tree trunk and metres long, that carry a huge electric current into a furnace to melt scrap steel. It is one of the world's larger producers of these electrodes, part of the Kolkata-based Krishna Kumar Bangur (KK Bangur) group, with an installed electrode capacity of about 98,000 tonnes a year across its Indian plants and a big share of output sold overseas. Alongside electrodes it runs a small clutch of other businesses — captive power, a steel unit, and speciality carbon products — but the electrode line is the engine that decides whether a year is a triumph or a write-off.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • The product is a consumable, not a machine. An electrode is not installed once and left alone — it is literally eaten away in the furnace as it does its job and must be replaced again and again. That single fact means demand tracks how much steel is being made in electric-arc furnaces, not how many furnaces get built. It is a razor-blade business dressed up as heavy industry.
  • The earnings are a price bet, not a volume story. Graphite India can run its plant flat-out (FY26 utilisation was ~104%) and still see profit collapse, because what moves the bottom line is the spread — the electrode selling price minus the cost of needle coke, the scarce imported raw material. When that spread is wide, this company mints money; when it narrows, earnings fall off a cliff. FY26 showed the down-leg in miniature: record volumes, but Q4 tipped into an operating loss as prices sagged and coke stayed dear.
  • It is sitting on a fortune it earned in one window. A global electrode shortage in 2017–18 sent prices to absurd highs and handed Graphite India a windfall it has never spent. It ended FY26 with roughly ₹3,767 Cr of net cash — close to a third of its entire market value — a debt-free balance sheet that lets it wait out the down-cycles that flatten leveraged rivals.
In plain terms

Graphite India sells the "spark plugs" of scrap-based steelmaking. Every electric-arc-furnace steel mill in the world needs these carbon rods, they wear out and get replaced constantly, and only a handful of companies globally can make the top grade at scale — so it is a genuinely important, hard-to-replicate business. The trouble is what it earns for that: the price of an electrode swings violently with the balance of global supply and demand, and the profit is whatever is left after paying for imported needle coke. Run the plant perfectly and you can still have a bad year if the price is against you. The compensation for that wildness is a cash-rich, debt-free balance sheet that has already banked one spectacular boom and can simply outlast the busts.

01

Graphite electrodes 101: the rods that melt steel

No metallurgy degree needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

To understand Graphite India you first have to understand the small, specialised object it sells and the exact job it does. There are two ways to make steel. The old way — the blast furnace — burns coal (coke) to smelt iron ore into fresh iron, then into steel. The other way — the electric-arc furnace (EAF) — skips the ore entirely and instead melts down scrap steel using electricity. And to push that colossal current into a furnace full of scrap, you need something that can carry it and survive temperatures of around 3,000°C. That something is a graphite electrode.

Picture three giant carbon rods lowered into a bath of scrap. Switch on the power and an electric arc jumps from the tips of the rods to the metal — a controlled bolt of lightning, hot enough to melt steel in minutes. The electrode is the conductor that makes that arc. Only graphite works: it conducts electricity superbly, tolerates ferocious heat without melting, and can be machined into precise rods that screw together end-to-end (via threaded "nipples") as the furnace consumes them.

The only terms you need

The one fact that reframes the whole business

An electrode is a consumable. It is not a piece of equipment a steel mill buys once — it is fuel-like stock that gets steadily burned away in the arc and has to be reordered, over and over, for as long as the furnace runs. That is why this is not a "steel capex" play at all: Graphite India does not care much whether new furnaces are being built; it cares how many tonnes of steel the world's existing furnaces are melting, because every one of those tonnes quietly eats a sliver of electrode. Demand is tied to steel production, not steel construction — a distinction that changes how you read the whole cycle.

The making of an electrode is slow, energy-hungry and genuinely hard. Needle coke is mixed with pitch and extruded into a "green" rod; it is then baked for weeks, impregnated to densify it, and finally graphitised — heated to around 3,000°C so the carbon rearranges into ordered graphite. The whole cycle takes roughly two months per batch and consumes enormous power. That long, capital-intensive process, plus the scarcity of needle coke and the quality bar for UHP grade, is why only a small club of producers worldwide can compete — a barrier that protects Graphite India even in bad years.

02

What Graphite India actually sells

One dominant product, one dominant cost, and a couple of sideshows.

Read the company through one lens: it converts imported needle coke into UHP graphite electrodes and sells them to electric-arc-furnace steelmakers around the world, who then burn them up and buy more. Everything else on the income statement — the captive power, the small steel unit, the speciality-carbon products — is a supporting act. The value chain below is the business in four steps, and the profit lives in the gap between the first box and the third.

The revenue lines, decoded

  • Graphite & Carbon (electrodes) — the overwhelming majority of revenue and virtually all the earnings volatility. This is the ~98,000-tonne electrode business plus some speciality-carbon products. When people say "Graphite India," this is what they mean.
  • Captive & other power — the group runs some of its own power generation, which both feeds the energy-hungry graphitising process and sells surplus. It softens the electricity cost of a business that uses vast amounts of it.
  • Steel & others — a small steel operation and sundry activities. Minor in the mix, and not the reason anyone owns the stock. Graphite India reports its numbers across Graphite & Carbon, Steel, and Others segments, but the first dwarfs the rest.
Why "one product, one cost" is the right mental model

Most industrial companies have several levers — volume, mix, cost, price — that partly offset each other, so a bad year in one line gets cushioned by another. Graphite India barely has that luxury. Its fortune rides on a single product's price and a single input's cost, both set in global markets it does not control. That is what makes it so leveraged to the cycle in both directions: there is no diversified base of earnings to steady the ride, only the electrode spread and how hard the plant is running. Strip the company down and you are left holding a bet on two prices moving in your favour at the same time.

03

The company, the group, and who controls it

A Kolkata industrial house, tightly held, conservatively run.

Graphite India is the flagship of the Krishna Kumar Bangur group, a Kolkata-based industrial family with roots going back generations in Indian industry. The company was incorporated in 1974 and, over the following decades, built itself into one of the world's larger integrated graphite-electrode makers. It is chaired by Krishna Kumar Bangur, and the family controls it firmly through a web of promoter holding companies.

Timeline

  • 2000s

  • 2017–18

  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Bangur family holds roughly 65% (approx.), through entities such as Emerald Company Private Limited (the single largest holder), GKW Limited, Emerald Matrix Holdings and family trusts. Notably, none of the promoter holding is pledged — a marker of the group's conservative, low-leverage temperament.
  • Leadership: chaired by Krishna Kumar Bangur. This is a promoter-run company in the classic Indian sense — the family's cautious, cash-first instincts are visible right across the balance sheet and capital-allocation history.
  • Balance sheet: debt-free with ~₹3,767 Cr of net cash at March 2026. The company funds its own operations and expansions from internal resources and the accumulated windfall, and has never needed to lever up to survive a down-cycle.
Read the promoter through the balance sheet: a family that banks a once-in-a-lifetime windfall and then sits on it — debt-free, unpledged, cash piled up — is telling you something about how it thinks about a violently cyclical business. It runs Graphite India to survive every bust, not to squeeze the last rupee out of every boom. That is a genuine strength in a commodity that periodically tries to kill its participants — and, to a growth-hungry investor, occasionally a frustration.
04

The 2018 supercycle: the reference frame for everything

The single episode that explains this company's cash, its cyclicality, and its risk.

You cannot understand Graphite India without understanding what happened in 2017–18, because that one episode is the reference frame the whole stock is priced against — the memory of what this plant can earn when the price goes vertical, and the warning of how fast it can vanish. For a few extraordinary quarters, a boring maker of carbon rods became one of the most profitable companies of its size in India.

The trigger was a supply shock. China, cleaning up its polluting industries, shut a swathe of electrode and needle-coke capacity. At the same time, needle coke was being diverted toward lithium-ion battery anodes, tightening the raw material further. EAF steelmaking — helped by China itself shifting toward scrap — was demanding more electrodes just as supply was being pulled. The result was a textbook squeeze: electrode prices rocketed from a few thousand dollars a tonne to, at the peak, roughly $10,000–12,000 a tonne (approx.). Because the raw material had been contracted far cheaper, the spread — and therefore the profit — went to levels no one had modelled.

Same plant. Same rods. The price did all the work.
The device that fits only Graphite India: profit is the spread, not the volume

Here is the non-transferable heart of this company. In FY26 Graphite India ran its ~98,000-tonne plant at ~104% utilisation — as hard as it has ever run it — and earned about ₹171 Cr of profit. In the FY18–19 windfall, that same plant, making the same rods at roughly the same volume, earned on the order of ₹2,000 Cr-plus (approx.) in a single year. That is a swing of roughly 10–12× in profit with almost no change in tonnes shipped. Nothing about the factory changed; the electrode-minus-needle-coke spread changed. When you own Graphite India, you are not really betting on how much it makes — you are betting on the price of what it makes. Volume is a rounding error next to the spread.

And then it reversed. As Chinese capacity restarted and the shortage eased, electrode prices fell back toward normal, the spread compressed, and profits deflated just as violently as they had inflated. The company that had earned thousands of crore in a boom year was, a couple of years later, earning a fraction of that from the identical asset. Crucially, Graphite India did not respond to the boom by borrowing to build — it kept the cash. That decision is why, years later, it still sits on a war chest rather than a debt pile.

05

The spread that is the whole story

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to this business.

If you take only one thing from this primer, take this: Graphite India's earnings are, to a first approximation, the electrode realisation minus the needle-coke cost, per tonne, times how many tonnes it sells. That is it. Volume is fairly stable and capped by capacity; the plant is either running or it isn't. What whips the profit around is the spread — and both sides of that spread are set in global markets Graphite India does not control.

The thin gold sliver at the bottom is where all the action is. Because it is a small residual between two large, independently-moving numbers, a modest move in either price flexes it wildly — which is exactly why FY18 and FY26 look like two different companies.

Watch how the two sides move out of step. In FY26, electrode volumes were excellent — utilisation of ~104% means the plant physically ran beyond nameplate — yet the year still ended with Q4 in an operating loss. Why? Because electrode prices softened while needle-coke costs stayed stubbornly high. Company commentary was blunt about it: needle coke and other raw-material costs did not fall in line with electrode prices. When the input stays dear and the output cheapens, the spread gets crushed from both ends — and no amount of running the plant harder can fix a spread problem.

Why running flat-out could not save FY26

It is counter-intuitive: the plant ran at its best-ever utilisation and the company still tipped into a quarterly loss. That is the whole point of a spread business. Utilisation determines how many units of spread you capture; it cannot change the size of the spread. If each tonne barely clears its raw-material and power cost, selling more tonnes just spreads a thin margin over more volume — or, when the spread turns negative on falling prices and old high-cost coke inventory, actively loses money on each one. FY26 is the clean illustration: maximum volume, minimum spread, and the spread won.

This is why forecasting Graphite India is close to impossible on a two-year view and why the stock trades on sentiment about the next turn in the cycle as much as on trailing numbers. The needle-coke market is opaque and import-dependent; the electrode market swings with global EAF-steel demand and with how much Chinese and global supply is on or offline. An analyst who tells you they can pin next year's electrode spread to a rupee is guessing. What you can do is watch the direction of the two prices and the inventory the company is carrying — which is exactly what the valuation section will point you to.

06

EAF steel & decarbonisation: the demand engine

The structural tailwind that could stretch the good part of the cycle.

Set the wild price cycle aside for a moment and look at the underlying demand. Electrodes are consumed making EAF steel, so the long-run question is simple: is the world going to make more steel in electric-arc furnaces, or less? The answer — driven by climate policy — points firmly toward more. That is the one genuinely structural leg under an otherwise cyclical business.

Steelmaking is one of the planet's biggest sources of carbon emissions, and the dirtiest part is the blast furnace, which burns coal to reduce iron ore. The clean alternative is the EAF route: melt existing scrap steel with electricity (which can be renewable) instead of smelting virgin ore with coal. As countries push to decarbonise heavy industry, steel is shifting — slowly but unmistakably — away from blast furnaces and toward scrap-melting EAFs. Every tonne of steel that moves from the blast-furnace route to the EAF route is a new tonne of electrode demand, because only EAFs consume electrodes.

The demand insight that separates the noise from the signal

The electrode price is a short-term, boom-bust variable that no one can forecast. But the quantity of electrodes the world needs has a slow, one-directional tailwind behind it: the more steel decarbonises, the more of it is made by melting scrap, and the more electrodes get burned. So the two halves of the story operate on different clocks. Price is the violent, unpredictable near-term driver of profit; EAF growth is the patient, structural driver of long-run volume. A patient owner is really betting that rising EAF demand eventually tightens the market enough to widen spreads again — turning the slow volume tailwind into the next price spike.

None of this smooths the ride. A structural demand tailwind can coexist with brutal short-term gluts — if China floods the market with cheap electrodes, or global steel output dips in a recession, the spread can collapse regardless of the decade-long trend. But the direction of travel matters: it is the reason to believe Graphite India is a cyclical with a rising floor under demand, rather than a melting ice cube.

07

The cash mountain: a third of the value in the bank

The balance sheet is not a footnote here — it is a load-bearing part of the thesis.

Most commodity companies are studied for their earnings. Graphite India has to be studied for its balance sheet, because roughly ₹3,767 Cr of net cash against a market value near ₹12,500 Cr means about a third of what you pay for the stock is simply cash sitting in the company. That changes how the whole valuation works — and it is the direct legacy of the 2018 windfall that the family chose to keep rather than spend.

Strip out the cash and the operating business — the electrode plant and its earnings power — is being valued at roughly ₹8,700 Cr, not ₹12,500 Cr. On a cyclical, that gap between market cap and enterprise value is where much of the debate lives.

Why does the cash matter so much beyond the arithmetic? Because in a violently cyclical commodity, the balance sheet is what decides who is still standing at the bottom. A leveraged electrode maker that hits a stretch of negative spreads — falling prices, dear coke, inventory write-downs, exactly the FY26 Q4 picture — can find its interest bill and working-capital needs colliding with collapsing cash flow. Graphite India has the opposite problem: with no debt and a fortune in the bank, it can absorb a loss-making quarter, keep the plant warm, hold inventory, and simply wait for the spread to turn. The cash is not idle ballast — it is staying power.

Two ways to read the same cash pile

The cash cuts both ways, and honest analysis holds both. To a value-minded buyer, it is a margin of safety: you are getting the operating business for less than the headline price, and the downside is cushioned because a debt-free company cannot be forced under in a bust. To a return-minded critic, a large cash pile earning modest interest is lazy capital — money that could be deployed into growth, returned to shareholders, or put to work at a higher return, but instead sits earning a bank rate while depressing the return on equity. Which reading wins depends on what the company finally does with the money — which is where the new projects come in.

The tell to watch: a company that hoards cash for years is, in effect, saying it hasn't found a use for it that beats holding it. The recently announced capacity expansion and the EV-anode project are the first signs of that changing. How much of the ₹3,767 Cr gets committed — and to what returns — will reshape the investment case more than any single quarter's electrode price.
08

The new bets: capacity expansion and EV anodes

The first real signs of the cash being put to work — including a genuine left-field move.

For years the knock on Graphite India was that it did nothing with its cash. That is starting to change on two fronts, and one of them is a surprise: alongside a straightforward expansion of electrode capacity, the company is stepping into synthetic graphite anode materials (SGAM) — a component of the lithium-ion batteries that power electric vehicles.

1 · Electrode capacity expansion

The core, unsurprising bet is more of what it already does well. Graphite India has flagged a phased expansion of electrode capacity, with the first phase expected to be commissioned in the current financial year. The logic is the structural EAF/decarbonisation demand from the previous section: if the world will burn more electrodes over the coming decade, a low-cost, debt-free incumbent adding capacity into that demand — and positioned in the premium UHP grade — is playing to its strengths. The risk is timing: adding capacity into a cyclical market only pays if the extra tonnes meet a healthy spread rather than a glut.

2 · Synthetic graphite anode materials (SGAM)

The more interesting move is the diversification into battery anode materials. Graphite is not only the stuff of electrodes — it is also the anode (the negative electrode) inside almost every lithium-ion cell. As EV and energy-storage demand grows, and as countries try to build supply chains outside China (which dominates anode material today), a company that already understands graphite at industrial scale has a credible adjacency to move into. The same needle-coke and graphitising know-how that makes an electrode is relevant to making synthetic anode material.

Why the anode bet is more than a press release

It is easy to dismiss a legacy commodity firm "pivoting to EVs" as a fashionable label. But the connection here is real chemistry, not marketing: needle coke — the very material Graphite India buys to make electrodes — is also a feedstock for synthetic battery anodes, and it was partly the diversion of needle coke toward batteries that helped trigger the 2018 electrode shortage in the first place. So the company is not lurching into an unrelated field; it is walking down its own supply chain into a fast-growing, China-dominated market where non-Chinese supply is strategically prized. Whether it becomes material to earnings is unproven and years away — but it is a logical use for the cash and the expertise, not a vanity project.

Keep it in proportion: for now, electrodes are ~everything and the anode business is a seed. Treat SGAM as optionality on the cash pile, not as a reason to own the stock today. It matters if and when it consumes real capital and starts contributing real revenue — milestones to track, not results to bank.
09

The industry and the rivals

A small global club, one domestic twin, and China always in the background.

Graphite-electrode manufacturing is a concentrated global industry — a handful of scaled producers, because the process is hard, the raw material scarce, and the quality bar for UHP grade high. In India the conversation is really Graphite India versus HEG, the two domestic majors, with a small set of global names competing worldwide and Chinese supply hovering over the whole market as the swing factor.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things define the competitive picture. First, the domestic pairing: Graphite India and HEG are the two Indian scaled players, and they largely rise and fall together on the same global electrode cycle — this is less a share-stealing duel than two boats on the same tide. The sharper contrast between them is temperament and structure, not market share. Second, and more important than any single rival: China is the swing producer. The 2018 boom happened because China took capacity offline; a future bust could happen because China puts capacity back on. For a company whose entire profit is a global price spread, the single most powerful competitive force is not a named competitor at all — it is the aggregate on/off switch of Chinese supply.

Financials & valuation

A trough year, a fortress balance sheet, and a P/E that lies about both.

FY26 was a down-leg of the cycle wearing the disguise of a growth year: revenue up on record volumes, but margins squeezed and Q4 in the red. The valuation, meanwhile, is unusually tricky to read because a huge slice of the market cap is cash and the earnings denominator is depressed — which makes the headline P/E close to useless unless you know what you are looking at.

The one number that settles the thesis

Forget the P/E. The metric that proves or breaks Graphite India is the electrode realisation-minus-needle-coke spread per tonne, watched alongside capacity utilisation and the direction of global EAF-steel demand. That spread is the entire earnings engine: it is what turned FY18 into a ₹2,000 Cr year and FY26 into a ₹171 Cr one on the same tonnage. If electrode prices firm while needle coke eases — spread widening — profit multiplies off this trough regardless of the P/E, and the cash-backed enterprise value of ~₹8,700 Cr looks cheap against normalised mid-cycle earnings. If the spread stays compressed or Chinese supply floods the market, the operating business keeps earning a thin return and the cash pile is doing most of the heavy lifting in the price. Track the spread and utilisation each quarter; they are the scoreboard, and the reported profit is just their arithmetic.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A debt-free cyclical at the mercy of a price it cannot control.

Graphite India enters the next phase of the cycle in an enviable financial position — debt-free, cash-rich, running flat-out, with a structural demand tailwind and its first real growth projects underway. What it cannot buy with any of that is control over the one variable that decides its earnings: the electrode spread. The forward debate is whether the decarbonisation-driven demand and the fortress balance sheet outweigh the boom-bust reality of a pure spread cyclical.

The bull case

  • Structural EAF tailwind — steel decarbonisation shifts output toward scrap-melting furnaces that consume electrodes, giving demand a rising floor.
  • Fortress balance sheet — ~₹3,767 Cr net cash, debt-free, unpledged; can outlast any bust that sinks leveraged rivals, and buys the operating business for an EV of ~₹8,700 Cr.
  • Spread leverage to the upside — the same plant that earns ₹171 Cr in a trough earned ₹2,000 Cr+ in a boom; a tightening market re-rates profit violently higher.
  • High barriers — UHP electrodes and needle coke are hard to make; only a small global club competes, protecting long-run pricing power.
  • Cash finally at work — capacity expansion plus the EV-anode (SGAM) optionality give the idle war chest a growth use.

The bear case

  • Extreme price cyclicality — earnings are boom-bust and near-impossible to forecast; a glut can push quarters into losses, as Q4 FY26 showed.
  • Needle-coke dependence — the key input is scarce, imported, FX-exposed and volatile; a coke spike with soft electrode prices squeezes the spread from both ends.
  • China / global overcapacity — Chinese swing supply plus GrafTech, Resonac, Tokai and domestic HEG cap pricing whenever capacity returns.
  • Lazy-capital critique — a large cash pile earning a bank rate depresses return on equity until it is deployed at a genuinely higher return.
  • Demand tethered to steel — ultimately hostage to the global steel cycle; a downturn cuts EAF output and, with it, electrode consumption.
The bottom line

Graphite India is a rare thing: a violently cyclical commodity maker that has already survived its own worst enemy — leverage — by banking a once-in-a-generation windfall and refusing to gear up. That leaves a debt-free plant, running flat-out, positioned in the premium grade, with a slow decarbonisation tailwind behind demand and a cash pile worth a third of the market cap. But none of it changes the fact that a good year and a bad year are the same rods at a different price. The next chapters will be written in three concrete, checkable events: the quarterly electrode-minus-needle-coke spread turning up or staying compressed, the phase-one capacity expansion commissioning into that spread, and the first real capital committed to the SGAM anode venture. Watch those three, and you are watching the actual company — not the P/E, which on trough earnings tells you only where in the cycle you happen to be standing.
