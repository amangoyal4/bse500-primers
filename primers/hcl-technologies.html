00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

HCL Technologies (it now brands itself HCLTech) is India's third-largest IT-services company, behind TCS and Infosys. It sells the technology work that runs the world's large companies — building and running their software, managing their data centres and cloud, digitising operations — to clients mostly in the United States and Europe, delivered largely by Indian engineers at a fraction of Western cost. FY26 (year ended March 2026) revenue was $14.7 billion (≈₹1,30,144 crore), with about 227,000 employees across 60 countries.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is built in three modes, not one. Unlike pure-services peers, HCL runs three distinct businesses: (1) IT & Business Services — the classic outsourcing/applications/infrastructure work (~72% of revenue); (2) Engineering & R&D Services (ERS) — designing the products themselves, from car software to chips to medical devices (~16%); and (3) HCLSoftware / Products & Platforms (P&P) — an actual enterprise-software products company that owns and sells software (~12%). That software arm makes HCL structurally different from TCS and Infosys.
  • Steady, cash-rich, dividend-heavy. HCL earns a ~17% operating (EBIT) margin, converts most of its profit to cash, carries net cash, and returns most of its profit to shareholders — FY26 dividend was ₹60/share and management has pledged to pay out at least 75% of net income. FY26 net profit was about ₹17,361 crore.
  • The whole sector faces one giant question: generative AI. The same AI that HCL sells to clients can also automate the very coding and support work that fills its timesheets. FY26 growth was a modest 3.9% in constant currency, and FY27 guidance is a cautious 1–4% — the industry is in a slow patch while it works out whether AI is a tailwind (more projects) or a headwind (fewer billable hours). HCL's answer: lean into it, with ~$620m of "Advanced AI" annualised revenue and 135,000+ staff trained on GenAI.
In plain terms

Think of HCL as a giant, highly disciplined engineering workforce for hire — 227,000 people the world's banks, carmakers, airlines and tech firms rent to build and run their technology. What makes HCL unusual among Indian IT majors is that it doesn't only rent out people: it also designs the physical and digital products its clients sell (the ERS arm — think the software inside a car or a jet engine), and it owns a shelf of real enterprise software products (BigFix, Domino, Commerce, AppScan and more, bought from IBM) that it licenses out like Microsoft or Oracle would. So HCL is part body-shop, part R&D lab, part software house. That mix gives it higher-margin, more annuity-like income than a pure-services rival — but it also makes the story more complicated, and the software arm has been a lumpy, slow grower.

01

IT-services economics 101: how a services giant earns

No coding required — just the handful of numbers that drive the whole industry.

For its biggest segment, an Indian IT-services company is, financially, a labour-arbitrage business: it hires engineers (mostly in India) at low cost, bills them out to Western clients at a markup, and pockets the spread. The art is keeping as many engineers as possible busy on paying work, at the lowest possible average cost, for the highest possible billing rate. A few terms unlock the entire model.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters)
HeadcountThe number of billable engineers. Revenue scales roughly with people — HCL has ~227,000 staff. Growing revenue usually means growing (or re-skilling) this base.
Billing rateWhat a client pays per engineer per hour/day. Higher-value work (cloud architecture, AI, engineering design) bills more than routine support. Rising rates lift margin without adding people.
UtilisationThe share of staff actually working on paid client projects vs. sitting "on the bench". Higher utilisation = more revenue on the same payroll. A primary margin lever.
PyramidThe mix of cheap juniors to expensive seniors. A "fat" base of freshers under fewer senior architects keeps average cost low. Managing the pyramid is core to margin.
AttritionHow fast staff quit. High attrition forces costly hiring, training and wage hikes; low attrition protects margin and client continuity. HCL's was ~12.5% in FY26 — comfortably low.
TCV / bookings"Total Contract Value" of new deals signed — the order book that converts into future revenue. HCL booked $9.3bn of new-deal TCV in FY26. The forward-looking demand gauge.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (Billing rate − cost per engineer) × billable engineers. So a services firm grows and protects margin in four ways — add people (volume), bill them higher (move up to premium work), keep them busy (utilisation), and keep average cost low (a fat pyramid, more offshore work, low attrition). Because so much of the cost base is people, small swings in utilisation, wage inflation or the rupee move margins quickly. This is why investors obsess over headcount, utilisation, attrition and the rupee–dollar rate every quarter.

Two ways to price the work

  • Time-and-materials (T&M) — the client pays per engineer-hour. Simple, but the firm only earns more by deploying more people, so AI that cuts hours directly threatens this model.
  • Fixed-price / managed-services — the firm commits to an outcome (run this whole system; deliver this project) for a set price. Here, if the firm can do the work with fewer people (via automation/AI), the saving becomes its profit, not the client's. This is why the industry is racing to shift toward outcome-based deals.
Why this is cyclical: IT-services demand rises and falls with the confidence of large Western enterprises — especially banks (BFSI is the biggest spender). When the economy is uncertain or interest rates bite, clients delay discretionary projects, deal cycles lengthen, and growth stalls — which is exactly the slow patch the industry, including HCL, has been in through FY26.
02

The three-mode model — and why P&P makes HCL different

The single most important thing to understand about HCL versus its peers.

TCS and Infosys are, overwhelmingly, pure services businesses — they sell people and outcomes. HCL is deliberately built in three modes, and the third one — owning and selling software products — is the structural differentiator. Read this section once and the rest of HCL clicks into place.

Mode 1 — IT & Business Services (ITBS): the core engine

This is the business most people mean when they say "Indian IT". HCL builds, modernises and runs the software and IT systems that keep large enterprises operating: application development and maintenance (writing and supporting business software), cloud and infrastructure (migrating systems to AWS/Azure/Google, running data centres and networks — historically HCL's strongest suit), digital transformation (rebuilding customer-facing systems), cybersecurity, and business process operations (BPO). It grew ~3.7% in constant currency in FY26 — steady, not spectacular, reflecting the cautious enterprise-spending environment.

Mode 2 — Engineering & R&D Services (ERS): the differentiated growth engine

Covered in depth in the next section. In short: instead of running a client's internal IT, ERS helps clients build the products they sell — the embedded software in a car, the firmware in a network router, the design of a semiconductor, the platform inside a medical device. It is HCL's signature strength and its fastest-growing services line (~9.8% CC in FY26).

Mode 3 — HCLSoftware / Products & Platforms (P&P): the part rivals don't have

This is the structural difference. HCLSoftware is a genuine enterprise-software products company housed inside an IT-services giant. It owns a portfolio of software it sells under licence and subscription — much of it acquired from IBM in 2018–19 for ~$1.8 billion (products like BigFix for device management, Domino/Notes for collaboration, AppScan for security testing, Commerce, Unica for marketing, and a data/AI platform). It also runs DevOps and automation products. Because customers pay recurring licence and subscription fees, this is a higher-margin, more annuity-like business than selling engineer-hours — closer in spirit to an Oracle or SAP than to a services firm.

Why owning software changes the economics

In services, you earn by selling people's time — to grow, you hire more people, and your margin is capped by what you pay them. In software products, you build the product once and sell the same code to many customers; each extra licence is almost pure profit, and customers pay year after year (an "annuity"). That's why software typically earns far higher margins than services. For HCL, the P&P arm does three things: it lifts the blended margin above what a pure-services mix would deliver; it provides recurring, less people-dependent revenue ($1.05bn of annual recurring revenue, or ARR); and it gives HCL its own intellectual property to wrap services around. The catch: it's lumpy and slow-growing — software revenue can swing on the timing of large licence renewals (Mode 3 declined sharply in Q4 FY26 on seasonality and delayed deals), and management is mid-way through a multi-year shift from one-off licences toward steadier subscriptions.

03

ERS — engineering as the differentiated growth engine

HCL's signature strength, and the part of the business hardest for rivals to copy.

Most Indian IT firms help a client run the technology inside their own offices — the email, the HR system, the banking platform. Engineering & R&D Services (ERS) is different: it helps a client design and build the products they sell to the world. When a carmaker needs the software that controls a vehicle, a chip company needs help designing a processor, or a medical-device firm needs the platform inside a scanner, that work is product engineering — and HCL is one of the largest providers of it globally.

What ERS actually does

  • Software-defined vehicles & automotive — the embedded software, infotainment, ADAS (driver-assistance) and connectivity stacks inside modern cars. As cars become "computers on wheels", this is a fast-growing pool of work.
  • Semiconductors & chip design — VLSI design, verification and the engineering services around silicon — a strategically prized, high-value capability.
  • Aerospace, industrial & medical devices — firmware, safety-critical systems, and platform engineering for regulated, complex products.
  • Telecom & networking — the software inside routers, 5G equipment and network gear; HCL has deep heritage with networking-equipment makers.
  • Digital engineering & IoT — connecting physical products to the cloud, and the data platforms that sit behind them.
Why ERS is a structurally better business

ERS matters for three reasons. First, it grows faster: R&D budgets are tied to product roadmaps, which large firms protect even in downturns, and the shift of every industry toward software-defined products (cars, machines, medical devices) keeps expanding the work. ERS grew ~9.8% in FY26 — more than double the rest of HCL. Second, it's stickier and higher-value: product engineering requires deep domain expertise (you can't design automotive safety software with a generic team), so clients don't switch easily and billing rates are higher. Third, it's a genuine differentiator — while all the majors now have ERS arms, HCL built this capability early and at scale, and it is proportionally larger and more central to HCL than to TCS or Infosys. It is, in a real sense, HCL's answer to "what do you do that your bigger rivals don't do as well?"

The AI angle on ERS: product engineering is, if anything, helped by AI more than threatened by it — generative-AI tools accelerate design, simulation, code generation and testing, letting engineers do more, while the volume of software going into products keeps exploding. That makes ERS a relatively defensive place to be exposed within the AI-disruption debate.
04

HCLSoftware (P&P) — the products business inside a services giant

The clearest break from the pure-services model — and its own little software company.

If ERS is HCL's standout services strength, HCLSoftware is its standout structural difference. It is a real, standalone-style enterprise-software products business — the kind of thing that sells licences and subscriptions, not engineer-hours — tucked inside an IT-services major. Understanding it explains both why HCL's margins are resilient and why its growth profile is lumpier than peers'.

How HCL came to own software products

The defining move was the ~$1.8 billion acquisition of seven IBM software products in 2018–19. Rather than just servicing other companies' software, HCL bought the software itself — taking over the products, the customers and the recurring revenue. That transformed a small products unit into a billion-dollar-plus software business and gave HCL intellectual property no pure-services rival possesses.

Why the business model is different — annuity software economics

  • Recurring revenue. Customers pay ongoing licence/subscription fees, giving a predictable annuity base — HCLSoftware's annual recurring revenue (ARR) is ~$1.05bn. That income doesn't depend on staffing a project.
  • Higher margin. Software built once and sold many times carries far higher incremental margins than billing engineer-hours, lifting HCL's blended profitability above a pure-services peer.
  • Subscription transition. Management is steering the mix from one-off perpetual licences toward subscriptions — better long-term (steadier, higher lifetime value), but it depresses reported revenue during the switch.
The honest weakness: HCLSoftware has been a lumpy, slow grower. Its revenue can swing on the timing of big licence renewals (concentrated in particular quarters), and Mode 3 fell sharply in Q4 FY26 on seasonality and delayed client decisions; ARR was roughly flat (−0.5% CC) for the year. The strategic promise — a high-margin annuity engine — is real, but the execution has been uneven, and bulls want to see HCLSoftware return to steady growth as the subscription pivot matures. It is simultaneously HCL's most differentiated asset and its most inconsistent one.
05

The company and who controls it

From a 1976 garage-era startup to a global major — still founder-family controlled.

HCL is one of the original Indian IT pioneers, founded in 1976 by Shiv Nadar and a small group of colleagues — predating the modern outsourcing boom. The listed entity, HCL Technologies, was carved out to focus on software/IT services and has grown into a top-three Indian IT major. Crucially, it remains tightly controlled by the founding Nadar family, now led by daughter Roshni Nadar Malhotra.

Timeline

  • 1976
    Shiv Nadar and co-founders start HCL, originally making calculators and early personal computers — one of India's first technology companies.
  • 1991
    HCL pivots decisively into IT and software services, riding the offshore-outsourcing wave alongside TCS, Infosys and Wipro.
  • 1999
    HCL Technologies lists on Indian exchanges — the software/services arm becomes a public company.
  • 2000s
    Builds a distinctive strength in infrastructure management and engineering services (ERS); the "Employees First, Customers Second" management philosophy draws global attention.
  • 2018–19
    Acquires seven IBM software products for ~$1.8bn, creating a billion-dollar-plus owned-software business (later branded HCLSoftware) — the move that sets HCL apart from pure-services peers.
  • 2022
    Rebrands to HCLTech; consolidates the three-mode structure (Services + ERS + Software).
  • 2024–25
    Founder Shiv Nadar transfers ~47% of his promoter stake to daughter Roshni Nadar Malhotra (SEBI-approved), formalising the family succession; she becomes the largest individual holder in the promoter group.
  • FY26
    Revenue $14.7bn / ₹1,30,144 Cr; ~227,000 staff; FY27 growth guidance a cautious 1–4% as the sector navigates the AI-driven demand reset.

Ownership & control

  • Promoter group ~60.8%. The Nadar family controls a clear majority — unusually high for a large Indian IT firm — primarily through Vama Sundari Investments (Delhi) (~44%) and related entities. This gives the family decisive, stable control.
  • Succession to Roshni Nadar Malhotra. Shiv Nadar's 2024–25 gift of ~47% of his stake to his daughter cements her as the controlling figure of the promoter group and chair of the broader HCL enterprise — a rare, clean generational handover at an Indian large-cap.
  • Professional management. Day-to-day, HCLTech is run by a professional CEO and leadership team; the family controls capital allocation and strategy at the top while operations sit with managers — the model most Indian promoter-led majors use.
Why the ownership structure matters

A ~61% promoter holding means the Nadar family's interests dominate — generally a positive for stability and long-term thinking (no activist can force a break-up, strategy doesn't lurch quarter to quarter), and the family's strong dividend preference is why HCL pays out so much of its profit. The flip side is the usual promoter-control caveats: minority shareholders rely on the family's judgement on capital allocation, and a clean succession (to Roshni Nadar) removes a key-person overhang but concentrates a great deal in one family line. For most of HCL's history this has been a steadying force rather than a problem.

Margins and the levers that move them

Where HCL's ~17% operating margin comes from — and what pushes it up or down.

HCL earned an EBIT (operating) margin of 17.2% in FY26 (about 17.9% excluding one-off restructuring costs) — solidly profitable, though structurally a touch below the very top of the pack (TCS and Infosys typically run a little higher). The margin story in one paragraph

Because people are the dominant cost, HCL protects margin chiefly by keeping engineers busy (utilisation), tilting the workforce toward cheaper juniors (a fat pyramid, helped by adding ~11,700 freshers in FY26), holding attrition low (~12.5%, so it isn't constantly paying up to replace leavers), and shifting work to higher-value, higher-billing services (engineering, AI, cloud) and toward its high-margin software products. Working against it: annual wage hikes, the cost of investing in AI and re-skilling, occasional restructuring charges, and currency swings (a weaker rupee flatters margins since costs are in rupees and revenue in dollars). 07

The generative-AI question

The defining force hanging over every IT-services company — including HCL.

No issue matters more to the sector right now. Generative AI can write code, answer support tickets, generate test cases and automate the routine work that fills millions of IT-services timesheets. That cuts both ways: it can shrink the billable hours a firm sells (a threat), or it can create a wave of new AI projects and let firms do more for clients (an opportunity). Which force wins — and how fast — is the single biggest swing factor in HCL's future.

The bear view: AI eats the billable hour

If AI lets one engineer do the work of three, a time-and-materials business that sells hours simply has fewer hours to sell. Routine application maintenance, basic coding, level-1 support and testing — exactly the high-volume, lower-value work that staffs the bottom of the pyramid — are the most automatable. In that scenario, growth slows, pricing comes under pressure, headcount growth stalls (notice the industry is hiring fewer freshers), and the classic labour-arbitrage model deflates. The cautious FY26 growth (~3.9% CC) and soft FY27 guidance (1–4%) are partly the market pricing in this uncertainty.

The bull view: AI is a new demand wave HCL can sell

Enterprises don't deploy AI by themselves — they need someone to integrate it into messy, decades-old systems, build "AI factories", govern the data, and re-engineer workflows. That is services work, and it is new work. HCL is positioning hard here:

  • ~$620m of "Advanced AI" annualised revenue reported in FY26 — a real, growing book of AI-specific business, including a $100m+ "AI factory" program win.
  • 135,000+ employees trained on generative AI — re-skilling the workforce so AI augments rather than replaces its billing base.
  • AI woven into delivery — using AI internally to deliver fixed-price contracts more cheaply (where the saving becomes HCL's margin), and embedding AI into its software products and engineering services.
How to actually think about it

The honest answer is "both, in sequence". Near term, AI is a deflationary drag: it compresses pricing on routine work and makes clients pause to figure out their own AI strategy, which lengthens deal cycles and caps growth — the slow patch we're in now. Medium term, AI is more likely a tailwind, because the world's enterprises will spend enormously to adopt it, and they'll hire firms like HCL to do it. HCL's specific edge in this transition is its mix: its ERS/engineering business is helped by AI (faster design, more software in products), and its software products can embed AI to add value — so HCL is less purely exposed to the "AI eats the billable hour" threat than a firm that does only commodity application support. The key things to watch: are headcount and billing holding up, is "Advanced AI" revenue compounding, and does growth re-accelerate once the enterprise-AI spending wave actually breaks?

What actually drives the demand

IT-services spending is fundamentally derived demand — it rises and falls with the confidence and budgets of HCL's clients. The big drivers: (1) the health of Western banks (BFSI is the largest buyer; when banks invest in modernisation and compliance tech, Indian IT booms; when they freeze budgets, it stalls); (2) the broad enterprise tech-spending cycle (cloud migration, digital, cybersecurity, and now AI adoption — each a multi-year wave); (3) cost pressure (paradoxically, when Western firms want to cut costs, they outsource more to India, so HCL can win share even in a downturn); and (4) the rupee (a weaker rupee boosts reported margins). The FY26–FY27 slow patch reflects cautious US enterprise spending and the AI-driven pause — not a loss of HCL's competitiveness.

The industry and the rivals

Where HCL sits in the global IT-services pecking order.

HCL competes in a global, consolidating IT-services market against both Indian peers and Western incumbents. It is firmly India's #3 by revenue — smaller than TCS and Infosys, larger than Wipro and Tech Mahindra — and differentiates itself through engineering depth (ERS) and its owned-software products (P&P).

How HCL wins (and where it's challenged)

Against the bigger Indian peers, HCL's pitch is differentiation, not scale: deeper engineering/R&D capability, an owned-software portfolio that lets it offer products plus services, and a strong heritage in infrastructure/cloud operations. That mix has let HCL grow at least as fast as — often faster than — TCS and Infosys over the past decade, narrowing the gap. Where it's challenged: its margins sit a notch below the leaders (partly the cost of the software business and a different revenue mix), and against Accenture and the global majors it lacks the front-end consulting brand that wins the very largest transformation mandates. The consolidation pressure runs one way — clients increasingly want fewer, larger vendors who can do everything — which favours the top tier HCL sits within.

How to think about the valuation

HCL trades like what it is — a high-quality, cash-rich, dividend-heavy IT major in a slow-growth window. Large-cap Indian IT typically commands a premium-but-not-frothy multiple (often in the low-to-mid-20s times earnings), with HCL usually a touch cheaper than TCS and Infosys, reflecting its slightly lower margins and the lumpier software business. The case for paying up: a wide, diversified book, a differentiated engineering/software mix, low attrition, a huge new-deal pipeline ($9.3bn TCV), and a >75% dividend payout that hands shareholders cash directly. The case against: growth has slowed to low single digits, FY27 guidance (1–4%) implies the sluggishness persists, and the AI question caps how much multiple-expansion the market will grant. Net: a defensive, income-generative way to own the Indian IT story, priced for steady — not spectacular — returns until growth re-accelerates.

11

Capital return and the promoter structure

One of the most generous dividend payers in Indian IT — by design.

HCL's capital-allocation policy is a defining feature of the stock. As a mature, cash-generative business with limited need to reinvest heavily, it returns the bulk of its profit to shareholders — a policy strongly shaped by the controlling Nadar family's preference for steady income.

  • FY26 dividend of ₹60/share. Paid across the year (including a ₹24 Q4 dividend), this represents a high payout against ~₹64 of EPS — i.e. the great majority of earnings handed back as cash.
  • A formal ≥75% payout commitment. Management has pledged to distribute at least 75% of net income to shareholders over a multi-year period — an unusually explicit, shareholder-friendly framework.
  • Net cash, low capital intensity. A services business doesn't need factories; HCL funds growth (and the occasional acquisition) from cash flow while still returning most profit, and carries little debt.
Why HCL is, in effect, a dividend stock

Because growth is modest and the business is asset-light and cash-generative, HCL doesn't need to hoard profit — so it pays most of it out. For shareholders this turns HCL into something close to a quasi-bond with growth optionality: a reliable, large dividend yield underpinned by a diversified, sticky revenue base, plus upside if the AI-demand wave re-accelerates growth. The ≥75% payout policy is a direct expression of the Nadar family's control and income preference — the same promoter dominance that some view as a governance caveat also delivers one of the most dependable capital-return stories in the sector. The risk to keep in mind: a payout that high leaves less buffer if earnings fall, and means growth must come from organic execution and bolt-on M&A rather than retained-earnings reinvestment.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A differentiated, cash-rich major — navigating the industry's biggest transition.

HCL is a genuinely well-built IT major: India's #3, with a diversified book, a differentiated engineering-and-software mix that pure-services peers lack, low attrition, a large order pipeline and a generous, dependable dividend. The investment debate is almost entirely about the pace of growth through the generative-AI transition — not the durability of the business.

The bull case

  • Differentiated three-mode model — ERS engineering strength + an owned-software (P&P) annuity that rivals don't have, lifting margin and stickiness.
  • AI-resilient mix — engineering and software are helped by AI more than threatened; ~$620m Advanced-AI revenue already compounding.
  • Cash machine + huge payout — net cash, strong free cash flow, ≥75% dividend commitment (₹60/share in FY26).
  • Big order book — $9.3bn FY26 new-deal TCV and broad-based bookings underpin forward revenue.
  • Low attrition, disciplined execution — ~12.5% attrition and a controlled cost base protect margin.

The bear case

  • Growth has stalled — ~3.9% CC in FY26 and just 1–4% guided for FY27; the demand environment is soft.
  • The AI threat to billable hours — automation could deflate pricing on routine services work and cap headcount growth.
  • Lumpy, slow software arm — HCLSoftware ARR roughly flat; revenue swings on licence timing and the subscription transition.
  • US/discretionary exposure — biggest market (the US) grew slowest; reliant on Western enterprise budgets and the rupee.
  • Margins below the leaders & promoter concentration — ~17% EBIT trails TCS/Infosys; ~61% family control concentrates governance.
The bottom line

HCL is a high-quality, differentiated, cash-generative IT major that has earned its top-three position and stands out from pure-services peers through its engineering depth and owned-software portfolio. The business risk is low; the open question is growth — whether the generative-AI transition turns from a near-term drag into the medium-term demand wave the bulls expect, and whether HCLSoftware can finally grow steadily. For now it offers a defensive, income-rich way to own the Indian IT story, with optionality if AI re-accelerates the sector. The numbers to watch each quarter: constant-currency growth and FY27 guidance (is the slow patch ending?), Advanced-AI revenue (is the new wave real?), HCLSoftware/ARR (is the annuity finally compounding?), and margin and attrition (is execution holding?).
