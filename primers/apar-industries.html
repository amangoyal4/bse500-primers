APAR Industries, the wire that carries the grid

A complete walk through the world's largest aluminium-conductor maker — how a company that sells overhead power lines, transformer oil and speciality cables actually earns money, why it is a near-pure bet on India's ₹9-lakh-crore transmission build-out, how "premiumisation" and exports lift margins in an otherwise commodity business, and why a ₹22,900-crore-revenue supplier trades at ~57× earnings.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

APAR Industries makes the physical hardware that moves electricity from where it's generated to where it's used — the bare aluminium and steel wires strung between transmission towers, the specialised oil that keeps power transformers cool and insulated, and the cables that run under floors, seas and factory racks. It is the world's largest manufacturer of aluminium overhead conductors, India's largest transformer-oil maker (~50% market share), and a fast-growing player in premium power and telecom cables. Founded in 1958 by the Desai family, it is a classic three-segment electrical-materials business — deeply cyclical, metal-and-crude-linked, and now riding one of the strongest capex cycles of its history.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a leveraged bet on grid build-out. FY26 revenue hit a record ₹22,902 Cr (+23.3%), with net profit at an all-time-high ₹977 Cr (+19%). The largest segment, Conductors (₹12,712 Cr, ~55% of revenue), sells directly into transmission-line projects — so APAR's order book is, in effect, a real-time gauge of how much power grid India and the world are building.
  • Margins are thin but the mix is improving. Blended EBITDA margin is only ~9% — this is a metals-conversion business, not a software one. The whole strategy is premiumisation: shifting toward high-efficiency HTLS conductors, speciality oils and elastomeric/e-beam cables that earn far more per tonne. Premium products are now ~44% of conductor sales, up from the mid-30s a few years ago.
  • The market pays a growth multiple for a cyclical. At ~₹57,000 Cr market cap on ₹977 Cr of profit, APAR trades near 57× earnings — a rich price for a commodity-linked supplier, justified only if the T&D/renewables capex super-cycle and the premium mix-shift keep compounding. It earns a genuinely high ~31% ROCE and is majority family-owned (promoters ~57.8%).
In plain terms

Every solar farm, wind park and new city needs power carried to it — and that power travels along aluminium conductors on towers, is stepped up and down by transformers (which run on APAR's oil), and is distributed by cables. APAR sells the picks-and-shovels of the electricity grid. When countries build or upgrade their grids — as India, the US and Europe are all doing at once to connect renewables — APAR sells more, at better prices. The catch: its raw materials are aluminium, copper and crude-derived base oil, whose prices swing wildly, and it competes on price for much of its volume. So the investment case is really: can APAR keep tilting its product mix toward the premium, harder-to-copy end fast enough to turn a low-margin commodity into a durable growth compounder?

01

What APAR actually makes: the grid, decoded

Three products, one theme — the movement of electricity. No engineering degree required.

To understand APAR you first have to picture the journey of electricity from a power plant to a wall socket, because APAR supplies a piece of hardware at almost every step. That journey has three stages, and APAR has a business in each.

One company, three products, one demand driver: the more grid the world builds, the more of all three it needs. That is why APAR is best understood as a single bet on electrification, sold three ways.

The three products in one sentence each

  • Conductors — the bare aluminium (and aluminium-steel) wires you see strung between transmission towers. They carry high-voltage electricity over long distances. APAR is the world's largest aluminium conductor maker and the largest conductor exporter from India, with customers in 100+ countries.
  • Transformer & specialty oils — highly refined oils (sold under the POWEROIL brand) that fill power transformers to cool and electrically insulate them, plus a wide range of industrial/automotive lubricants, white oils and process oils. APAR is India's largest transformer-oil maker (~50% share) and among the world's top three or four.
  • Cables — insulated power cables, telecom and optical-fibre cables, and specialty cables (elastomeric, e-beam irradiated, solar, railway, defence). This is APAR's highest-growth, highest-margin segment and the one it is investing hardest in.
Why "bare wire" is a real business

It's tempting to think a metal wire is a pure commodity anyone can extrude. But a modern transmission conductor is engineered: it must carry more current without sagging into trees, survive decades of heat, wind and corrosion, and meet exacting utility specifications. The premium end — HTLS conductors ("High-Temperature Low-Sag") — lets a utility push two to three times more power down an existing tower line without rebuilding it. For a grid operator that can't easily acquire new land for towers, that is enormously valuable — and it earns APAR a far fatter margin per tonne than plain wire. The whole APAR thesis, across all three segments, is this same move: from commodity to engineered premium.

02

The three-segment model and how the money splits

Conductors are the biggest by revenue; cables and oils shape the margin.

APAR reports as three segments, and understanding their different economics is the key to the business. Conductors are the volume giant but a low-margin, project-driven business. Oils are a steady, crude-linked cash cow. Cables are the small-but-rich growth engine. The blended result — ~9% EBITDA margin — hides very different underlying stories.

Why one company holds all three

These segments look unrelated — wire, oil, cable — but they share a customer (power utilities, EPC contractors, transformer makers), a demand driver (electrification), a distribution reach (100+ export countries), and a raw-material sophistication (metallurgy and refining). Holding all three lets APAR cross-sell into the same grid projects, smooth out the cycles of any one product, and reinvest the steady oil cash into the higher-growth conductor and cable capacity. The trade-off is complexity and a blended margin that looks unimpressive next to a pure premium-cable play — which is exactly why the market watches the mix shift so closely.

03

The company and who controls it

A 1958 family business, still run by the founder's grandsons.

APAR is a Desai-family business, founded in 1958 by Dharmsinh D. Desai as "Power Cables Pvt. Ltd." during India's post-Independence electrification drive. Nearly seven decades on it remains promoter-controlled, run by the founder's grandsons — a rare case of a large, professionally-managed Indian industrial that is still both family-led and majority family-owned.

Timeline

  • 1958
    Incorporated by Dharmsinh D. Desai as a maker of power-transmission conductors, with capital under ₹1 lakh — riding India's rural-electrification push.
  • 1968

  • 2000s

  • 2007–08
    Signs a licensing deal with Italy's Eni S.p.A. to make and sell automotive/industrial lubricants under the Eni brand in India; acquires Uniflex Cables (2008) to enter the cables segment.
  • 2010s
    Pioneers HTLS / high-efficiency conductors and specialty cables (elastomeric, e-beam, optical fibre); deepens exports to the US and Europe.
  • FY22–FY26
    Rides the India T&D capex super-cycle: revenue roughly triples to ₹22,902 Cr; premium-product mix climbs toward ~44%; large greenfield cable capacity added; raises ~₹1,000 Cr via QIP to fund expansion.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Desai family holds ~57.8%, with essentially no pledged shares — a strong, aligned, skin-in-the-game ownership base. Kushal Desai is Chairman & Managing Director and Chaitanya Desai is Managing Director; both are grandsons of the founder with ~three decades in the business.
  • Balance sheet: APAR carries modest debt (~₹956 Cr of borrowings at FY26, up from ~₹585 Cr as capex and working capital rose) — the business is working-capital-heavy but not over-leveraged. Book value ~₹1,343/share.
  • Capital returns: it pays a healthy dividend — a ₹60/share final dividend recommended for FY26 — while retaining enough to fund the cable and conductor capacity expansion. Return ratios are strong: ROCE ~31%, ROE ~20%.
04

Conductors — the transmission-capex proxy

APAR's biggest business, and the one that ties it most directly to the grid cycle.

The conductor division is APAR's flagship: ~55% of revenue (₹12,712 Cr in FY26, +32.7%) and its global claim to fame as the world's largest aluminium conductor manufacturer. It is also the segment that makes APAR a near-pure play on transmission-line construction — because a conductor is only bought when someone is actually building or upgrading a power line.

From commodity wire to engineered conductor

  • Standard conductors (AAAC, ACSR) — the workhorse aluminium and aluminium-steel-reinforced wires for conventional lines. High volume, low margin, competed on price.
  • HTLS / high-efficiency conductors (HEC) — the premium tier. HTLS ("High-Temperature Low-Sag") conductors let a utility reconductor an existing line to carry 2–3× the power without new towers or land — a decisive advantage where right-of-way is scarce. These earn far higher margins and are the heart of the "premiumisation" story.
  • OPGW & conductor accessories — optical ground wire (a transmission cable with fibre inside for grid communications) and hardware. APAR has strung optical ground wire over 10,000+ route-km and conductors over 35,000+ km of line globally.

Because conductor pricing largely passes through the aluminium price, APAR guides investors to watch EBITDA per tonne and the premium mix % — the true measures of profitability — rather than headline revenue, which balloons and shrinks with metal prices.
Why the order book is the number to watch

Conductors are sold into projects, so APAR carries a pending order book that tells you how much work is already booked. At the end of FY26 the conductor order book stood at ₹7,671 Cr, with a 38.9% export mix, on new-order inflow of ~₹11,450 Cr for the year (+24%). Read that as a leading indicator: a rising order book with a healthy export share means grid customers — in India and abroad — are placing firm orders, which underpins the next several quarters of conductor revenue. A shrinking book would be the first sign the capex cycle is cooling.

05

Transformer & specialty oils — the steady cash cow

Lower-growth, but India's #1 position and a crude-linked stabiliser.

The oils division (~23% of revenue, ₹5,373 Cr in FY26) is APAR's oldest non-conductor business and its steadiest. APAR is India's largest transformer-oil maker — roughly 50% national share (higher, ~60%, in power-transformer oil) — and among the world's top three-to-four transformer-oil producers. It is a cash-generative, capital-light anchor that funds the flashier growth segments.

Two halves of the oils business

  • Transformer oil (POWEROIL) — highly refined mineral oil that fills power and distribution transformers to insulate the windings and carry away heat. Every new transformer needs oil, and existing ones need top-ups and replacement — so this rides the same grid-and-electrification wave as conductors, but more steadily.
  • Specialty oils & lubricants — white oils and petroleum jelly (used in cosmetics, pharma, food-grade applications), process oils, and industrial/automotive lubricants (sold under the Eni licence). APAR is a leading domestic auto-lube supplier to tractor and engine OEMs (TAFE, Eicher, Escorts, ITL) and blends ~350+ grades sold to 120+ countries, with sizeable UAE operations.
Why this segment is crude-linked, not grid-linked

Transformer and specialty oils are made from refined base oil, a crude-oil derivative. So the segment's revenue and margins move with oil prices and the base-oil spread, not just with grid capex. When crude and base-oil prices are stable and APAR can pass costs through, it earns a reliable spread (guided in the region of a few thousand rupees of EBITDA per kilolitre). When base-oil costs spike or the rupee weakens, margins get squeezed until pricing catches up. The upshot: oils grow slowly (single-digit % — FY26 revenue rose just ~5.6%) but throw off dependable cash that smooths the more volatile conductor and cable earnings.

06

Cables — the small-but-rich growth engine

The highest-margin segment, and where APAR is investing hardest.

The cables division (~27% of revenue, ₹6,220 Cr in FY26, +25.8%) is the youngest of the three — built off the 2008 Uniflex acquisition — and the one management is betting on for the next leg of growth. Unlike bare conductors, cables are insulated, value-added and less commoditised, so they carry a richer margin (EBITDA around 10%+, the best of the three segments) and a longer premium runway.

The cable menu (jargon decoded)

  • Power cables — insulated cables (LV/MV/HV) that carry electricity underground and within buildings and industrial plants, where overhead lines aren't practical.
  • Elastomeric & e-beam cables — rubber-insulated and electron-beam-irradiated cables that withstand heat, flexing and harsh environments (mining, ports, renewables, railways, defence). Higher-spec, higher-margin, harder to copy.
  • Optical-fibre & telecom cables — for data and communications networks, riding the telecom/data-centre build-out.
  • Solar, railway & specialty cables — application-specific cables for the fast-growing renewable, rail-electrification and infrastructure markets.
Why cables are the margin story

A bare conductor competes almost purely on aluminium price and conversion cost. A specialty cable, by contrast, wins on engineering, certification and reliability — a mining or defence customer can't afford a cheap cable that fails. That lets APAR earn a structurally better margin here than in conductors, and grow it faster. The strategy is explicit: pour capital (a large greenfield cable expansion, part-funded by the ~₹1,000 Cr QIP) into premium cables so this segment's share of profit rises faster than its share of revenue. If it works, the whole company's blended margin drifts up over time even without conductors changing — which is a big part of why the market pays a growth multiple. The pending cable order book was ~₹1,800–1,900 Cr at FY26.

The competitive reality: cables is also the most crowded of APAR's segments, pitting it against well-capitalised specialists — Polycab, KEI, RR Kabel, Havells and (in optical fibre) Sterlite Technologies domestically, and Nexans/Prysmian globally. APAR's edge is its specialty/elastomeric niche and its grid relationships, not scale in mass-market house wire — so execution and mix discipline matter more here than anywhere else.
07

The export engine and premiumisation

The two levers that turn a commodity supplier into a margin-expander.

If you strip APAR down to its investment DNA, two intertwined forces drive the bull case: a large-and-growing export franchise, and a relentless shift toward high-value products. Both do the same thing — lift the profit APAR earns on each tonne of metal or kilolitre of oil it converts — and both are what separate APAR from a plain conductor mill.

Exports ran near ~30% of revenue in FY26 but fell during the year as US business dropped on the prevailing tariff situation; domestic demand (grid capex) surged ~30% and more than offset it. The conductor order book's ~39% export mix shows overseas demand remains structurally strong.

The premiumisation lever is the more durable of the two. Across all three segments, APAR is trading commodity volume for engineered value:

  • Conductors: premium HTLS/HEC mix up to ~44%, earning multiples of standard-wire margin per tonne.
  • Oils: shifting toward higher-grade specialty and white oils rather than plain transformer oil.
  • Cables: tilting toward elastomeric, e-beam and specialty cables over commodity power cable.
Why premiumisation matters more than revenue growth

Because so much of APAR's headline revenue is just the aluminium/copper/crude price passing through, top-line growth can be illusory — revenue can jump simply because metal got dearer, with no extra profit. The real signal is whether APAR earns more margin per physical unit. Premiumisation and exports are the two ways it does that: a US utility reconductoring with HTLS, or a European buyer of specialty cable, pays for engineering and reliability, not just metal. Every point of premium-mix and every export order that sticks is worth more to the bottom line than a point of commodity volume — which is precisely why analysts track EBITDA/tonne and mix %, and why the tariff-driven US softness in FY26 is watched closely.

08

The grid-capex super-cycle — APAR's defining tailwind

The single structural force an analyst leads with: the world is rebuilding its grids at once.

APAR's fortunes rise and fall with one number above all others: how much money is being spent building and upgrading electricity grids. And on that measure, APAR is sitting in the middle of one of the strongest capex cycles in its history — driven by a rare global coincidence of India, the US and Europe all needing to expand transmission at the same time to connect renewable power.

India's transmission network is set to expand from ~5 lakh circuit-km (early 2026) toward ~6.48 lakh by 2032. Every new line needs conductors; every new substation needs transformers (and oil); renewables integration needs both — a demand pull APAR sells directly into.

Why is this happening now, everywhere at once? Three reasons converge:

  • Renewables need new grid. Solar and wind are built where the sun and wind are — deserts, coasts — far from cities. Connecting them requires vast new transmission that didn't exist for fossil plants sited near demand. India alone is wiring ~280 GW of solar into the grid by 2030.
  • Ageing Western grids need rebuilding. US and European grids are decades old and congested; reconductoring with HTLS and adding lines is now a policy priority — a direct addressable market for APAR's premium conductors and specialty cables.
  • Electrification of everything. EVs, data centres and industrial electrification lift power demand structurally, forcing grid expansion on top of the renewables build.
Why this is APAR's "China question" — the force that defines the story

For a solar-panel maker, the defining variable is China and tariffs; for a children's hospital, it's the birth rate. For APAR, it is grid capex. The entire investment case rests on the belief that the T&D super-cycle is structural and multi-year, not a blip — that ₹9-lakh-crore-by-2032 of Indian transmission spend (plus Western reconductoring) keeps the order book full and lets APAR sell an ever-richer premium mix into a supply-constrained market. That is genuinely powerful. But it cuts both ways: capex cycles are lumpy. FY26 already saw a temporary slowdown in ordering (only ~16 transmission schemes awarded versus ~45 the prior year, on bandwidth constraints), a reminder that even in a structural boom the flow of orders can stall for a few quarters. A supplier priced at ~57× earnings is priced for the cycle to keep running — so the pace of scheme awards is the single most important thing to watch.

The honest tension: the structural demand is real and long, but APAR doesn't control its timing. If awards keep pausing — for grid-planning bottlenecks, land/right-of-way delays, or tariff-driven export softness — order books can plateau even as the decade-long story stays intact. Bulls own the cycle's length; bears own its lumpiness.
09

Cyclicality & input costs — the metals-and-crude machine

The flip side of a commodity-linked supplier: thin margins and heavy working capital.

APAR's raw materials are aluminium, copper and crude-derived base oil — three of the most volatile commodities there are. That volatility, plus the project-based nature of its biggest segment, makes APAR a fundamentally cyclical, working-capital-heavy, margin-sensitive business. Understanding this is the key to reading its financials honestly.

How input costs flow through

  • Pass-through pricing. Most conductor and oil contracts pass raw-material cost changes on to customers — so APAR is largely insulated from the level of metal/oil prices over time. But this makes revenue misleading (it inflates when aluminium is dear) and leaves APAR exposed to timing — the gap between buying metal and getting paid.
  • Margin sensitivity. Because base margins are thin (~9% blended), even small swings in the metal spread, conversion cost or product mix move profit meaningfully. This is why APAR guides to per-tonne and per-KL margins, not headline revenue.
  • Working-capital intensity. Long project cycles, inventory of expensive metal, and receivables from utilities/EPC contractors tie up substantial cash — so faster growth can actually consume cash and require borrowing (FY26 debt rose to ~₹956 Cr partly for this reason).
  • Forex exposure. With ~30% of revenue exported and imported base oil/metal, the rupee's moves cut both ways — a weaker rupee helps export competitiveness but raises input costs; hedging manages, but doesn't remove, the risk.
Why this is the mirror-image of the tailwind

Section 08 is the reason to own APAR; this section is the reason it trades cyclically. A supplier of commodity-linked hardware to lumpy capex projects will always have thin margins, volatile revenue and hungry working capital. The premiumisation strategy is, in large part, an attempt to escape this — to earn engineering margin instead of conversion margin, so profit depends less on the aluminium price and the award calendar. The degree to which APAR succeeds at that escape is the difference between a cyclical re-rated too high and a genuine structural compounder. Watch EBITDA/tonne, the premium mix, and the cash-conversion cycle together: if margins-per-unit and cash generation keep improving through the cycle, the premium multiple is earning its keep; if they don't, it's a cyclical wearing a growth costume.

Financials & valuation

Record numbers, high returns — and a demanding multiple.

APAR's recent record is strong: revenue has roughly tripled in five years (~29% sales CAGR), profit compounded even faster (~44% 5-year CAGR), and it earns high returns on capital. The debate, as with most quality cyclicals in a hot capex cycle, is entirely about the price — a ~57× multiple on a ~9%-margin metals-linked supplier is a lot to defend.

How to think about the ~57× multiple

At ~₹57,000 Cr on ₹977 Cr of profit, APAR trades near 57× trailing earnings — extraordinary for a commodity-linked, thin-margin supplier, and well above where such businesses historically sat. The multiple only makes sense if you believe two things compound together for years: (1) the grid-capex super-cycle keeps volumes and order books full, and (2) premiumisation and exports keep lifting margin-per-unit so profit grows faster than the underlying metal tonnage. Bulls see a category leader with a multi-year structural tailwind and improving mix, deserving of a premium. Bears note the obvious: a ~9%-margin cyclical priced at 57× has no margin for error — a stalled award cycle, a metal-spread squeeze, tariff-hit exports, or a working-capital shock could each trigger a sharp de-rating from a high base. Both are right at different points in the cycle, which is why the order book, EBITDA/tonne and scheme-award pace are the quarterly tells.

11

The rivals and the competitive set

Different competitors in each segment — and that's part of the point.

APAR doesn't face one competitor; it faces a different rival in each segment. That fragmentation is both a defence (no single peer attacks all three businesses) and a challenge (it must stay sharp on three fronts at once). Its strongest moats are in conductors and transformer oil, where scale and reputation are hardest to replicate; its most contested ground is cables.

Where APAR's moat is real — and where it isn't

In conductors and transformer oil, APAR's moat is genuine: decades of utility approvals, global scale, export relationships in 100+ countries, and premium-product know-how (HTLS, OPGW) that a new entrant can't quickly build. Utilities are conservative buyers — they qualify suppliers slowly and stick with proven ones — which protects the incumbent. In cables, the moat is thinner and the field is crowded with well-funded specialists; here APAR is a challenger differentiating on specialty/high-margin niches rather than out-scaling Polycab or KEI. The strategic bet is to use the cash and credibility from the moaty segments to win a durable slice of the richer cable market — a sensible plan, but one where execution, not entrenched advantage, decides the outcome.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine category leader in a super-cycle — priced for the super-cycle to continue.

APAR is one of the clearest ways to own the global electrification and grid-build theme: a world-leading conductor maker, India's #1 in transformer oil, and a rising specialty-cable player, all run by an aligned founding family. The business quality and the tailwind are real. The debate is whether a thin-margin cyclical, however well-positioned, deserves a ~57× multiple — and how lumpy the ride will be.

The bull case

  • Structural grid super-cycle — ₹9-lakh-crore Indian T&D capex to 2032 plus Western reconductoring; APAR sells directly into all of it.
  • Category leadership — world #1 in aluminium conductors, India #1 in transformer oil; deep moats in two of three segments.
  • Premiumisation lifting margins — premium conductor mix ~44%, richer specialty cables and oils raising profit-per-unit.
  • Export optionality — ~30% export share and ~39% conductor order-book export mix; global grid demand beyond India.
  • High returns, aligned owners — ~31% ROCE, ~58% promoter holding with ~nil pledge, healthy dividend.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~57× earnings on a ~9%-margin cyclical leaves no room for error; vulnerable to sharp de-rating.
  • Lumpy order flow — transmission-scheme awards paused in FY26 (~16 vs ~45); the cycle is structural but not smooth.
  • Commodity & forex risk — aluminium/copper/crude and rupee swings squeeze thin margins; working-capital-hungry growth.
  • Export/tariff headwind — US business fell sharply in FY26 on tariffs; a key growth lever is currently pressured.
  • Contested cables — the growth segment faces well-funded rivals (Polycab, KEI, Sterlite Tech); execution, not moat, decides it.
The bottom line

APAR is a genuinely well-positioned electrical-materials leader in the middle of a powerful, multi-year grid-build cycle, with a real premiumisation lever and an aligned owner. The business risk is moderate; the valuation and cyclicality risks are the live ones. The story over the next few years will be told in a handful of numbers each quarter — the conductor order book and scheme-award pace (is the capex cycle still running?), EBITDA per tonne and premium mix (is premiumisation actually lifting margins?), and export recovery (does the US come back?). Get those right and APAR earns its premium; disappoint on any, and a 57× cyclical has a long way to fall.
