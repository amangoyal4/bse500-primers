00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Aurionpro Solutions Limited is a mid-cap enterprise-technology company that writes and sells mission-critical software — and, increasingly, the systems it runs on — to two very different sets of customers. On one side sit banks and financial institutions across India, South-East Asia, West Asia and Africa, which buy Aurionpro's transaction-banking, lending, treasury and payments platforms. On the other sit governments and infrastructure operators, which buy its automated fare-collection gates for metros, its tolling systems for highways, its data-centre builds, and its cybersecurity and smart-city products. It is headquartered in Mumbai (Navi Mumbai), listed on both the NSE and BSE, and it has spent the last few years loudly repositioning itself away from being a plain IT-services vendor and toward being a software-product and platform company.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Two engines, one share. FY26 revenue of ~₹1,411 Cr splits almost evenly between a Banking & Fintech segment (~₹751 Cr, +19%) and a Technology Innovation Group / TIG segment (~₹660 Cr, +22%). One sells software that moves a corporate treasury's money; the other sells systems that open a metro gate. They share a balance sheet and a management team but almost no customers — and they behave very differently through a cycle.
  • Growth has been fast, and partly bought. Revenue has roughly tripled from the ~₹500 Cr region a few years ago to ₹1,411 Cr, with PAT up to ~₹212 Cr in FY26. Some is organic; a meaningful slice came from a string of acquisitions — smart-city, AI, and transaction-banking IP — funded by a ~₹568 Cr QIP-plus-preferential raise and followed by a 1:1 bonus. That growth came with equity dilution, which is why promoter holding has slipped to roughly 27%.
  • The whole re-rating rests on one word: recurring. A services company is valued like a body-shop; a software-product company that earns durable, high-margin annually recurring revenue (ARR) is valued like an asset. Aurionpro trades at ~19× earnings precisely because the market is paying up for the second story. Whether it deserves that depends on how much of the ₹1,411 Cr is licence-and-SaaS software versus one-off implementation and hardware-heavy project work.
In plain terms

Picture two businesses stapled together inside one listed company. The first is a banking-software house: it licenses platforms like transaction-banking and lending suites to banks in India, Singapore, the Gulf and Africa, then earns fees to implement them and — the prize — subscription income to keep running them. The second is an infrastructure-technology contractor: it wins government and operator orders to install fare-collection gates in metros, toll lanes on highways, and to build data centres, booking lumpy revenue when projects execute. Aurionpro is trying to convince the market that both halves are really "software products" deserving a rich multiple. The banking half genuinely is becoming that. The infrastructure half is partly a project-execution business dressed in software language. The investment case is the tension between those two truths.

01

The two engines: what Aurionpro actually is

One company, two segments that share almost nothing but a cap table.

The single most useful thing to grasp about Aurionpro is that it is not one business but two, reported as two segments with roughly equal revenue and genuinely different economics, customers and rhythms. Read the company as a pair and everything else — the margins, the lumpiness, the valuation debate — falls into place.

The two engines do not merely sell different things; they keep score differently. Banking software is a game of reference customers and renewal rates: win a marquee bank, implement well, and you earn implementation fees now and subscription fees for years. Transit and infrastructure is a game of tenders and execution: win a metro's fare-collection order, manufacture and install the gates, book revenue as milestones clear, then chase the next tender. One compounds quietly; the other lurches. Blending them into a single P&L is convenient for headline growth but obscures which half is doing the work in any given quarter.

The one distinction that decides how you value the whole thing

Whenever a company markets itself as a "software-products" business, ask what fraction of revenue is genuinely software — licence and subscription — versus services and hardware wrapped around it. For Aurionpro the candid answer is: the Banking & Fintech engine is well down the road to a real product-and-ARR model, while a chunk of TIG is closer to systems integration and capital-project execution with software content. Both can grow fast and profitably. But a rupee of recurring bank-software subscription and a rupee of one-off fare-gate installation are worth very different multiples. The market is currently paying a product multiple on the whole; the bear worry is that part of it is really a project business.

02

Services → products: the re-rating thesis, explained

Why the same rupee of revenue is worth two or three times as much once it recurs.

To understand why Aurionpro's shares carry a software multiple rather than an IT-services one, you need to understand the ladder every Indian tech company dreams of climbing — from selling people's time at the bottom to owning intellectual property that earns money in its sleep at the top. The distance between the rungs is the entire investment case.

The top rung — annually recurring subscription on owned IP — is where software valuations live: predictable, high-margin, sticky. Aurionpro's stated strategy is to shift as much revenue as possible up this ladder, especially in Banking & Fintech. The re-rating is the market betting it will.

The mechanics are worth spelling out. At the bottom rung, an IT-services firm sells engineer-hours: revenue grows only if you hire more people, margins are thin, and nothing recurs — every year you start from zero. Climb to the top rung and the picture inverts. You build a platform once, sell the right to use it to many banks, and each one pays you every year to keep using it. Revenue arrives whether or not you add headcount; gross margins on software are high; and because ripping out a core banking platform is painful and risky, customers rarely leave. That stickiness is why investors pay perhaps 4–6× revenue for a genuine banking-software product business and barely 1–2× for a services shop doing similar top-line numbers.

Aurionpro's pitch is that it is deliberately walking up this ladder: converting bespoke bank projects into repeatable licensed platforms, and licensed platforms into cloud-delivered subscriptions with growing ARR. The acquisitions (section 06) are, in this frame, IP purchases — buying rungs rather than building them. When management talks about "platform-led" growth, rising subscription mix, and higher margins, this ladder is what they mean.

Why the word "recurring" carries the valuation

A one-off ₹10 Cr project and a ₹10 Cr-a-year subscription look identical in this year's revenue line and could not be more different in worth. The project must be re-won next year from scratch; the subscription arrives again automatically, at high margin, from a customer who would find it costly to switch. Put a few years of those subscriptions together and you have an annuity — and markets value annuities richly. So the number that actually moves Aurionpro's valuation is not this year's growth rate but the share of revenue that recurs. Every percentage point of the ₹1,411 Cr that shifts from one-off to subscription is worth a multiple of itself in market value. That is the re-rating engine — and also its vulnerability, because if the recurring share stalls, the rich multiple has nothing to stand on.

03

The banking-software stack: what Aurionpro sells to lenders

The higher-quality engine — transaction banking, lending, payments, and now AI.

The Banking & Fintech segment is the half of Aurionpro that most looks like the software-product company it wants to be. It sells a stack of platforms that sit inside a bank and run core money-movement and lending functions — the kind of software that, once installed, becomes load-bearing infrastructure a bank is loath to replace. Its customers are lenders across India, South-East Asia, the Gulf and Africa, and its edge is being a nimbler, cheaper, more configurable alternative to the giant global core-banking vendors.

The product lines

  • iCashpro+ — transaction / corporate banking. The flagship. An integrated platform for cash management, liquidity, trade finance and corporate digital banking — the software a bank's corporate clients use to move and manage money. This is the stickiest, most reference-driven product in the stable.
  • SmartLender & Omnifin — lending and credit. End-to-end credit-risk and loan-origination/management systems, spanning the life of a loan from application to monitoring. Lending platforms are in structural demand as banks and NBFCs digitise credit.
  • AuroPay / AuroPaybiz — payments. Payment and merchant/business-payments products, positioning Aurionpro in the fast-growing digital-payments layer around the core bank.
  • FXConnect — treasury & FX. Treasury and foreign-exchange dealing software for banks' own market operations.
  • Arya.ai — enterprise AI / GenAI. An AI platform-as-a-service for banking and insurance, acquired in 2024. This is the segment's bet that AI/GenAI becomes a paid feature layer across the whole banking stack — the newest and most speculative rung.

The geography that matters — and its wrinkle

Aurionpro's banking software sells well beyond India. Its heartland has historically leaned on Asia and the Gulf, where mid-sized banks want configurable, cost-effective platforms without paying global-vendor prices, and it has references across the Middle East, South-East Asia and Africa. That international exposure is a genuine strength — it widens the addressable market far past domestic banks — but it also imports geopolitical risk. In FY26 management flagged that Middle East conflict stalled both deal closures and project execution in the banking segment during the fourth quarter, a reminder that a Gulf-heavy client base rises and falls with a volatile region.

The surprise hiding in the geography line

It is tempting to read "global banking software, Middle East and Asia heavy" as the whole story — but the reported numbers tell a more Indian one. India is the largest revenue geography by far (roughly two-thirds of the group), with Asia-Pacific the next slice and the US/Europe and Middle East/Africa smaller still. So Aurionpro is, today, primarily a company selling into India that also exports — not a Gulf software house with an Indian office. That matters two ways: the domestic digitisation of Indian banking and government is its biggest tailwind, and the celebrated international wins, while strategically valuable as references, are not yet the bulk of the revenue. The overseas story is a promise the company is building toward, not the ground it stands on today.

04

The transit & smart-city arm: software that also pours concrete

The fast-growing, lumpier engine — fare gates, tolls, data centres, and the earnings-quality catch.

The Technology Innovation Group is where Aurionpro gets most interesting — and most misunderstood. Marketed under a software banner, a large part of TIG is really an infrastructure-technology execution business: it wins big orders from metros, highway authorities and data-centre operators, then designs, manufactures, installs and commissions physical systems. It is growing fast (TIG revenue rose ~22% in FY26) and it lands headline-grabbing orders — but it books revenue in lumps as projects execute, and it carries hardware and construction content that a pure software business does not.

What TIG actually does

  • Automated Fare Collection (AFC). The gates, validators, card-readers and back-end that let you tap into a metro. Aurionpro won AFC technology orders from the Delhi Metro and Chennai Metro Phase II, and manufactures the gates in India at a facility in Ghaziabad. It has also delivered transit solutions internationally (including work referencing California and Mexico).
  • Tolling & smart mobility. Electronic tolling and intelligent-transport systems for highways — a government-order-driven business tied to India's road-infrastructure build-out.
  • Data centres. Design-and-build of data-centre facilities. In FY26 Aurionpro won its largest such mandate — a ~₹350 Cr hyperscale order to develop an ~30 MW AI-ready facility in South India — plus a Mumbai brownfield build and other operator orders. This is a capital-project line, not a licence line.
  • Smart-city, kiosks, cybersecurity & IAM. Self-service kiosks, queue-management, customer-experience systems, and identity/access-management (IAM) and cybersecurity products — the more genuinely software-and-product corner of TIG, partly built on the acquired SC Soft smart-city IP.
Why the "lumpiness" is structural, not a one-off: government and infrastructure orders are large, tender-won, and billed against physical milestones — a fare-gate rollout or a data-centre build lands revenue in uneven chunks over quarters and can slip when a client delays. In FY26, Aurionpro flagged exactly this: revenue from a large hyperscaler data-centre contract slipped from Q4 into the next financial year because of client-side planning delays. That is normal for project businesses and abnormal for software ones — and it is why TIG's growth, while real, is bumpier and lower-margin than the banking engine's.
The counter-intuitive fact about a "software" company

Here is what surprises people who buy Aurionpro as a clean software play: some of its most celebrated wins are, in accounting terms, closer to engineering-procurement-construction than to licensing code. Manufacturing fare-gates in Ghaziabad and building a 30 MW data centre involve steel, sites, supply chains and commissioning risk — the economics of a contractor, not a SaaS vendor. This is not a criticism; the transit and data-centre markets are large, fast-growing, and Aurionpro's software content genuinely differentiates its bids. But it means the consolidated margin and cash-flow profile is a blend of a high-margin software annuity and a working-capital-hungry project book. Anyone modelling the company as if all ₹1,411 Cr behaves like subscription software will be repeatedly surprised by the quarters.

05

The company, the owners, and the dilution

A three-decade-old firm reinvented — funded by selling a lot of stock.

Aurionpro is not a start-up. It was founded in the late 1990s and has been listed for two decades, but the business investors are buying today was substantially rebuilt over the last several years through acquisitions, capital raises and a strategic pivot. Understanding who owns it, and how much stock was issued to get here, is central to the case — because the reinvention was paid for in equity.

Timeline

  • 1997
    Aurionpro Solutions founded; grows over the 2000s as an IT-services and banking-technology provider, listing on Indian exchanges (BSE code 532668).
  • 2010s
    Builds and buys banking-software and cybersecurity capability; establishes an international footprint across Asia and the Gulf, but remains a mid-tier services-plus-products company.
  • ~2020

  • FY24
    Revenue jumps ~35% to ~₹887 Cr. Raises ~₹568 Cr via a QIP (~₹368 Cr at ₹2,000/share, to Goldman Sachs, Malabar, Alchemy and others) plus a ~₹200 Cr preferential issue; issues a 1:1 bonus.
  • FY25
    Revenue ~₹1,173 Cr, PAT ~₹188 Cr. Acquires Arya.ai (67% stake, ~$16.5m) for enterprise AI, plus Fintra Software and Fenixys to deepen transaction-banking IP.
  • FY26
    Revenue ~₹1,411 Cr (+20%), EBITDA ~₹282 Cr, PAT ~₹212 Cr. Wins Delhi/Chennai Metro AFC orders and a ~₹350 Cr hyperscale data-centre mandate; ₹2/share final dividend. Q4 dented by Middle East disruption, a data-centre revenue slip, and a one-off labour-code cost.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter holding is unusually low for an Indian mid-cap — around 27%. It has fallen from roughly 31% in 2023 to about 26.9%, diluted by the equity issued to fund the growth sprint. The key promoter figure, Paresh Chandulal Zaveri, is associated with a mid-teens personal stake.
  • Institutions have leaned in. Foreign institutional investors hold roughly 15–16%, and the FY24 QIP drew marquee names (Goldman Sachs funds, Malabar). A wide public float (~55%) and low promoter stake mean the shareholder register, not a dominant family block, effectively sets the tone.
  • The reinvention was equity-funded. The QIP-plus-preferential raise and the acquisitions it paid for are why revenue and profit have compounded so fast — and why the share count has grown and return ratios (ROE ~13%) sit below where a pure, capital-light software business would.
Read the dilution honestly, both ways. Issuing ~₹568 Cr of equity and buying IP is a legitimate way to build a platform company quickly, and Aurionpro deployed the cash into real capability (AI, transaction-banking, smart-city). But dilution is not free: it grows the numerator (profit) while also growing the denominator (shares), so per-share value compounds more slowly than headline revenue, and return ratios are held down by the enlarged equity base. A low promoter stake (~27%) also means less founder skin-in-the-game than a typical Indian mid-cap, and raises the ordinary governance vigilance any acquisitive, related-party-active company warrants. None of this is a red flag by itself; all of it is why per-share metrics and capital-allocation discipline deserve close watching.
06

The acquisition machine: buying rungs on the ladder

How Aurionpro assembled its platform — and the risk that comes stapled to it.

A striking amount of Aurionpro's transformation was purchased rather than built. Over the last few years it has bolted on a series of targeted acquisitions, each buying a specific capability or piece of intellectual property that it could plug into its two engines. This is a deliberate strategy — buy a rung on the ladder from section 02 instead of climbing it slowly — and it is both the reason for the fast growth and a source of the risks a buyer must weigh.

The logic is sound: rather than spend years and uncertain R&D building an AI platform or a smart-transit stack from scratch, Aurionpro bought proven IP and teams and integrated them into a distribution machine that already sells to banks and governments. Arya.ai gives it an AI layer to upsell across its banking customers; SC Soft gave it the transit capability now winning metro orders; Fintra and Fenixys thickened the transaction-banking product. Bought well and integrated well, each acquisition should climb the whole company a rung.

The device that captures this company: a QIP funding both a shopping spree and a work-in-progress pile

Aurionpro is best understood as a single pool of raised equity being pushed simultaneously into two very different uses. Part of the ~₹568 Cr went to buy IP — Arya.ai, Fintra, Fenixys — the high-multiple, software-annuity ambition. Another part effectively underwrites the working capital of a project book — the receivables and execution of fare-gate rollouts and data-centre builds that tie up cash before they pay out. So the same balance sheet is asked to behave like an acquisitive software platform and like an infrastructure contractor at once. That dual demand on capital is why growth has been fast, why return ratios are only mid-teens rather than software-like, and why capital allocation is the discipline that will make or break the story. It is a portrait that fits no other primer in this library.

Acquisition-fuelled growth carries specific, well-known risks that apply here: integration risk (bought teams and code must actually merge into the platform), goodwill and intangibles on the balance sheet that could impair if a target underperforms, dilution from equity-funded deals, and heightened related-party and governance scrutiny that any serial acquirer with a low promoter stake attracts. The strategy can compound value beautifully or quietly destroy it depending entirely on execution — verify integration progress and cash conversion, not just the deal announcements.
07

Margins, cash and the quality-of-earnings question

Healthy headline margins — with a blend and a working-capital tail underneath.

On the surface Aurionpro's profitability looks like a software company's: FY26 EBITDA of ~₹282 Cr on ~₹1,411 Cr of revenue is a ~20% margin, and PAT margin runs around 15%. Those are respectable, product-like numbers — far above a commodity IT-services shop. But they are a blend of a high-margin banking-software annuity and a lower-margin, hardware-inclusive project book, and the cash-conversion behind them carries the working-capital tail that comes with government orders and acquisitions.

What to watch below the margin line

A 20% EBITDA margin is only as good as the cash it turns into, and this is where the two-engine, acquisitive structure earns extra scrutiny. Government and data-centre projects tie up receivables and inventory before they pay; acquisitions add goodwill and amortising intangibles that flatter EBITDA relative to reported profit and cash. So the earnings-quality checklist for Aurionpro is specific: is operating cash flow keeping pace with reported PAT, or is profit accumulating in receivables and work-in-progress? Are the acquired businesses contributing real cash, or carrying impairment risk? And is the recurring/subscription share of banking revenue actually rising, lifting the durable-margin base? Answer those and you know whether the ~20% margin is a solid floor or a flattering blend.

08

The industry and the rivals

Two competitive arenas, two different sets of giants.

Because Aurionpro runs two engines, it fights on two battlefields against two entirely different rosters of competitors — and it is a challenger, not the leader, in both. In banking software it takes on specialist product houses and global core-banking majors; in transit it takes on infrastructure-technology and fare-system specialists. Its differentiation is being smaller, nimbler and more configurable than the giants, and more software-led than the pure integrators.

The benchmark that matters most is the banking-software peer set, because that is where the premium multiple is earned or lost. Intellect Design Arena is the closest listed Indian comparator — a larger, product-pure banking-software company whose valuation frames how the market prices this space. Newgen Software (content/process platforms for banks) is another reference for a product-and-ARR Indian tech story. Against these, Aurionpro is smaller and less purely a product house, but it is growing fast and pushing its subscription mix — the question is whether it can close the quality gap or remains a hybrid.

The competitive truth to hold in mind

Aurionpro wins where the giants are too expensive or too rigid and the pure integrators are too undifferentiated. A mid-sized Gulf or South-East Asian bank that cannot justify a Temenos-scale core-banking replacement, but wants a modern, configurable transaction-banking or lending platform, is squarely Aurionpro's customer. A metro that wants India-made fare-gates with integrated software at a competitive price is TIG's. That "good-enough, cheaper, more flexible" position is a real and defensible niche — but it also caps pricing power and means Aurionpro must keep winning on cost, configurability and reference wins rather than on being the category-defining platform. It is a challenger's economics, which is exactly why the recurring-revenue climb matters so much: it is the route from challenger margins to owner's margins.

09

Financials & valuation

A fast-growing hybrid on a full-but-not-extreme multiple — the debate is the mix, not the growth.

Aurionpro trades at roughly 19× trailing earnings for a market capitalisation near ₹3,910 Cr, on a business that has compounded revenue ~20–35% a year and earns ~15% net margins. That is a full valuation — the market is clearly paying for the software-product story, not pricing a services shop — but it is not a stratospheric one. The whole valuation question reduces to a single judgement: how much of this company is a durable, recurring software platform, and how much is a lumpy, capital-hungry project book?

The one number that settles the thesis: the recurring-revenue (ARR) share of banking software

At ~19× earnings, Aurionpro does not need to be cheap — it needs its recurring, subscription revenue to keep rising as a share of the mix, especially inside Banking & Fintech. That single metric — call it the ARR share, tracked quarter by quarter and read alongside operating cash conversion (is PAT turning into cash, or into receivables and work-in-progress?) — is what proves or breaks the case. If the subscription share climbs and cash follows profit, the multiple is justified and per-share earnings compound as the acquired IP monetises. If growth stays parked at ~20%, the recurring share stalls, and the reported margin turns out to lean on lumpy project revenue and amortising intangibles, then the market is paying a software price for a hybrid contractor — and the multiple, not the business, is the thing at risk. Rising ARR share with cash to match is the scoreboard; everything else is noise around it.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A challenger scaling into two big markets — judged on whether the revenue truly recurs.

Aurionpro enters FY27 as a genuinely different company from the services firm of a few years ago: two roughly equal engines, a stack of acquired IP, marquee banking and metro references, and a stated march up the software-value ladder. What it must now prove is that the growth is durable and high-quality — that the banking engine keeps converting to recurring software revenue, that the project engine executes its swelling order book into cash, and that the one-off FY26 headwinds were exactly that. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Two large, growing markets — bank digitisation across Asia/Gulf/Africa and India's public-infrastructure build (metros, tolls, data centres) give a long runway on both engines.
  • Real mix-up the ladder — ~20% EBITDA and ~15% PAT margins are product-like, and a rising subscription/ARR share can re-rate the banking half toward pure-software multiples.
  • IP bought and integrating — Arya.ai (AI), SC Soft (transit), Fintra/Fenixys (transaction banking) add capability the company now sells through an existing distribution machine.
  • Marquee references & order wins — Delhi/Chennai Metro AFC and a ₹350 Cr hyperscale data-centre win are credibility markers that beget more orders.
  • One-off FY26 drags should clear — Middle East disruption, a slipped data-centre quarter and a labour-code cost were largely non-recurring; FY27 can re-accelerate.

The bear case

  • Part project, priced as product — a chunk of TIG is lumpy, hardware-inclusive, lower-margin execution that does not merit a software multiple.
  • Decelerating growth on a full multiple — FY26 slowed to ~20%; ~19× earnings needs the recurring share to keep climbing, and it may not.
  • Equity-funded, working-capital-heavy — dilution and project receivables hold ROE at mid-teens; per-share value compounds slower than headline revenue.
  • Acquisition & governance risk — serial IP purchases carry integration and goodwill-impairment risk, and a ~27% promoter stake invites extra scrutiny of capital allocation and related parties.
  • Geopolitical & order-timing exposure — Gulf conflict and client-driven project slippage can dent quarters, as FY26 showed; challenger economics cap pricing power against Intellect/Temenos/Newgen.
The bottom line

Aurionpro Solutions is a mid-cap that has done the hard, expensive work of reinventing itself — from a services vendor into a two-engined banking-software and smart-infrastructure company — and is now asking the market to believe both engines deserve to be valued as software. The banking half is earning that belief; the transit-and-build half is partly a project business wearing a product label, and the consolidated numbers blend the two. The next test is concrete and datable: whether the recurring/subscription share of Banking & Fintech keeps rising through FY27, with operating cash flow tracking reported profit, as the AI and transaction-banking acquisitions monetise and the metro and data-centre order books convert to cash. Clear that bar and the ~19× multiple looks like fair pay for a compounding platform; miss it — recurring share flat, cash lagging profit, growth stuck at 20% — and the market will have paid a software price for a hybrid that still bills a lot of one-off work. The banks and the turnstiles both keep the lights on; only the recurring line tells you which kind of company is switching them.
