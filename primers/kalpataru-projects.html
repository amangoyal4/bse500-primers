Kalpataru Projects International, the contractor that wires the world

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Kalpataru Projects International (KPIL) is an engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) contractor — a firm that wins fixed-scope contracts to build infrastructure for other people, then gets paid to design it, buy the materials, and execute it on the ground. It does this across an unusually wide spread: power transmission lines and substations, factory and building shells, oil-and-gas pipelines, railway lines, water systems and urban infrastructure — in India and across roughly 70 countries.

Four facts frame the whole business:

  • Its heritage and edge is power transmission. KPIL began in 1981 as Kalpataru Power Transmission, and it is today one of the world's largest builders of transmission lines and substations — the towers and wires that move electricity from where it is generated to where it is used. That vertical is ~44% of the order book and the reason KPIL is a direct play on the global energy-transition grid spend.
  • The order book is the story. Because EPC revenue is booked as a project is built, the amount of signed-but-not-yet-executed work — the order book — is the single best gauge of the next two-to-three years. KPIL's stands at a record ₹65,457 Cr (Mar 2026), roughly 2.4× annual revenue, split 61% domestic / 39% international.
  • FY26 was a step-change year. Revenue rose 22% to ₹27,143 Cr, but net profit jumped 82% to ₹1,031 Cr — the first time it has crossed ₹1,000 Cr. That gap between 22% and 82% is not an accident; it is the deleveraging engine (below) doing its work.
  • It is deliberately deleveraging. KPIL has been selling non-core assets — transmission lines it once owned, a completed expressway, an agri-logistics arm — and using the cash to cut debt. Net debt fell ~53% to ₹915 Cr in FY26, which is why interest costs dropped and profit outran revenue.
In plain terms

A utility in Sweden, a government in Kenya, or a chemicals company in Gujarat needs a transmission line, a substation, a pipeline or a factory built — but doesn't want to run a construction army itself. It writes a fixed-price contract and hands the job to a specialist like KPIL. KPIL surveys the route, designs it, buys the steel, aluminium and cement, mobilises thousands of workers, and hands back a finished asset. It keeps nothing at the end — it isn't a tollway owner or a power producer collecting revenue for decades; it is a builder that moves on to the next contract. The money is made by winning tenders at a workable margin and then executing on time and on budget. The margins are thin (single-digit), so the two things that can wreck an EPC firm are cost overruns and clients who pay late — which is exactly why KPIL's recent obsession with cash and debt matters as much as its order wins.

01

What an EPC contractor actually does

No engineering background needed — just five ideas that explain the whole model.

"EPC" stands for Engineering, Procurement and Construction. It is the turnkey model of building large infrastructure: a client specifies what it wants, and a single contractor takes responsibility for designing it (E), sourcing all the materials and equipment (P), and physically building it (C) — usually for a fixed, agreed price. Grasp these five ideas and KPIL's financials stop being mysterious.

Why the order book ≠ profit

A record ₹65,457 Cr order book tells you KPIL will be busy; it does not by itself tell you it will be profitable. A book won at aggressive prices, or executed slowly while commodity costs rise, can convert into thin or negative margins. That is why analysts watch three things together: is the book growing (demand), at what margin was it won, and how fast is it being executed and collected (cash). KPIL's pitch is that it is now winning "high-margin, large-scale" work and being choosier — quality of book over sheer size.

02

The six verticals — diversification as strategy

One contractor, six businesses, two continents of spread — by design.

Most EPC firms are specialists. KPIL is deliberately the opposite: it runs six distinct construction businesses and spreads them across dozens of countries. The logic is simple risk management — a slump in one segment or one geography (a bad monsoon delaying water projects, a pause in Indian railway tenders, a Middle-East disruption) can be offset by strength elsewhere. Diversification is not a slogan here; it is the core of how the company survives the cyclicality that regularly wrecks single-line contractors.

What each vertical actually builds

  • Power Transmission & Distribution (T&D) — the towers, overhead lines and substations that carry high-voltage electricity across countries. KPIL's oldest and strongest business, and a genuine global leader (see §04).
  • Buildings & Factories (B&F) — industrial buildings, factories, warehouses, commercial towers, residential complexes, hospitals and data centres. This is the fastest-scaling civil business, largely inherited from JMC Projects, and grew ~19% in FY26.
  • Oil & Gas — cross-country pipelines, terminals and gas infrastructure. Small (~7% of book) but grew a striking ~55% in FY26 as India's gas-grid build-out accelerated.
  • Water — water supply schemes, treatment plants and irrigation, mostly for state and central government programmes. High-volume but the segment most exposed to slow government payments (a live issue — see §10).
  • Railways — track-laying, electrification and railway civil works, riding India's massive rail-capex cycle.
  • Urban Infrastructure — metros, flyovers, elevated corridors, tunnels and roads; grew ~49% in FY26 off a small base.
The one-line takeaway on structure: KPIL is essentially a T&D powerhouse with a large, diversified civil-and-energy contractor bolted on. That mix is why its revenue is less volatile than a pure T&D or pure roads player — but it also means no single vertical is large enough to make it a "pure" bet on any one theme.
03

The company and who controls it

A 1981 transmission firm, a transformational 2023 merger, and a name collision worth understanding.

KPIL is the engineering-and-construction flagship of the Kalpataru group, controlled by the Munot family of Mumbai. It was founded in 1981 as Kalpataru Power Transmission Limited by Mofatraj P. Munot, built a global transmission business through the 1990s and 2000s, and then transformed itself through a landmark merger.

  • Dec 2022 – Apr 2023

  • FY24–FY26

The surprise: there are two listed "Kalpatarus"

A generalist will assume "Kalpataru" is one company. It is not. KPIL is the EPC/power-transmission contractor — it builds infrastructure for others and owns almost nothing at the end. In July 2025 a completely separate group company, Kalpataru Limited, listed on the exchanges — that one is a real-estate developer building apartments in the Mumbai region and Pune. Same founding family (Mofatraj Munot; his son Parag Munot leads the property business), same brand, entirely different businesses, separate balance sheets and separate shares. If you are researching KPIL, be careful not to pull up the developer's numbers by mistake — it is one of the easiest errors to make with this name.

Ownership

  • Promoters (Munot family / Kalpataru group): ~33.6% — a controlling but not overwhelming stake, which leaves a large institutional float.
  • Institutions: combined Indian and foreign institutional ownership is high (broadly around half the company), reflecting KPIL's status as a widely-followed infra name; the JMC merger and improved governance pulled in more institutional capital.
  • Leadership: a professional management team runs day-to-day execution across the verticals and geographies, with the promoter family setting strategy and capital allocation.
04

Power T&D — the heritage business and the global grid play

The oldest vertical is also the biggest reason to own the stock.

Strip everything else away and KPIL's founding business — power transmission and distribution — is what makes it distinctive. It builds the physical grid: the lattice steel towers, the overhead conductors slung between them, and the substations that step voltage up and down. And it does this not just in India but as one of the largest transmission-line EPC contractors on the planet, with live projects across the Nordics, sub-Saharan Africa, the Middle East and South America.

Why the world's grid is the tailwind

The energy transition is usually told as a story about solar panels and wind turbines. But renewable power is generated in the wrong places — sunny deserts, windy coasts, remote hydro — and must be carried, often hundreds of kilometres, to cities and factories. That requires an enormous, multi-decade build-out of transmission lines and substations worldwide. Grids are the bottleneck of the energy transition, and every country racing to add renewables, interconnect regions, or simply strengthen an ageing network is a potential customer for a firm like KPIL. This is the structural demand that a pure Indian-infra contractor does not have.

Genuine surprise: most of its transmission wins are foreign

The intuitive assumption is that an Indian EPC company mostly builds in India. In transmission, KPIL is the reverse: in FY26, roughly 65% of its new T&D orders came from outside India — the Nordics, Latin America, Africa and the Middle East. It even owns local operating subsidiaries (Sweden's Linjemontage, Brazil's Fasttel) so it can execute like a domestic player in those markets. That global reach is the company's crown jewel and its biggest differentiator versus a purely Indian contractor — but it is also the source of the currency and country risk covered in §07.

The T&D business also carries the best margins in the portfolio, because it is the one where KPIL's decades of specialist know-how — route surveys, tower design, high-voltage substations, tricky terrain — command a premium and keep competition narrower. Management has explicitly said it is steering T&D toward "high-margin, large-scale" projects rather than chasing volume, and expects ₹13,000–15,000 Cr of T&D inflows a year.

06

Deleveraging and the asset-monetisation story

The most important thing that happened to KPIL's profit has nothing to do with winning orders.

Here is the number that should stop you: in FY26 KPIL's revenue grew 22% but its net profit grew 82%. Order wins do not explain a gap that wide. The explanation sits below the operating line, on the interest and debt rows — and it is the result of a multi-year plan to sell assets KPIL never needed to own and use the cash to pay down borrowings.

The device: decomposing 22% into 82%

Take the same order book, the same six verticals, the same ~₹27,000 Cr of building. Revenue rose 22%. Then three things stacked on top. First, margins widened — pre-tax margin improved ~120 basis points to 4.9% as richer, better-priced work flowed through. Second, and bigger, net debt was cut by more than half, from roughly ₹1,950 Cr to ₹915 Cr, so the interest bill fell — every rupee not paid to lenders drops almost straight to profit. Third, operating leverage on a larger base did the rest. Same company, same building activity; profit nearly doubled largely because it stopped feeding so much of its earnings to its balance sheet. For a thin-margin EPC firm, the finance-cost line is a profit lever as powerful as the order book — and that is the non-obvious heart of the KPIL FY26 story.

What it sold, and why it could

KPIL's deleveraging is funded by monetising non-core assets — things the group had accumulated that were not central to being a contractor:

  • Transmission BOOT assets — lines KPIL had built and then owned and operated (Build-Own-Operate-Transfer). Its stake in Kohima-Mariani Transmission was sold down to Apraava Energy — a large earlier tranche, and the residual 26% stake exiting in 2026 — turning a capital-heavy asset into cash.
  • Road BOOT assets — the Vindhyachal Expressway was sold to Actis for ₹775 Cr, with the transfer completing in early 2026 and freeing up capital tied in a completed road.
  • Non-core subsidiaries — the group has been reducing exposure to peripheral businesses such as Shree Shubham Logistics (agri-warehousing), sharpening KPIL back toward its core EPC identity.
Why this matters more than it looks

An EPC contractor should ideally be asset-light — it builds and moves on, rather than parking capital in roads or power lines it owns for decades. Over the years KPIL had drifted into owning such assets, which soaked up cash and inflated debt. Selling them does two things at once: it raises cash to cut debt, and it frees that capital to fund the growing order book (which itself hungers for working capital). The result is a virtuous loop — lower debt, lower interest, higher profit, higher returns on capital (ROCE crossed 21% in FY26) — and it is the clearest reason the market has been willing to re-rate the stock. The catch: the monetisation runway is finite. Once the non-core assets are sold, the deleveraging tailwind fades, and profit growth must come the hard way — from margins and execution.

07

The global footprint — and the risk that rides with it

The international book is KPIL's edge and its exposure at the same time.

Roughly 39% of KPIL's order book is outside India, spanning around 70 countries across the Nordics, sub-Saharan and North Africa, the Middle East, South America and CIS/Central Asia. That reach is what separates KPIL from a domestic-only contractor and gives it the global-grid theme — but building on four continents carries risks an India-only firm never faces.

It manages this by hedging roughly 90% of its base-metal exposure. When you read that KPIL is "90% hedged on aluminium, zinc and copper and 85% on FX," that sentence is the risk-management strategy for a global, fixed-price contractor in miniature.
08

The industry and the rivals

A crowded Indian EPC field with one giant, one twin, and a fringe of specialists.

Indian infrastructure EPC is a large, competitive arena feeding off a historic public-and-private capex cycle — grid expansion, roads, railways, water missions, factories and pipelines. KPIL competes on two fronts at once: as a global T&D specialist and as a diversified domestic civil contractor. Its competitive set differs by vertical.

The rivalry that defines the sector

For a generation, Indian power transmission has effectively been a two-horse race: Kalpataru versus KEC International. Both are global T&D EPC leaders, both bid the same international grid tenders, and both have spent years extending beyond towers-and-wires into railways, civil and cables. The key difference today is balance: KEC is more concentrated in T&D (~68% of revenue), which makes it a purer grid play but more exposed to that single cycle; KPIL is more diversified across B&F, water, oil-and-gas and urban, which smooths its earnings but dilutes the "pure grid" narrative. Neither model is strictly better — they are two different bets on the same structural theme.

The structural backdrop: the demand side is unusually favourable — India's transmission and distribution capex is projected to run into lakhs of crores this decade, global grid investment is scaling with the energy transition, and India's building, water and gas programmes are all in expansion. The constraint on a firm like KPIL is rarely demand; it is execution capacity, working capital and margin discipline in a competitive tender market.

The number that settles the thesis

An EPC contractor is not valued like an asset owner — no toll or tariff annuity to discount — so KPIL trades on earnings and order-book strength, typically at a discount to firms that own their infrastructure. At ~22–23× earnings it is priced for the profit inflection to continue, not reverse. The one metric that will prove or break that: net debt (and net-debt-to-equity) staying low as revenue scales toward ₹30,000 Cr and beyond. The FY26 re-rating was bought with deleveraging; the danger for an EPC firm chasing a bigger book is that working capital creeps back up and debt climbs again, quietly reversing the very engine that drove the profit jump. Watch net debt each quarter alongside the EBITDA margin: if the book keeps growing while net debt stays near ₹1,000 Cr and margin holds toward 9%, the story is intact. If debt starts climbing to fund the order book, the re-rating loses its foundation — regardless of how large the order book looks.

10

The working-capital machine

Where an order-rich EPC firm can still get into trouble.

A contractor can have a record order book and still be starved of cash. The reason is working capital — the gap between paying for a project and being paid for it. KPIL buys steel, aluminium, cement and diesel and pays wages continuously; its clients, especially government bodies, pay in stages and hold back retention money until the job is signed off. The bigger the order book grows, the more cash gets tied up in that gap.

The live example: in FY26 KPIL's Water business alone had roughly ₹1,600 Cr of receivables stuck in delayed government payments — enough to drag the whole segment. This is the textbook EPC hazard: the work is done, the revenue is booked, but the cash has not arrived, so the company must fund the shortfall with debt. Management expects recovery over the coming half-year, but it is a permanent feature of the water/government business, not a one-off.

This is exactly why the deleveraging story and the working-capital story are two sides of one coin. KPIL has been improving collections and operating cash flow (up 68% to ₹1,535 Cr in FY26) precisely so that a growing order book does not re-inflate its debt. The tension is structural: growth consumes working capital, and working capital is funded by debt — so the faster KPIL grows, the harder it must run on collections just to keep net debt from rising. For this business, cash conversion is not a footnote; it is the discipline that decides whether the profit inflection lasts.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A diversified global builder mid-way through a clean-up — with real execution stakes.

KPIL enters FY27 in its best shape in years: a record order book, halved debt, a profit that crossed ₹1,000 Cr, and returns on capital above 21%. Management is steering toward roughly ₹30,000 Cr of revenue in FY27 with margins inching up. The debate is not whether the demand exists — the global grid, India's capex, gas and water programmes all point the same way — but whether KPIL can grow the book and keep its balance sheet clean at the same time.

The bull case

  • Global-grid tailwind — one of the world's largest transmission EPC contractors, with ~two-thirds of T&D wins from abroad, riding a multi-decade grid build-out.
  • Diversification — six verticals and ~70 countries smooth the cyclicality that sinks single-line contractors.
  • Deleveraging delivered — net debt cut ~53% to ₹915 Cr; interest falling; ROCE 21%+; profit outrunning revenue.
  • Record visibility — ₹65,457 Cr order book, ~2.4× sales, still growing via ₹26,400 Cr of FY26 inflows.
  • Re-rating headroom — trades at a discount to asset owners; cleaner balance sheet and rising returns support a higher multiple.

The bear case

  • Thin, fixed-price margins — ~8% EBITDA; a commodity spike or cost overrun on a fixed contract bites straight into profit.
  • Working-capital drag — growth consumes cash; the ₹1,600 Cr of stuck water receivables shows how quickly debt can creep back.
  • International exposure — Middle-East disruption already cost ~₹200–250 Cr of revenue; Africa/LatAm carry payment, currency and country risk.
  • Deleveraging is finite — the asset-sale tailwind fades once non-core assets are gone; profit growth then depends on harder-won margin gains.
  • Execution is the whole game — a diversified EPC firm is only as good as its delivery across dozens of simultaneous sites and currencies.
The bottom line

KPIL is a rare thing among Indian mid-caps: a genuinely global contractor with a defensible specialism (transmission), a diversified book that dampens the sector's cyclicality, and a management team that has spent three years cleaning up the balance sheet rather than chasing headline growth. The FY26 profit leap was engineered as much in the treasury — through halved debt — as on the construction site. The next chapter is a test of whether it can hold that discipline while pushing revenue past ₹30,000 Cr: can it keep net debt near ₹1,000 Cr and margins near 9% while the order book climbs? If the towers keep going up across Sweden, Brazil and India without the debt coming back with them, KPIL will have proved that a diversified global EPC firm can grow and stay lean at once — the exact combination the market is now paying a re-rated multiple to see repeated.
