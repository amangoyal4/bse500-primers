Eureka Forbes, sell it once, earn for years

A complete walk through the company behind Aquaguard — India's best-known water-purifier brand — and its vacuum cleaners and air purifiers. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Eureka Forbes Limited makes the appliances that clean the air, water and floors of an Indian home — above all the Aquaguard water purifier, India's most recognised name in the category, plus Euroclean vacuum cleaners and a growing range of air purifiers and water softeners. For four decades it was famous for one thing: the door-to-door salesman who demonstrated a purifier at your kitchen sink. Today it sells the same products across shops, e-commerce and that direct force at once — but the part of the business that actually matters most is invisible from the shop floor: the servicing, annual contracts and replacement filters it sells, year after year, to the millions of homes that already own an Aquaguard.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The profit is the service after the sale, not the machine. A purifier is bought once. But it needs its filters changed and its unit serviced for as long as it runs — and Eureka Forbes, with an after-sales network serving over 10 million customers, is the natural provider. Service was ~31% of FY26 revenue and is the highest-margin, most predictable slice of the whole company. The device gets you into the home; the annuity is where the money compounds.
  • It is a turnaround, not a start-up. By its own CEO's admission the company "had not grown for 10 years" — Aquaguard sales had actually been declining for a decade under old ownership. Since private-equity firm Advent International took control in 2022 and installed professional management, revenue has grown double-digits and the EBITDA margin has roughly doubled — from ~6.3% (FY23) to ~12.2% (FY26), a third consecutive year of expansion. The story is fixing a stalled classic, not inventing something new.
  • It rides clean water and clean air. Fewer than half of Indians have reliable access to safe drinking water, purifier ownership is still confined to a minority of homes, and worsening urban air keeps adding demand. Eureka Forbes is the leader in a large, under-penetrated, structurally growing market — with a stated ambition to roughly double revenue to ₹5,400–5,600 Cr and triple EBITDA by FY30.
In plain terms

Picture a company whose salesman knocked on ten million doors over forty years and left an Aquaguard in each kitchen. The machine sale was almost the least of it: every one of those homes now needs filters replaced and the unit serviced, on repeat, for a decade — and the company that installed it is the obvious one to call. That recurring servicing is quieter, stickier and far more profitable than the one-time device sale. For years the business coasted on the brand and let that annuity go under-tapped; a private-equity owner has spent the last few years professionalising the machine, pushing service and filters harder, and lifting margins in the process. The device is the hook. The installed base is the asset. The service is the profit.

01

Why purifiers exist: the problem Eureka Forbes sells against

No product makes sense until you see the need — start with India's water and air.

A water purifier is not an aspirational gadget; it is a response to a public-health gap. Across much of India, what comes out of the tap cannot be trusted to be safe — contaminated by bacteria, dissolved salts, heavy metals or industrial run-off — and boiling every litre is tedious and incomplete. A purifier does at the point of use what the municipal supply does not: it makes household water reliably drinkable. That single, un-glamorous fact is the entire foundation of the category, and of Eureka Forbes.

The only category terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
ROReverse osmosis — pushes water through a fine membrane to strip dissolved salts and heavy metals. Needed where water is "hard" or high-TDS (much of India). The membrane and pre-filters wear out and must be replaced — the heart of the consumables annuity.
UV / UFUltraviolet and ultrafiltration — kill or block microbes without removing dissolved salts. Suited to softer municipal water. Cheaper than RO, still needs periodic cartridge/lamp changes.
TDSTotal dissolved solids — how much salt/mineral is in the water. High TDS pushes a household toward RO; it is the single technical variable that decides which purifier a home needs.
AMCAnnual Maintenance Contract — a paid yearly plan that bundles service visits and (often) filter replacements. The core recurring-revenue product; renewing AMCs is the annuity in action.
Installed baseThe total number of Eureka Forbes machines already running in homes and offices. Every unit is a future stream of service and filter revenue — the company's most valuable, least visible asset.
The demand engine: safe water is still a minority privilege

India has hundreds of millions of households and, on most estimates, fewer than half the population enjoys reliably safe drinking water. Purifier ownership remains confined to a minority of homes — concentrated in cities and among the middle class — which means the category is not near saturation; it is early. Rising incomes, urbanisation, health awareness after the pandemic, and visibly deteriorating water and air quality all push penetration upward year after year. A leader in a category this under-owned has a long runway simply from the market growing into the product — before it does anything clever at all. That structural tailwind is why a forty-year-old brand can still be described, credibly, as an early-stage opportunity.

Two more needs the company sells into

  • Clean floors — vacuum cleaners, where Eureka Forbes (Euroclean) has been the pioneering brand for decades and holds a commanding share of the organised market. A smaller, more discretionary category than water, but a natural extension of the same "healthier home" pitch and the same service network.
  • Clean air — air purifiers, a newer, fast-growing line riding worsening urban air pollution, especially in North Indian winters. Still a small slice of revenue but a structurally expanding one, and it reinforces the company's identity as a whole-home health brand rather than a single-product maker.
02

The installed-base annuity: sell the Aquaguard once, earn for years

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to Eureka Forbes.

Most people value a purifier company by how many machines it sells this year. That is the wrong number. The device is a near-one-time, competitive, thinner-margin sale. The real economic engine is what happens after the machine is installed: for the next several years that home needs its filters replaced and its unit serviced — and Eureka Forbes, having installed it, is the natural, trusted provider. Every Aquaguard sold is therefore not a transaction; it is the opening of a multi-year revenue stream. The company's true worth is closer to a simple product:

Value ≈ (purifiers already in homes) × (share on a paid service/AMC plan) × (rupees of service & filters per home, per year) — plus whatever new units get sold to widen the base.

The device is the least profitable step and the annuity the most — which is why the base of installed machines, not this year's unit sales, is the number that really matters.

Two figures make the point concrete. Eureka Forbes runs one of India's largest consumer after-sales networks, serving over 10 million customers. And in FY26 the service line was ~31% of revenue — nearly a third of the entire company — while carrying structurally higher margins than the hardware. Put those together and the strategic logic is obvious: the company is not really a maker of purifiers that also happens to service them; it is a services and consumables business that uses machine sales to keep enlarging its captive customer base.

The device that only fits Eureka Forbes: a decade of doorsteps, monetised

Here is a construct that could describe no other primer in this library. For forty years the "Eurochamp" salesman knocked on doors and left an Aquaguard behind — building, one kitchen at a time, an installed base now past ten million homes. That base is a physical, un-replicable asset: to compete for its service revenue, a rival would have to first sell ten million machines and then build a nationwide field-service force to reach them. The genius — and the past failure — of Eureka Forbes is the same fact. The genius is that the annuity exists at all. The failure was that, for years, the company under-charged and under-collected on it, leaving AMC penetration at only around a quarter of the base. The whole current strategy can be read as: stop giving the annuity away. Sell the AMC, sell genuine filters, digitise the renewal — and turn a dormant ten-million-home asset into a rising, high-margin income stream.

What Eureka Forbes actually sells: the four revenue pillars

Two product categories, an air line, and — biggest of all — the service business.

It is tempting to think of Eureka Forbes as "the Aquaguard company," but its revenue actually stands on four distinct legs, and the biggest surprise for a newcomer is how large the non-product leg is. Here is the FY26 split — and why each piece behaves differently.

The four pillars, and how each behaves

  • Electric water purifiers (Aquaguard) — the flagship and largest single line (~43% of revenue). India's most recognised purifier brand, market leader with an estimated 40%+ share of the organised category. This is the hook that opens the annuity, but as a hardware sale it is the most competitive and price-sensitive line.
  • Service & AMC — nearly a third of revenue and the profit centre. Recurring, high-margin and sticky: annual contracts, service visits, and — crucially — genuine replacement filters, an area where the company is fighting to win back sales lost to cheap third-party spares. The strategic priority.
  • Vacuum cleaners (Euroclean) — the category Eureka Forbes actually started with in 1982, where it holds a dominant organised-market share (well over half). Steady and profitable but more discretionary; increasingly sold through retail and e-commerce rather than door-to-door.
  • Air purifiers, water softeners, B2B & institutional — the smaller, faster-growing basket. Air rides urban-pollution demand; B2B/institutional (office and commercial water systems) diversifies the customer mix beyond the home. Small today, a source of future optionality.
Why the mix is the whole quality of the business

Two purifier companies can post identical revenue and be worth very different amounts, and the difference is the mix. A company that is 90% hardware sells a competitive, one-shot, discountable product and must re-win every customer every year. A company where a third of revenue is recurring service on an installed base it already owns has predictability, pricing power and margins a pure hardware seller can only envy. Eureka Forbes is deliberately steering toward the second shape — pushing service attach, filter genuineness and AMC renewals — because every point of revenue that shifts from one-time hardware to recurring service raises the durability, and the value, of the entire company.

04

From door-to-door to omnichannel: reinventing how it sells

The most famous direct-selling force in India — and why it had to change.

For most of its life Eureka Forbes was defined not by a product but by a method: the direct salesman. Long before e-commerce, an army of "Eurochamps" knocked on doors, carried a purifier into your kitchen, and demonstrated on the spot how dirty your "clean" tap water really was. It was a brilliant way to build a category from nothing — trust, education and a sale, all at the sink. It is also, four decades on, an expensive and partly outdated way to sell in a country that now buys appliances on its phone.

The shift under way is from that single channel to omnichannel — selling the same products through whichever route the customer prefers:

  • Direct selling — still valuable for the high-consideration first purifier sale and for reaching homes that want a demo, but no longer the only route and a smaller share of the effort than before.
  • Modern & general retail — purifiers and vacuums on shelves in electronics and appliance stores, where a large share of buyers now start.
  • E-commerce — the company's own site and the big marketplaces, capturing the digitally-savvy buyer who researches and orders online.
  • Service as a channel — the field-service network doubling as a sales and cross-sell touchpoint, since a technician in the home is a warm channel for filters, upgrades and AMC renewals.
Why moving beyond direct selling was hard — and necessary

Direct selling built Eureka Forbes, and legacy is heavy: a large, proud field force, a culture organised around it, and a cost base to match. But relying on it alone was quietly strangling growth — it missed the online buyer entirely, it was costly per sale, and it fenced the company off from the fastest-growing routes to market. The omnichannel transition is therefore not a marketing tweak; it is a structural re-plumbing of how a forty-year-old company reaches customers, and it has to be done without alienating the direct force or the trust it built. Get it right and the brand's reach widens dramatically at lower cost; get it wrong and you disrupt the very channel that made the name. It is one of the central execution challenges of the turnaround.

The counter-intuitive risk of a great sales legacy: the direct-selling model was so successful for so long that it became the thing hardest to change. A company whose entire identity and cost structure is built around doorstep demonstration cannot simply switch to shelves and screens overnight. The very asset that built the franchise — the field army — is also the anchor that made the business slow to modernise. Managing that transition, channel by channel, without losing either the legacy strength or the new-age reach, is exactly the kind of unglamorous operational work a private-equity owner was brought in to force through.
05

The company, its owners, and the Advent reset

Tata to Shapoorji to private equity — and why the latest owner matters most.

Eureka Forbes has passed through three very different owners, and its current chapter — control by a global private-equity firm — is the one that explains almost everything about the stock today. The ownership history is not trivia; it is the reason the business spent years drifting, and the reason it is now being run hard for improvement.

Timeline

  • 1982
    Founded as a joint venture between the Tata group's Forbes & Company and Sweden's Electrolux, starting in vacuum cleaners and soon pioneering the Aquaguard water purifier and the door-to-door "Friends for Life" direct-selling model.
  • 1982–2001
    Under the Tata group; builds Aquaguard and Euroclean into household names and creates one of India's largest consumer direct-sales and after-service networks.
  • 2001–2022
    Passes to the Shapoorji Pallonji (SP) group as part of Forbes & Company. A period in which, by later management's own account, the business essentially stopped growing for a decade — Aquaguard sales declining, the organisation fragmented into warring divisions.
  • 2022
    A pivotal reset: the SP group, deleveraging, sells its controlling stake to private-equity firm Advent International (a deal valuing the business at roughly ₹4,400 Cr for the controlling interest). Eureka Forbes is demerged from Forbes & Company and listed on the NSE and BSE (BSE code 543482).
  • 2022 onward
    Advent installs professional management — Pratik Pota (formerly of Jubilant FoodWorks / Domino's India) as MD & CEO — and launches a full turnaround: unify the silos, push service & AMC, go omnichannel, and lift margins.
  • FY23–FY26
    The plan shows in the numbers: revenue back to double-digit growth, EBITDA margin roughly doubling from ~6.3% to ~12.2%, and PAT rising from ~₹17 Cr (FY23) to the ~₹160 Cr range — a genuine operational re-rating.

Ownership & structure today

  • Promoter: funds advised by Advent International, one of the world's larger private-equity firms, which took majority control in 2022. As is typical for a PE owner, the intent is a multi-year value-creation project — professionalise, grow, expand margins — with an eventual exit (a stake sale or block trades) in mind.
  • Management: a professional team led by CEO Pratik Pota, brought in from a consumer-execution background rather than from the company's own direct-selling lineage — a deliberate break from the past that signals the turnaround is being run as a discipline, not a defence of legacy.
  • Public float: the balance is held by public and institutional shareholders. Investors should confirm the exact promoter percentage in the latest shareholding filing, as PE owners commonly reduce their stake over time.
Why a private-equity owner changes the read on this stock

Ownership matters here in a very direct way. Under a large diversified group, Eureka Forbes was one asset among many and could drift; under a private-equity firm whose entire model is to buy, improve and sell, it became the project, with capital, management attention and an explicit margin-and-growth agenda pointed straight at it. That is why the margin doubled in three years — it was managed, not lucky. But the same ownership carries the mirror-image caveat: private equity does not hold forever. At some point Advent will look to exit, and the manner and timing of that — a strategic sale, a series of market blocks, a full float — becomes a real overhang and swing factor for the share price, independent of how the business itself performs.

The genuine surprise: India's best-known purifier brand had gone nowhere for a decade

Ask a smart generalist about Aquaguard and they will assume a steadily growing market leader. The reality before 2022 was the opposite: by the current CEO's own account the business "had not grown for 10 years," with Aquaguard sales in outright decline and the organisation split into feuding divisions — direct sales, retail and service each run as its own fiefdom, competing internally instead of against rivals. That is the counter-intuitive heart of the story. The value on offer was never a hot new product; it was a famous, under-managed asset whose problems were fixable — silos to merge, an annuity to monetise, a cost base to tighten. The margin doubling is what "fixable" looks like when someone finally does the work.

07

The service moat: why filters and AMC are hard to copy

The part of the business a rival cannot simply out-spend its way into.

Any well-funded competitor can build a good purifier and discount it onto a shelf. What no competitor can quickly replicate is what happens over the ten years after that purifier is installed — and that is precisely where Eureka Forbes' durable advantage lives. The moat is not the machine; it is the nationwide capability to reach millions of existing customers, service their units, and sell them genuine filters, reliably, year after year.

Why is this so hard to copy? Because it takes three things at once, and each takes years to build:

  • An installed base to service. You cannot sell service on machines that aren't there. A rival must first place millions of units in homes — a decades-long effort — before it has an annuity to chase. Eureka Forbes already has it.
  • A nationwide field-service force. Reaching ten million homes for a filter change or a repair needs technicians, logistics and spares across the whole country — a heavy, unglamorous physical network that cannot be spun up quickly or cheaply.
  • Trust at the kitchen sink. Customers let this brand's technician into the home and buy "genuine" filters from it partly on faith. That trust, built over forty years of the direct-selling relationship, is the intangible that converts an installed machine into a paying, renewing service customer.
The leaky-annuity problem management is fixing

The moat has one notorious weakness, and closing it is a core plank of the turnaround. Because filters are consumables, a thriving grey market of cheap third-party cartridges and independent servicemen has long siphoned off revenue that "should" have been the company's — a customer who buys a ₹300 unbranded filter instead of a genuine one is annuity income walking out the door. Much of the current service push is about plugging that leak: making genuine filters and AMC plans more attractive and accessible, digitising renewals so contracts don't lapse, and using the service visit itself to re-sell the customer. The prize is large precisely because so much of the annuity has historically leaked away — every point of "leakage" recaptured is high-margin revenue that drops almost straight to profit.

08

The industry and the rivals

A large, under-penetrated, fragmented market — with a reshuffling competitive top end.

Indian water purification is a big and growing market — on the order of ~$1 bn-plus a year and compounding at low-teens rates — but it is only moderately consolidated, with a handful of organised leaders holding roughly two-thirds of the market and a long tail of regional and unbranded players fighting on price beneath them. Eureka Forbes is the incumbent leader by brand and by installed base, but it competes hard on the hardware sale, and the top end of the market has just been reshuffled.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two features define this market. First, competition is fiercest on the hardware sale — where brand, features and price collide and where a Kent or a newly-combined A.O. Smith–Pureit can win a customer with a sharper machine or a bigger ad budget. But it is far weaker on the service annuity, where Eureka Forbes' installed base and field network give it an edge rivals cannot cheaply match. So the company's strategic instinct is sound: compete respectably on the device, but win decisively on what comes after it. Second, the unbranded tail matters more than in most categories, because filters are commoditisable — which is exactly why recapturing genuine-filter and AMC revenue is both the opportunity and the fight.

09

The growth runway: penetration plus monetisation

Two independent engines — a market that keeps growing, and a base that can be squeezed harder.

What makes the forward story more than a one-off margin fix is that Eureka Forbes has two distinct ways to grow, and they are largely independent of each other. One is the market coming to it; the other is it extracting more from what it already has. Either alone would be worth something; together they underpin an ambitious medium-term plan.

Engine 1 — penetration: the market grows into the product

Purifier ownership is still confined to a minority of Indian homes, safe drinking water remains a genuine daily problem for hundreds of millions, and clean-air demand is rising with urban pollution. As incomes rise and health awareness deepens, more households cross into buying a purifier for the first time — expanding the whole category and, with it, the leader's hardware sales and, in time, its service base. This is growth the company earns simply by holding its share as the market enlarges.

Engine 2 — monetisation: extract more from the installed base

The second engine is entirely within the company's control: raise AMC penetration from ~27% toward something far higher, win back genuine-filter sales from the grey market, cross-sell air and softeners into purifier homes, and digitise renewals so fewer contracts lapse. Because this revenue is high-margin, growth here lifts profit more than the same growth in hardware would. It is the lever the turnaround is leaning on hardest.

Why the two engines together are more than the sum of the parts

The reason to care about both engines running at once is that they reinforce each other. Every new purifier sold as the market penetrates deeper does not just book a one-time hardware sale — it enlarges the installed base, which is the raw material Engine 2 monetises for years afterward. So penetration growth today seeds annuity growth tomorrow, and the annuity growth is where the margin lift lives. That flywheel — sell more machines into an under-penetrated market, then earn rising high-margin service on an ever-larger base — is the logic behind doubling revenue while tripling profit. Whether the company hits those exact FY30 numbers is far less important than whether the flywheel turns.

Financials & valuation

A rising-margin consumer leader priced for the improvement to continue.

Eureka Forbes is not a cheap cyclical; it is a consumer-durables brand in the middle of a margin re-rating, and the market prices it accordingly — on a rich earnings multiple that already assumes the turnaround keeps going. The debate is therefore not whether the business is improving (it clearly is), but whether the improvement is fast and durable enough to justify a valuation that leans on the future rather than the present.

A few things stand out about the financial profile. It is a capital-light, cash-generative business — a brand, a distribution and service network and working capital, not heavy factories — so improving margins convert efficiently into cash and returns on capital. The one number that settles the thesis: service/recurring revenue mix, read against the margin

At a rich multiple, the case does not rest on the market growing — that is largely given. It rests on the company turning its installed base into ever more high-margin recurring revenue. So the metric that proves or breaks the thesis is the share of revenue coming from service and AMC (~31% in FY26) and its trajectory, tracked alongside the EBITDA margin it drives. If service mix and AMC penetration keep climbing and pull the margin toward management's FY30 ambition, the premium valuation is earned and profit compounds faster than revenue. If service mix stalls, filter leakage persists, and the margin plateaus near today's ~12%, then the stock is paying a growth price for a business that has already banked most of its easy turnaround gains. Watch the recurring-mix percentage and the margin together, every quarter — they are the scoreboard that tells you which of those two worlds you are in.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A famous brand, professionally re-run, priced for the re-run to keep working.

Eureka Forbes enters its next phase as a genuinely different company than the one Advent bought in 2022: growing again, margins doubled, organisation unified, and an under-tapped ten-million-home annuity in front of it. What it must now prove is that the easy phase of a turnaround — fixing an obviously broken base — gives way to a harder phase of durable, self-sustaining growth, and that it can do so while a private-equity owner eventually heads for the exit. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • The annuity is barely tapped — AMC penetration ~27% of a 10M+ base; monetising it is high-margin revenue the company already owns the right to.
  • Structural demand — low purifier penetration, unsafe water for hundreds of millions, and worsening air give years of category growth to the brand leader.
  • Proven margin lever — EBITDA margin has already doubled to ~12%; management targets materially higher by FY30, with mix shift as the engine.
  • Capital-light, cash-generative — a brand-and-service model with little debt converts margin gains efficiently into cash and returns.
  • Professional owner & management — a focused PE owner and consumer-execution leadership have delivered visible operational improvement, not just promises.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — a high multiple already prices in continued improvement; a margin plateau near ~12% would leave the stock over-extended.
  • Competition on hardware — Kent, a newly-combined A.O. Smith–Pureit, Havells and cheap unbranded RO all pressure the device sale and filter share.
  • The direct-selling transition — modernising a legacy field-force-led model without disrupting it is genuinely hard and not yet complete.
  • Discretionary demand — purifiers and especially vacuums/air are postponable purchases; a consumption slowdown hits volumes.
  • PE-exit overhang — Advent will eventually sell; the timing and manner of that stake reduction is a real, valuation-independent share-price risk.
The bottom line

Eureka Forbes is a rare thing: a household-name brand that had quietly stopped working, bought cheaply by an owner who knew how to fix it, and visibly improving three years on. The device is the hook, the ten-million-home installed base is the asset, and the rising service annuity is where the value is being created — a business finally being run to monetise what it spent forty years building. The near-term test is concrete and datable: whether the service-and-AMC share of revenue keeps climbing above its ~31% FY26 level through FY27, pulling the EBITDA margin further past ~12% toward the FY30 ambition. Keep the recurring mix rising and the premium the market pays looks deserved; let it stall while competition bites the hardware line and the PE owner edges toward the door, and the stock will have paid a growth price for a turnaround whose best chapter was the first one.
