Berger Paints India, the challenger in a shaken-up oligopoly

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Berger Paints India makes the paint on the walls of Indian homes and the coatings on cars, bridges, pipelines and factory steel. It is India's second-largest paint company — the clear #2 behind Asian Paints — with roughly a 20% share of the organised decorative-paints market, a network of ~36,000 dealers, and a brand that traces back to a 1760 London pigment-maker.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A high-quality, near-debt-free compounder. FY26 revenue was ₹11,880 Cr and net profit ₹1,128 Cr, on a ~15% operating margin, a 22% return on capital (ROCE) and ~75% promoter ownership. Paints has historically been one of India's best compounding sectors — asset-light, cash-generative, pricing-powerful — and Berger is a prime example.
  • Decorative is the engine; complexity is the moat. About 84% of sales are decorative (the wall paint you buy for a home), with the rest in industrial & automotive coatings, protective coatings, powder and a fast-growing construction-chemicals/waterproofing business. The real barrier to entry isn't the paint — it's the distribution: tens of thousands of dealers, tinting machines in every shop, and a brand homeowners trust for a once-in-7-years purchase.
  • The whole story right now is disruption. A decades-long cosy oligopoly is being invaded. Grasim's Birla Opus (Aditya Birla group) spent ₹10,000+ Cr to build the #2 paint capacity in India from scratch, and JSW bought Akzo Nobel India (now JSW Dulux). The question the market is asking about every incumbent, Berger included: can they defend share and margin as deep-pocketed new entrants discount their way in?
In plain terms

Think of Berger as the strong #2 brand in a business that behaves more like consumer goods than chemicals. Paint is technically a commodity — pigment, resin, solvent, additives — but you don't buy it like one. You repaint your home rarely, you don't know or care what it costs to make, and you trust the painter and the brand. That lets a big paint company earn fat, sticky margins if it controls the shelf: the dealer, the shade card, and the tinting machine. Berger has spent decades building exactly that shelf presence. The drama today is that the two richest business houses in India — Birla and JSW — have decided they want a piece of it, and are willing to spend billions and cut prices to get it.

01

How a paint company actually earns

No chemistry degree required — just the handful of numbers that drive the P&L.

A paint business is, at heart, a volume-times-price business sitting on a swinging cost base. It buys crude-oil and mineral derivatives, mixes them into paint, and sells litres through a dealer network at a healthy markup. Four levers explain almost the whole income statement.

The levers that move the P&L
LeverWhat it means (and Berger's read)
Volume growthLitres sold. This is the purest measure of share and demand. Berger grew volumes ~11.8% in Q4 FY26 — a strong rebound after muted quarters — but FY26 value growth was only ~2.9%, because prices were cut/held to defend volume.
Value (revenue) growthVolume × realisation per litre. When paint companies cut prices or push cheaper products, value can lag volume — exactly the FY26 pattern (volumes up double-digit, revenue up low-single-digit).
Gross marginThe gap between selling price and raw-material cost. This is the single most volatile line — it moves with crude oil and titanium dioxide (TiO2) prices. Softer input costs in FY26 helped margins recover.
EBITDA marginGross margin minus the fixed costs of running the machine (advertising, dealer incentives, distribution, staff). Berger's Q4 FY26 EBITDA margin hit ~16.8%, a ~10-quarter high, as input costs eased and mix improved.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ Litres × (Price − Raw-material cost) − Fixed costs. So a paint company gets more profitable three ways — sell more litres (volume/share), raise the value of each litre (premiumisation — luxury emulsions, textures, waterproofing), and widen the gap to input costs (when crude/TiO2 fall, or via price hikes). The catch is the middle term: raw materials are ~55–60% of the cost of a can of paint and swing with global crude, so gross margin is never stable. A paint company's skill is managing that swing — hiking prices when inputs rise, holding them to grab share when inputs fall.

Why the tension between volume and value matters

In a normal year a paints company wants both volumes and pricing to rise. FY26 was not normal: with new entrants attacking, Berger (like Asian Paints) leaned into protecting volume and market share even at the cost of revenue growth — accepting a soft top line and only implementing "calibrated" price hikes from end-March 2026. The good news buried in the numbers: because raw-material costs also eased, margins expanded even as pricing was restrained, and Q4 PAT still jumped ~27.5%. That combination — strong volumes, recovering margins, muted value growth — is the defining shape of Berger's current phase, and it is a direct fingerprint of the competitive war (Section 06).

02

The distribution moat: dealers, tinting machines & the shade card

Why paint is a distribution business dressed up as a chemicals business — read this once and the whole sector clicks.

Ask why paints has been such a wonderful sector and the answer is almost never "the chemistry." It's distribution and brand. Anyone can make white paint. Almost nobody can quickly build the shelf presence, the dealer relationships, the in-store colour technology and the consumer trust needed to actually sell it at a premium. That is Berger's — and the industry's — real moat.

Every step is a moat. The tinting machine means dealers don't hold thousands of SKUs; the dealer holds inventory and gives credit; the painter is the trusted recommender. A new entrant must recreate all four — nationwide — before it can meaningfully sell.

The tinting machine — the quiet weapon

  • What it is: a computerised dispenser sitting in the dealer's shop that mixes a small set of "bases" plus colourants into any of thousands of exact shades, on demand, per can.
  • Why it's a moat: it lets a dealer offer every colour without stocking every colour, and it locks the dealer to your system — once your machine and your colourants are installed, switching to a rival is costly and disruptive.
  • The scale gap: Asian Paints has ~50,000+ tinting machines; Berger and Kansai Nerolac together have deployed ~46,000. That machine base is precisely what a challenger has to replicate — and precisely why Birla Opus's reported ~45,000 tinting machines (built in ~18 months) was such a shock to the industry (Section 06).
Why paint is "working-capital-light" and cash-rich

Here's the financial magic. The dealer holds the inventory and often extends credit to the painter; the paint company sells to the dealer and gets paid relatively quickly. So a paint company runs on modest working capital and doesn't need heavy factories relative to sales — it's fairly asset-light. Combine that with pricing power (a homeowner repainting after 5–7 years won't quibble over a 5% price difference) and you get the hallmark of the sector: high returns on capital, strong free cash flow, and the ability to fund growth and pay dividends without much debt. Berger's ~22% ROCE and near-debt-free balance sheet are the direct result. This is why paint stocks have historically traded at rich, FMCG-like multiples rather than commodity-chemical multiples.

03

The company and who controls it

A 1760 London pigment-maker, an Amritsar shopkeeping dynasty, and a company bought from Vijay Mallya.

Berger's story is two histories stitched together: a British paint lineage dating to 1760, and the Dhingra family of Amritsar, whose own paint roots go back to an 1898 hardware shop and who bought the Indian Berger business from Vijay Mallya's UB Group in 1991.

Timeline

  • 1760
    Lewis Berger (originally Louis Steigenberger, of Frankfurt) begins making dyes and pigments in London — famously perfecting Prussian Blue, the colour of military uniforms. The Berger name is born.
  • 1898
    In Amritsar, the Dhingra family's forebears open a hardware/paint shop — the seed of UK Paints ("Uttam Singh Kesar Singh"), the family's original paint business.
  • 1923
    Berger's Indian operations are established (as Hadfield's India); the business that becomes Berger Paints India takes root in the country.
  • 1991
    Kuldip Singh Dhingra and brother Gurbachan Singh Dhingra acquire Berger Paints from Vijay Mallya's UB Group — the pivotal deal that put the modern company under the Dhingra family.
  • 2010s
    Under CEO Abhijit Roy, Berger scales the dealer/tinting-machine network, pushes premiumisation, and builds out industrial & protective coatings; forms the Nippon automotive JV.
  • 2019
    Acquires STP Limited (founded 1935) — a construction-chemicals and waterproofing specialist — the platform for the fast-growing construction-chemicals push.
  • FY26
    Revenue ₹11,880 Cr; ~20% decorative share held despite the entry of Birla Opus and JSW Dulux; CEO targets ₹20,000 Cr turnover by 2030.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Dhingra family (Kuldip Singh Dhingra, Gurbachan Singh Dhingra and family entities incl. U.K. Paints) hold ~74.98% — a very high, aligned promoter stake. Kuldip Singh Dhingra is Chairman.
  • Management: Abhijit Roy is Managing Director & CEO — a long-tenured, well-regarded operator who has run Berger through its modern growth phase and set the ₹20,000 Cr-by-2030 ambition.
  • Balance sheet: almost debt-free, cash-generative, funding capacity expansion and dividends internally. Face value is Re 1 per share; FY26 dividend recommended at ₹4 per share.
A note on the name: the British "Berger" lineage and the Dhingra family's own "UK Paints" heritage are distinct threads that converged in the 1991 acquisition. Today Berger Paints India is wholly Indian-controlled — the Dhingras — with the historic Berger brand and a global footprint (Nepal, Russia, Poland via the group). The 1760 date is brand heritage, not the age of the listed Indian company.

Decorative vs industrial — the two halves of Berger

One business you can see on your walls; one you can't — but both matter.

Berger is overwhelmingly a decorative (home-and-building) paint company, but it carries a meaningful industrial and coatings tail that most casual observers miss. Understanding the split matters because the two halves have very different economics, customers and cyclicality.

Decorative — the high-quality core

  • What it is: interior and exterior wall paints, emulsions, enamels, primers, putty and waterproofing sold to homeowners via the dealer network. This is the ~84% that behaves like an FMCG business — brand-driven, high-margin, repeat, low-ticket.
  • Why it's the core: it's where the pricing power, the tinting-machine moat and the premiumisation opportunity all live. When analysts talk about Berger's "market share," they almost always mean decorative share (~20%).

Industrial, automotive & protective — the B2B tail

  • General industrial & automotive (~9%): coatings for appliances, containers, and — via the Berger Nippon Paint Automotive Coatings JV — coatings for cars, SUVs, three-wheelers and automotive plastic parts. Lower-margin, order-driven, tied to auto/industrial cycles, but sticky B2B relationships.
  • Protective coatings (~6%, "Protecton"): anti-corrosion coatings for steel structures, bridges, pipelines, refineries and infrastructure. A direct play on India's infrastructure and "Make in India" capex — a genuine structural tailwind and a segment where Berger holds a strong position.
  • Construction chemicals & waterproofing (STP): Berger's 2019 acquisition of STP Limited gave it a platform in construction chemicals; management says it holds the #3 position in this space, and the segment is growing at ~25% a year — one of the fastest-growing parts of the group.
Why the industrial tail matters for the thesis

Two reasons. First, diversification: protective coatings and construction chemicals are tied to infrastructure and industrial capex, not just household repainting — a different demand driver that partly offsets consumer softness. Second, and more importantly right now, these are the segments where Berger can grow away from the decorative price war. As Birla Opus and JSW crowd the decorative shelf, Berger's ability to lean on high-growth adjacencies — waterproofing, construction chemicals, protective coatings — is a key part of its defence and its ₹20,000 Cr-by-2030 plan.

05

The raw-material cycle — where the margin swings come from

The single most important thing to understand about paint-company profits quarter to quarter.

A paint company's revenue is fairly stable, but its gross margin lives and dies on input costs, and those inputs are almost entirely crude-oil and mineral derivatives. This is why paint stocks whipsaw on quarterly margins even when volumes are fine — and why "margin recovery" was the FY26 headline for Berger.

What goes into a can of paint
InputWhat it isWhy it matters
Titanium dioxide (TiO2)The white pigment that gives paint opacity — the single biggest raw material.A globally traded mineral; prices spiked on supply disruptions and stayed elevated. The largest swing factor in gross margin.
Resins / monomersThe binder (acrylics, alkyds) — largely crude-oil derivatives.Move with Brent crude. When oil rises, paint gross margins compress unless prices are hiked.
Solvents & additivesThinners, driers, biocides — also petro-linked.Smaller, but reinforce the crude sensitivity of the whole basket.
PackagingMetal and plastic cans.Ties input costs to steel/plastic prices as well as crude.

Raw materials are roughly 55–60% of a paint company's cost base. Because they move with global crude and TiO2, a paint company's gross margin is never stable — it's a spread the company constantly manages. The playbook is well-worn: when inputs rise, hike prices (with a lag); when inputs fall, either take the margin or pass it on to grab volume.

The FY26 read: soft inputs rescued the margin

FY26 is a textbook illustration. Berger held back on price hikes to defend volume against new entrants — normally a margin headwind. But raw-material prices softened through much of the year, and mix improved (more premium products), so the gross-margin gap actually widened. The result: Q4 FY26 EBITDA margin reached ~16.8% (a ~10-quarter high) and PAT jumped ~27.5%, even though value growth was muted. The tension for FY27: with Brent reportedly back above ~$90/barrel and TiO2 still elevated, and with only "calibrated" price hikes taken, the input tailwind could reverse — putting the margin recovery to the test just as the competitive pressure peaks.

06

The disruption threat: Birla Opus and the end of a cosy oligopoly

The one force an analyst leads with today — the structural question that governs the whole stock.

For decades, Indian paints was one of the most stable, profitable oligopolies in the country: Asian Paints ~50%+, then Berger, then Kansai Nerolac, then the rest — a club protected by that distribution moat, growing steadily, earning FMCG-like returns, and rarely fighting on price. Between 2024 and 2026 that club was gate-crashed. This is the defining current question for Berger's stock, and it deserves its own section.

Birla Opus — the ₹10,000 Cr challenger built from scratch

  • Who: Birla Opus, the paints arm of Grasim Industries (Aditya Birla group) — one of India's largest, deepest-pocketed conglomerates. It entered in 2024 with a declared intent to become #2 fast.
  • How much: a reported ₹10,000+ Cr invested in capacity — six manufacturing plants — reaching ~24% of India's decorative paint capacity by late 2025, second only to Asian Paints. And it built ~45,000 tinting machines at dealers in ~18 months, directly attacking the distribution moat.
  • Traction: by early 2026 Birla Opus had crossed ~10% revenue market share in decorative — growing at roughly 3× the industry rate — a genuinely rapid land-grab, driven by aggressive dealer incentives and discounting.

JSW Dulux — a second heavyweight arrives

  • The deal: in Dec 2025, JSW Paints (of the ~$23bn JSW Group) completed the acquisition of a majority stake (~61%) in Akzo Nobel India — the maker of Dulux, previously India's #4 with ~10% share. In March 2026 it was renamed JSW Dulux Limited (FY26 turnover ~₹3,600 Cr).
  • Why it matters: JSW instantly bought a top-tier brand and distribution, rather than building from zero — a second well-funded, ambitious industrial house now inside the paints club.

Why this is the whole game — and how it hits Berger specifically

New entrants change paint economics in three ways. (1) Pricing/discounting. To buy share, Birla Opus discounts and showers dealers with incentives — forcing incumbents to raise their trade spend and hold back price hikes, which compresses margins (visible in Asian Paints' gross margin slipping toward ~40%). (2) Dealer poaching. The moat is the dealer; a new entrant with 45,000 tinting machines and fat margins for the dealer can peel loyalty away. (3) Capacity glut. Far more paint-making capacity now exists than the market needs, which structurally pressures prices for years. For Berger, the tell is FY26 itself: strong volumes but muted value growth — the signature of a company choosing to defend share by not fully pricing up. So far Berger has held its ~20% share better than Asian Paints has held its lead — arguably the challenger has less to lose and clearer room to fight for.

The honest bear framing: the entire "paints is a wonderful compounding sector" thesis rested on a stable oligopoly that rarely competed on price. That premise is now genuinely in question. If Birla Opus keeps taking ~1 point of share a quarter and the price war persists, the sector's historic pricing power — and its premium multiples — could structurally reset lower, hitting every incumbent including Berger. Bulls counter that distribution moats take a decade to build, that Birla Opus is buying share unprofitably, and that a rational duopoly-plus can still earn well. Which of these is right is the single most important debate in the stock.
A live signal to watch: reporting around a Birla Opus leadership change (a CEO departure) in 2026 prompted a relief rally in incumbents like Asian Paints — a sign of how tightly incumbent share prices are now tethered to the entrant's momentum. Track Birla Opus's share gains, dealer additions and profitability each quarter: they are, in effect, a leading indicator for Berger's margin outlook.

The competitive set

Berger sits second in a market that has just gone from a comfortable oligopoly to a crowded, contested field. Knowing each rival's angle explains where the pressure comes from and where Berger's relative position is strongest.

Where Berger's relative position is genuinely strong

Three points in Berger's favour. First, it is the challenger, not the incumbent under attack — Asian Paints has ~50% to defend and lose; Berger has ~20% and room to gain, and so far has held its share better than the leader. Second, Berger has historically strong distribution in East and North India, its home turf, which is harder for a newcomer to crack than the metros. Third, its diversified tail (protective coatings, construction chemicals, auto JV) gives it profit pools the pure-decorative price war doesn't touch. None of this makes it immune — but it means Berger enters the disruption era from a more defensible starting point than the market leader.

08

Growth vectors and the defence plan

How Berger intends to reach ₹20,000 Cr by 2030 — and out-flank the entrants.

Berger's answer to the disruption is not just to defend the wall-paint shelf, but to grow the pie and grow into adjacencies where it can compound away from the fiercest price competition. CEO Abhijit Roy has set a public target of ₹20,000 Cr revenue by 2030 (from ~₹11,900 Cr in FY26) — roughly an 11% CAGR — resting on several stacked vectors.

The strategy in one sentence

Berger's plan is to hold the decorative core on volume and premiumisation while compounding faster in the adjacencies — waterproofing, construction chemicals, protective and industrial coatings — that the new entrants haven't attacked yet. If it works, Berger keeps its ~20% decorative share, lifts realisation through mix, and grows the group at a double-digit rate to ₹20,000 Cr — turning a defensive war into an offensive expansion. The risk is that the decorative price war is so intense it drags down the whole group's margin faster than the adjacencies can compensate.

The next few years are less about who makes the best paint and more about who can defend the dealer, the tinting machine and the painter's recommendation while the two richest houses in India try to take them away.

How to think about the ~52× multiple

Berger trades at roughly 52× trailing earnings — a premium that only makes sense in a world where paints keeps its pricing power and Berger keeps compounding at low-teens with high returns on capital. The disruption puts a question mark on both the growth rate and the margin, which is why paint stocks have been a battleground between bulls ("the moats will hold; the entrants are buying share unprofitably") and bears ("the sector's golden era of pricing power is structurally over; a 50×+ multiple has a long way to fall"). Berger's ~20% share, strong ROCE and near-debt-free balance sheet argue for a durable business — but at this multiple, the stock is priced for the oligopoly to survive largely intact.

10

Why paints has been a great compounding sector

The structural qualities that made the whole industry a wealth-creator — and why they're being tested.

Step back from Berger and ask: why have Indian paint companies been among the market's best long-term compounders? The answer is a rare stack of favourable qualities — and understanding them explains both the bull case and exactly what the new entrants threaten.

  • Consumer economics, commodity inputs. Paint is bought like a consumer product (brand, trust, low ticket, infrequent) but priced with real markup — so companies earn FMCG-style gross margins on what is technically a commodity.
  • Pricing power. A homeowner repainting once in 5–7 years is highly price-insensitive; a 5% price difference is trivial versus the pain of a bad finish. That lets companies pass on input-cost rises.
  • Distribution moat. Tens of thousands of dealers and tinting machines take a decade and huge capital to build — a formidable entry barrier (the thing now under direct assault).
  • Asset-light, cash-rich. Modest working capital, dealers hold inventory, factories are cheap relative to sales — so returns on capital are high and free cash flow funds growth and dividends without debt.
  • Structural demand. Rising incomes, urbanisation, shrinking repainting cycles, "premiumisation" of homes, and a shift from unbranded to branded paint — a long runway of volume growth.
The one thread that could unravel it

Notice that four of those five qualities depend on pricing power and the distribution moat — and both rest on the oligopoly staying rational and hard to enter. That is precisely what Birla Opus and JSW are testing. If a deep-pocketed entrant proves the moat can be replicated with enough capital, and if a capacity glut forces sustained discounting, then the sector's pricing power erodes and its premium multiple compresses. The long-term structural tailwinds (rising incomes, formalisation, premiumisation) remain intact and large — the demand story isn't the question. The question is how the enlarged pie gets divided, and at what margin. That is the hinge on which the next five years of Berger's stock turns.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely high-quality #2 — meeting the toughest competitive test in its history.

Berger is a well-run, cash-generative, high-return #2 in a structurally growing category, backed by an aligned promoter family with a long track record. The investment debate is no longer really about business quality — it is about whether the disruption reprices the whole sector, and how well the challenger defends and grows through it.

The bull case

  • Best-positioned challenger — ~20% share with room to gain, holding share better than the leader through the disruption.
  • Distribution + brand moat — ~36,000 dealers, tinting-machine base, decades of painter/dealer trust that take a decade to replicate.
  • Volume rebound + margin recovery — Q4 FY26 volumes +11.8%, EBITDA margin at a ~10-quarter high, PAT +27.5%.
  • Adjacency growth — waterproofing/construction chemicals (~25%/yr), protective & industrial coatings tied to infra capex, away from the price war.
  • High quality, self-funded — ~22% ROCE, near-debt-free, ~75% aligned promoter holding, ₹20,000 Cr-by-2030 ambition.

The bear case

  • Structural disruption — Birla Opus (~10%+ share, ~24% capacity) and JSW Dulux threaten the pricing power the whole thesis rests on.
  • Capacity glut → price war — far more capacity than demand structurally pressures prices and margins for years.
  • Rich valuation — ~52× earnings prices in the oligopoly surviving intact; leaves little room for a margin or share stumble.
  • Muted value growth — FY26 revenue +2.9% and PAT −4.6% show the top line already feeling the squeeze despite volume strength.
  • Input-cost swing — crude/TiO2 back up could reverse the FY26 margin tailwind just as competition peaks.
The bottom line

Berger is a structurally high-quality business at a structurally uncertain moment. The company is fine — strong brand, real distribution, high returns, a good balance sheet, and adjacencies to grow into. The sector is the question: a decades-old, pricing-powerful oligopoly is being invaded by two of India's richest houses, and nobody yet knows whether the moats hold or the golden era of paint margins is ending. Watch three numbers every quarter: Berger's decorative volume & share (is it holding?), gross/EBITDA margin (is the price war biting?), and Birla Opus's momentum (is the entrant still accelerating?). Get those right and a ~52× multiple is defensible on a durable compounder; get them wrong and a premium paint stock has a long way to de-rate.
