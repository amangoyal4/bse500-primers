Aegis Logistics, India's private LPG & terminals landlord

A complete walk through India's leading private oil, gas & chemical logistics company — not an oil producer, but the owner of giant port-side storage tanks. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Aegis Logistics is India's largest private oil, gas & chemical logistics company. It does not pump oil or refine fuel. It is, in plain terms, a landlord and toll-collector for energy in liquid form: it owns and operates the enormous storage tanks at India's ports where imported LPG (cooking gas), chemicals and petroleum products are received, stored and dispatched — and it also imports LPG itself and sells it on to India's state oil companies and to private industrial, commercial and auto-LPG customers.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It runs two very different businesses. A high-margin, stable terminals / storage business (throughput and tolling fees on other people's cargo — annuity-like infrastructure income), and a high-volume, thin-margin LPG sourcing & distribution business (importing and selling gas). In FY26 the LPG business earned revenue of ₹7,689 Cr with EBITDA up 68%, while the liquid-terminals business earned ₹644 Cr of revenue but ₹472 Cr of EBITDA — a reminder that in Aegis you watch profit, not revenue.
  • The defining recent event is the Vopak JV. Aegis pooled its terminal assets with Dutch tank-storage giant Royal Vopak into Aegis Vopak Terminals Ltd (AVTL) — India's largest third-party tank-storage platform — which was itself IPO'd and listed on 2 June 2025. Aegis holds ~50.1% of AVTL; the listing gave the terminal empire its own currency to fund a multi-billion-dollar expansion.
  • The tailwind is structural. India imports roughly 60% of its LPG, and demand keeps rising as cooking-gas connections spread. That means more cargo through Aegis's tanks, more gas to distribute, and — newly — the option to store ammonia for the energy transition. FY26 delivered ₹8,333 Cr revenue (+23%), ₹1,599 Cr normalised EBITDA (+36%) and ₹1,107 Cr profit (+41%).
In plain terms

Picture the ports where ships full of cooking gas and chemicals arrive from the Middle East. Someone has to own the giant tanks that catch that cargo, the pipelines that move it ashore, and the trucks and bottling plants that carry it onward. That "someone", for the private sector, is largely Aegis. It makes money two ways: as a landlord — charging a fee every time cargo passes through its tanks (steady, high-margin, contracted) — and as a trader — buying LPG cheaply abroad and selling it dearer at home (huge volumes, thin margin per tonne, but rising fast). The exciting new chapter is that it merged the landlord half with a Dutch storage giant and listed it separately, then aimed that vehicle at India's relentless growth in imported energy.

01

What Aegis actually is (and isn't)

The single most common misunderstanding: this is infrastructure, not an oil company.

Aegis sits in the NSE "Oil Gas & Consumable Fuels" sector, which invites the wrong mental model. It is not exposed to the oil price the way an explorer or refiner is. It owns the plumbing between the ship and the customer. Get this right and the whole company snaps into focus.

The three things Aegis owns and operates

  • Gas (LPG) terminals — cryogenic and pressurised tanks at ports that receive imported LPG (propane, butane) from ships, hold it, and dispatch it by pipeline, rail and road. This is the crown jewel: import capacity is scarce, permitted, and located at exactly the coastal points where India's LPG shortfall lands.
  • Liquid / chemical terminals — tank farms that store petroleum products, chemicals, vegetable oils and other bulk liquids for third parties. Customers rent capacity; Aegis charges a storage/throughput fee. This is the purest "landlord" income in the group.
  • LPG sourcing & distribution — Aegis buys LPG on global markets, brings it through its own terminals, and sells it to (a) the state oil marketing companies (OMCs) who need imports to meet subsidised cooking-gas demand, and (b) its own private book of commercial, industrial and autogas (vehicle-LPG) customers under the "Aegis" brand.
Why the distinction matters for your money

An oil producer's profits swing violently with crude prices. Aegis's terminal income does not — it earns a fee for handling and storing cargo, whether the underlying molecule is expensive or cheap. That's why the terminals half is valued like infrastructure (steady, contracted, recurring) rather than like a commodity business. The sourcing half is more price-sensitive — margins can wobble with global LPG prices and the rupee — but even there, Aegis largely earns a spread (a per-tonne handling-and-distribution margin) rather than betting on the flat price of gas. So the right question about Aegis is never "where's the oil price going?" It's "how much cargo is flowing through India's ports, and how much of it touches an Aegis tank?"

02

Terminal economics 101: how a tank earns money

No engineering degree required — just the handful of ideas that drive the profit.

A storage terminal is, financially, a capacity business — like a hospital with beds or a warehouse with pallet slots. It has a fixed amount of tankage and a largely fixed cost base (the tanks, the jetty, the pipelines, safety systems, staff), and it earns by moving cargo through and by renting space. A few concepts explain almost all of it.

The vocabulary of a terminals business
TermWhat it means (and why it matters)
Static capacityThe physical size of the tanks — how much can be held at once. Measured in metric tonnes (MT) for LPG and cubic metres (cbm / KL) for liquids. It's the ceiling on the business.
ThroughputHow much cargo actually passes through over a year. A tank can turn over many times a year, so throughput can far exceed static capacity — this is the real revenue driver. Aegis's LPG terminal throughput was ~5.15 million tonnes in FY26 (+14%).
Tolling / terminalling feeThe fee charged per tonne or per cbm handled/stored. This is annuity-like income — often under take-or-pay contracts where the customer pays for reserved capacity whether or not they use it.
Take-or-payA contract that guarantees Aegis a minimum payment regardless of actual volumes — the mechanism that turns a tank into a bond-like cash flow and de-risks new capacity before it's even full.
UtilisationWhat share of capacity is contracted/used. Higher utilisation on a fixed cost base means near-pure profit on each extra tonne — the operating leverage that makes terminals so attractive once built.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Terminal profit ≈ Capacity × Utilisation × Fee − (mostly fixed) costs. Because a tank's costs barely change whether it's half-full or full, once a terminal crosses its break-even utilisation, almost every extra rupee of fee drops to EBITDA. That's why the liquid-terminals business earns a ₹472 Cr EBITDA on just ₹644 Cr of revenue — a ~73% margin. It's also why the whole strategy is to build capacity in the right ports and then fill it under long contracts: the up-front capex is heavy, but a filled, contracted tank is one of the most durable cash flows in industry. The catch is the mirror image — a new tank sitting under-utilised burns fixed cost until the contracts ramp, and capacity that's built in the wrong place, or ahead of demand, is expensive dead weight.

The two engines: infrastructure income vs trading volume

Read this once and Aegis's income statement stops being confusing.

Aegis's revenue and its profit tell almost opposite stories, and confusing the two is the classic mistake. The sourcing/distribution engine is a revenue giant but a margin minnow; the terminals engine is a revenue minnow but a margin giant. You must look at where the EBITDA comes from.

Why the thin-margin engine still matters

If terminals are so much more profitable per rupee of revenue, why bother with low-margin distribution at all? Three reasons. (1) Volume feeds the tanks. The more LPG Aegis distributes, the more throughput flows through its terminals — the two engines reinforce each other. (2) The distribution margin is growing, not just big. Management reported gas-distribution EBITDA rising to roughly ₹7,000 per tonne (from historical ~₹4,000) as the private commercial/industrial/autogas book scales — that's a real, expanding spread, not just churn. (3) It builds the customer relationships (OMCs, industrial buyers) that make Aegis the natural home for India's imported gas. So distribution is the flywheel that keeps the high-margin terminals busy — you want both engines running.

04

The company and who controls it

A family-controlled group with a fifty-year history in liquid logistics.

Aegis is a promoter-controlled company run by the Chandaria family. It grew from a chemicals-and-liquid-terminals business at Mumbai into India's leading private energy-logistics platform, and in 2025 crystallised its terminal assets into the separately listed AVTL vehicle.

Timeline

  • 1950s–70s
    The Chandaria group's roots in liquid handling and chemicals; Aegis emerges as a bulk-liquid terminalling business anchored at Mumbai.
  • 1990s–2000s
    Pivots into LPG import terminals and distribution as India's cooking-gas demand outstrips domestic supply — building the gas-logistics franchise that defines it today.
  • 2016
    Founder-chairman Kapoorchand Chandaria passes away; leadership continues within the family. Raj Chandaria serves as Chairman & Managing Director.
  • 2010s–2020s
    Aggressive terminal build-out across Kandla, Pipavav, Mangalore, Kochi, Haldia, JNPT; liquid capacity doubled toward ~2 million cbm; LPG static capacity built past ~226,000 MT.
  • 2024
    Forms the Aegis Vopak Terminals (AVTL) joint venture with Royal Vopak of the Netherlands — pooling terminal assets into India's largest third-party tank-storage platform.
  • Jun 2025
    AVTL IPO — a ~₹2,800 Cr issue; AVTL lists on NSE & BSE on 2 June 2025. Aegis retains ~50.1%.
  • FY26
    Record year: revenue ₹8,333 Cr (+23%), normalised EBITDA ₹1,599 Cr (+36%), PAT ₹1,107 Cr (+41%). India's first independent ammonia terminal (Pipavav) nears completion; a ~$5bn capex vision through 2030 laid out.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Chandaria family holds ~58.1% of Aegis Logistics (with the family holding vehicle Huron Holdings alone holding ~31.67%). Raj Chandaria is Chairman & Managing Director. A high, unencumbered promoter stake signals long-term alignment.
  • Institutional interest: foreign institutions hold ~19.6% and domestic institutions ~3.7% — a meaningful, well-followed float, consistent with Aegis's status as a recognised infrastructure name.
  • Structure: the terminal assets now sit largely inside the separately listed AVTL (Aegis ~50.1%, Vopak ~47.3%), while sourcing/distribution and certain other operations run through Aegis Logistics. So Aegis is increasingly a holding-plus-operating structure over a listed terminals platform — something to keep in mind when valuing it (see §10).
A structural nuance worth flagging: because much of the terminal empire is now held via a separately listed subsidiary (AVTL), Aegis Logistics's own results consolidate AVTL but its stock partly trades as a "holding company" over that stake. Watch for (a) minority interest in the P&L, and (b) the usual holdco-discount debate — the market may not give Aegis full credit for the AVTL stake's market value.
05

The terminal network — where the tanks are

A necklace of coastal terminals at exactly the ports where imports land.

Aegis's (and now largely AVTL's) terminals sit at the strategic import gateways of India's coastline. Collectively, the platform handles a striking share of the country's seaborne energy: its port locations account for roughly 61% of India's LPG imports and ~23% of its liquid imports. Location is the moat — you cannot easily build a new import terminal at an already-congested, permitted port.

Why "you can't just build another one" is the moat

A port terminal isn't a factory you can drop anywhere. It needs a berth deep enough for gas carriers, a port concession, pipelines to shore, cryogenic engineering, and a mountain of safety and environmental permits — all at a coastal site near existing demand. Those sites are scarce and slow to permit, and the incumbents (Aegis/AVTL among them) already hold the best ones. That scarcity is exactly what lets Aegis sign long take-or-pay contracts and earn infrastructure-grade margins — and it's why a rival can't simply undercut the tolling fee overnight. The flip side: that same permitting difficulty makes Aegis's own expansion slow and capital-heavy, and ties it to port authorities and concession terms it doesn't fully control.

06

The Aegis Vopak JV — the defining growth story

The single most important development of the last two years — read this closely.

In 2024–25 Aegis did something structurally important: it pooled its terminal assets with Royal Vopak, the Dutch tank-storage giant, into a new company — Aegis Vopak Terminals Ltd (AVTL) — and then listed AVTL separately in June 2025. AVTL is now India's largest third-party owner and operator of LPG and liquid tank-storage terminals. This is where the multi-year growth engine lives.

Why this JV is such a big deal

  • Scale & dominance. AVTL controls roughly 11.5% of India's LPG storage and ~25.5% of the country's third-party liquid storage — a genuinely dominant position in a business where scale and location are the barrier to entry.
  • A world-class partner. Vopak operates tank terminals globally and brings engineering, safety and capital discipline. For India's energy-import build-out, having Vopak's expertise inside the vehicle is a real credential — and Vopak holds ~47.3%, so it is a committed co-owner, not a passive investor.
  • Its own listed currency. By IPO-ing AVTL (a ~₹2,800 Cr issue, listed 2 Jun 2025), the group gave the terminals platform its own balance sheet and equity currency to fund expansion — without Aegis Logistics having to carry all the capex itself. It also created a clean, standalone "pure infrastructure" stock that the market can value on infrastructure multiples.
  • Value unlock for Aegis. Aegis Logistics retains ~50.1% of AVTL. As AVTL grows and the market ascribes it a premium infrastructure valuation, Aegis's stake is marked to that — a "sum-of-the-parts" value-unlock that is central to the bull case (and to the holdco-discount debate on the bear side).
The mechanism, in plain terms

Think of AVTL as a separately listed "toll-road company" for energy tanks, part-owned by Aegis and part-owned by a Dutch specialist. Before, all the tanks sat inside Aegis and got lumped in with the low-margin trading business, so the market arguably under-valued them. Now the tanks live in their own listed vehicle that investors can value cleanly as infrastructure — steady, contracted, growing — while Aegis keeps majority control and consolidates the profits. The JV also solved the funding problem: India's import-capacity build-out needs billions of dollars of tanks, and putting that capex inside a dedicated, listed, Vopak-backed vehicle is a far more efficient way to raise it than loading it onto the parent. In short, the JV is simultaneously a scale play, a partnership play, a funding play and a valuation-unlock play — which is why it dominates the Aegis story.

The AVTL listing turned Aegis's tanks from a hidden asset inside a trading company into a visible, separately valued, Vopak-backed infrastructure platform — and gave it the money to grow.
07

The LPG-import super-cycle — the structural tailwind

The demand-side force that makes the terminals fill up year after year.

Aegis's tanks only compound if the cargo keeps growing. Here the structural picture is unusually favourable: India is a chronic, growing importer of LPG, and that import shortfall is precisely the volume that flows through Aegis's terminals. This is the demand engine behind the whole investment case.

India imports roughly 60% of its LPG, and domestic production simply can't keep pace with a household base of ~332 million connections. Every incremental cylinder of cooking gas that must be imported is potential throughput for the terminals.

The drivers are structural and slow-moving, which is exactly what an infrastructure owner wants:

  • Rising household penetration. Government schemes like Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (PMUY) put clean cooking gas into ~104 million lower-income homes; total connections have swelled to ~332 million. More cylinders in more kitchens means more LPG consumed — and India can't produce enough, so imports rise.
  • Domestic supply can't catch up. India's own LPG output grows slowly; the gap is filled at the coast. That gap is the terminals' business.
  • The OMCs need private import capacity. The state oil marketing companies (IOCL, BPCL, HPCL) handle 90%+ of the subsidised retail market but rely on infrastructure like Aegis's to physically receive and store imported cargo — making Aegis a critical-path partner, not a competitor.
  • A growing private book. Beyond the OMCs, Aegis sells to commercial, industrial and autogas customers where margins are richer and rising — the ₹7,000/tonne distribution EBITDA figure reflects this mix improving.
The policy caveat: LPG is a politically sensitive, subsidised fuel. Cooking-gas prices are held low for households (PMUY cylinders around ₹600), the subsidy burden falls on the government and OMCs, and India's heavy import reliance runs largely through the Strait of Hormuz — a geopolitical chokepoint. None of this directly hits Aegis's tolling income (Aegis gets paid to handle cargo regardless of the subsidy), but policy shifts, price controls or supply disruptions can affect volumes and the sourcing margin. It's a tailwind with a political undertow.
08

Ammonia & the energy-transition optionality

The free call option embedded in a tank-storage business.

Here is the part of the story that could matter most in the 2030s. The same cryogenic-tank expertise that lets Aegis store LPG lets it store ammonia — and ammonia is emerging as a key carrier for the hydrogen / clean-energy economy. Aegis (via AVTL) is building India's first independent ammonia storage terminal, positioning the group at the front of a potentially large new market.

  • Pipavav ammonia terminal. A 36,000 MT cryogenic ammonia terminal at Pipavav — India's first independent third-party ammonia storage — is under construction, targeted for commissioning around Q1 FY27. It is underpinned by a 15-year take-or-pay contract with industrial off-takers (reportedly including Hindustan Zinc) — i.e. contracted, annuity-like income from day one.
  • A committed global partner in the sub. Japan's ITOCHU Corporation has taken a ~10% stake in the ammonia subsidiary (Aegis Terminal Pipavav), with an option to raise it toward 25% — validation from a major trading house.
  • Green-ammonia optionality. A next phase envisages a large green-ammonia storage plant (~80,000 tonnes) in Odisha — a bet that ammonia becomes a workhorse for hydrogen transport, clean fertiliser and low-carbon fuel. Broker estimates suggest ammonia terminals at Kandla and Pipavav could add a couple of hundred crore of EBITDA by FY28.
Why ammonia is the smart adjacency

Ammonia (NH₃) is one of the most practical ways to move hydrogen: it's easier to liquefy and ship than pure hydrogen, and it's already a global commodity (for fertiliser). As the world builds a clean-energy supply chain, someone has to own the import/export tanks where green ammonia lands — and that is exactly the same competence Aegis already has for LPG: cryogenic storage at a port, under long contracts. So the ammonia push isn't a risky diversification into an unrelated field — it's the company applying its core skill to a new molecule with a potentially enormous, policy-supported market. Crucially, because the first terminal is on a 15-year take-or-pay contract, Aegis isn't speculating on the hydrogen economy; it's being paid to build the infrastructure whether or not the boom arrives on schedule. That's a low-risk way to hold a high-upside option — the definition of good optionality.

Keep it in proportion: the ammonia/green-hydrogen theme is genuine optionality, not today's earnings. It depends on the clean-energy transition actually scaling, on off-takers honouring long contracts, and on green-ammonia economics working. Treat it as a valuable call option on the future — not as a number to put in a near-term model.
09

The industry and the rivals

A scarce, consolidated business — and Aegis/AVTL is the biggest private player.

India's third-party tank-storage and private LPG-logistics market is concentrated and infrastructure-like — a handful of players hold the scarce port sites. Aegis, through AVTL, is the clear private leader; its main "competitor" for household LPG is really the state itself.

The competitive tension to watch

Two dynamics matter. First, scarcity protects the incumbents: because you can't easily build a new import terminal at a good port, Aegis/AVTL's leading positions are genuinely defensible — this is closer to owning toll roads than running a factory. Second, the relationship with the state OMCs is symbiotic, not merely adversarial: the OMCs need private import-and-storage capacity to serve subsidised demand, so Aegis is a critical supplier to the very players who dominate retail. The real competitive risks are subtler: OMCs building more of their own terminals (reducing third-party reliance), a large new entrant winning a port concession, or pricing pressure on tolling fees. But in a business defined by permitted coastal sites, the moat is the map — and Aegis holds a lot of the map.

How to think about the ~50× multiple

Fifty times earnings is a steep price — so what is the market paying for? Three things. (1) Infrastructure quality: scarce, permitted, contracted port terminals with take-or-pay income deserve an infrastructure multiple, not a trading multiple. (2) A long growth runway: the LPG-import super-cycle, the AVTL capex pipeline, and the ammonia optionality point to years of throughput growth. (3) The AVTL value-unlock: Aegis owns ~50.1% of a separately listed platform the market values richly. The bear's reply is equally clear: at 50×, a lot of that future is already in the price; the sourcing margin can wobble; the build-out is lifting debt and interest cost; and a "holdco over a listed sub" can trade at a discount rather than a premium. Both views are defensible — which is why the numbers to track each quarter are terminal throughput, distribution margin per tonne, and AVTL's own progress.

The growth runway

A multi-billion-dollar capex pipeline aimed at India's import gateways.

Aegis's growth is unusually visible because it's capex you can see being poured. The group (largely through AVTL) has laid out a large, staged build-out of new tanks — LPG, liquid and ammonia — across its ports, funded by the listed vehicle's balance sheet.

Three growth engines, stacked

  • Capacity — new LPG, liquid and ammonia tanks across the port grid, much of it pre-contracted under take-or-pay before it opens.
  • Utilisation & throughput — India's rising LPG imports filling existing and new capacity, lifting fee income on a fixed cost base (operating leverage).
  • Distribution margin — the private commercial/industrial/autogas book scaling, pushing distribution EBITDA per tonne higher (~₹7,000 vs ~₹4,000 historically).
The capex trade-off: a ~$5bn build-out is the growth engine and the risk. It requires the LPG-import and ammonia demand to show up on schedule; it is lifting debt and interest cost (flagged in FY26 results); and returns depend on filling the new tanks under good contracts. The take-or-pay model de-risks a lot of this — but heavy capex ahead of demand is always the thing to watch in an infrastructure builder.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A scarce infrastructure franchise — priced for it to keep executing.

Aegis is one of the cleaner infrastructure stories in Indian energy: scarce, permitted port terminals throwing off contracted, high-margin income; a fast-growing LPG-distribution flywheel on top; a world-class partner in Vopak; and genuine ammonia optionality. As with most quality infrastructure, the debate is about price and execution, not whether the business is good.

The bull case

  • Scarce infrastructure moat — permitted port terminals at India's key import gateways; ~61% of LPG imports touch these ports. Very hard to replicate.
  • Structural demand — India imports ~60% of its LPG and demand keeps rising; the terminals are built to catch exactly that shortfall.
  • The AVTL platform — India's #1 third-party tank storage, Vopak-backed, separately listed with its own capital and a ~$5bn pipeline; Aegis owns ~50.1%.
  • Two-margin model — annuity-like terminal income (liquid terminals ~73% EBITDA margin) plus a distribution spread that is widening (~₹7,000/t).
  • Ammonia optionality — India's first independent ammonia terminal on a 15-yr take-or-pay contract; a low-risk call on the hydrogen economy.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~50× earnings leaves little margin for error; any stumble can de-rate an infrastructure multiple hard.
  • Capex & leverage risk — a ~$5bn build-out is lifting debt and interest cost; returns depend on filling new tanks on schedule.
  • Sourcing-margin volatility — the trading/distribution spread can wobble with global LPG prices, the rupee and competition.
  • Policy & concession risk — LPG is subsidised and political; terminals depend on port concessions and permits Aegis doesn't fully control; imports run through the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Holdco discount — much of the value now sits in a separately listed sub (AVTL); the market may not credit Aegis with the full stake value.
The bottom line

Aegis is a scarce, structurally advantaged energy-infrastructure franchise — the private landlord of India's imported LPG, chemicals and (soon) ammonia — with a fast-growing distribution flywheel, a world-class JV partner, and a long, visible capex runway. The business risk is relatively low for an energy name because so much income is contracted and infrastructure-like. The valuation and execution risk is real: a 50× multiple already prices in years of successful build-out, and the story now depends on filling a multi-billion-dollar pipeline of tanks under good contracts while the AVTL structure is fairly valued. Track three numbers each quarter — terminal throughput (is the cargo flowing?), distribution EBITDA per tonne (is the spread widening?), and AVTL's own progress (is the platform delivering?). Get those right and the compounding justifies the premium; stumble on capex or margins, and a premium multiple has a long way to fall.
