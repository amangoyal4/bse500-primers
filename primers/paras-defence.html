Paras Defence, the eyes of the armed forces

A complete walk through India's most specialised listed defence company — a niche maker of the precision optics, electro-optics and hardened electronics that India historically had to import. How a small firm ends up as ISRO's optics supplier and Asia's only submarine-periscope maker, why its value is really an order book multiplied by an import-substitution premium, and why a genuinely rare business trades at one of the market's most demanding multiples.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Paras Defence and Space Technologies makes the hard-to-build bits inside India's weapons and satellites — precision optics and lenses, submarine periscopes, space-grade mirrors, rugged military electronics, blast-hardened "EMP" shielding, and the flow-formed tubes that become rocket and missile bodies. It is not a builder of whole tanks, jets or ships; it is a component-and-subsystem specialist that sells into the big defence and space organisations — DRDO, ISRO, the shipyards, the ordnance factories and the large private primes. Almost everything it does sits in a niche India used to buy from abroad, which is precisely the point: an indigenous supplier in a strategic import-substitution niche gets both a customer and a national-priority tailwind.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is genuinely rare, not just small. Paras is described as the only submarine-periscope manufacturer in the Asia-Pacific region, the exclusive supplier of certain optical systems to ISRO, and the sole Indian private maker of space-grade hyperspectral cameras. These are not commodity parts you can shop around for — they are certified, single-source, strategic capabilities. That scarcity is the whole reason a ~₹477 Cr-revenue company carries a ~₹10,500 Cr market value.
  • Two engines: optics and defence engineering. Revenue splits into Optics & Optronic Systems (~₹199 Cr in FY26) and Defence Engineering (~₹277 Cr — defence electronics, EMP protection and heavy engineering). On top sits a fast-growing new leg in drones and anti-drone systems, run through subsidiaries. The order book of ~₹1,432 Cr is roughly three years of revenue already booked, tilted toward the higher-margin optics side.
  • The business is real; the price is demanding. FY26 revenue rose ~31% to ~₹477 Cr and net profit ~46% to ~₹89 Cr, with an operating margin around 25%. But the stock trades near 120× earnings — among the richest multiples in the entire market — pricing in years of compounding from the optics ramp and the drone push. The gap between a fine business and an extraordinary valuation is the entire debate.
In plain terms

Paras is a precision-parts house for war and space. When the navy needs a periscope, when ISRO needs a mirror ground to a fraction of a wavelength, when a command bunker must survive an electromagnetic pulse, or when a missile needs a seamless metal tube, someone has to make those to exacting, certified standards — and for a long time India imported most of them. Paras built the in-house capability to make them here. That gives it a defensible, high-barrier niche and a patriotic tailwind. The catch is arithmetic: the market has already paid up for a decade of flawless execution, so the shares can be right about the business and still be expensive on the price.

01

Defence optics 101: why these niches are so hard to enter

No physics degree needed — just why a lens or a shielded box can be a strategic moat.

To understand Paras you have to understand why its products are difficult. A rifle sight, a satellite camera and a submarine periscope look nothing alike, but they share a trait: they demand precision, certification and secrecy that ordinary manufacturing can't supply. That combination — not the raw part itself — is the barrier to entry, and it is what turns a small engineering firm into a strategic asset.

The handful of terms that run through the whole business
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Optics / electro-opticsLenses, mirrors and prisms that bend and focus light — and the electronics that turn that light into an image (a night-vision sight, a thermal camera, a periscope). "Optronics" = optics + electronics fused. Paras's crown jewel.
Space-grade opticsMirrors and lenses for satellites, ground to nanometre precision and built to survive launch and the vacuum of space. A different league from a camera lens — few firms on earth can make them, fewer in India.
EW & EMPElectronic Warfare (jamming, sensing, countering enemy signals) and Electro-Magnetic Pulse protection (shielding a bunker, ship or data centre so a burst of electromagnetic energy — from a weapon or a nuclear event — doesn't fry its electronics).

IndigenisationMaking at home what India used to import. Defence policy actively favours it (import bans on listed items, higher local-content rules), so an Indian supplier of a formerly-imported part enjoys a captive, protected demand.
The economics that decide everything: the import-substitution premium

Here is the mechanism that makes Paras valuable. In a normal industry, a small supplier competes on price and lives on thin margins. In strategic defence optics and electronics, the picture inverts. The part must be certified (years of qualification with the armed forces or ISRO), single-source (once you're designed into a weapon programme, switching you out is enormously costly), and increasingly mandated to be domestic (policy bans importing what India can make). Each of those raises the wall around the incumbent. So Paras earns roughly a 25% operating margin on niche products a generalist engineering firm would never touch — the reward for doing certified, indigenous, security-cleared work that almost no one else in India can do. That premium is the moat.

Who Paras sells to

  • The government & DPSUs — DRDO's labs, the Ordnance / Opto-Electronics factories, and defence public-sector units that need optics and electronics for their platforms. The traditional bulk buyer.
  • ISRO & the space ecosystem — the national space agency and the new crop of private space firms, buying mirrors, lenses and hyperspectral cameras. A prestige customer that certifies Paras's world-class capability.
  • The big defence primes — India's large public and private platform builders (the shipyards, the missile and aircraft programmes) that assemble whole systems and buy Paras's subsystems to put inside them.
  • Export customers — foreign defence firms such as Elbit (Israel) and CERBAIR (France) buying Paras's electro-optics and counter-drone systems. Small today, but a proof-point that the quality is world-standard.
02

What Paras actually makes: two verticals, one specialist DNA

The product map — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Paras organises itself into two reported verticals, but they share one identity: precision components that go inside someone else's platform. It rarely sells a finished weapon. It sells the eye, the brain, the shield or the body — the specialised subsystem that the platform can't work without.

Paras is the specialist the platform-builders buy from. Its value is not the whole weapon — it is the certified, single-source subsystem that makes the weapon see, think, survive or fly.

Vertical 1 — Optics & Optronic Systems (~₹199 Cr, FY26)

This is the higher-margin, harder-to-replicate half and the reason Paras is a household name among defence watchers. It spans defence optics (sighting systems, night-vision and thermal imaging, laser range-finders), naval optronics (submarine periscopes and attack/search optronic masts), and space optics (large mirrors, diffractive gratings, infrared lenses and hyperspectral cameras for satellites). Grinding and coating optics to the tolerances a satellite or a submarine needs is a craft measured in nanometres — a capability that takes years and heavy investment to build, and once built is very hard for a rival to match.

Vertical 2 — Defence Engineering (~₹277 Cr, FY26)

  • Defence electronics — ruggedised military computing, hardened displays, command-and-control consoles and electronic-warfare subsystems built to survive shock, heat, vibration and the battlefield. The "brains and nerves" of a platform.
  • EMP protection — turnkey shielding that hardens command centres, ships and critical infrastructure against an electromagnetic-pulse strike. A rare, specialised niche where Paras is a recognised Indian name — the kind of capability that becomes strategically urgent the moment planners take the threat seriously.
  • Heavy engineering — the low-profile foundation: flow-formed seamless tubes that become the bodies of rockets and missiles, titanium structures, and precision mechanical assemblies for the space and missile programmes. Less glamorous than optics, but it plugs Paras directly into India's biggest strategic projects.
Why "subsystem specialist" is the identity to hold on to

A tank-maker or shipbuilder wins a headline contract and everyone can name it. Paras works one layer down, and that layer has two very different consequences. On the upside, being a certified single-source of a critical part inside many platforms means Paras rides every programme it's designed into — one periscope design can equip a whole class of submarines, one optics line can serve dozens of missions. On the downside, it depends on the primes' programmes actually moving: if a submarine line slips or a satellite launch is delayed, Paras's beautifully-made part waits in inventory. It owns the hardest, highest-value slice of the value chain — and inherits the timing of everyone it supplies.

03

The company, the IPO frenzy, and who controls it

A promoter-led specialist that arrived on the market in one of India's most oversubscribed listings.

Paras Defence is a founder-controlled company built up over decades by the promoter family before it went public in 2021 in a listing that became a piece of market folklore for the sheer weight of demand. It is headquartered in Navi Mumbai / Mumbai, and it has grown by adding capability — optics, electronics, EMP, heavy engineering, and now drones — rather than by chasing scale in any one commodity product.

Timeline

  • 2009
    Paras Defence and Space Technologies incorporated, consolidating the promoter family's defence-optics and engineering interests into one company.
  • 2010s

  • Sept 2021
    IPO on NSE & BSE — one of the most heavily oversubscribed Indian issues on record (subscribed around 300× overall), the stock listing at a huge premium. Defence indigenisation was the theme investors piled into.
  • 2022–2024
    Expands into drones and anti-drone and allied electronics via subsidiaries and joint ventures (counter-UAV, RF/microwave, EO/IR avionics, quantum technologies); wins first export orders.
  • 2025
    Announces a landmark Optical Systems Development Park in Navi Mumbai (a multi-hundred-crore investment) to scale space-grade and hyperspectral optics — a bet that the space and optics opportunity runs for years.
  • FY26
    Revenue ~₹477 Cr (+31%), net profit ~₹89 Cr (+46%); order book ~₹1,432 Cr; fresh export deals (CERBAIR counter-UAV, Elbit electro-optics). The stock re-rates to a triple-digit multiple.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the founding Munjal family (Sharad Shah and Munjal Shah and associated promoter entities) hold the controlling block — roughly 53% (approx.) as of mid-2026. The company has disclosed no promoter share encumbrance for FY26 — worth noting, because pledged promoter stakes are a common risk flag in small-cap defence names and here there is none.
  • The group map: the listed parent is Paras Defence and Space Technologies Ltd (NSE: PARAS). The newer drone / anti-drone, RF and avionics activities sit in subsidiaries and joint ventures (e.g. Paras Anti-Drone Technologies), so as you value the company, keep track of what is consolidated versus held through partly-owned ventures.
  • Balance sheet: broadly modest debt and a book value around ₹90/share — but with a heavy working-capital footprint (long receivable cycles, discussed in §08) that is the characteristic strain of a defence supplier waiting on government payment.
Why the IPO frenzy still matters: the ~300× oversubscription in 2021 was not really about Paras's near-term numbers — it was the market voting on a theme: an indigenous, high-tech defence-and-space pure-play at the dawn of Make-in-India defence. That origin story explains the stock's DNA to this day: it has always traded on the promise of the niche, not on the modest scale of the current P&L. Understanding that is half of understanding the valuation.
04

The optics crown jewel: India's eyes in space and under the sea

The single most distinctive asset — and the source of the genuine surprise in this story.

If you strip Paras down to one thing that no ordinary company can copy, it is its optics capability. This is where the barriers are highest, the margins richest, and the strategic value greatest — and it is where a smart generalist gets a jolt of surprise about just how much of India's most advanced hardware runs through one mid-cap in Navi Mumbai.

The genuine surprise: a sub-₹500-crore-revenue Indian company is the exclusive supplier of certain optical systems to ISRO, the only submarine-periscope maker in the Asia-Pacific, and the sole Indian private producer of space-grade hyperspectral cameras. The lenses and mirrors that help India's satellites see the earth, and the periscopes through which its submarines see the surface, are ground and coated by this one specialist — capabilities India otherwise had to buy abroad, now made at home by a firm most investors had never heard of before 2021.

What "space and naval optics" actually covers

  • Space optics for ISRO & missions — large space mirrors, infrared lenses, diffractive gratings and hyperspectral cameras that ride on satellites. Hyperspectral imaging (seeing the earth in hundreds of colour bands, not three) is prized for agriculture, minerals and defence surveillance — and Paras is the lone Indian private maker.
  • Submarine periscopes & optronic masts — the optical systems through which a submarine observes the surface without surfacing. Being the region's only maker gives Paras a near-captive naval customer as India expands its submarine fleet.
  • Defence sighting & imaging — thermal and night-vision sights, laser range-finders and targeting optics that make a soldier's or a vehicle's weapon accurate in the dark. A steady, repeatable order stream tied to army modernisation.
Why the Optical Systems Development Park is the tell

Paras is investing heavily to build a dedicated Optical Systems Development Park in Navi Mumbai — inaugurated with the former ISRO chairman in attendance — to scale space-grade and hyperspectral optics. A company only pours that kind of capital into a single capability when it expects the demand to run for many years, not a few quarters. It is the clearest signal management sends about where it believes the durable growth lies: not in competing on ordinary electronics, but in widening the one moat — advanced optics — that almost no Indian rival can cross. The flip side is that this capacity only earns its return if the space and optics orders arrive at the scale the investment assumes.

05

The drones and anti-drone growth leg

The newest engine — small today, but aimed at a threat that only grows.

The most talked-about part of the 2026 Paras story is not the century-old craft of optics but the newest one: drones and, more importantly, anti-drone systems. Cheap drones and drone swarms have become a front-line battlefield and border threat, and the counter to them — detect, track, jam or destroy — is a fast-growing global defence market. Paras has pushed into it through subsidiaries and partnerships.

How Paras is playing the drone war

  • Counter-UAV (anti-drone) systems — through Paras Anti-Drone Technologies, systems that detect and neutralise rogue drones and swarms. Paras signed a ~₹22 Cr export deal with France's CERBAIR around its CHIMERA-class counter-UAV technology — a foreign customer validating an Indian anti-drone capability, which is telling.
  • RF & microwave and EO/IR avionics — the sensing and signal technologies that sit at the heart of both drones and the systems that hunt them, plus electro-optical / infrared payloads for airborne platforms.
  • Adjacent deep-tech — the group has flagged forays into quantum technologies and allied areas, keeping a foot in the next generation of defence electronics rather than only today's.
Why anti-drone fits Paras's DNA — and why to keep it in proportion

Anti-drone is not a random diversification; it draws on exactly what Paras already owns — electro-optics to see the drone, RF/microwave to sense and jam it, and rugged electronics to run the system. It is the optics-and-electronics moat pointed at a new, growing threat. That is the appeal. The discipline is to keep it in proportion: today the drone leg is a small slice of a ~₹477 Cr company, much of it housed in partly-owned ventures, and export deals of ₹20–35 Cr are promising proof-points rather than needle-movers yet. It is a credible option on a large future market — valued sensibly as a call on growth, not booked as if the growth has already happened.

06

The order book, the funnel, and why defence revenue is lumpy but sticky

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a niche defence supplier.

A defence supplier lives and dies by two numbers most companies don't have: the order book (work already contracted, not yet delivered) and the opportunity funnel (bids and programmes it is chasing). For Paras, these are the real gauges — because current-year revenue is a lagging, lumpy output of contracts signed years earlier. Understanding how those numbers convert is the whole game.

The order book (~₹1,432 Cr) is roughly three years of revenue already booked; management points to a multi-year opportunity funnel of well over ₹12,000 Cr across lasers, optical systems, periscopes and electronic warfare. The story is about converting funnel → order book → revenue.

Defence revenue has two defining traits, and Paras shows both. It is lumpy: a big periscope or optics contract might be signed one year, executed over the next two or three, and recognised in uneven chunks as milestones are hit — so any single quarter can swing hard on the timing of a few deliveries. And it is sticky: once Paras is qualified and designed into a programme, it tends to keep supplying that programme for its life, and repeat orders flow with minimal fresh competition. Lumpy on the way in; durable once it's in.

The device that only fits Paras: value = order book × the indigenous-content premium

Most companies you can value on revenue and growth. A niche defence-optics specialist is better read as an order book multiplied by an import-substitution premium. The order-book size tells you the volume of certified, single-source work locked in. The premium — the extra margin and pricing power that comes from being an indigenous supplier of a formerly-imported, security-cleared, hard-to-certify part — tells you how much each rupee of that backlog is worth. A generic engineering backlog earns a generic multiple; Paras's backlog of periscopes, space optics, EMP shielding and EW is worth more per rupee precisely because so few others in India can supply it and policy actively protects it. Widen the funnel or deepen the premium (more optics, more space, more export) and the value compounds; let either slip and a triple-digit multiple has nothing to stand on.

That is why the metrics to watch are not this quarter's sales but order inflow (is the funnel converting to booked orders?), the optics and drone share of that inflow (is the mix tilting to the highest-premium niches?), and whether the margin holds near the mid-20s as it scales. Those three, read together, are the honest scoreboard for whether the premium is real and durable.

07

Make-in-India and the opening of space

The structural tailwind — two national pushes that point straight at Paras's niches.

Paras did not create its own demand; it sits in the path of two deliberate national programmes. One is defence indigenisation — the drive to make India's military kit at home. The other is the opening of the space sector to private industry. Both convert directly into orders for the exact things Paras makes.

The insight that separates a niche supplier from a platform giant

The tailwind matters differently for Paras than for a large defence PSU. A giant that builds whole aircraft or ships benefits from bigger budgets in a broad, diffuse way. Paras benefits in a concentrated, structural way: indigenisation policy specifically targets the high-tech imported components that are its product line. When the state says "stop importing advanced optics and electronics, make them here," it is, in effect, writing demand toward the handful of firms already qualified to make them — and in several of its niches Paras is that firm, or one of very few. The tailwind is not a rising tide that lifts everyone equally; in these niches it points almost like an arrow.

None of this removes the dependence on the state. Budgets can be re-prioritised, big programmes can slip, and a supplier this concentrated on government and a handful of primes is exposed to their pace and their payment cycles. But the direction of policy — buy Indian, build the space economy, counter the drone — is aimed squarely at what Paras does, which is what lets it talk in terms of a multi-year, ₹12,000-crore-plus opportunity funnel rather than a single good year.

08

The working-capital reality: the strain behind the story

The least glamorous number — and the one that most often bites a defence supplier.

Behind every celebrated defence-technology story sits a stubborn, unglamorous mechanic: getting paid. Selling to the government and to large primes means long, milestone-based contracts and slow settlement, and that ties up cash for months. For a fast-growing supplier, this working-capital drag is the constraint that can quietly cap how fast it can actually grow.

Paras earns a genuinely strong ~25% operating margin and ~19% net margin — impressive for an engineering firm and the clearest financial proof of the import-substitution premium. But margin is an accounting measure, and for a defence supplier it can sit a long way from cash. Paras runs very long receivable cycles — debtor days measured in the high-200s — meaning cash it has "earned" in the accounts can sit as an unpaid bill for the better part of a year. This is not unique to Paras; it is the defence-supplier condition, where the customer is the state and payment follows inspection, acceptance and budget cycles. But it has two real consequences: growth consumes cash (every new order needs to be funded before it's paid for), and the gap between accounting profit and actual cash generation can be large. A reader impressed by the margin should always ask the second question — how much of that profit has turned into cash?

Why working capital is the governor on the growth engine

Picture the constraint plainly. Paras wins a periscope or optics contract; it must buy materials, machine the parts, and carry them through months of manufacturing and inspection before a rupee comes back. Multiply that across a rising order book and the cash tied up in work-in-progress and receivables balloons faster than revenue. If the company can't fund that — through internal cash, modest debt, or fresh capital — it simply cannot execute the backlog as fast as it would like, no matter how strong demand is. The order book sets the ceiling on the opportunity; working capital sets the ceiling on how fast Paras can actually climb toward it. For a stock priced for rapid compounding, the pace of that cash conversion is not a footnote — it is a core part of the thesis.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded defence-supplier field where Paras competes on niche, not scale.

India's listed defence space runs from giant platform builders down to specialist component makers, and Paras sits firmly at the specialist end. It does not really compete with the big integrators; it competes — and often partners — inside their supply chains, and its true rivals are other niche technology suppliers and the foreign firms India is trying to displace.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, Paras's moat is capability, not size — it will never out-scale a BEL, and it doesn't need to; its defence is being one of very few Indian firms that can make a space mirror, a submarine periscope or an EMP shield to spec. That kind of moat is deep but narrow, which is exactly why the order book and the premium (not market share) are the right lenses. Second, the fastest-growing arena it's entering — anti-drone — is also the most contested, with Zen and others already established. So Paras is strongest where it is oldest (optics, periscopes) and most exposed to competition where it is newest (drones). The investment case leans on the durable niches carrying the company while the new leg proves itself.

The one number that settles the thesis: order inflow into the optics/drone niches

At ~120× earnings and ~₹10,500 Cr of market value against ~₹89 Cr of profit, Paras is not priced as a small-cap engineer; it is priced as a compounding franchise that will convert a ₹12,000-crore-plus funnel into years of 30%-plus growth at premium margins. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is order inflow into the highest-premium niches — optics, periscopes, space and anti-drone — quarter after quarter, read against two checks: does the operating margin stay near the mid-20s as it scales, and does cash actually convert (do those high-200s debtor days come down rather than up)? If inflow stays rich, margin holds and cash follows, the funnel is real and the premium is earned. If inflow lumps, margin slips, or receivables keep swelling, a triple-digit multiple has an unforgiving distance to travel. The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely rare business, asked to justify a genuinely rare price.

Paras Defence enters the next phase with a real, widening niche — optics scaling, space opening up, a drone market emerging, a full order book and a supportive policy backdrop. What it must now prove is not that the business is special, but that it can compound fast enough, and convert to cash cleanly enough, to grow into a valuation that already assumes it will.

The bull case

  • A rare, defended moat — certified single-source in space optics, submarine periscopes, EMP and EW; capabilities almost no Indian rival can copy, and policy actively protects.
  • Structural tailwinds aimed at its niches — indigenisation, rising defence budgets, space privatisation and the drone threat all point straight at Paras's product lines.
  • Full order book, huge funnel — ~₹1,432 Cr booked (≈3× revenue) and a stated ₹12,000-crore-plus multi-year opportunity in lasers, optics, periscopes and EW.
  • Premium margins — a ~25% operating margin that has held through the growth, evidence the import-substitution premium is real.
  • Optionality — the anti-drone / RF / quantum ventures and export wins (CERBAIR, Elbit) offer upside beyond the core.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — ~120× earnings prices in flawless, years-long compounding; any stumble in growth or margin can trigger a steep de-rating.
  • Lumpy revenue & program dependence — reliance on government/defence budgets and a few large programmes; a slipped submarine line or delayed launch idles Paras's parts.
  • Working-capital drag — high-200s debtor days mean growth eats cash and reported profit can outrun cash generation.
  • Execution & technology risk — scaling new optics capacity and unproven niches (drones, quantum) against capable rivals like Zen and Data Patterns.
  • Small-cap concentration — a narrow customer base, subsidiary-held ventures, and the volatility of a hot defence-theme stock.
The bottom line

Paras Defence is one of the few Indian companies you can accurately call irreplaceable in its corner: the eyes of the navy's submarines, the optics on the nation's satellites, the shield around its command centres — made here, by design, where they used to be imported. That is a real and rare moat, and the policy winds blow directly into it. The unresolved question is arithmetic, not strategic: whether a company earning ~₹89 Cr can convert a ₹12,000-crore funnel into cash-backed profit fast enough to earn a ~₹10,500 Cr price. The next several quarters answer it in two checkable prints — the pace of order inflow into the optics and drone niches, and whether those high receivable days finally start falling. Watch those two together; they will tell you whether the premium is being earned or merely assumed.
