00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

NMDC Steel Limited is, in essence, one very large, very new steel plant that has been given its own stock listing. The plant is at Nagarnar in Chhattisgarh: a ₹24,000-crore, 3-million-tonne-a-year integrated steel works — meaning it takes iron ore and coal in at one end and pushes finished hot-rolled steel coil and plate out the other, doing every step in between itself. It was built by NMDC Ltd, India's largest iron-ore miner, as a bet to move downstream from digging ore into making steel. In 2023 that plant was carved out of NMDC and floated as a separate government company: NMDC Steel.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • It is basically a single asset. Unlike a SAIL or a JSW with plants scattered across the country, NMDC Steel's entire existence is the one Nagarnar plant. Its revenue, its profit, its debt, its risk — all of it is the output of a single blast furnace complex. That makes it unusually easy to understand and unusually concentrated: there is nowhere to hide if that one plant has a bad year.
  • A brand-new plant loses money before it makes money. A fresh integrated steel plant carries the full weight of its ₹24,000 Cr build — depreciation and interest — from day one, but it only produces a trickle of steel while it is stabilising. That mismatch is why NMDC Steel bled cash early: in FY25 it lost a staggering ~₹2,373 Cr. In FY26, as the plant finally ran near ~80% of capacity, it swung to a small full-year profit of ~₹59 Cr. The whole story is that climb.
  • The government wants out. NMDC Steel is a marquee privatisation candidate. New Delhi has approved selling its controlling stake (~50.79%) plus management control to a strategic buyer — an efficient private steelmaker who could run Nagarnar harder. So the stock trades on two clocks at once: the plant's operating ramp, and a government sale process that keeps moving in fits and starts.
In plain terms

NMDC Steel is a start-up wearing a heavy-industry costume. It has exactly one product line — steel from the Nagarnar plant — and its financial life is the classic curve of any new factory: enormous fixed costs land immediately, revenue arrives slowly, so early years show deep losses that flip to profit only once the machine runs near flat-out. FY26 was the year the flip began. Sitting on top of that operating story is a separate, government-driven event: the state built this plant, listed it, and now intends to hand the keys to a private owner. You are buying a plant that is learning to run at full tilt, wrapped in a disinvestment that may or may not complete on schedule.

01

Steel-from-ore 101: what "integrated" actually means

No metallurgy degree needed — just the handful of terms that decide the economics.

To follow NMDC Steel you need to understand one distinction: the difference between making steel from scratch and making it from scrap. NMDC Steel does it from scratch — the "integrated" route — and that choice explains both its huge fixed cost and its cost advantage. Here is the chain, in plain language.

An integrated steel plant starts with iron ore (rock rich in iron) and coking coal (a special coal that is baked into coke). Ore and coke are fed into a blast furnace, a giant tower where they react at extreme heat to produce molten pig iron — liquid iron with too much carbon to be useful yet. That hot metal goes into a basic oxygen furnace (BOF), where oxygen is blown through it to burn off impurities and turn it into crude steel. The crude steel is then cast and hot-rolled into the shapes customers buy — flat HR coil (rolled-up sheet) and HR plate. Every one of those stages happens inside the Nagarnar fence.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Integrated plantMakes steel from raw ore and coal, in-house, end to end. Big up-front cost, but low per-tonne cost once it runs at scale. Nagarnar is one of these — the opposite of a small scrap-melting "mini-mill."
Blast furnace / BOFThe two hearts of the plant. The blast furnace makes molten iron from ore + coke; the BOF turns that iron into crude steel. Both need to run continuously and near-full to be economic.
Crude steelThe headline output unit — raw steel before final shaping. Nagarnar is rated at 3.0 million tonnes (MT) of crude steel a year. This is the number the whole business is measured in.
HR coil / plateThe finished, sellable products — hot-rolled sheet in coils and thick plate — used in autos, pipes, construction, engineering and appliances. What NMDC Steel actually invoices.
Capacity utilisationHow much of the 3.0 MT rating the plant actually produces. A new plant starts low and climbs. This single percentage is the make-or-break metric for NMDC Steel — everything below flows from it.
Why integrated plants live and die on utilisation

An integrated steel plant is almost all fixed cost. The blast furnace, the coke ovens, the oxygen plant, the rolling mill — they cost the same to keep hot whether you make one tonne or three million. The raw materials (ore, coal) scale with output, but the crushing weight of depreciation, interest and a large permanent workforce does not. So the cost of producing each tonne falls sharply as volume rises: spread ₹24,000 Cr of assets over 1.5 MT and every tonne carries a fortune in fixed charges; spread it over 3 MT and each tonne carries half as much. That is the entire reason a new plant loses money at low utilisation and makes money at high utilisation — same furnace, more tonnes, radically different profit per tonne.

Who buys this steel

  • Pipe and tube makers — HR coil is the feedstock for welded steel pipe, a big volume outlet.
  • Auto, engineering & appliance makers — flat steel for bodies, structures, white goods and machinery.
  • Construction & infrastructure — plate and heavy sections for projects, fabrication and general engineering.
  • Traders & distributors — who move standard flat products into the wider market. As a ramping plant, NMDC Steel has leaned heavily on selling into this open market to move its rising output.
02

One plant, four stages: the Nagarnar machine

The whole company is this value chain — read it once and NMDC Steel clicks into place.

Because NMDC Steel is the Nagarnar plant, the best way to see the company is to walk the metal through it. Raw ore and coal arrive; finished coil and plate leave; and value is added — or destroyed — at each stage depending on how hard the plant is running. The plant sits in Bastar district, Chhattisgarh, roughly 100 km from its ore source.

One site, one chain. The plant's profit is the gap between the price of Stage 4 output and the cost of Stage 1 inputs — minus the fixed charges that only shrink per-tonne as the whole line runs faster.

What makes Nagarnar distinctive

  • It is genuinely new. Most of India's integrated capacity is decades old and expanded in bolt-ons. Nagarnar was built as a single, modern, greenfield 3.0 MT works — commissioned in stages, with its blast furnace lit and the plant dedicated to the nation in October 2023. New plants can be efficient once stable, but they also have to be learned — every ramp has teething problems.
  • It is next to the ore. The plant sits about 100 km from the Bailadila iron-ore belt, the source of some of India's highest-grade ore and the crown jewel of parent NMDC. Short haulage of the single heaviest input is a structural cost edge — provided the ore actually comes on good terms (more on that in §06).
  • It is a flat-products plant. Nagarnar makes flat steel — HR coil and plate — not long products like the TMT rebar that the Godawaris and Gallantts of the sector specialise in. That places it in competition with the flat-steel majors (JSW, Tata, SAIL) rather than the regional rebar players.
The single-asset reality, stated plainly

When you own a share of NMDC Steel, you own a slice of one plant in Bastar. There is no second factory to average out a bad quarter, no diversified product book, no geographic spread. If Nagarnar runs well, the company thrives; if a furnace trips, an ore contract sours, or the flat-steel price sags, there is nothing else on the income statement to cushion it. This concentration is a double-edged blade: it makes NMDC Steel refreshingly simple to analyse — you are really just modelling one plant's tonnes, prices and costs — and it makes the stock unusually exposed to that one plant's fortunes.

03

The demerger, the parent, and who controls it

A government company, freshly cut out of India's biggest iron-ore miner.

NMDC Steel exists because NMDC Ltd — a Central Public Sector Undertaking under the Ministry of Steel, and India's largest iron-ore producer — decided years ago to move downstream. Digging and selling ore is a good business, but it is a commodity with a ceiling; turning that same ore into steel captures far more value per tonne. So NMDC built the Nagarnar plant. Then, rather than keep a capital-hungry, loss-making new steel works inside a cash-rich mining company, it demerged the plant into a standalone listed entity.

How the demerger worked

In a demerger, the parent's own shareholders receive shares in the new company in proportion to what they already hold — no cash changes hands, the business is simply split into two separately traded pieces. NMDC Ltd shareholders were allotted shares of NMDC Steel, which listed on the NSE and BSE in 2023 under the symbol NSLNISP. Because the Government of India is the dominant shareholder of NMDC Ltd, it became the dominant shareholder of NMDC Steel too — which is precisely what sets up the privatisation story in §05.

Ownership & structure

  • Controlling shareholder: the Government of India is the promoter, holding a controlling majority (roughly ~60%+, approx.). It has cleared the sale of a 50.79% block together with management control to a strategic buyer, after which the parent NMDC Ltd is expected to be offered a residual ~10% stake. Treat the exact holding split as approx. and confirm against the latest shareholding filing.
  • Classification: a listed CPSU under the Ministry of Steel — until any sale completes, it is run as a government company, with the governance style, board and pace that implies.
  • The parent link: NMDC Ltd (NSE: NMDC) remains the natural ore supplier and is a separately listed miner in its own right. Do not confuse the two: NMDC Ltd mines and sells iron ore; NMDC Steel buys ore and makes steel. Their fortunes are linked (via the ore contract) but they are distinct companies with distinct shareholders.
Why the parentage matters: NMDC Steel inherited two things from being a government-built, government-owned plant — a fortress balance sheet backing (the state funded a ₹24,000 Cr build) and a bureaucratic operating culture that a private buyer would argue can be run leaner. The disinvestment is, in effect, a bet that the same plant in private hands would sweat its assets harder. That is the whole logic of the sale.
04

The new-plant J-curve: the ramp that is the whole thesis

The single most important idea in the entire case — and it is specific to a brand-new single plant.

Here is the mechanism that governs everything. A new integrated steel plant does not glide smoothly into profit; it traces a J-curve. On day one it carries the full annual weight of a ₹24,000 Cr build — depreciation of well over a thousand crore a year, plus interest on the debt that funded it — while producing far too little steel to cover those charges. So it loses money, deeply, at low utilisation. Then, as the furnace stabilises and tonnes climb, those fixed charges get spread over more and more output, per-tonne cost collapses, and the plant crosses from loss into profit. NMDC Steel is walking that curve right now, and you can watch it in three numbers.

Now overlay the profit line on that ramp and the J-curve leaps out. Same plant, same furnace, same 3.0 MT of steel capacity — but the profit result moved violently as the tonnes arrived. This is the analytical heart of NMDC Steel, and it is not transferable to any diversified steelmaker.

The device that only fits NMDC Steel: one furnace, two numbers, a ₹2,430 Cr swing

In FY25, running at roughly half capacity, the Nagarnar plant lost about ₹2,373 Cr. In FY26, running at roughly 80%, the same plant made a small profit of about ₹59 Cr — a swing of over ₹2,430 Cr in a single year, driven overwhelmingly by pushing output from ~1.44 MT to ~2.33 MT. Nothing about the asset changed; the plant simply ran harder, spread its fixed costs over ~62% more tonnes, and crossed the line. That is the J-curve in one comparison — and it tells you exactly where the next leg of value comes from: not from a new business, not from an acquisition, but from grinding utilisation from 80% toward 100% and holding it there. The metric that is the thesis is capacity utilisation, and its financial echo, EBITDA-per-tonne.

The FY26 exit is more telling than the full-year average. The fourth quarter alone did revenue of ~₹3,879 Cr — the highest in the company's short history — with EBITDA of ~₹832 Cr and a quarterly net profit of ~₹392 Cr, a hard reversal from a loss of ~₹474 Cr in the same quarter a year before. In other words, the plant is not just averaging 80%; it is exiting the year running hot. Whether it can hold and extend that run-rate is the operational question that everything else hangs on.

A genuine surprise: the country's largest iron-ore miner spun off its downstream steel bet as a separate, loss-making listed company — and did so while the plant was still learning to run. NMDC Steel arrived on the exchange not as a mature earner but as a J-curve in progress, which is why its first two years read like a start-up's: a brutal loss, then a knife-edge maiden profit. Investors are underwriting a ramp, not a track record.

05

The privatisation ticket: the second clock

A government sale that could re-rate the stock — or keep slipping.

Alongside the operating ramp runs a completely separate storyline: NMDC Steel is one of the flagship names on India's strategic disinvestment list. The government's plan, cleared years ago, is to sell its controlling stake and hand management control to a private steelmaker who can run Nagarnar more aggressively. For a chunk of the shareholder base, that sale — not this year's earnings — is the reason to own the stock.

What the sale actually involves

  • The block: the Government of India has approved selling ~50.79% of NMDC Steel together with management control — i.e. a genuine change of ownership, not a token stake sale.
  • The residual: after a strategic buyer is chosen, parent NMDC Ltd is expected to be offered a roughly 10% holding — keeping the ore-and-steel relationship alive even after control passes.
  • The process: NMDC Steel sits in a basket of CPSEs the government is working through (alongside names like IDBI Bank, BEML, SCI, HLL Lifecare and PDIL). The Expression of Interest / preliminary bidding stage has been run; it is now a matter of the process converting into a binding, priced transaction.
  • The rationale: a private owner with steel-operating DNA — the majors most often mentioned in the sector are the likes of JSW, Tata and Jindal (JSPL), though no bidder is confirmed and any name should be treated as speculation — could push utilisation, procurement and product mix harder than a government-run plant typically does.
Why a privatisation is a real, separable source of value

Two forces make the disinvestment matter beyond mere sentiment. First, a strategic buyer usually pays a control premium and often runs the asset better, so a completed sale can lift both the price paid and the plant's steady-state earnings. Second, the government's own steel plant, run to bureaucratic norms, arguably carries a "PSU discount" — the market prices state-run companies below what a private operator's cash flows would justify. Removing the state as owner can shrink that discount. The catch is timing and certainty: a strategic sale of a large steel plant is a slow, politically sensitive, cyclically fragile thing. It has been on the runway for years, and disinvestment timetables in India routinely slip past elections, budget cycles and soft-market windows.

The honest tension in the disinvestment: a stock partly valued on a government sale has no natural price floor from that source if the sale stalls. If the process drifts — as such processes have before — the shares fall back on plant fundamentals alone, and a single ramping plant in a soft steel market is a thin thing to stand on. The disinvestment is a catalyst, not a guarantee; size it as an option, not as owned value.
06

The captive iron-ore link to the parent

The structural cost question that outlives the demerger.

The most important input into any integrated steel plant, after coal, is iron ore — and NMDC Steel's ore comes from the one place in India that has the most of the best of it: the Bailadila deposits in Chhattisgarh, mined by its own former parent, NMDC Ltd. That proximity is a genuine structural edge, but it is also a negotiation that never quite ends.

Here is why it matters. Iron ore is heavy and low-value relative to steel, so where you source it and how far you haul it moves the cost sheet meaningfully. Nagarnar sits roughly 100 km from Bailadila, some of the highest-grade ore in the country. A steel plant that can pull premium ore over a short distance starts each tonne of steel with a cost advantage over rivals who rail ore across states or import it. In principle, NMDC Steel should be one of the lowest ore-cost plants in India.

The subtlety: "next to the ore" is not the same as "cheap ore"

Proximity guarantees short haulage; it does not guarantee a favourable price. NMDC Ltd is a separate listed company answerable to its own shareholders, and it sells ore at market-linked rates. So the terms on which NMDC Steel buys from its former parent — the price, the grade allocation, the reliability of supply — are a live commercial matter, not a family gift. If those terms are keen, Nagarnar enjoys a durable raw-material moat. If the parent extracts full market price, the "captive" advantage narrows to just the freight saving. And once a private buyer takes control through disinvestment, the ore relationship becomes an arm's-length contract between two unrelated firms — one of the quiet due-diligence questions any acquirer will price. Watch the ore linkage as carefully as the utilisation number; it is the second lever on the cost per tonne.

The coal side of the ledger: the other big input, coking coal, India largely lacks and must import — so NMDC Steel is exposed to global coking-coal prices like every Indian integrated maker. A plant with a strong domestic ore position but an imported coal bill is only half-hedged against raw-material swings; a spike in seaborne coking coal hits Nagarnar's margins directly, ore advantage or not.

How a ~₹2,373 Cr loss became a small profit in twelve months.

FY26 is the year NMDC Steel stopped being a cash-burning construction project and became — just barely — a profitable company. Understanding how it turned, and how fragile that turn still is, is the core of the financial story.

Three levers did the work. The balance sheet improved in step. Borrowings fell to ~₹4,602 Cr from ~₹5,898 Cr a year earlier, as the now cash-generating plant began paying down the debt that built it. Why the FY26 profit is real but fragile

A ~₹59 Cr profit on ~₹13,642 Cr of revenue is a net margin under half a percent — the company cleared the bar by a whisker. That is exactly what you expect at the bottom of a J-curve: the first profitable year is razor-thin because depreciation and interest still loom large over an 80%-run plant. The encouraging read is the trajectory — a ₹392 Cr profit in Q4 alone shows the run-rate is far healthier than the full-year average suggests. The cautious read is the fragility — at this margin, a soft steel quarter or a coking-coal spike could tip the full year back toward breakeven. The turn is directional, not yet durable; that is the plain state of play.

08

The steel cycle and commodity risk

Even a well-run plant is a passenger on prices it does not set.

Owning a steel plant means owning a spread — the gap between what your finished steel sells for and what your ore and coal cost — and that spread is set by global forces well beyond any single company. For a plant still climbing its ramp, an adverse move in that spread is doubly painful: it lands on a company with no diversification and only a wafer-thin margin to absorb it.

The cruel arithmetic of ramping into a downturn

Timing is everything for a new plant. If NMDC Steel completes its utilisation ramp into a firm steel market, the operating leverage and a healthy spread compound — rising tonnes meet good prices, and profit accelerates fast. If it ramps into a soft market — Chinese oversupply, weak domestic demand, a coal spike — then the very volume that was supposed to rescue the economics instead pours out at a poor spread, and the plant can stay stuck near breakeven even at high utilisation. The plant controls its tonnes; it does not control the price those tonnes fetch. That is why the steel cycle is not a footnote to this story — it is a co-author of it.

09

The industry and the rivals

A single 3 MT plant standing among the integrated majors.

In the pecking order of Indian steel, NMDC Steel is a minnow by capacity — 3 MT against rivals running 20–35 MT — but it competes in the same flat-products arena as the giants. Understanding where it sits explains both its vulnerability and the logic of handing it to a bigger operator.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things define NMDC Steel's place. First, sub-scale in a scale game: at 3 MT it lacks the purchasing power, product breadth and cost curve of the 20–35 MT majors, which is exactly why a bigger operator could extract more from Nagarnar than the government does — the industrial rationale behind the sale. Second, it is a commodity flat-steel producer, not a niche specialist: it does not make the high-margin auto-grade or electrical steels that command premiums, so it competes squarely on cost and the benchmark price. Its edge is not product; it is location — the ore next door — plus the improvement a private owner might unlock. Everything else on the field is bigger.

Financials & valuation

A knife-edge maiden profit, a big asset, and a sale that muddies the multiple.

Valuing NMDC Steel on a normal earnings multiple is close to meaningless — a ~₹59 Cr profit against a ~₹12,700 Cr market cap implies a headline P/E in the hundreds, which tells you nothing except that FY26 earnings were a sliver. The sensible lenses are asset-based and forward-looking: what did the plant cost, what is it worth per tonne, and what could it earn at full run-rate.

The one number that settles the thesis: sustained utilisation, read through EBITDA-per-tonne

For NMDC Steel the scoreboard is not P/E and it is not the order book — it is capacity utilisation held near full, expressed as EBITDA-per-tonne turning solidly and steadily positive. That single chain — more tonnes → fixed costs absorbed → positive, rising EBITDA per tonne → a durable profit rather than a wafer-thin one — is what proves the J-curve has genuinely completed. Read it alongside three supports: net debt (is the plant paying down its build?), the ore-supply terms from parent NMDC (is the cost base protected?), and disinvestment progress (is a strategic buyer actually going to arrive?). If utilisation sits near 100% at a healthy per-tonne margin quarter after quarter, the below-replacement valuation and the sale optionality both look like gifts. 11

The expansion runway beyond the ramp

What happens after the first plant hits full tilt.

The immediate story is getting the existing 3 MT plant to full utilisation. But the longer arc — and part of what would make Nagarnar attractive to a strategic buyer — is that the site was master-planned for far more than a single plant.

Greenfield integrated sites are chosen and permitted with future phases in mind: land, water, rail links, ore access and a workforce assembled once can support brownfield expansion at a fraction of the cost and time of building fresh. The Bastar location, its proximity to Bailadila ore, and the infrastructure already sunk into Nagarnar give the plant a credible path to add capacity in future phases — the kind of optionality a large private operator, with the balance sheet to fund it, could move on faster than a government owner. In that framing, the current 3 MT is not the ceiling; it is phase one of a site that could grow.

Why the runway matters to the disinvestment

The expansion potential and the privatisation are linked. A strategic acquirer is not just buying a plant running at 80%; it is buying a permitted, ore-adjacent site with room to grow, plus the chance to run the existing works harder. That combination — an under-utilised present asset and a low-cost future expansion — is precisely what lets a bidder justify a control premium. For the current shareholder, it means the value case does not end when utilisation hits 100%; the site's next phase is a further, longer-dated call on the same location. But keep the sequencing straight: none of the expansion optionality pays off until the first plant is reliably profitable at full run-rate. Phase one has to work before phase two is worth discussing.

The near-term reality check: expansion is a story for after the ramp, not during it. With borrowings still around ₹4,602 Cr and a margin under half a percent, the priority for the next stretch is stabilising and deleveraging the existing plant — not committing fresh capital. Treat the runway as an option the eventual owner holds, valued at close to zero until the current asset proves itself.
12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A new plant that just crossed into profit — asked to prove it can stay there, and to find a new owner.

NMDC Steel enters FY27 having done the hardest thing a new integrated plant has to do: reach ~80% utilisation and post a maiden profit. What remains unproven is whether it can push utilisation to full and hold a real margin through the steel cycle — and whether the government's long-promised sale finally converts into a signed deal.

The bull case

  • The J-curve has turned — ~80% utilisation, record 2.33 MT output, a maiden profit and a ~₹832 Cr Q4 EBITDA exit-rate show the operating leverage arriving.
  • Below replacement cost — an EV of ~₹17,300 Cr for a plant that cost ~₹24,000 Cr to build; full-run economics could look cheap.
  • Ore next door — proximity to Bailadila's high-grade ore gives a structural raw-material edge few Indian plants match.
  • Privatisation optionality — a strategic sale (~50.79% + control) could bring a sharper operator, a control premium and the removal of the PSU discount.
  • Deleveraging — borrowings already falling as the plant turns cash-generative; expansion runway on a permitted site for the eventual owner.

The bear case

  • Single-asset concentration — everything rides on one plant; any furnace disruption, outage or accident is existential, with nothing to cushion it.
  • Wafer-thin profit — a <0.5% net margin means the turn is directional, not durable; a soft quarter can tip it back to breakeven.
  • Commodity & import risk — flat-steel prices, imported coking-coal costs and Chinese steel dumping set a spread the company cannot control.
  • Disinvestment slippage — the sale has been on the runway for years; if it stalls, the stock loses a key catalyst and its floor.
  • Ramp & ore-terms uncertainty — holding 80%+ is not guaranteed, and ore pricing from a now-separate parent is a live cost question a buyer will scrutinise.
The bottom line

NMDC Steel is the rarest thing on the exchange: a single brand-new integrated steel plant, laid bare as its own listed company, caught in the exact moment its J-curve crosses from loss into profit. FY26 answered the first question — the plant can run near 80% and make money — but only just, and only at the very tail of the year. Two milestones now decide the case, and both are checkable, not rhetorical: first, Nagarnar sustaining full run-rate with EBITDA-per-tonne rising quarter after quarter, turning a wafer-thin annual profit into a thick one; and second, a signed, priced strategic-sale agreement that hands the plant to an operator who can push it harder. Watch the quarterly utilisation print and the disinvestment newsflow side by side — one tells you whether the plant works, the other whether it changes hands. Until both land, this is one furnace in Bastar, running hot, waiting on its numbers and its new owner.
