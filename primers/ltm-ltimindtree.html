00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

LTM Limited — the company that until March 2026 was called LTIMindtree — is an IT-services company and the flagship technology arm of the Larsen & Toubro (L&T) group, India's largest engineering-and-construction conglomerate. Like TCS, Infosys, Wipro and HCLTech, it supplies large global enterprises — banks, insurers, technology firms, manufacturers and retailers across the US and Europe — with software engineers and consultants who build, run and modernise the technology those businesses depend on. It is India's sixth-largest IT-services firm, with FY26 revenue of US$4.76 billion (₹42,308 crore) and about 88,000 employees. Its defining feature is its DNA: it was itself born from a merger — L&T Infotech (LTI) plus Mindtree, combined in November 2022.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A merger-built, L&T-owned "tier-1.5" player. LTM is not an organically grown giant like TCS; it was assembled in 2022 by fusing L&T's own IT arm (LTI, strong in banking) with Mindtree (strong in digital and consumer-tech). That created a ~$4bn+ business at a stroke and vaulted it above the mid-caps — but it also means the story is still, four years on, partly an integration story: cross-selling the two client books, blending two cultures, and steadying leadership. The L&T parent still owns ~68.5%.
  • It grows faster than the giants but earns less. For years LTM/LTIMindtree posted above-peer revenue growth, yet its operating margin has run below TCS (~25%) and Infosys (~21%). FY26 EBIT margin was 15.4% — up 90 bps on the year, but still short of even its own 17–18% aspiration (which management has repeatedly deferred). Closing that margin gap is the single biggest re-rating lever for the stock.
  • A softer demand year, a big AI bet, and a fresh name. FY26 growth cooled to +6.0% constant currency (from double-digits) as global IT clients throttled discretionary spending. Against that, LTM is pushing its BlueVerse agentic-AI platform and a five-year "Lakshya 2031" plan to double dollar revenue — and, on 17 March 2026, it dropped the "LTIMindtree" name for the punchier LTM.
In plain terms

Think of LTM as a global outsourcing partner that rents out brainpower — the same basic business as TCS and Infosys — but with two twists. First, it's the L&T group's IT company, majority-owned by the engineering giant, which gives it a blue-chip parent and a promise of enterprise relationships. Second, it's a merger of two firms (LTI + Mindtree), so a lot of its recent history is about welding those two into one smooth machine. The result is a company that punches above the mid-caps on size and often on growth — but earns a thinner profit margin than the top three, and whose whole investment case now rests on one question: can it lift that margin toward the leaders while the merger finally "clicks" and AI reshapes the work?

01

IT-services economics 101: how LTM earns

No engineering degree required — just the levers that turn people into profit, and why LTM's sit a notch below the leaders'.

An IT-services firm is, financially, a labour-arbitrage business: it employs a large pool of engineers at (largely Indian) cost, places them on client projects, and bills the client at a higher rate. Profit is the gap between what it pays its people and what it charges for them — multiplied across ~88,000 employees. LTM plays exactly the same game as TCS and Infosys. The difference is in the settings of a handful of levers, and LTM's have historically been tuned for growth-over-margin — which is precisely why it grows fast but earns less. Understanding these levers is the key to the whole primer.

The only levers you need — and where LTM stands
TermWhat it means (and LTM's position)
HeadcountThe number of billable employees — the raw capacity. LTM ended FY26 with ~87,950 people across 40+ countries. More heads only helps if they're billed; in a soft market the firm holds or trims headcount while lifting output per person.
UtilisationThe share of employees actually working on (billable) client projects vs sitting "on the bench". Higher utilisation = more revenue on the same payroll — one of the most direct margin levers, and one LTM has been pushing hard.
The pyramidThe mix of cheap juniors to expensive seniors. A "fat" base of freshers under fewer senior architects keeps average cost low. A merged firm often carries a heavier senior layer (two managements, duplicate roles) — flattening the pyramid is a slow-burn margin fix.
SubcontractorsOutside contractors hired to fill skill gaps — expensive, margin-dilutive. LTM had leaned on them during the fast-growth years; trimming subcontractor cost is one of the quickest, most visible margin levers in its "Fit4Future" programme.
Pricing / billing rateWhat the client pays per hour or per outcome. Rises with skill (cloud, data, AI, cyber) and falls when clients squeeze. LTM's growth-led book carried some lower-priced work; enriching the mix lifts the average.
AttritionHow fast people quit. High attrition means costly re-hiring and training. LTM's FY26 attrition was ~13.3% — moderate, and calmer than the post-merger spikes of 2023.
Constant currency — the phrase you'll see everywhere

LTM earns in dollars, pounds and euros but reports in rupees, so currency swings can flatter or dent headline growth. To strip that out, the industry quotes growth in "constant currency" (cc) — what growth would have been if exchange rates hadn't moved. That's why LTM's FY26 rupee revenue rose ~11% (helped by a weaker rupee) while dollar revenue rose 6.0% and constant-currency revenue also rose about 6.0%. The cc figure is the one analysts anchor to, because it measures real volume and pricing rather than a currency tailwind. Always read cc as the "true" growth.

Time-and-material vs fixed-price — two ways to bill

  • Time-and-material (T&M) — the client pays for hours worked at agreed rates. Lower risk, but revenue is tied directly to bodies on the project — and this is the model AI most directly threatens.
  • Fixed-price / outcome-based — the firm commits to deliver a defined result for a set fee. Higher margin if delivered efficiently (the natural home for AI-driven productivity), but the firm carries the execution risk. LTM's new "BlueVerse Currency" pricing (billing on business outcomes rather than headcount) is a bet on this end of the spectrum.
02

The defining story: a merger, and the L&T parent

This is the structural fact an analyst leads with — LTM is an assembled company, owned by an engineering giant.

Every large IT firm has a founding story; LTM's is unusually recent and unusually consequential. It did not grow to $4.7bn — it was fused to that size in one step. In November 2022, L&T combined its own IT subsidiary, L&T Infotech (LTI), with Mindtree — a company L&T had acquired control of in 2019 — into a single listed entity, LTIMindtree. Mindtree shareholders received 73 LTI shares for every 100 Mindtree shares. Overnight, a scaled tier-1.5 IT house was born, with a combined client base of 750+ global enterprises and ~90,000 staff.

Why merge — the strategic logic

The rationale was that the industry was tilting toward scale. Clients increasingly wanted one large partner for end-to-end, multi-year transformation deals, and awarded ever-bigger contracts to firms that could staff them. Two sub-scale specialists were each too small to win the biggest deals alone; together they cleared the size bar. Crucially, their strengths were complementary, not overlapping:

  • LTI — an "engineering-DNA" firm, strong in banking & financial services (BFSI), insurance, energy and manufacturing, with L&T's enterprise heritage behind it.
  • Mindtree — an "experience-DNA" firm, strong in digital transformation for consumer-facing industries: hi-tech, media, retail, travel and hospitality.
  • Minimal client overlap — the two books barely competed, so the merger promised cross-sell: take Mindtree's digital skills into LTI's banks, and LTI's scale into Mindtree's consumer accounts.

The L&T parent — moat and nuance both

LTM is not independent: Larsen & Toubro owns ~68.5%. That parentage cuts two ways, and holding both in your head is the key to the ownership picture:

  • The moat. L&T is a AAA-rated, ~$30bn conglomerate spanning construction, defence, energy and finance. Being its IT arm confers a blue-chip pedigree, balance-sheet comfort, deep enterprise relationships, and a captive-plus source of technology work from the wider group. It also anchors a stable, long-term-oriented promoter rather than a rotating cast of financial owners.
  • The nuance. A single promoter at 68.5% means limited free float and a controlling shareholder whose priorities (dividends up to the parent, group strategy) steer the company. And "the parent is also a client" is a double-edged fact — helpful for baseline revenue, but it means a slice of the book is related-party rather than won in open competition. Investors weigh how much of LTM's growth is truly external.
Where integration actually stands

Mergers of people-businesses are hard: you're blending two cultures, two pay structures, two sets of processes and two leadership benches. In LTM's case that friction was real — a dozen-plus senior leaders exited in the run-up to and after the merger, attrition spiked in 2023 as staff faced uncertainty, and revenue-synergy realisation "took more time than expected." The 17 March 2026 rename to LTM — retiring both legacy brand names — is management's signal that the two firms are now genuinely one company. Whether that's a finished fact or a hopeful flag is the crux: if integration is truly done, the promised cross-sell and margin gains should now show up; if it isn't, the rename is cosmetic and the margin gap persists.

03

The margin gap — the core investment question

LTM grows like a leader but earns like a challenger. Closing that gap is the whole re-rating bet.

Here is the single most important tension in the LTM story. On growth, LTM has for years matched or beaten the tier-1 giants. On profitability, it lags them — by a wide, structural margin. FY26 EBIT margin was 15.4%. That is respectable, and up 90 bps on the year — but set it beside TCS at ~25% and Infosys at ~21% and the gap is stark. Even LTM's own stated aspiration of 17–18% sits below the leaders, and management has repeatedly deferred hitting it. Why does a fast-growing, well-run firm earn 6–10 percentage points less margin than its peers?

Why the margin is lower — four honest reasons

  • Merger overhang. Integrating two firms carries duplicate costs, a heavier senior layer, and one-off frictions — a drag no organically grown peer carries.
  • Growth-over-margin history. LTM chased above-peer growth, which often meant taking on lower-priced work and leaning on expensive subcontractors to staff it quickly — good for the top line, corrosive to the margin.
  • Sub-scale vs the giants. At ~$4.7bn, LTM lacks the sheer operating scale of $30bn TCS, whose fixed costs spread over a far larger base. Scale itself is a margin advantage LTM hasn't fully reached.
  • Mix and pyramid. A relatively senior-heavy pyramid and a case mix still maturing toward the highest-value work keep average cost up and average price down versus the leaders.

The levers to close it — "Fit4Future" and the Lakshya target

LTM's margin-improvement programme — badged Fit4Future — pulls the classic levers: raise utilisation, cut subcontractor cost, flatten the pyramid (more freshers, fewer duplicate seniors), prune low-margin accounts, and bake AI-driven productivity into delivery. At its FY26 investor day, management framed a longer arc — Lakshya 2031 — targeting ~200 bps of EBIT-margin expansion over five years alongside doubling dollar revenue.

Why this is the re-rating lever

Because LTM's growth already resembles a leader's, the market isn't waiting for the top line to prove itself — it's waiting for the margin. Every 100 bps of margin, at ₹42,000 Cr of revenue, is roughly ₹420 Cr of extra operating profit that drops toward the bottom line. Lift the margin from 15.4% toward 17–18% and profit rises meaningfully without needing faster growth — and a higher, more stable margin typically earns a higher valuation multiple too. That's a double win: more earnings and a richer multiple on them. The catch is credibility: management has deferred the 17–18% aspiration before, and the Lakshya target is gradual. Until the margin actually climbs, sceptics treat the gap as structural, not temporary — and that scepticism is exactly what caps the stock.

The one-off to watch: in FY26 LTM agreed to acquire parts of Randstad's European and Australian IT operations (~€160 mn). Analysts flagged that the acquired unit is onsite-heavy and lower-margin — so in the short run it could dilute the very margin LTM is trying to lift. A margin-improvement story that bolts on a margin-dilutive deal is a tension worth tracking quarter by quarter.

Why BFSI dominance matters both ways

Banks and insurers are the biggest spenders on IT in the world — so a BFSI-led book gives LTM a huge, deep addressable market and long, sticky client relationships. But it also means LTM's fortunes ride the banking IT-spending cycle. When banks tighten discretionary budgets — as they did through the FY26 slowdown — LTM's largest vertical is the one that stalls first. FY26 saw exactly this: BFSI revenue actually fell ~4% for the year even as smaller verticals like healthcare and consumer grew double digits. Concentration in the best market is a strength in an upcycle and an exposure in a downcycle.

05

The growth profile and the demand cycle

LTM historically outgrew the giants — until the whole sector's discretionary demand cooled.

One of LTM's genuine claims to fame is that, for much of its life, it grew faster than the tier-1 leaders. A more digital, more consumer-tilted book (thanks to Mindtree) and an aggressive, growth-first posture meant LTIMindtree often printed double-digit revenue growth while TCS and Infosys grew mid-single-digit. But growth in this industry is cyclical, and FY26 was a down-leg of the cycle for everyone — including LTM.

What drives the cycle — "discretionary" is the word to know

Client IT spending splits into two buckets, and the difference explains almost every up- and down-cycle in this sector:

  • Run-the-business (non-discretionary) — keeping existing systems running, cost-takeout, maintenance. Sticky and defensive; clients can't switch it off. This is the floor under revenue.
  • Change-the-business (discretionary) — new projects: cloud migrations, digital products, data platforms, transformation. High-margin, high-growth — but the first thing clients pause when the economy wobbles or interest rates bite.

Through FY25–FY26, nervous clients — especially banks — froze discretionary projects, took more year-end "furloughs" (paid-time-off shutdowns that dent billing), and stretched decision-making. That hit the whole sector, and LTM's more discretionary, digital-heavy book was squarely exposed. The result: growth that had run double-digit decelerated to mid-single-digit.

Why the cycle turning is the near-term swing factor

LTM's revenue is unusually geared to discretionary spending recovering. When US banks and enterprises regain confidence and un-pause their transformation budgets, a firm like LTM — digital-tilted, growth-oriented — should reaccelerate faster than the defensive giants. That's the bull's near-term catalyst: "the cycle bottoms, discretionary comes back, LTM outgrows again." The bear's retort is that this cycle is different — because AI is simultaneously a demand driver and a demand destroyer (next section) — so the old pattern of "downturn, then snap-back to double digits" may not repeat cleanly. Watch two numbers each quarter: constant-currency growth (is it re-accelerating?) and BFSI (has LTM's biggest, most cyclical vertical turned?).

06

The AI question & the large-deal engine

Generative AI is both LTM's biggest opportunity and the biggest threat to its people-based model — and the order book is how you keep score.

Hanging over every Indian IT firm is one question: what does generative AI do to a business that bills for human hours? If AI lets a client's work be done by fewer engineers, the linear "more people = more revenue" model is under threat. LTM's answer — like its peers' — is to try to be the company that sells AI transformation faster than AI erodes its own headcount economics. Its vehicle for that is a platform called BlueVerse.

BlueVerse — LTM's agentic-AI platform

  • What it is. BlueVerse is an "agentic AI" ecosystem — a toolkit for building, deploying and running software "agents" (autonomous AI helpers) embedded inside a client's business processes. It ships with 1,000+ ready-to-deploy AI agents, supports multiple clouds and multiple large-language-models, and bakes in governance for standards like GDPR and HIPAA.
  • BlueVerse Foundry. A no-code/pro-code "launchpad" so enterprises can assemble and scale their own agentic-AI solutions quickly, with reusable components and built-in guardrails.
  • BlueVerse Currency. Launched June 2026 — a pricing model that bills clients on measurable business outcomes rather than human effort. This is LTM directly confronting the AI threat: if AI shrinks the hours, price the result, not the hours.
  • AI 1000. A talent programme to build a specialised workforce of 1,000+ advanced AI-certified engineers — reskilling the pyramid for the AI era.
The pricing/productivity dynamic, in plain terms

Here's the mechanism that keeps IT executives up at night. Traditionally, if a client wanted more work done, LTM added more engineers and billed for them — revenue and headcount rose together. AI breaks that link: an AI-assisted engineer can do more, so the same project needs fewer billed hours. In a pure time-and-material world, that's revenue deflation. The industry's escape route is to shift to outcome-based pricing — charge for the business result delivered, keep the productivity gain as margin, and win more transformation projects because AI makes them cheaper and faster to attempt. LTM's BlueVerse Currency is exactly that pivot. Whether it works is the defining medium-term question: get it right and AI is a margin tailwind; get it wrong and AI quietly deflates the top line.

The deal engine — why the order book matters

Because reported revenue is backward-looking, analysts watch the order book — the value of new contracts signed (order inflow, sometimes called TCV, total contract value) — as the leading indicator of future revenue. In FY26 LTM booked $6.60 billion of order inflow, up 10.3%. That order inflow running ahead of revenue growth is a positive sign: it means the future pipeline is filling faster than the present is being delivered — the raw material for a growth re-acceleration when the cycle turns.

07

Leadership, and the rename to LTM

A CEO transition and a brand reset — both signals about where the company is in its integration journey.

Two people-and-brand events frame LTM's recent chapter. First, a CEO transition: in May 2025, Debashis Chatterjee — the ex-Mindtree chief who had led the merged firm since 2022 — retired ahead of schedule, and Venu Lambu stepped up as CEO & MD (effective 31 May 2025). Second, the rename: on 17 March 2026, LTIMindtree formally became LTM Limited, after 99.99% shareholder approval.

The leadership reset

  • Venu Lambu (CEO & MD). A returning insider-outsider — he previously led LTIMindtree's global markets (2020–2023) before running Randstad Digital, the ~$3bn digital arm of the Dutch staffing giant. His mandate is explicit: steady the integration, close the margin gap, and reaccelerate growth.
  • The churn context. His appointment came after a bruising stretch — a dozen-plus senior exits, elevated attrition, and revenue softness — that had weighed on the stock and on merger-synergy delivery. A steadier leadership bench is itself part of the investment thesis.
Why the rename is more than cosmetics

Retiring "LTIMindtree" for "LTM" isn't just a logo change. The old name literally spelled out the two firms that were joined — a daily reminder that this was two companies bolted together. Collapsing it to LTM is management's way of declaring the welding done: one brand, one culture, "one company." For investors, the rename is best read as a signal of confidence that the integration phase is behind them — which raises the stakes on the margin and cross-sell payoff that integration was supposed to unlock. (Regulators require the old name to be displayed alongside the new one for two years, so you'll still see "formerly LTIMindtree" on filings into 2028.)

The tier-1 frame: where LTM sits in Indian IT

Bigger than the mid-caps, smaller than the giants — a "tier-1.5" player in a consolidating pack.

To value LTM you have to place it in the pecking order of Indian IT. It is comfortably larger than the fast-growing mid-caps (Coforge, Persistent, Mphasis) but materially smaller and lower-margin than the top four. That in-between position — scaled enough to win big deals, not yet efficient enough to earn top-tier margins — is why it's often called a "tier-1.5" firm.

Financials & valuation

Solid growth, improving-but-lagging margin, a cash-rich balance sheet — and a discount to the giants.

LTM's financial record is that of a good-quality, cash-generative IT firm that has been through a soft demand patch. Revenue kept rising through FY26, profit grew, the balance sheet is net-cash, and returns on capital are high (ROCE ~30%). The valuation debate is about whether a scaled but margin-lagging major deserves to close its discount to the leaders.

How to think about the discount to TCS/Infosys

At ~₹1.1 lakh crore market cap on ~₹4,983 Cr of FY26 profit, LTM trades around 20–23× trailing earnings — a clear discount to TCS and Infosys, which command higher multiples on their fatter, more predictable margins. That discount is the entire debate in one number. Bulls argue it's an opportunity: a scaled, high-ROCE, faster-growing IT major, backed by a blue-chip parent, whose margin has started to climb — as Fit4Future and Lakshya 2031 deliver, the margin closes toward the leaders and the multiple re-rates up, a double win. Bears argue the discount is deserved: the 17–18% aspiration has slipped before, the merger's cross-sell hasn't fully shown in the numbers, growth just decelerated, and a margin-dilutive acquisition sits on top. Both sides agree on the mechanism — the stock lives or dies on margin. They disagree on whether management delivers it.

10

Lakshya 2031 and the growth runway

A five-year plan to double revenue and lift margins — the ambition, and why analysts want proof.

LTM's forward story is captured in its FY26 investor-day plan, "Lakshya 2031" ("Lakshya" = target/goal). The ambition is bold: double dollar revenue by FY31 — implying roughly a 15% revenue CAGR — while expanding EBIT margin by around 200 bps and reorienting the whole firm around AI.

Why the market is sceptical — for now

The Lakshya 2031 targets are attractive but, as analysts noted after the investor day, the synergies needed to hit them remain "largely theoretical," LTM's historical integration track record invites scrutiny, and the margin path is gradual against a company that has deferred margin promises before. The plan also leans on a demand recovery that hasn't yet arrived and an AI transition no one has fully proven out commercially. In short: the strategy is coherent and the levers are the right ones — but a market that has been told "margins are coming" before will re-rate the stock on evidence, not ambition. Each quarter's margin print and cc-growth number is the scorecard.

The next chapter is less about proving the merger was a good idea and more about proving it finally pays — in margin points and in cross-sold deals.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A scaled, well-parented IT major — priced at a discount that it must earn its way out of.

LTM is a genuinely good business: scaled, cash-rich, high-ROCE, backed by a blue-chip parent, and growing at or above the sector. The investment debate is not about whether it's a quality company — it's about whether it can close the margin gap and reaccelerate growth fast enough to justify re-rating toward the leaders, against a real risk of execution disappointment.

The bull case

  • Margin re-rating ahead — Fit4Future + Lakshya 2031 lift EBIT toward 17–18%; every 100 bps is ~₹420 Cr of profit and a richer multiple.
  • Above-peer growth engine — digital-tilted, BFSI-led book that reaccelerates faster than the giants when discretionary demand returns.
  • Merger synergy still to harvest — cross-sell across the combined LTI + Mindtree client base largely still ahead.
  • Blue-chip parent — L&T's ~68.5% ownership brings pedigree, stability and enterprise relationships.
  • Valuation discount — trades below TCS/Infosys; strong order book ($6.6bn, +10%) and net-cash balance sheet.

The bear case

  • The margin gap may be structural — the 17–18% aspiration has been deferred repeatedly; sub-scale vs the giants is a permanent handicap.
  • Growth just decelerated — FY26 +6% cc; BFSI (a third of revenue) actually shrank; the cycle may not snap back cleanly.
  • AI is a double-edged sword — could deflate the linear people-based model faster than outcome-pricing offsets it.
  • Integration & leadership risk — a history of senior exits; a new CEO with a lot to prove; a margin-dilutive Randstad deal bolted on.
  • Concentration — ~72% North America, ~33% BFSI, ~68.5% single promoter — low free float and cyclical exposure in one region/vertical.
The bottom line

LTM is a scaled, well-owned, faster-growing IT major that earns a thinner margin than it should — and its entire investment case is the wager that management finally fixes that. The business quality is real; the discount to the leaders is the price of unproven margin delivery and a demand cycle at a low ebb. The story over the next few years will be written in two numbers each quarter — EBIT margin (is it climbing toward 17–18%?) and constant-currency growth (has the cycle turned and is BFSI back?). Deliver on both and a discounted "tier-1.5" name re-rates toward the giants; stall, and the market's patience with "margins are coming" runs thin.
