00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Schaeffler India makes the precision components that let machines move without wearing out — ball and roller bearings, clutches, transmission parts, chassis systems and the specialised motion components used in engines, gearboxes, wind turbines, railways, steel mills and general machinery. It is the listed Indian subsidiary of Schaeffler AG, a German family-owned "motion technology" giant with roughly €18 billion of global revenue. Schaeffler AG owns ~74% of the Indian company; the remaining ~26% is what trades on the NSE and BSE.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A high-quality MNC compounder, not a commodity part-maker. Revenue grew to ₹9,395 Cr in CY2025 (+16%), net profit to ₹1,196 Cr (+22%); it earns a ~19.6% EBITDA margin and a ~26% return on capital employed, is essentially debt-free, and pays out ~45% of profit as dividend. This is a premium-engineering business that sells reliability, not price.
  • It is diversified across three end-markets. The mix is roughly auto ~46% : industrial ~39% : aftermarket ~12%, with a fast-growing export stream on top (~15% of revenue, sold back into the Schaeffler global network). That industrial-and-aftermarket ballast is what lets it ride out the notorious cyclicality of the Indian auto sector.
  • It is largely powertrain-agnostic — and that's the whole pitch. A car needs bearings whether it runs on petrol, hybrid or battery; on top of that, Schaeffler now sells EV-specific products (e-axles, bearings for electric motors) that raise the content it earns per vehicle. So the electric transition, which threatens many engine-part suppliers, is a tailwind here rather than a threat.
In plain terms

Think of Schaeffler India as the Indian franchise of a world-class German engineering house. Its job is to make the tiny, unglamorous, hard-to-copy parts — the bearings and clutches — that virtually every moving machine depends on, and to make them to a precision that cheaper rivals can't reliably match. Because it sells into cars, factories, wind turbines and railways all at once, one weak market rarely sinks the whole business. Because it's owned by its German parent, it gets first access to global technology (including the electric-vehicle stuff) and is being turned into an export base that ships bearings back to the rest of the world. The catch: all that quality is fully appreciated by the market — the stock trades at a rich ~52× earnings, so it is priced to keep compounding.

01

What a bearing is, and why the world can't move without one

No engineering degree required — just the one idea that explains the whole business.

Every machine with a spinning or sliding part — a car wheel, an engine crankshaft, a factory motor, a wind-turbine rotor, a train axle — has a problem: friction. Two metal surfaces rubbing directly against each other generate heat, waste energy and grind themselves to failure. A bearing is the small precision component that sits between them, letting one part rotate against another with almost no friction, usually by rolling hardened steel balls or rollers between two rings. It is one of the most fundamental machine elements ever invented — and one of the hardest to make well.

Why "precision" is the entire moat

A bearing looks simple, but a good one is machined to tolerances measured in microns (thousandths of a millimetre). If the steel is slightly impure, the surface slightly rough, or the roundness slightly off, the bearing runs hot, vibrates, and fails early — and in a wind turbine 100 metres up, a car doing 120 km/h, or a railway axle, a failed bearing is a catastrophe, not an inconvenience. So customers don't buy the cheapest bearing; they buy the one they trust. That trust — built over decades of metallurgy, tooling and clean-room manufacturing — is why the global bearing market is dominated by a handful of names (Schaeffler, SKF, Timken, NSK, NTN) and why a new low-cost entrant can't simply undercut them. Reliability, not price, is the product.

The three things that make bearings a good business

  • They wear out — so demand recurs. Bearings and clutches are consumable: they have a finite life and must be replaced. That creates a large, steady aftermarket (replacement) business that keeps earning long after the original machine was sold — high-margin, recurring, and far less cyclical than new-vehicle sales.
  • They are everywhere — so the market is enormous and diversified. The same core competence (making a precise, durable rolling element) sells into cars, two-wheelers, tractors, trucks, wind turbines, railways, steel plants, cement kilns, mining gear and household appliances. One skill, dozens of end-markets.
  • They are mission-critical but a tiny share of cost. A bearing may be a few hundred rupees inside a machine worth lakhs. The customer will not risk the whole machine to save a little on the bearing — which gives the trusted supplier pricing power and stickiness.

Schaeffler takes that core bearing competence and extends it into systems — not just the rolling element, but the clutch, the tensioner, the actuator, the whole e-axle. The further up that ladder it goes, the more it earns per machine and the harder it is to displace.

02

The MNC-arm identity: a listed slice of a German giant

The single most important thing to understand about who and what this company is.

Schaeffler India is not an independent Indian company that happens to have a foreign name. It is the Indian operating arm of Schaeffler AG, a ~110-year-old, family-controlled German industrial group headquartered in Herzogenaurach, with around €18 billion of global revenue, ~110,000+ employees and operations in 55+ countries. The parent is one of the world's two or three largest bearing makers and, after absorbing the powertrain-and-chassis firm Vitesco Technologies in 2024, a full-spectrum "motion technology" company spanning bearings, automotive powertrain, chassis and electric mobility. Everything about the Indian company flows from that parentage.

What the parent relationship gives — and costs

  • Technology on tap. The Indian arm gets access to the parent's global R&D, product designs, quality systems and — crucially — its electric-mobility portfolio (e-axles, motor bearings), without having to invent them from scratch. It plugs into a global engineering machine.
  • Global brand and customer relationships. The product brands — FAG (bearings), INA (engine components), LuK (clutches) — are internationally trusted, and the parent's relationships with global automakers open doors for the Indian business.
  • A role in the global network. The parent is deliberately turning India into a sourcing / export hub, shifting production of certain bearings out of higher-cost Europe into Indian plants — a structural growth lever the company would not have as a standalone (covered in §07).
  • The costs: royalty and control. In return, the Indian company pays its parent a royalty / technology fee on relevant sales, and the parent — owning ~74% — controls the board, strategy and capital allocation. Minority shareholders are along for the ride, benefiting from the parent's quality but dependent on it treating them fairly.
Why the market pays up for an MNC arm

Listed Indian subsidiaries of global engineering leaders — Schaeffler, SKF India, Timken India, Bosch, Siemens, ABB, Honeywell — tend to trade at premium valuations. The logic: you get world-class technology and governance, a debt-free balance sheet, high returns on capital, generous dividends, and exposure to a fast-growing economy, all in one listed vehicle. Investors treat them as "compounders" — businesses that quietly grow earnings at a steady double-digit clip for years. The flip side is that the premium is already in the price: at ~52× earnings, Schaeffler India offers little cushion if growth stumbles, and the parent's control means the free float is small and minority interests always come second. You are buying quality — and paying full freight for it.

03

The company and who controls it

From a 1962 bearings plant to a merged "One Schaeffler" platform.

Today's Schaeffler India is the product of a long consolidation. What started as a single bearings company in the 1960s absorbed its sister Schaeffler businesses in India — engine components and clutches — into one listed platform, so that a single share now gives you the whole Schaeffler franchise in India rather than a fragment of it.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter: Schaeffler AG (via group holding companies) owns ~74.1% — the maximum-ish stake a listed MNC arm typically holds, leaving a ~26% public float. This is a controlled subsidiary, run to the parent's global standards.
  • Board & management: a professional management team (led by the Managing Director & CEO) operates within the parent's global divisional structure; there is no Indian promoter-family — the "promoter" is the German group itself.
  • Balance sheet: near debt-free (borrowings of only ~₹39 Cr against ~₹6,100 Cr of reserves), funding its entire expansion internally — a hallmark of the conservative MNC-arm model.
  • Free float: the ~26% public holding is split among domestic and foreign institutions and retail; the small float and high quality are part of why the stock commands a scarcity premium.

05

Auto vs industrial vs aftermarket — the diversification engine

Why having three end-markets is the quiet superpower of this business.

The single most important structural feature of Schaeffler India is that it does not depend on any one end-market. Indian auto demand is famously cyclical — it swings with the economy, interest rates, fuel prices and monsoons. A pure auto-component maker lives and dies by that cycle. Schaeffler blunts it by earning roughly two-fifths of revenue from industry and another chunk from a recurring aftermarket — so when new-vehicle sales dip, the other legs keep the business standing.

What each leg actually does for the story

  • Automotive Technologies (OEM) — sells to vehicle makers as they build cars, two-wheelers, tractors and trucks: clutches, tensioners, transmission and chassis parts. Higher growth when auto is booming, but cyclical. This is where the content-per-vehicle and EV story plays out (§06).
  • Bearings & Industrial Solutions — sells into an enormous, diverse industrial base: wind turbines (large bearings for rotors and gearboxes), railways (axle-box bearings — a structural beneficiary of India's rail capex), steel, cement, mining and general machinery. Its cycle is different from auto's, driven by industrial capex and infrastructure — so it often zigs when auto zags. Now the biggest single segment.
  • Vehicle Lifetime Solutions (aftermarket) — replacement parts for the vehicles already on the road. This is the golden leg: it recurs regardless of new-vehicle sales (an old car still needs a new clutch), carries higher margins, and grows steadily with the ever-expanding vehicle parc. A pure counter-cyclical stabiliser.
Why diversification is worth a premium here

Auto-component investors live in fear of two things: the cycle (demand collapses in a downturn) and obsolescence (the product is designed out — the classic risk as engines give way to electric motors). Schaeffler India is unusually well-insured against both. The cycle is dampened because industrial and aftermarket — more than half the business — march to different drummers than new-car sales. Obsolescence is dampened because bearings and motion parts are needed by every powertrain, and because the industrial and export legs have nothing to do with what powers a car at all. That is why the market is willing to value it like a diversified industrial compounder, not a fragile single-product supplier — and why a bad quarter in autos rarely breaks the whole thesis.

06

The EV-agnostic story: why electric is a tailwind, not a threat

The question every auto-component investor asks — and Schaeffler has an unusually good answer.

For most makers of engine or transmission parts, the electric-vehicle transition is an existential threat: no engine means no pistons, no fuel injectors, no gearbox as we know it — the product is simply designed out. Schaeffler India faces the opposite dynamic. Its core products survive the transition, and it is layering new EV-specific products on top. So instead of shrinking as cars electrify, its content per vehicle can actually rise. This is the bespoke crux of the bull case.

Three reasons electrification doesn't hurt — and can help

  • Bearings are powertrain-neutral. A wheel still turns, a motor still spins, an axle still rotates — an EV has plenty of bearings, often high-precision ones for the electric motor that run at far higher speeds than an engine and demand better engineering, not worse. This slice of revenue simply doesn't care whether the car burns fuel or electrons.
  • New EV-specific products replace and exceed the lost content. Schaeffler sells e-axles — integrated units combining an electric motor, gearbox and power electronics ("2-in-1", "3-in-1", up to "4-in-1" with thermal management). Its EMR4 e-axle drive spans ~80–230 kW. The company won a ~₹2,700 Cr (€300m), seven-year e-axle order from an Indian automaker — its first system-level EV win — with series production ramping. An e-axle is a far larger, higher-value part than a humble clutch.
  • Hybrids are the best of both. A hybrid vehicle has an engine and an electric drive — so it needs Schaeffler's traditional clutch/transmission content plus the new electric content. As India's transition runs through a long hybrid phase, that is a content-per-vehicle sweet spot.
The mechanism that matters

The number to watch is content per vehicle — the rupees of Schaeffler product in an average car. In a pure engine world, a rival could argue that content shrinks as clutches and engine parts get designed out of EVs. Schaeffler's counter is that it is simultaneously losing a little (the clutch) and gaining a lot (e-axle systems, high-speed motor bearings, actuators, thermal-management parts). If the additions outweigh the subtractions — which is the company's whole strategy, backed by the parent's global EV portfolio — then Schaeffler grows its take from each vehicle even in a flat car market. Add the fact that ~39% of revenue (industrial) and ~15% (exports) have nothing to do with car powertrains at all, and the "EV kills auto suppliers" narrative largely bypasses this company. The honest caveat: e-axles are a competitive, capital-intensive, lower-margin system business early on, and Schaeffler is one of many chasing those orders — so this is an opportunity, not a guaranteed windfall.

07

Localisation and the export-hub bet

A structural growth lever that only a global parent can hand you.

Two related moves are quietly reshaping Schaeffler India's growth: deepening localisation (making more of what it sells here, in India, rather than importing it), and becoming a global sourcing / export hub for the parent — manufacturing bearings in India for shipment back to Europe and the rest of the Schaeffler world. Both convert India's low-cost, high-quality manufacturing base into durable revenue that a standalone company could never access.

Why the export-hub angle is a genuine structural lever

  • Cost arbitrage the parent controls. Making large- and mid-size bearings in India is materially cheaper than in Europe. The parent has earmarked a meaningful slice of Indian capex (~30% of a multi-year ₹1,500 Cr programme) specifically for relocating bearing production from Europe to India — de-risking Europe's cost base while handing the Indian arm captive export revenue.
  • Localisation lifts margins and de-risks supply. The Indian operations run at roughly ~75% localisation; every percentage point that shifts from import to local manufacture improves cost, reduces currency and logistics risk, and captures more value in India.
  • Exports are the fastest-growing line. "Exports & Others" grew ~33% in CY2025 to ~₹1,433 Cr — evidence the hub strategy is already showing up in the numbers, and a lever largely independent of the domestic cycle.
Why this is a moat a local rival can't copy

A purely Indian bearings company can win domestic orders, but it cannot make itself the export base of a €18 billion global group — that only happens if a global parent chooses to route its worldwide production through you. Schaeffler AG has made exactly that choice, giving the Indian arm a growth avenue (captive, hard-currency, cycle-independent export revenue) that is unavailable to standalone competitors and that scales with the parent's global "China+1 / Europe+1" de-risking. It is the clearest example of the MNC-arm advantage turning into rupees. The dependency cuts both ways: this revenue exists at the parent's discretion, and a shift in the parent's global footprint strategy could slow it.

08

The industry and the rivals

A consolidated, quality-driven oligopoly — dominated by global names.

The Indian bearings market is large, structurally growing and highly consolidated — the top handful of players control the bulk of the organised market, because (as §01 explained) bearings are a trust-and-precision business where a few global leaders and one or two strong domestic names dominate. Schaeffler competes primarily against other global bearing majors and a small set of Indian specialists.

The competitive tension to watch

Two things define the rivalry. First, this is a rational oligopoly: the leaders compete on engineering, reliability and service more than on ruthless price, which protects margins across the group — but it also means Schaeffler's "moat" over SKF, Timken or NEI is real yet narrow; these are all capable, well-run firms. Second, the growth pie is expanding: India's industrialisation, railway and infrastructure capex, wind-energy build-out, rising vehicle content and the "China+1" export shift all lift demand for the whole cohort. The strategic question for Schaeffler is whether its unusual breadth (bearings + auto systems + aftermarket + export hub) lets it grow faster and steadier than more single-focused rivals — the bull thesis — or whether that breadth simply averages it into the pack.

Structural tailwinds behind the whole sector: India's manufacturing and infrastructure push (railways, roads, renewables), the vehicle parc expanding faster than the population, "Make in India" / localisation incentives, and global supply-chain diversification into India all feed durable, multi-year demand for precision bearings and motion components — a rising tide the organised leaders are best placed to catch.

How to think about the ~52× multiple

At ~₹65,000 Cr market cap on ~₹1,196 Cr of CY2025 profit, Schaeffler India trades around 52× trailing earnings and roughly 10× book value — expensive even for a quality industrial. That price only makes sense if you believe the compounding continues: revenue growing mid-teens, margins drifting up with localisation and mix, exports scaling, EV content rising, all on a debt-free balance sheet earning ~26% on capital. Bulls argue that a scarce, world-class, cash-generative MNC compounder with multiple structural tailwinds deserves a premium — and that it has consistently earned its multiple. Bears argue ~52× leaves no room for a cyclical air-pocket in autos, an industrial slowdown, margin pressure from the lower-margin e-axle ramp, or simple multiple compression. Both are right at different points in the cycle — which is why the numbers to track each quarter are revenue growth, EBITDA margin and the segment mix.

10

Governance, royalty and the parent question

The distinctive risks and comforts of owning a controlled MNC subsidiary.

Owning shares in Schaeffler India means being a minority partner alongside a controlling German parent. That arrangement brings genuine comforts — world-class governance, technology access, a conservative balance sheet — but also a specific set of things a minority investor must keep an eye on, because the parent's interests and the minority's are not always perfectly aligned.

The comforts

  • Governance & disclosure to global standards. As the listed arm of a large German group, the company runs tight controls, clean accounting and professional (non-family) management — a meaningful comfort versus many domestic peers.
  • Conservative capital allocation. Near-zero debt, internally funded capex, and a consistent ~45% dividend payout signal a business run for durability rather than empire-building.
  • Technology & product pipeline. Direct access to the parent's global R&D and product portfolio (including e-mobility) — an innovation engine no standalone Indian bearings firm can match.

The things to watch

  • Royalty / technology fees. The Indian company pays the parent for technology and brand use. This is normal and value-for-money when the tech is genuinely enabling, but it is a recurring transfer of profit to the majority owner that minorities should size and monitor over time.
  • Related-party transactions & transfer pricing. With significant purchases, sales (including exports) and services flowing between the Indian arm and the group, the fairness of intercompany pricing matters — regulators and minorities both watch it.
  • Parent primacy. Schaeffler AG controls the board and strategy; if global-group priorities ever diverged from Indian-minority interests (e.g. on capital return, capacity allocation, or which products India gets), the parent's view prevails. The small ~26% float also limits liquidity.
  • Delisting / open-offer optionality. As with any tightly-held MNC arm, corporate actions by the parent are a permanent background variable — occasionally a source of upside, occasionally of overhang.
The balance: for most of its history Schaeffler India (and its FAG-Bearings predecessor) has been a well-behaved MNC arm — fair dealing, steady dividends, quality growth. The royalty and related-party lines are the price of admission for the technology and brand, not red flags in themselves. But they are exactly the items a diligent minority investor re-checks every year, because the whole thesis rests on the parent continuing to treat the listed vehicle as a genuine growth platform rather than a captive supplier.

Four stacked engines — and none of them depends on the others.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely high-quality business — priced for it to keep executing.

Schaeffler India is one of the cleaner compounding stories in Indian manufacturing: a debt-free, high-return, technology-rich MNC arm with several structural tailwinds and unusual resilience to both the auto cycle and electrification. As with most such names, the investment debate is almost entirely about price, not business quality.

The bull case

  • Diversified & resilient — auto + industrial + aftermarket + exports; no single end-market can sink it.
  • EV-agnostic, content rising — bearings needed whatever the powertrain; e-axles & motor bearings add content, not subtract it.
  • Export-hub structural lever — parent relocating production to India; captive, fast-growing, cycle-independent revenue.
  • Elite economics — ~26% RoCE, ~19.6% margins and rising, near debt-free, self-funded, ~45% payout.
  • MNC quality — world-class tech, brands (FAG/INA/LuK) and governance; a scarce listed compounder.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~52× earnings and ~10× book leave no margin for error; vulnerable to de-rating.
  • Auto cyclicality — a third of revenue is auto OEM; an industry downturn or slow economy hits growth.
  • e-axle margin drag — the EV systems business is competitive and lower-margin early; the order book is a bet, not a certainty.
  • Parent dependence & minority status — royalty, related-party pricing, small ~26% float, parent controls everything.
  • Global & commodity swings — steel/input costs, currency, and the parent's own global cyclicality feed through.
The bottom line

Schaeffler India is a structurally advantaged, high-quality precision-engineering franchise — diversified across end-markets, largely immune to the electrification threat that stalks its peers, handed a genuine export-hub growth lever by its global parent, and financed by a fortress balance sheet. The business risk is low; the valuation risk is real. The story over the coming years will be written in a handful of numbers each quarter — revenue growth (do the four engines keep firing?), EBITDA margin (does localisation and mix keep lifting it, or does the e-axle ramp weigh?), and the segment mix (is industrial and export offsetting any auto softness?). Get those right and the compounding justifies the premium; stumble, and a ~52× multiple has a long way to fall.
