Cyient, the firm that draws the aircraft, not the app

A complete walk through one of India's leading engineering R&D companies — why designing a jet's wiring harness is a fundamentally different business from writing a bank's software, how a Hyderabad firm ended up engineering European rail signalling and American fibre networks, and why a company whose revenue tracks its customers' product programmes has spent the last two years quietly breaking itself into separately valued pieces.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Cyient is an engineering R&D (ER&D) company. That phrase does a lot of work, so start here: when Boeing, Airbus, Pratt & Whitney, Alstom, a European rail operator or a large American telecom company needs engineering done — the actual design of a physical thing — a big share of that work is now outsourced, and a meaningful slice of it lands in Hyderabad. Cyient's engineers draw aircraft structures and cabin wiring, write and certify the software inside rail signalling systems, design where fibre-optic and power lines physically run across a city, and build the electronics and now the silicon inside industrial products. The deliverable is a design, a drawing set, a certification file — not an app.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is not an IT-services company, and the distinction is the whole primer. An IT-services firm builds and runs a client's business software — the payroll system, the core banking platform, the e-commerce site. Cyient works on the client's product: the aircraft, the train, the network, the medical device, the chip. That changes what drives revenue. Cyient's work is funded out of a customer's R&D and engineering budget, tied to a specific product programme with a specific start and end date — not out of the corporate IT budget.
  • FY26 was a hard year. Consolidated revenue was roughly flat-to-down at ~₹7,268 Cr (FY25: ~₹7,360 Cr), group EBIT margin fell about 254 basis points to ~9.5%, and net profit dropped to ~₹463 Cr from ~₹648 Cr. The services business (which Cyient calls DET — Digital, Engineering & Technology) ran at roughly $650M (approx.) for the year and shrank slightly in constant currency. Management has since reaffirmed an aspiration of mid- to high-single-digit organic growth and a 15% EBIT margin for FY27.
  • It has been busy dismantling itself — deliberately. In 2023 it listed its electronics-manufacturing arm, Cyient DLM, through an IPO. In 2025 it set up Cyient Semiconductors as a separately capitalised subsidiary, and in May 2026 that subsidiary raised ₹300 Cr at a reported ~₹4,650 Cr valuation. The parent's own market capitalisation around that time was roughly ₹9,400 Cr. Read those two numbers next to each other and you have the most interesting arithmetic in the company.
In plain terms

Every physical product you use — a plane, a train, a CT scanner, a broadband network — required thousands of hours of engineering before it existed. Somebody had to decide where each bracket goes, how the wiring routes through a fuselage, how a signalling system fails safely, where a fibre trench is dug. Historically the manufacturer's own engineers did all of it. Over the last three decades a large chunk has been handed to specialist firms in lower-cost engineering hubs, and Cyient is one of the Indian companies that grew up doing exactly that. Its revenue is therefore an engineering-hours business attached to physical product programmes — and that is both its moat and its vulnerability. When a customer launches a new aircraft variant or rolls out a signalling upgrade, Cyient's headcount on that programme rises. When the customer defers the programme, the work simply stops, regardless of how healthy the wider technology market looks.

01

ER&D 101: engineering services vs IT services

The single most important distinction for a first-time reader — get this and the rest follows.

India's technology export industry is usually discussed as one thing. It is really two, and they behave very differently. Understanding which one Cyient belongs to is worth more than any ratio in this primer.

Why the certification angle is the real moat

Designing an aircraft bracket is not simply drawing it. In aerospace, rail and medical devices, a design must be certified against safety standards before it can fly, run or be implanted — DO-178C for airborne software, EN 50128 for rail software, and FDA-regulated design-control processes for medical devices, to name three. Building an organisation that can produce work an aviation regulator will accept takes years of audited process, trained people and demonstrated history. That is why an aerospace supplier does not casually switch its engineering partner: it is not just changing a vendor, it is re-qualifying a safety-critical process. This regulatory ballast is what an ER&D firm has that a generalist coding shop does not — and it is why margins in ER&D can, at their best, sit above commodity IT services.

Why manufacturers hand engineering work out at all

  • Cost per engineering hour. The blunt driver. A qualified mechanical or avionics engineer in India costs a fraction of one in Toulouse, Seattle or Munich. On a programme requiring hundreds of thousands of hours, that arithmetic is decisive.
  • Elastic capacity. Product programmes are peaky — an OEM may need 400 extra engineers for eighteen months and then not need them. Hiring and firing that many in Europe is difficult and expensive; renting them through a partner is not.
  • Scarcity of engineers in the West. Ageing engineering workforces in aerospace and rail mean OEMs genuinely cannot staff every programme domestically, irrespective of cost.
  • Depth in a niche. A firm that has drawn thousands of wiring harnesses or laid out thousands of kilometres of fibre routes gets faster and more accurate at it than a customer's generalist team ever will.

Set against that, ER&D has a structural drag that pure IT services do not carry: engineering work is closer to the customer's crown jewels. Intellectual property, export-control rules (particularly in defence), and simple institutional caution mean OEMs keep the most strategic design work in-house and outsource the layers beneath it. Cyient therefore competes for a large but bounded slice of the pie, and it competes on domain credibility rather than on scale of headcount alone.

Inside DET — the five domains

The services core, which the company reports as DET, is organised by industry vertical rather than by technology. That is telling in itself: in ER&D, knowing the industry is the product.

  • Aerospace & Defence — the flagship and historically the largest vertical (roughly 30% of DET, approx.). Structural design, wiring and cabin systems, avionics software, engineering support for aircraft OEMs, engine makers and tier-one suppliers, plus aftermarket and MRO engineering.
  • Transportation — largely rail: signalling design and safety-critical software, rolling-stock engineering, train-control systems and interlocking design, with a genuinely strong European franchise built partly through acquisition. Roughly 15–17% of DET (approx.).
  • Connectivity — designing and engineering communications networks for telecom operators: where the fibre goes, how a network is planned, engineered and documented. Roughly 15–18% of DET (approx.).
  • Sustainability (energy & utilities) — engineering for electricity grids, utilities, mining and renewables: network design, asset data, grid modernisation. Roughly 15–18% of DET (approx.).
  • New Growth Areas — the company's stated growth engines: medical technology (device design under regulated design controls), hi-tech and industrial, automotive, mining, and semiconductors. Collectively around 20% of DET (approx.) and the pool from which the next decade's mix shift is meant to come.

The mix matters because these verticals do not move together. The unit of production is a billable engineer on a programme

Strip the corporate language away and the engine is simple: Cyient puts qualified engineers onto a customer's product programme and bills for their time, increasingly bundled into fixed-price work packages and outcome-based deals. Growth therefore comes from three levers and only three — more engineers deployed, a higher rate per engineer (won by moving up from drafting toward design ownership and full programme responsibility), and more work delivered per engineer (automation, reuse, and now AI tooling). Any story about this company's future must ultimately show up in one of those three.

03

The company, the founder, and who owns it

A Hyderabad institution — with a surprisingly small promoter stake.

Cyient was founded in 1991 in Hyderabad by B.V.R. Mohan Reddy as Infotech Enterprises, at a moment when the idea of doing Western engineering work from India was still novel. Its earliest business was, appropriately, unglamorous and physical: converting engineering drawings and maps into digital form, then geospatial services for utilities and telecom firms. From that base it moved steadily up the value chain into design, embedded software and full programme engineering, and renamed itself Cyient in 2014 to reflect the shift.

A genuine surprise: Cyient's founding family owns less than a quarter of the company, while Indian mutual funds and insurers own about forty-one percent. That inverts the usual Indian mid-cap picture. Practically, it means two things. First, the stock's price behaviour is set largely by domestic institutional flows rather than by a controlling family's actions — and DIIs at that weight can move a ₹9,400 Cr company hard in either direction. Second, control is genuinely contestable in a way it is not at most founder-run peers; the company operates more like a professionally managed corporation than a family holding, and the ₹720 Cr buyback and the dividend record (a yield above 3% — unusual for anything labelled "IT") are consistent with a board answering to institutions rather than to a dominant owner.
04

Aerospace & defence: the flagship

The vertical that defines Cyient's identity — and its longest, slowest cycles.

If Cyient has a signature business, this is it. Roughly a third of services revenue (approx.) comes from aerospace and defence, and the work spans nearly the entire life of an aircraft programme — from early structural design through detailed engineering, manufacturing engineering, avionics and cabin systems, all the way to the aftermarket work that keeps decades-old fleets flying.

What the work actually looks like

  • Structures and mechanical design — the airframe parts: brackets, fittings, panels, doors, secondary structures. Each has to be modelled, stress-analysed, weight-optimised and released as a manufacturable drawing package.
  • Wiring and cabin systems — a modern airliner contains many kilometres of wiring. Routing it, harness design, electrical load analysis and interior/cabin engineering (seats, galleys, lavatories and other cabin "monuments", in-flight entertainment integration) is enormous, detailed, repeatable engineering — exactly the kind of work that migrates well.
  • Avionics and embedded software — safety-critical code written to standards such as DO-178C, plus verification and validation. The certification burden here is what keeps the work sticky.
  • Manufacturing engineering & tooling — translating a design into something a factory can actually build: jigs, fixtures, assembly sequences, process plans.
  • Aftermarket and MRO engineering — repair design, modification packages, obsolescence management and documentation for aircraft already in service. This is the least cyclical slice, because a flying fleet needs supporting whether or not new programmes are launched.
Why an aerospace engineering relationship, once won, is remarkably durable

Aerospace runs on configuration control: every part on a certified aircraft is traceable to an approved design, produced under an approved process, by an approved organisation. When a partner has spent years being audited into an OEM's approved-supplier framework, has its engineers trained on that OEM's tools and standards, and holds the accumulated knowledge of a specific aircraft's design intent, switching partners mid-programme is not a procurement decision — it is a re-qualification project with schedule risk attached to a certified product. That is why these relationships are measured in decades, and why aerospace revenue tends to be far more predictable than the headline growth rate of the company suggests.

The flip side: aerospace moves on aerospace's clock

The same properties that make aerospace sticky make it slow. Aircraft programmes are decided years ahead; a delayed engine certification, a supply-chain problem at an airframer, a shift in defence budget priorities, or simply an OEM deciding to slow a development programme by two quarters flows almost directly into an engineering partner's staffing plan. There is no substitute demand to redeploy those engineers onto overnight. Cyient's aerospace franchise is a genuine asset, but it delivers its returns on a schedule set by a handful of very large customers — and industry-wide supply-chain strain and production-rate wobbles at the major airframers over recent years have been felt right down the engineering chain.

Transportation: the European rail franchise

Signalling, rolling stock, and safety-critical software for railways — a business most investors miss.

Cyient's transportation vertical is overwhelmingly a rail business, and much of it sits in Europe, built up through acquisitions and long relationships with rail OEMs and infrastructure operators. It is one of the least-understood parts of the company and one of the most defensible.

Two kinds of rail engineering

  • Signalling and train control — the systems that decide which train may occupy which section of track, at what speed, and that must fail safely. This includes interlocking design, ETCS/ERTMS (the European standard) rollouts, and the safety-critical software written to standards such as EN 50128. Getting this wrong kills people, so the process rigour and independent-safety-assessment requirements are extreme — a very high barrier to entry.
  • Rolling stock — the trains themselves: car-body and bogie engineering, interiors, electrical systems, traction and control electronics, plus the modification and refurbishment engineering that keeps fleets running for thirty-plus years.
Rail's quiet advantage: the spending is political, not commercial

Aerospace demand ultimately reflects airline profitability and OEM order books. European rail demand reflects something steadier — government policy. Decarbonisation targets, mandated ERTMS signalling migration across the European network, urban congestion, and national infrastructure programmes create engineering work that is budgeted years in advance and is politically difficult to cancel outright. That does not make it fast-growing or free of delay; public infrastructure timelines slip routinely. But it does mean this vertical's demand driver is largely uncorrelated with the enterprise technology spending cycle that determines whether an IT-services peer has a good year. When Cyient reported year-on-year and sequential growth in transportation and mobility in a period when the rest of the portfolio was soft, this is why.

The strategic value of the rail vertical goes beyond its revenue share. It gives Cyient a second, genuinely differentiated safety-critical franchise — one where the credentials are as hard-won as in aerospace, but where the customer base and the funding cycle are entirely different. For a company whose central problem is that its verticals occasionally soften at the same time, having rail on a public-infrastructure clock rather than a corporate-capex clock is real diversification rather than the cosmetic kind.

Worth knowing: India is itself becoming a rail engineering market of consequence — dedicated freight corridors, metro systems in a dozen-plus cities, station redevelopment, and the domestic Kavach train-protection system. A firm with European signalling credentials is unusually well placed to work on Indian rail modernisation, and vice versa. Whether Cyient converts that adjacency into meaningful domestic revenue is a live question rather than a settled fact.
06

Connectivity & sustainability: engineering the invisible networks

The oldest part of the company — mapping and designing where fibre and power physically go.

These two verticals — together roughly a third of services revenue (approx.) — trace directly back to Cyient's founding business. Both are about networks that exist physically in the ground and on poles, and both involve a kind of engineering that almost nobody thinks about until it fails.

Connectivity: telecom network engineering

When a telecom operator rolls out fibre-to-the-home across a city, or densifies a mobile network, an enormous amount of unglamorous engineering has to happen first. Somebody must survey what already exists, decide the physical route of every cable, design where splitters and cabinets sit, calculate optical budgets, produce the permit drawings a municipality will accept, and then maintain an accurate record of what was actually built. Cyient does this at scale for operators, principally in the US and Europe: network planning, engineering design, GIS-based asset records, field-data capture and the ongoing documentation that keeps a network's digital record accurate.

Sustainability: utilities, grid and mining

The same discipline applied to electricity. Utilities need their distribution networks designed, mapped and modernised — where the lines run, what condition the assets are in, how to connect a wave of rooftop solar and EV chargers without destabilising a feeder, how to plan grid reinforcement. Add mining engineering and renewables-related design work and you have a vertical squarely aimed at the energy transition, which is a genuinely multi-decade capital cycle.

The counter-intuitive part: this is field-and-drawing work, not software

It is tempting to file "connectivity" and "sustainability" under digital transformation. Much of the actual output is closer to civil and electrical engineering documentation — geospatial datasets, route designs, permit packages, as-built records. The economics follow accordingly: volume-driven, competitive, priced per unit of design output, and sensitive to how much capital an operator or utility is deploying in a given year. That is exactly why the connectivity vertical has been a drag. Telecom operators worldwide pulled back sharply on network capex after the 5G and fibre build-out surge, and when an operator halves its build programme, the engineering partner's volumes halve with it. There is no clever mitigation; the work simply is not there to be done.

Cyient DLM: the manufacturing arm it chose to list

A services company that also builds circuit boards — and gave the market a separate price for it.

Cyient DLM is an electronics manufacturing services (EMS) business: it builds electronic assemblies — printed circuit boards, box-builds, cable harnesses, complete electro-mechanical units — for customers in aerospace and defence, medical devices and industrial markets. It listed via IPO in June 2023, with Cyient retaining a controlling stake (roughly two-thirds, approx. — confirm against the latest shareholding disclosure).

Why a services firm owns a factory

The logic is "design-led manufacturing", which is the D-L-M in the name. In regulated, low-to-medium-volume, high-reliability electronics — a defence sub-system, a medical instrument, an avionics box — the hard part is not stamping out millions of units cheaply. It is producing complex assemblies to exacting standards, in modest volumes, with full traceability and qualification. A firm that already designs such systems has a natural claim to building them: it can take a customer from concept through design to a qualified, manufacturable product, which is a genuinely differentiated proposition versus a pure contract manufacturer competing on cost per board.

The tension inside owning an EMS business

An engineering-services business and a contract-manufacturing business have almost opposite financial shapes. Services is asset-light: the balance sheet is people and receivables, cash conversion is good, and the constraint is billable capacity. EMS is capital- and working-capital-intensive: you buy components months ahead, hold inventory, run a factory, and margins depend on utilisation and materials pricing. Bolting them together produces a consolidated set of accounts where the group's revenue growth, margin and working-capital ratios are a blend of two unlike things — which is precisely why the market usually prefers to price them apart. Listing DLM separately was, in effect, an admission of that: let each business be valued on its own terms rather than averaged into a single confusing multiple.

DLM's own recent record has been mixed — FY26 saw PAT of about ₹73 Cr, up ~7.6%, on a revenue dip, i.e. profitability improving while the top line went backwards. As a listed subsidiary it carries its own volatility, its own investor base and its own story, but it consolidates into Cyient's reported numbers. For anyone analysing the parent, that means two housekeeping rules: strip DLM out when you want to see how the services business is really doing, and remember that a share of DLM's profits belongs to DLM's minority shareholders, not to Cyient's.

08

The semiconductor carve-out, and the arithmetic it creates

The most consequential — and most contestable — thing happening at this company.

In April 2025 Cyient established Cyient Semiconductors as a dedicated subsidiary for chip design: ASIC and custom-silicon turnkey work, power semiconductors, and the design services that sit between a customer's requirement and a fabricated chip. In May 2026 the board approved a ₹300 Cr raise for the subsidiary through a mix of debt and equity instruments, reported at a valuation of roughly ₹4,650 Cr, with the parent providing a corporate guarantee and pledging its shareholding as security. The stated purpose was to give the semiconductor business an independent capital structure and the operational freedom to chase large customer programmes.

What a chip-design services business actually does

  • ASIC / custom silicon turnkey — a customer wants a chip purpose-built for its product. The design partner takes the specification through architecture, RTL design, verification, physical design, tape-out to a foundry, packaging and test — handing back working silicon rather than a document.
  • Power semiconductors — the devices that switch and convert electrical power in EVs, chargers, industrial drives and renewable inverters. A different discipline from digital logic, and one with strong secular demand from electrification.
  • Design services — augmenting a semiconductor company's own engineering teams on verification, physical design and embedded firmware.
Why it needed to be carved out rather than run inside the parent

Chip design is a poor fit inside a services P&L. It requires heavy up-front investment in EDA tool licences (which cost a fortune), IP blocks, specialised engineers who are bid over by global chip firms, and — in turnkey work — actual capital tied up in mask sets and inventory. The payback arrives years later, if the customer's product succeeds. Run that inside a company being judged quarterly on services margin, and it looks like nothing but a cost. Give it a separate balance sheet, separate investors and a separate valuation, and it can be funded on its own timeline and judged on its own milestones. The carve-out is a governance and financing decision as much as a strategic one.

The device that fits only Cyient: the private mark against the public one

Here is the construct that cannot be lifted onto any other primer in this library. At mid-2026, Cyient Ltd was capitalised at roughly ₹9,400 Cr on the exchange. A private financing round marked Cyient Semiconductors — which Cyient wholly owns — at about ₹4,650 Cr. Cyient also owns a controlling stake in listed Cyient DLM, which carries its own daily quoted price. So the public market was, on the face of it, assigning a strikingly modest residual value to a ~$650M engineering services business with a three-decade aerospace franchise and a European rail practice, once you credit those two stakes at anything close to their marked values. Either the ₹4,650 Cr private mark is optimistic for a business that has yet to prove commercial scale — entirely possible, since private rounds price a plan while public markets price a print — or the listed services business is being valued as an eroding asset rather than a franchise. Both cannot be right. Reconciling those two numbers is, in a single sentence, the analytical work this stock requires, and it is a question no other Indian ER&D company poses in this form.

The bear reading is straightforward and should be stated plainly. Chip design services is a fiercely competitive field populated by global specialists with decades of tape-out history and by the captive design centres of the chip companies themselves. Talent is scarce and expensive. Programmes take years to convert into revenue. A subsidiary financed partly with debt, guaranteed by the parent and secured on a pledge of the parent's shareholding, means the parent's balance sheet is genuinely on the hook if the venture underdelivers. A private valuation is not a realisation; it becomes real only when the business generates revenue and profit, or when a third party pays cash for the stake at or above that mark.

The programme clock: why growth stalled in a good market

The central operating problem — and the reason Cyient behaves unlike an IT-services stock.

FY26 delivered an uncomfortable result: revenue slightly down, EBIT margin down about 254 basis points to ~9.5%, PAT down roughly a quarter. This was not a year in which global technology spending collapsed. It was a year in which Cyient's specific customers' specific product programmes ran slow — and that is the distinction that explains the whole company.

Revenue has moved within a ~3% band for three years while profit has fallen ~34% from the FY24 peak. Flat revenue with falling profit is the signature of a services business carrying cost for growth that has not arrived — and of pricing and utilisation pressure inside the delivered work.

How a programme-driven business goes wrong

  • A deferral is not a lost deal — but it costs the same. When an OEM slides a design programme by two quarters, the contract is not cancelled and the pipeline still looks healthy. But the engineers staffed for it are on the payroll now. Utilisation falls, margin compresses, and the revenue simply moves out of the year.
  • Verticals can go quiet simultaneously. Connectivity weakened with telecom capex discipline; parts of the industrial and hi-tech base softened; aerospace supply-chain normalisation ran slower than hoped. Rail growing does not offset three verticals pausing.
  • Cost cannot be cut at the same speed. Domain engineers — an avionics software specialist, a signalling safety engineer — take years to train and cannot be released and rehired on a quarterly cycle without destroying the very capability that wins the next programme. So the company absorbs the margin hit and waits.
  • One-off costs land in a bad year. FY26 also carried a charge for due-diligence expenses on "Project Astro", a planned acquisition the company paused amid geopolitical uncertainty and the changing AI landscape — real money spent on a transaction that did not happen.
The frame that separates Cyient from an IT-services peer

Ask what would have to be true for an application-services company to grow. Answer: enterprises must spend more on their technology estate. Ask what must be true for Cyient to grow, and the answer is entirely different — industrial companies must be actively developing new physical products. An airframer launching a variant. A rail authority migrating to a new signalling standard. A device maker taking a new instrument through regulatory approval. A telecom operator building network. When those programmes are running, Cyient's engineers are billable and the revenue compounds. When boards defer product development to protect cash — which is what a nervous industrial company does first — the work evaporates even while enterprise software budgets are perfectly healthy. Cyient can therefore have a bad year in a good technology market, and a decent year in a poor one. Anyone benchmarking it against Infosys's growth rate is reading the wrong industry.

The AI question, without the hedging

Generative AI is a real variable for engineering services, and it cuts both ways with no settled verdict yet. On the deflationary side, AI-assisted design, automated code generation, simulation surrogates and document automation could compress the number of engineering hours a given work package needs — and in a business that bills hours, fewer hours is fewer rupees unless pricing shifts to outcomes. On the expansionary side, the regulated, safety-critical, physically consequential work at Cyient's core is precisely the category where an unverifiable machine output is unacceptable: an aviation regulator will not certify a wiring design because a model produced it. Companies that redeploy the productivity gain into taking on more programme scope per client — moving from executing packages to owning whole work-streams at fixed price — can convert the threat into share gain. The company's own reference to AI developments contributing to the pause of Project Astro is a candid signal that management does not consider the question resolved either.

The industry and the rivals

A small, specialised peer group — and a global set of much larger competitors.

India's listed ER&D universe is short. Unlike IT services, where a dozen companies of scale compete, engineering services in India is a handful of firms that mostly specialise by vertical — which means Cyient's competitive position differs from one vertical to the next.

The shape of the competition, in one observation

Cyient's peer set splits into two groups that squeeze from opposite directions. Above it sit the European ER&D majors — bigger, embedded next to the OEMs, and holding the prime relationships on the largest programmes. Alongside it sit narrower Indian specialists like KPIT, which have chosen one theme, gone deep, and been rewarded with premium valuations for the clarity. Cyient occupies the middle: broader than the specialists, smaller than the majors. Breadth genuinely helped when it meant no single vertical could sink the company. It hurts when it means no single vertical is big enough to carry the company either, and the market has no obvious growth theme to attach to the name. That positional question — is diversification resilience or dilution? — sits underneath the valuation discount.

The balance sheet is the reassuring part

Whatever the profit-and-loss says, the balance sheet is in good order. Cyient built its cash position to a record of roughly $157M (about ₹1,240 Cr) at the close of FY25 and has continued to generate cash through the softness — a reminder that engineering services, even when growth stalls, is an asset-light business that converts profit into cash reasonably well. Book value per share is around ₹511, return on equity around 8.6% and return on capital employed around 12.3% — both depressed versus history by the FY26 profit fall. The board approved a ₹720 Cr buyback, and the dividend yield sits above 3%, which is remarkable for a company in a sector where 1% is generous.

What the market is actually saying

A trailing multiple near 20× is a long way below where Indian ER&D names have historically traded, and far below the specialists. The market is not paying for the aerospace franchise's durability, nor pricing in the semiconductor subsidiary's private mark, nor giving much credit to the DLM stake. It is pricing three years of flat revenue and falling profit, and it is asking whether the FY27 aspiration — mid- to high-single-digit organic growth with a 15% EBIT margin — is a plan the company can execute or a target it has now missed for consecutive years. Getting from ~9.5% to 15% EBIT margin is not a rounding adjustment; it is a roughly 550-basis-point climb that requires utilisation, pricing and mix all to move together.

The one number that settles the thesis: constant-currency DET growth, with the margin attached

For this company the decisive figure is organic constant-currency revenue growth in the DET services business, read together with group EBIT margin — and it must be both, because either alone can mislead. Growth bought by discounting proves nothing; margin squeezed out of a shrinking book proves less. Cyient exited FY26 with DET at roughly $650M (approx.) and Q4 DET revenue of $163.5M, down 2.4% sequentially and 1.5% year on year in constant currency, at a normalised DET EBIT margin of 12.4%. The thesis turns on those two lines moving in the same direction at once: constant-currency DET growth crossing decisively into positive territory and holding through consecutive quarters, while group EBIT margin walks up from ~9.5% toward the stated 15% aspiration. Two clean quarters of that combination and the ₹4,650 Cr semiconductor mark and the DLM stake start being treated as value rather than as a distraction. Without it, the sum-of-the-parts argument stays an argument — a spreadsheet exercise the market declines to endorse — and the discount persists precisely because the core keeps shrinking underneath it.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A differentiated engineering franchise that has stopped growing — and is being rebuilt in public.

Cyient enters FY27 as a genuinely unusual proposition: a three-decade engineering franchise with credentials that are hard to replicate, trading at a multiple that implies decline, while simultaneously running a semiconductor venture that private capital has marked at half the parent's public value. The company is not in distress — it is cash-generative, dividend-paying, buying back stock and debt-light at the core. What it has not done is grow.

What has to go right

  • Connectivity has to stop shrinking. The largest single drag on group growth is telecom-operator capex discipline. Stabilisation there, even without recovery, changes the arithmetic of the whole company.
  • Aerospace programme volumes have to normalise. As airframer production rates and supply chains steady, engineering demand follows. This is the vertical with the most operating leverage attached to it.
  • The margin bridge has to be real. Roughly 550 basis points from ~9.5% toward the 15% aspiration, via utilisation, pyramid mix, offshore share, pricing on renewals and automation gains. Each quarter's EBIT print is a public progress report.
  • The carve-outs have to produce something. Cyient Semiconductors converting programmes into revenue, and DLM growing its top line rather than only its margin, are what turn a sum-of-the-parts thesis from arithmetic into cash.

The bull case

  • Hard-to-replicate credentials — audited aerospace approvals, DO-178C and EN 50128 safety-critical capability, and decades of programme history that a new entrant cannot buy.
  • Two clocks, not one — European rail runs on government infrastructure policy, aerospace on OEM programmes; neither is tied to the enterprise IT cycle.
  • Sum-of-the-parts — a ~₹4,650 Cr private mark on the wholly owned semiconductor arm plus a controlling stake in listed Cyient DLM, against a ~₹9,400 Cr parent.
  • Balance sheet and shareholder returns — record cash built through the downturn, a ₹720 Cr buyback, and a dividend yield above 3%.
  • Low expectations — near 20× trailing earnings on depressed profit, with an ROE at a cyclical low; modest execution improvement moves both the E and the multiple.

The bear case

  • Three flat years — revenue within a ~3% band since FY24 while PAT fell ~34%. Inconsistent organic growth is the core indictment, and targets have been missed before.
  • Programme and customer concentration — revenue tracks specific OEM product programmes and a handful of large accounts; a deferral, a cancelled variant or a budget cut lands directly on the P&L.
  • Semiconductor execution and funding risk — a capital- and talent-hungry field with multi-year payback, financed partly with debt guaranteed by the parent and secured on a pledge of its shareholding.
  • AI in engineering — an hours-based delivery model faces genuine deflation risk if automation compresses work packages faster than scope expands.
  • Structural noise — a consolidated DLM with minority interests, a separately financed semiconductor arm, and a paused acquisition whose costs still hit the P&L make the reported numbers harder to read than a single-business peer's.
  • Wage and currency exposure — engineering salary inflation and rupee/euro/dollar swings move margin without any change in operating performance.
The bottom line

Cyient built something durable and then stopped compounding it. The credentials are real — the wiring diagrams, signalling logic and certification files it produces sit inside aircraft and trains that carry people, and no amount of price competition dislodges an approved engineering partner mid-programme. But durability is not growth, and for three years the top line has not moved while profit has fallen by a third. Management's answer has been structural: list the manufacturing arm, carve out the silicon business, buy back stock, and let each piece find its own capital and its own price. That answer has an unavoidable test date. In the coming quarters, DET constant-currency revenue has to cross from negative into positive and stay there while group EBIT margin climbs off ~9.5% toward the promised 15% — and Cyient Semiconductors, marked at ₹4,650 Cr on a plan, has to start reporting customer programmes and revenue against that mark. Until the design work in Hyderabad is growing again, the carve-outs are financial engineering wrapped around an engineering company that needs more engineers billing.
