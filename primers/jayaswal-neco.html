Jayaswal Neco, own the ore, cut the debt

A full walk through a Nagpur-based, integrated iron-and-steel maker that also runs India's largest iron-castings business — how mining its own iron ore hands it a real cost edge, why losing its captive coal in a 2014 court ruling still shapes its margins, and how a decade of grinding down a mountain of debt turned a modest revenue year into a near-doubling of profit.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Jayaswal Neco Industries makes iron, steel and iron castings. It runs two connected businesses out of a single group: a steel arm — a blast furnace that turns iron ore into molten pig iron, then into steel and long products (bars, rounds, sections) — and a castings arm that pours molten iron into moulds to make engineered grey and ductile-iron parts for automobiles, tractors, engineering equipment and the railways. At roughly 140,000 tonnes a year of castings capacity, it is India's largest maker of iron castings. The company is based in Nagpur and, unusually for its size, mines its own iron ore.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It owns the most important raw material. Iron ore is the single biggest input in steelmaking, and Jayaswal Neco digs its own out of captive mines at Metabodeli and Chhotedongar (together roughly 3.95 MTPA of ore) — leaving it close to 100% self-sufficient in ore. That is a structural cost advantage most Indian steel companies of its size do not have, and it is the reason the business can earn double-digit EBITDA margins in a commodity where a few rivals buy every tonne of ore on the open market.
  • But it has to buy its coal. The other big input — coking coal, which fires the blast furnace — is not captive. Jayaswal Neco held its own coal blocks until the 2014 Supreme Court ruling cancelled 214 coal-block allotments across India, and it lost them. Today it purchases and imports coking coal, so a swing in global coal prices flows straight into its cost line. It owns half its raw-material problem and rents the other half.
  • The real story is the debt coming down. Jayaswal Neco carried ₹5,759 Cr of debt in March 2020. By March 2025 that was ₹2,721 Cr, and a December-2025 refinancing cut both the amount (toward ~₹2,300 Cr) and the interest rate (from about 17.5% to 12.5%). Falling interest expense is why FY26 profit nearly doubled to ₹463 Cr on only modest revenue growth. The share trades near 19× earnings — no longer distressed, not yet a fortress.
In plain terms

Picture one mine feeding two very different kitchens. Jayaswal Neco pulls iron ore out of the ground and sends it two ways: some becomes ordinary steel — a cyclical, price-taking commodity where the market sets your selling price and you have little say — and some becomes engineered castings, specific ductile-iron parts made to a customer's drawing, where the relationship is stickier and the value added is higher. Owning the ore lowers the cost of feeding both kitchens. The complication is that the fuel for the furnace, coking coal, has to be bought at whatever the world charges. And sitting on top of the whole operation, for years, was a heavy loan book. The investment case is really about how much of that furnace's output flows through to shareholders now that the interest bill is finally shrinking.

01

Integrated steel 101: where the money leaks and where it's saved

No metallurgy degree needed — just the cost stack that decides who makes money in steel.

To understand Jayaswal Neco you first have to understand how molten iron is made, because the company's whole advantage lives inside that recipe. Making steel from scratch — the "integrated" route — starts with two raw materials heated together in a blast furnace: iron ore (the metal) and coking coal (the fuel and the chemical reducing agent). Out comes pig iron, a crude molten iron that is then refined into steel or cast into shapes. Every rupee of profit in this business is decided by what those two inputs cost you versus what the finished metal sells for.

The five words that run the business
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)

Why the raw material — not the mill — decides the margin

In most manufacturing, the factory is the moat. In integrated steel, the ore body is. Roughly speaking, the two raw materials — ore and coking coal — make up the lion's share of the cost of a tonne of crude steel. A mill that has to buy both at market prices is a passenger: when ore or coal spikes, its margin is squeezed and it can do nothing about it. A mill that owns its ore has taken the single largest cost off the open market and fixed it at the cost of digging, which barely moves. That is the difference between a business that merely survives the steel cycle and one that can actually earn through it — and it is precisely the lever Jayaswal Neco pulls with its captive mines. The catch, which we come to, is that it only owns one of the two big inputs.

Who buys what this company makes

  • Construction & infrastructure — buyers of the long products (bars, rounds, sections) that go into buildings, bridges and general fabrication. A commodity relationship driven by the going steel price.
  • Automotive & tractor makers — the core castings customers, buying engineered ductile- and grey-iron parts (housings, brackets, brake components) to a specification. Stickier, because a qualified casting supplier is hard to switch out.
  • Engineering, railways & pipe — machine builders, the railways and pipe-fitting buyers taking castings and pig iron. Adds end-market breadth beyond autos and construction.
02

Two arms under one roof: the commodity and the craft

One furnace, two businesses with completely different economics — read this once and the company clicks into place.

Jayaswal Neco is genuinely two companies sharing a raw-material supply. The steel arm is a cyclical commodity business: it makes pig iron and long products and sells them at whatever the market pays that week. The castings arm is an engineered-components business: it pours iron into precise moulds to make parts a customer designed, qualified and depends on. They come from the same molten iron, but they behave nothing alike.

The two arms, decoded

  • The steel arm — blast-furnace pig iron plus steel long products (bars, rounds, wire rod, sections). This is the volume business and the cyclical one. Its selling price is set by the national steel market; when steel is strong the arm prints cash, when steel is weak it earns little. Captive ore is what keeps it profitable in the soft patches other mills can't survive.
  • The castings arm — grey- and ductile-iron castings for autos, tractors, engineering machinery and the railways. Each part is engineered to a customer's specification and the supplier has to be qualified onto that customer's line — a process that takes time and, once done, is not casually reversed. That makes castings revenue stickier and its margins less hostage to the raw steel price than the long-products arm.
  • The shared feedstock — both arms start from the same molten iron out of the same furnace, fed by the same captive ore. That is the point of the integration: one cost advantage (owning the ore) subsidises two very different end-markets at once, spreading the fixed cost of the mine and furnace across both.
Why running both is worth explaining

A pure steel company lives and dies by the steel price. A pure castings company lives and dies by auto and tractor demand. Jayaswal Neco holds both, fed by one mine — which means its cost base is lower than a stand-alone caster (which would buy its pig iron) and its revenue is more diversified than a stand-alone long-products mill (which sells only commodity steel). The flip side is complexity: two businesses, two customer sets, two demand cycles and one balance sheet that has to fund all of it. When steel is weak but autos are strong, the castings arm cushions the blow; when both are weak — which does happen — there is nowhere to hide, and that is when the debt load used to hurt most.

03

The company, the family, and who controls it

A Nagpur promoter house that built an integrated mill — and then spent a decade repairing it.

Jayaswal Neco is a promoter-run business built by the Jayaswal family of Nagpur. It grew from an engineering-and-castings base into a fully integrated iron-and-steel producer — adding iron-ore mines, a blast furnace, a captive power plant and steel-making — the capital-hungry expansion that gave it its cost edge but also loaded it with the debt that would dominate its story for years afterwards.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Jayaswal family holds the controlling majority stake (roughly 60%+, approximate — held). As with many promoter-heavy Indian mid-caps that went through a heavy-debt phase, investors should read the shareholding filings directly for the exact promoter holding and any pledged portion, since pledge levels are a real risk signal for a company with this leverage history.
  • What it owns end-to-end: captive iron-ore mines (Metabodeli, Chhotedongar), a blast furnace and steel-making, a large iron-castings operation, rolling for long products, and captive power. The vertical integration is the asset — ore-to-part under one owner — but it is also what cost so much to build and financed with debt.
  • Balance sheet, transformed: from ₹5,759 Cr of debt (Mar-20) to ~₹2,300 Cr (post the Dec-25 refinancing). Still leveraged for a cyclical, but a fundamentally lighter load than five years ago — and now at a materially cheaper interest rate.
The structure matters because the debt is the story: Jayaswal Neco's plants and mines were never the problem — a company that owns its iron ore and makes India's largest volume of iron castings has real assets. What held the equity down for years was the interest cost eating the operating profit before it could reach shareholders. The whole re-rating case rests on the balance-sheet repair being durable, not on a sudden leap in what the mills produce.
04

The captive-ore edge: owning the biggest cost

Why mining its own iron ore is the structural advantage the whole business is built on.

Return to the cost stack from Section 01: in integrated steel, iron ore is the largest raw-material cost. Jayaswal Neco's answer to that is simple and powerful — it doesn't buy its ore, it mines it. Its captive mines at Metabodeli and Chhotedongar supply roughly 3.95 MTPA, enough to make it close to 100% self-sufficient in iron ore. That single fact reshapes its margins versus almost every mid-sized mill that has to buy ore on the market.

The company owns the larger of the two inputs and buys the other. Captive ore fixes the biggest cost at the price of digging; imported coal leaves the second-biggest cost floating on global markets. That asymmetry is the single most important thing to hold in mind about the cost base.

The mechanism is worth being concrete about. When iron-ore prices rise, a mill that buys ore watches its costs climb and its margin shrink, with no defence. Jayaswal Neco's cost of ore is essentially the cost of extracting it — mining, hauling, processing — which barely moves with the market price. So when ore is expensive, Jayaswal Neco's relative advantage widens; when ore is cheap, the advantage narrows but never disappears, because it still isn't paying a supplier's margin on top. The captive mine is a permanent structural discount on the biggest line in the cost sheet.

The device that only fits this company: one mine, two mouths

Here is the construct that makes Jayaswal Neco specifically itself and no one else. Its captive iron ore doesn't just feed a steel mill — it feeds two businesses from one hole in the ground: a blast-furnace steel operation and India's largest iron-castings business. The same low-cost tonne of ore lowers the cost base of both a cyclical commodity (long products) and a value-added engineered product (ductile-iron parts) at the same time. Most steel companies with captive ore run only a steel mill; most large casters buy their pig iron. Jayaswal Neco is the rare case where a single ore body subsidises a commodity and a craft together — a cost edge that, thanks to the deleveraging, is now increasingly reaching shareholders instead of being consumed by interest. The one thing that edge does not cover is the fuel, and that is the next section.

05

The coal it lost: the swing cost nobody controls

The genuine surprise — this ore-owner does not own its fuel, because a court took it away.

A company that mines its own iron ore sounds like it has the whole raw-material problem solved. It doesn't. The second essential input to the blast furnace is coking coal, and Jayaswal Neco does not mine that. It used to — it held captive coal blocks — but the 2014 Supreme Court judgment cancelled 214 coal-block allotments across the country, ruling the original allocations improper, and Jayaswal Neco's coal went with them. Since then it has bought and imported its coking coal at whatever the global market charges.

A genuine surprise: the instinct with a vertically integrated miller is "it owns its inputs, so it's insulated." For Jayaswal Neco that is only half true. It owns the ore but rents the coal — and not by choice. A single court ruling in 2014 stripped away captive coal blocks it had already been allotted, converting a would-be cost advantage into a permanent cost exposure. So the same company that is close to 100% self-sufficient in one major input is close to 0% self-sufficient in the other, and the gap between those two numbers is not a strategy — it is the residue of a legal decision.

The consequence shows up directly in the margin. Because coking coal is imported and globally priced, a spike in international coal costs raises Jayaswal Neco's cost per tonne of iron with no captive buffer to absorb it. This is exactly what pressures margins in the softer years — FY25's dip in profit came partly from steel realisations easing while the bought-coal cost stayed stubborn. When you model this company, the captive ore is the reason the margin has a floor; the imported coal is the reason the margin still has a ceiling that global markets can lower at any time.

How to hold the two inputs together

The cleanest way to read Jayaswal Neco's cost base is as a hedge with one leg missing. Iron ore — the bigger input — is hedged permanently by the captive mines. Coking coal — the smaller but still large input — is fully exposed to global prices. So the company captures the upside of owning ore in every year, but it also eats the volatility of coal in every year. That is why its EBITDA margin can sit in a respectable mid-to-high teens (~18% in FY24) yet slip a few points when coal runs hot and steel runs soft (~15.8% in FY25). The captive ore is the durable good news; the bought coal is the recurring variable that keeps this from being a fully de-risked cost structure.

06

India's biggest iron-castings maker: the value-added half

The arm that turns a commodity mill into something a little more defensible.

If the steel arm is the volume, the castings arm is the quality. At roughly 140,000 tonnes a year, Jayaswal Neco is India's largest producer of iron castings — grey-iron and ductile-iron parts made for automobiles, tractors, engineering machinery, pumps, valves and the railways. This is where the business earns more than the commodity steel price alone, and where its customer relationships are worth something beyond the next tonne sold.

A casting is not a commodity. When a truck maker or a tractor manufacturer designs a component — a gearbox housing, an axle part, a brake disc, a heavy bracket — it specifies the exact metallurgy, dimensions and tolerances, and then qualifies a foundry to make it. Getting onto that qualified-supplier list takes engineering work, sample approvals and time. Once a caster is designed into a customer's part, switching it out means re-qualifying a new supplier for a safety-critical component — a hassle the customer avoids unless something goes wrong. That is what makes castings revenue stickier than long-products revenue, and its pricing less brutally tied to the weekly steel market.

Why the castings arm changes the risk profile

A pure long-products mill is a price-taker with no memory: every tonne is a fresh negotiation against the market price, and loyalty counts for nothing. A qualified castings supplier is the opposite — its parts are engineered into a customer's product, so the relationship carries forward and the margin reflects capability, not just the metal. For a company whose steel arm is a cyclical commodity, having the country's largest castings franchise attached is a genuine diversifier: it ties a slice of revenue to automotive and tractor demand (a different cycle from raw steel), and it adds engineering value that a stand-alone mini-mill simply doesn't have. It won't make Jayaswal Neco immune to the steel cycle — the steel arm is still the larger business — but it softens the edges of it.

Each has its own demand rhythm, so a soft patch in one is rarely a soft patch in all — which is exactly the point of holding a broad castings book alongside a commodity steel mill.

07

The deleveraging: why FY26 profit nearly doubled

The mechanism that turned modest revenue growth into a leap in earnings — and the crux of the equity case.

This is the section that matters most for a shareholder. Jayaswal Neco's revenue grew respectably in FY26, but its profit did something far more dramatic — it nearly doubled, from ₹113 Cr in FY25 to ₹463 Cr in FY26. Almost none of that leap came from selling more steel. It came from paying less interest. Understand this and you understand the entire re-rating.

Now watch the interest line, because that is where the profit came from. The refinancing cut the borrowing rate from roughly 17.5% to 12.5% — a five-percentage-point reduction on billions of rupees of debt — on top of the falling balance. Interest expense of about ₹400 Cr in FY26 is guided toward ~₹288 Cr in FY27. Every rupee shaved off the interest bill drops, almost untouched, to the bottom line, because the operating profit above it is largely unchanged. That is the arithmetic behind a profit that nearly doubled on modest revenue growth.

The non-obvious point: this is a financial turnaround dressed as a steel story

It is tempting to read Jayaswal Neco as a bet on steel prices. But look at what actually moved the FY26 result: not steel realisations, which were only modestly better, but the interest bill. The operating business — captive ore, blast furnace, castings — was already profitable at the EBITDA line for years; the problem was that a mountain of expensive debt sat between EBITDA and net profit and ate most of it. As that debt shrinks and re-prices cheaper, more of the same operating profit reaches shareholders. So the biggest swing factor in the equity over the last two years has been the treasury desk, not the rolling mill. That is unusual, and it is the single most important thing a new investor should internalise: the near-term earnings power is being unlocked by deleveraging, and it will keep improving only as long as the debt keeps falling and doesn't get re-loaded by fresh capex.

A fair-minded skeptic should sit with the history here, not just the trajectory. Debt that peaked near ₹5,759 Cr in 2020 did not appear by accident — it was the cost of building an integrated mill, and it made the equity a hostage to lenders for years. The repair to ~₹2,300 Cr is real and impressive, but ~₹2,300 Cr is still a meaningful load on a cyclical business, and the improvement is only as durable as the discipline behind it. The bull point and the bear point are the same fact seen from two ends: the debt has come down a long way, and there is still a long way it needs to stay down.

The steel cycle and the demand behind it

What actually moves the operating profit once you strip out the interest line.

Deleveraging explains the profit leap, but the operating profit still rides the steel cycle and the demand for castings. Both arms ultimately depend on India building, driving and farming. It's worth being clear about what that demand is and how exposed the company is to the parts of it that swing.

The way to think about the cycle here

Jayaswal Neco's operating margin is a band, not a line. Captive ore sets the floor of that band — the company will not swing to losses on the cost side the way an all-purchased mill can. Imported coal and market steel prices decide where inside the band it lands in any given year. So the operating profit is genuinely cyclical, but it is a cushioned cyclical: it moves with steel and coal, but from a higher and more stable base than a peer without captive ore. Layer the falling interest bill on top of that cushioned operating profit, and you get why the net profit can rise even in a year when the steel cycle itself is only lukewarm.

09

The industry and the rivals

A mid-sized integrated player sitting between commodity steel giants and specialist casters.

Jayaswal Neco doesn't fit neatly into one competitive box, which is both its distinctiveness and its awkwardness. In steel long products it competes against far larger integrated majors and a swarm of secondary mills; in castings it competes against dedicated foundries. Its edge is being integrated across both — but it is not the biggest in either.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, Jayaswal Neco wins on cost, not size — it will never out-scale the national steel majors, but its captive ore lets it out-earn the many secondary mills that buy every input, especially in soft years when those mills are underwater and it is not. Second, its castings business is the part rivals find hardest to copy: a large steel major generally isn't focused on qualified auto castings, and a dedicated foundry generally doesn't own an ore mine and a blast furnace. Being the country's largest iron-castings maker and an ore-owning integrated mill is an unusual combination — narrow, but genuinely hard to replicate. The vulnerability is the same as every Indian steelmaker's: a flood of cheap imports can cap the selling price no matter how good the cost base is.

At roughly ₹8,970 Cr of market value against ₹463 Cr of FY26 profit, the stock is on the order of 19× trailing earnings — clearly re-rated from distress, but not priced as if the turnaround is finished and de-risked. That middle ground is the point of tension: the market is paying for a continued improvement in earnings that depends on two things it cannot control (steel and coal prices) and one it can (the interest bill).

The number that settles the thesis

For a hospital it's occupancy; for a refiner it's the GRM. For Jayaswal Neco, the thesis-settling read is a combination, and it has to be read together: the castings-versus-steel mix (how much revenue comes from the sticky, value-added arm), the captive-ore volumes (is the cost edge fully utilised?), and above all the net-debt and interest-cost trajectory — all read against where the steel cycle and coking-coal price sit. If net debt keeps falling toward and below ~₹2,300 Cr, the interest bill keeps dropping to ~₹288 Cr and lower, the castings mix holds, and margin stays in its mid-to-high-teens band, then FY26's earnings power is a floor to build on rather than a cyclical peak. If debt gets re-loaded by fresh capex, or coal spikes into a soft steel market, the same leverage that amplified the recovery will amplify the disappointment. The mill sets the operating profit; the debt schedule decides how much of it you keep.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cost-advantaged mill that fixed its balance sheet — now asked to keep it fixed through a cycle.

Jayaswal Neco enters FY27 in the strongest financial shape of recent memory: captive ore intact, India's largest castings book, debt more than halved and re-priced cheaper, and profit running at a multi-year high. The open question is no longer whether the business works — it plainly does at the operating level — but whether the deleveraging holds through the next soft patch in steel, and whether the coal exposure and any new capex are managed so the interest bill keeps falling rather than creeping back.

The bull case

  • Captive-ore cost edge — near-100% ore self-sufficiency fixes the biggest input cost, giving a durable margin floor most mid-sized mills lack.
  • Deleveraging still running — debt from ₹5,759 Cr to ~₹2,300 Cr, rate cut ~17.5%→12.5%; each further cut in the interest bill drops straight to profit.
  • India's largest castings franchise — sticky, qualified auto/tractor/engineering customers diversify away from pure commodity steel.
  • Operating leverage to steel — a genuine up-cycle in steel prices would compound on top of the lower interest cost, lifting net profit sharply.
  • Re-rating room — at ~19× a recovering cyclical, further debt reduction and margin stability could support the multiple.

The bear case

  • Coking-coal exposure — no captive coal since 2014; an imported-coal spike compresses margin regardless of how steel sells.
  • Still-leveraged cyclical — ~₹2,300 Cr of debt on a commodity business; a soft steel year plus fresh capex could re-load the balance sheet.
  • Price-taker on steel — cheap imports and global prices cap the steel arm's realisations no matter how low the cost base is.
  • Mining & regulatory risk — the ore edge depends on lease renewals and clearances; the 2014 coal ruling is a reminder that captive resources can be taken away.
  • Earnings volatility — the net line swings far more than revenue (FY25 PAT ₹113 Cr vs FY26 ₹463 Cr); a leveraged cyclical rarely earns in a straight line.
The bottom line

Jayaswal Neco is a mid-sized integrated miller with one real structural advantage — it owns the iron ore that feeds both a commodity steel arm and India's largest iron-castings business — and one real structural exposure it did not choose, the coking coal a 2014 court ruling forced it to start buying. For a decade the equity's problem wasn't the plant; it was the debt sitting on top of it, eating the operating profit before shareholders saw it. That is the part that has genuinely changed: debt more than halved and re-priced cheaper, and a profit that nearly doubled as the interest bill fell. The next chapter turns on one checkable thing each year — whether net debt and the interest cost keep falling while the captive-ore margin holds through the steel cycle. Watch the debt schedule and the coal price together every quarter; between them they will decide how much of this cost-advantaged mill's output actually reaches the people who own it.
