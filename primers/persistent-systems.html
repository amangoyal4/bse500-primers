00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Persistent Systems is an Indian digital-engineering and software-services company — but with a distinctive DNA. It did not grow up running back-office processes or maintaining legacy IT for banks. It grew up building software products for the world's technology companies (Outsourced Product Development, or "OPD"), and that engineering-first heritage still shapes everything: the client base, the skills, and the premium the market pays for the stock.

Three facts frame the whole story:

  • It is the growth leader of the mid-cap IT pack. FY26 revenue was $1,654.4M, up 17.4% year-on-year (up 23.5% in rupee terms to ₹14,748 Cr) — a pace roughly double the large-cap Indian IT majors, and its 24th consecutive quarter of sequential growth. That consistency, in a soft demand environment for the industry, is Persistent's calling card.
  • Three verticals, a few very large accounts. Revenue splits across Software, Hi-Tech & Emerging Industries (~40%), Banking, Financial Services & Insurance / BFSI (~35%) and Healthcare & Life Sciences (~25%). Its top handful of accounts — anchored by a long-standing IBM relationship, hyperscalers and independent software vendors (ISVs) — drive a large share of revenue, which is both an efficiency and a concentration risk.
  • Priced for continued excellence. Profit (PAT) rose ~33% to ₹1,865 Cr in FY26; EPS ₹118. At a ~₹74,000 Cr market cap the stock trades around ~38–44× earnings — a clear premium to peers that only holds if the company keeps compounding revenue in the high teens and walks its EBIT margin higher toward its stated ambition.
In plain terms

Think of Persistent as an engineering shop for software itself. When a bank, a healthcare company, or a software vendor needs to build a new digital platform, modernise a creaking system, or wire artificial intelligence into its products, Persistent supplies the engineers, the accelerator tools and the delivery muscle to do it. Unlike a classic outsourcer that competes on the cheapest headcount, Persistent leads with product and platform engineering — deeper, more technical work that grows faster and (in theory) resists commoditisation. The market loves the growth streak and pays a premium for it. The debate is whether AI makes that engineering work more valuable (Persistent sells more of it) or cheaper (clients need fewer engineers) — and whether a business leaning on a few big accounts can keep the streak alive.

01

Why Persistent isn't a mini-TCS

The single most important thing to understand before anything else.

Most Indian IT companies you have heard of — TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCLTech — are, at heart, enterprise IT-services and BPO giants: they run and maintain the technology estates of large corporations, do application development and maintenance, and (historically) some business-process work. Persistent's origin story is different, and that difference explains its faster growth, its client base and its valuation.

The OPD heritage

Persistent was built as an Outsourced Product Development (OPD) partner. Its earliest and most important customers were not banks needing their payroll systems maintained — they were software and technology companies (ISVs and, most famously, IBM) that needed help building their own products. That is a fundamentally more technical, higher-value kind of work than running someone's IT helpdesk. It seeded three durable advantages:

  • An engineering-first culture and talent pool — the firm is stacked with software engineers who build products, not just support tickets. That is exactly the skill set the current "digital engineering" wave demands.
  • Deep, sticky relationships with technology companies and hyperscalers — Persistent is a preferred partner to Microsoft, AWS, Google Cloud, IBM, Salesforce, Snowflake and others, giving it early access to new platforms and a channel to sell into their customers.
  • A "products-and-platforms" mindset — Persistent doesn't just deploy people; it builds reusable accelerators and its own AI platforms (SASVA, iAURA) that it layers on top of client engagements.
Why this framing matters for the numbers

The OPD/digital-engineering positioning is the reason Persistent grows at ~17% while the large-caps grow low-single-digits. Enterprise IT budgets (the giants' bread and butter) are mature and slow; digital engineering, cloud modernisation and AI enablement — Persistent's turf — are the fastest-growing slices of tech spend. It is a mid-cap (~$1.65bn revenue vs TCS's ~$30bn) growing off a smaller base into faster-growing pools, run by engineers who like building software. That is a genuinely distinct animal from a generic Indian-IT writeup — and it is why the market awards it a growth multiple rather than a value multiple.

The flip side of the same coin: being engineering-led and concentrated in technology/ISV clients also means Persistent is more exposed to the AI-productivity question (§06) than a process-heavy outsourcer — its work is precisely the software-building activity that AI coding tools automate. The bull says AI is a tailwind (more software gets built); the bear says it is the thing eating Persistent's own core. Hold that tension in mind — it recurs throughout this primer.
02

How an IT-services firm actually earns

No coding required — just the handful of levers that drive every rupee.

An IT-services company is, financially, a people-and-utilisation business: it hires engineers, bills their time and skills to clients, and profits on the gap between what it charges and what it costs to employ and support them. A few numbers explain almost everything.

The simple profit equation

Roughly: Revenue ≈ Billable engineers × Utilisation × Billing rate, and profit ≈ that revenue minus wages and overhead. So an IT-services firm grows three ways — add engineers, keep them busy (utilisation), and charge more per hour (rate, driven by moving into higher-value work). Margins expand when the firm bills more work per person, offshores more of it to lower-cost locations, and stops being dragged down by half-empty new accounts. Because the biggest cost is salaries, wage inflation, the rupee, and the mix of onshore-vs-offshore work swing margins quarter to quarter. Watch CC growth, TCV, utilisation and margin together — they tell you if the machine is accelerating or stalling.

The pyramid — and why AI threatens its base

Traditionally, IT-services firms run a "pyramid": a broad base of cheap junior engineers doing routine coding, narrowing up to a few expensive senior architects. Profit comes from leverage — lots of low-cost juniors billed at a healthy markup. The reason the AI question (§06) is existential for the whole industry is that AI coding assistants automate exactly the routine work at the base of that pyramid. For an engineering-led firm like Persistent, that is simultaneously the biggest opportunity (do more with fewer people, sell AI transformation) and the biggest threat (clients pay for fewer bodies).

03

The company and who runs it

A founder-led engineering house, now professionally managed by a growth-focused CEO.

Persistent is a founder-built, engineer-led business. Computer scientist Dr. Anand Deshpande founded it in Pune in 1990, grew it as a product-engineering specialist, listed it in 2010, and — crucially — handed operational control to a professional CEO while staying on as chairman. That founder-plus-professional-management structure is a big part of the culture and the growth discipline.

Why the three-unit structure is a strength

05

Clients, concentration & the order book

A few very large accounts, a widening base — and the deal pipeline that fuels it all.

Persistent's revenue quality rests on two things pulling in opposite directions: a small number of very large, deep accounts that give it scale and efficiency, and a broadening long tail of clients that reduces dependence over time. The tension between the two is one of the most important things to track.

The concentration question

  • The anchor accounts. Persistent's largest relationships historically centre on a long-standing IBM partnership plus hyperscalers (Microsoft, AWS, Google) and large ISVs. Its top-5 accounts span healthcare, technology and financial-services clients. These deep relationships are efficient — big, repeatable engagements — but they concentrate risk.
  • The FY26 soft spot. Management flagged that the top-5 accounts saw softness during FY26, tied to clients extracting "promised cost savings and offshoring" — a reminder that when a giant account pushes for efficiency, it directly dents Persistent's revenue and pricing.
  • The offsetting broadening. Persistent has been steadily adding and scaling million-dollar-plus accounts — the "$1M / $5M / $20M / $50M / $100M client" buckets the industry tracks — so growth increasingly comes from a wider base and new logos, not just the anchors.

Why TCV is the number to watch

Revenue tells you what Persistent earned last quarter; TCV tells you what it has lined up for the next several. When trailing-twelve-month new TCV ($1.47bn) runs close to or above annual revenue, the tank is refilling — the growth streak has fuel. The concentration trade-off in one line: big anchor accounts (IBM, hyperscalers, top ISVs) give Persistent scale, repeat work and privileged access to new platforms — but they also mean a single client's cost-cutting drive, or the loss of one marquee relationship, can visibly dent growth. The de-risking is the widening client base; watch whether the million-dollar-account buckets keep climbing.
06

The AI / GenAI question — engine or eraser?

The defining force for an engineering-led IT firm — and Persistent sits right in the blast radius.

Every IT company now talks about AI, but for Persistent the question is unusually sharp. Because its core business is building software — the exact activity that AI coding assistants make faster and cheaper — generative AI is simultaneously the biggest opportunity and the biggest threat it faces. How this resolves will do more to determine the stock's next five years than any other single factor.

The threat: cannibalisation

Classic Indian IT profits on billable engineer-hours. If an AI assistant lets one engineer do the work of two — or lets a client's own staff do work they used to outsource — then the number of billed hours falls. For a firm whose product is software engineering itself, that is a direct hit to the base of the pyramid (§02). The bear's version: AI compresses the very work Persistent sells, forcing rates and headcount down faster than new demand appears.

The engine: AI-led delivery and platforms

Persistent's answer is to get ahead of the tool, not be replaced by it. Its response has three prongs:

  • SASVA — Persistent's proprietary AI-led software-engineering platform (now in its 4.x generation), designed to embed AI agents into the delivery process and boost engineering productivity. Rather than let clients capture all the AI savings, Persistent uses SASVA to deliver projects faster and win more of them — selling outcomes and platforms, not just bodies.
  • iAURA and a widening AI stack — additional platforms and a "3C" (Core, Context, Coordination) framework for enterprise AI transformation, plus 120+ AI patents filed — evidence it is building intellectual property, not just reselling others' models.
  • Hyperscaler & ISV partnerships — deep alliances with Microsoft, AWS, Google Cloud, IBM, Salesforce and Snowflake mean Persistent is a go-to partner when those platforms' AI capabilities need to be implemented at enterprise clients — turning the AI wave into a demand channel.
The bull-vs-bear mechanism, spelled out

Here is the crux. If AI mostly expands the pie — enterprises build far more software because it's cheaper to, and they need a partner to do AI transformation, data readiness and modernisation at scale — then Persistent sells more, higher-value work, and SASVA lets it deliver at better margins. Growth and margins both rise. If AI mostly shrinks the effort per project without a matching surge in volume — clients simply need fewer engineers — then billed hours and pricing fall, and even a productivity platform can't fully offset it. The honest truth is that nobody yet knows which effect dominates, and it may differ by vertical and over time. What we can observe: Persistent is still growing ~17% and booking record TCV, which suggests that so far the pie-expanding effect is winning. The bear's rejoinder is that the reckoning is deferred, not avoided.

Why this is existential, not incremental: an engineering-led shop like Persistent is more exposed to this than a process-outsourcing firm — its revenue is the software-building activity being automated. That cuts both ways: it is first in the firing line, but also best-positioned to sell AI-transformation work because it has the engineering depth clients need. Track two things each quarter — revenue growth vs headcount growth (if revenue grows much faster than heads, AI is helping margins) and TCV (is AI-related demand refilling the pipeline?).
07

The margin-expansion journey

Fast growth has come at a lower margin than peers — closing that gap is the second half of the story.

Persistent's revenue growth is best-in-class, but its operating margin has historically trailed the large-cap Indian IT majors (who run low-20s EBIT margins). Persistent's FY26 EBIT margin was 15.6%. Management's explicit ambition is to walk that margin up by ~200–300 basis points even as it keeps growing fast — and how credibly it does so is central to the profit trajectory and the valuation.

The Q4 FY26 exit margin of 16.3% is above the full-year average — a positive signal that the walk is underway. The stated ambition is to add roughly 200–300 bps over the medium term.

Where the extra margin is supposed to come from

  • Operating leverage on scale — as revenue grows past $1.65bn toward $2bn, fixed costs (management, infrastructure, brand) spread over a bigger base.
  • Higher utilisation and pyramid optimisation — keeping more engineers billable, and rebalancing the mix of senior-to-junior staff.
  • AI-led delivery (SASVA) — if AI accelerators let Persistent deliver the same outcome with fewer hours, some of that saving can be retained as margin rather than passed entirely to clients.
  • Offshore mix and cost discipline — shifting more work to lower-cost offshore locations and holding sub-contractor and travel costs down.
  • Improving deal quality — richer, platform-led engagements at better pricing than pure staff-augmentation work.
Why the margin walk is the swing factor for profits

Here's the leverage. On ~$1.65bn of revenue, every 100 bps of EBIT margin is worth roughly $16–17M of operating profit — and it drops almost straight to the bottom line. So the combination Persistent is chasing — keep growing revenue in the high teens and add 200–300 bps of margin — would compound profit much faster than revenue. That is precisely the recipe a premium multiple is paying for. The catch is the tension with growth: fast-growing new accounts and aggressive hiring can dilute margin in the short run (new work starts less efficient), so the walk tends to be gradual and lumpy, not a straight line. Q4 FY26's 16.3% exit margin says the direction is right; the question is pace.

People — the raw material

In a services business, the engineers are the product. Two numbers matter.

Because Persistent sells engineering talent, its two most important operational metrics after revenue are how many people it has and how many it is losing. Attrition and headcount growth are the pulse of the delivery machine.

Why attrition and headcount move the P&L

When attrition is high, a firm spends heavily to recruit and re-train replacements, loses productivity while new hires ramp, and sometimes has to pay up to retain scarce skills — all of which squeeze margin. Persistent's attrition easing to ~13% is therefore a margin-friendly signal. Headcount growth tells the opposite side of the story: a firm hiring aggressively is signalling confidence in future demand — but if revenue does not keep pace with the wage bill, utilisation and margin fall. The metric to watch in the AI era (§06) is revenue-per-employee: if Persistent can grow revenue faster than headcount, it means AI-led delivery is lifting productivity — the single cleanest proof that AI is an engine rather than an eraser.

Indian IT is a vast, mature export industry. Persistent's competitive position is best understood by size tier and by style — it is a mid-cap that competes on digital-engineering depth and growth, not on the sheer scale-and-price of the large-caps.

The competitive tension to watch

Persistent lives in a happy but exposed spot. It is small enough to grow fast (off a ~$1.65bn base into fast-growing digital pools) yet large enough to win serious enterprise deals — the mid-cap sweet spot. The threats come from two directions. From above, the large-caps are pouring resources into digital and AI and can undercut on price using their scale. From the side, global engineering specialists (EPAM, Globant, Endava) chase the same premium work. Persistent's defence is its OPD-rooted engineering culture, its hyperscaler/ISV partnerships, and its speed — but in a slowing-industry, AI-disrupted environment, everyone is fishing in the same shrinking near-term budget pool. The growth gap Persistent maintains over peers is the single clearest scorecard of whether its differentiation is real.

The industry backdrop: Indian IT export demand has been soft for two years — clients cautious on discretionary spend amid macro uncertainty, higher-for-longer rates and now AI-driven budget reallocation. Persistent's ability to keep compounding through that softness is exactly why it stands out; it also means any broad industry recovery would be an added tailwind, and any deepening of the slowdown a shared headwind.

How to think about the multiple

At a ~₹74,000 Cr market cap on ~₹1,865 Cr of FY26 profit, Persistent trades around 38–44× trailing earnings (the exact figure moves with the share price) — a large premium to the large-cap Indian IT majors, which sit closer to 20–30×. Three engines behind the roadmap

  • Vertical depth — pushing further into BFSI (the FY26 star), Healthcare & Life Sciences and Hi-Tech, deepening the top accounts while broadening the client base.
  • AI-led transformation demand — positioning SASVA and the partner ecosystem to capture enterprise spend on AI enablement, data readiness and modernisation — turning the disruptor into a demand source.
  • Deal momentum — a TCV engine ($1.47bn TTM) that keeps the pipeline refilling faster than it empties, plus selective acquisitions to add capability and scale.
The next chapter is less about proving the model works and more about proving it scales — can a mid-cap engineering house keep growing at double the industry rate all the way to $5bn, while lifting margins, in the teeth of the AI transition?
12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely excellent growth business — priced to keep being excellent.

Persistent is one of the cleanest growth stories in Indian IT: an engineering-led, debt-free, high-ROCE mid-cap that has out-grown the industry for six straight years and 24 straight quarters, run by a founder-plus-professional team with a clear ambition. The investment debate is almost entirely about durability of growth, the AI transition, and price — not business quality.

The bull case

  • Growth leadership — ~17% USD growth and a 24-quarter streak, roughly double the large-cap majors, in a soft market.
  • Right side of the mix — engineering-led, aimed at the fastest-growing digital, cloud and AI pools, not mature legacy IT.
  • Margin runway — a clear ~200–300 bps EBIT-expansion path (16.3% Q4 exit) that would compound profit faster than revenue.
  • AI as an engine — SASVA/iAURA platforms, 120+ AI patents, and deep hyperscaler/ISV alliances position it to sell AI transformation.
  • Pristine economics — debt-free, ROCE ~34%, ROE ~27%, strong cash, rising dividend (₹40 FY26).

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~38–44× earnings leaves little room for error; highly de-rating-prone on any disappointment.
  • The AI cannibalisation risk — an engineering-led firm is first in line if AI shrinks billable software work faster than it expands demand.
  • Client concentration — a few large accounts (IBM/hyperscalers/ISVs) drive revenue; FY26 already saw top-5 softness on client cost-cutting.
  • Industry softness & margin-vs-growth tension — cautious IT budgets; margin walk can stall when fast growth dilutes it.
  • Currency & wage/macro swings — USD/INR moves and wage inflation can whipsaw reported growth and margins quarter to quarter.
The bottom line

Persistent is a structurally advantaged, engineering-led growth compounder in the most attractive slices of IT — with a balance sheet and returns profile that let it self-fund the ambition. The business risk is low relative to peers; the valuation and AI-transition risks are real. The story over the next few years is written in a handful of numbers each quarter: constant-currency growth (is it still lapping the majors?), TCV (is the pipeline refilling?), EBIT margin (is the walk to ~18% happening?), and revenue-per-employee (is AI helping or hurting?). Get those right and the premium is justified by years of double-digit compounding toward $2bn and beyond; stumble on any of them, and a ~40× multiple has a long way to fall.
