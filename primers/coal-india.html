00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Coal India Limited (CIL) is the single largest coal-producing company on earth and a near-monopoly at home: it digs roughly 75–80% of all the coal mined in India. It is a government-controlled "Maharatna" holding company that does almost no mining itself — instead it owns eight producing subsidiaries (Mahanadi, South Eastern, Northern, Central, Eastern, Western, Bharat Coking, plus a planning arm) that run the mines. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) the group dug 768 million tonnes of coal, sold 745 MT of it, booked ~₹1.68 lakh crore of revenue and ₹31,071 Cr of net profit, and paid out a ~6%-yielding dividend.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It runs a two-tier pricing system, and that is the whole game. CIL sells the bulk of its coal cheaply under regulated Fuel Supply Agreements (FSAs) — long-term contracts mostly with power plants at government-influenced "notified" prices. It sells a smaller slice (~12–14% of volume) on the open market through e-auctions at a big premium. That premium — and how much it can route through it — is the single biggest swing factor in CIL's profit, far more than the headline tonnage.
  • It is wired to India's electricity grid. Coal still fuels ~70%+ of India's power generation, and the power sector takes the lion's share of CIL's coal. So CIL's demand engine is, in plain terms, how much electricity India burns — which means weather (monsoons), economic growth, and the pace of renewables all feed straight back into CIL's order book.
  • A high-dividend cash cow staring down the energy transition. CIL throws off enormous free cash, runs almost no net debt, and is one of the market's most generous dividend payers (FY26: ₹26.75/share). It trades on a single-digit P/E precisely because investors fear the long-term story — a decarbonising world that wants less coal — even as near-term Indian demand keeps climbing. CIL's answer is to keep mining hard and diversify into renewables and critical minerals.
In plain terms

Think of Coal India as the country's coal tap, owned by the government. It turns out the fuel that runs most of India's power stations, and because it is a near-monopoly it doesn't really compete on price — most of its coal is sold at a regulated, deliberately-cheap rate to keep electricity affordable. The clever bit is the side valve: a slice of coal is auctioned to the open market at a much higher price, and that premium is where the real profit lever sits. CIL mints cash, carries no real debt, and hands most of the cash to shareholders (above all the government, which owns ~63%) as a fat dividend. The catch is the calendar: the world is trying to use less coal, so CIL is a cash machine today that has to figure out what it becomes in 20 years — hence its push into solar and lithium.

01

The master concept: two-tier pricing

FSA coal vs e-auction coal — read this once and the rest of CIL clicks into place.

CIL does not sell its coal at one market price. It sells it through two completely different channels at two completely different prices, and the split between them is the most important thing to understand about the company. Get this, and you understand why CIL's profit can swing even when the tonnage it mines barely moves.

Channel 1 — Fuel Supply Agreements (FSA): the cheap, regulated bulk

The vast majority of CIL's coal — roughly 85–88% of volume — goes out under Fuel Supply Agreements. These are long-term, take-or-pay contracts, overwhelmingly with power utilities (and some to steel, cement, fertiliser and other "non-regulated" industries). The price is the company's "notified price" — a grade-wise rate CIL administers, which the government strongly influences in the national interest. The whole point of FSA coal is to be cheap and reliable: it keeps the cost of electricity down for hundreds of millions of Indians. CIL is effectively asked to under-price its coal to the power sector as a public-service obligation. FSA realisation in FY26 ran around ₹2,000–2,150 per tonne (and rose ~38% year-on-year in Q4 after a notified-price revision flowed through).

Channel 2 — e-auctions: the small, premium open market

The rest — roughly 12–14% of volume — CIL sells on the open market through electronic auctions (the main one is "spot" e-auction; there are also exclusive auctions for specific sectors). Here, buyers who can't get enough FSA coal — or want it without a long-term contract — bid against each other, and the price floats up to whatever the market will pay. Because Indian coal demand chronically outstrips cheap FSA supply, e-auction coal sells at a hefty premium over the notified price — a premium that has ranged from ~30% to well over 60% depending on how tight the market is. In FY26 the average premium was around 38%, with e-auction realisation near ₹2,350 per tonne versus FSA's ~₹2,100.

The e-auction price sat ~38% above the notified FSA price in FY26. Because that premium falls on a small slice of volume, even a small shift in the e-auction price (or in how much coal is routed to e-auction) moves CIL's profit disproportionately. This is the lever everything else turns on.
Why this two-tier system exists — and why it matters so much

India faces a constant tension: it wants cheap power (which argues for cheap coal) but CIL is a commercial company that pays dividends to the exchequer (which argues for high coal prices). The two-tier system is the compromise. Most coal goes out cheap under FSA to protect electricity prices; a controlled slice is auctioned at market rates to capture some upside and let CIL earn a real margin. For an investor this means CIL's earnings power is not really about how much coal it mines — it's about the mix and the premium: what fraction goes to high-priced e-auction, and how big the e-auction premium is. Management has openly said it wants to lift e-auction's share of volume toward 15–20% over time — that, plus periodic FSA price hikes, is the legitimate way CIL grows realisation when tonnage is flat.

02

The company and who controls it

A 1975 nationalised monopoly that became a Maharatna holding company.

Coal India was born out of nationalisation. In the early 1970s the Indian state took over the country's fragmented, often poorly-run private coal mines — first the coking-coal mines (1971–72), then the rest (1973) — to secure energy supply and improve mine safety. Coal India Limited was formed in 1975 as the holding company for the nationalised industry. It has been a near-total monopoly ever since, and is today a "Maharatna" — the top tier of operational autonomy granted to India's largest, most successful public-sector enterprises.

How it's structured — a holding company over eight miners

Crucially, CIL itself barely mines anything directly. It is a holding company that owns and coordinates eight subsidiaries, each of which actually runs the mines in a region:

  • Mahanadi Coalfields (MCL) — Odisha; the largest producer, very low-cost opencast mining.
  • South Eastern Coalfields (SECL) — Chhattisgarh/MP; another huge producer (and the fastest-growing in FY26).
  • Northern Coalfields (NCL) — MP/UP; large, efficient opencast.
  • Central Coalfields (CCL) — Jharkhand; significant coking-coal washery plans.
  • Eastern Coalfields (ECL) & Western Coalfields (WCL) — older, higher-cost underground-heavy regions.
  • Bharat Coking Coal (BCCL) — Jharia; India's main domestic coking-coal source for steel.
  • Central Mine Planning & Design Institute (CMPDI) — the planning, exploration and consultancy arm (not a producer).

This matters because CIL's consolidated numbers are really the sum of eight regional miners with very different cost structures — MCL/NCL are cheap opencast cash machines; ECL/WCL drag on costs. The mix of which subsidiary grows fastest quietly shapes group margins.

Ownership

  • Government of India — ~63% (pre-June 2026 OFS: 63.13%). The dominant shareholder. This makes CIL's pricing, dividend and capital allocation partly an instrument of state policy — New Delhi leans on CIL for cheap power-sector coal and for a large dividend cheque, and periodically sells small stakes (like the June 2026 OFS) to raise divestment revenue.
  • Public / institutions — the rest. CIL is a heavyweight in the indices and a staple of dividend-income and PSU funds.
The "PSU duality": CIL's government ownership is both its anchor and its discount. As majority owner the state wants the dividend, so payouts are large and reliable — a plus. But the same control means CIL can be steered toward national objectives (cheap FSA coal, social spending, sometimes absorbing policy costs) that a pure profit-maximiser would resist — which is part of why the market values CIL on a low single-digit multiple.
03

The demand engine: why coal still keeps India's lights on

CIL's order book is, in plain terms, how much electricity India burns.

CIL doesn't have a marketing problem so much as it is India's electricity supply chain. Coal still fuels roughly 70%+ of the electricity India generates (and coal is ~55% of the country's total primary energy). The single biggest buyer of CIL's coal is the power sector — state and central thermal power plants, led by giants like NTPC. So understanding CIL's demand means understanding India's power demand.

Why coal is so hard to displace in India

  • It's domestic, abundant and cheap. India has vast coal reserves and a built-out mining and rail network around them. Coal is the lowest-cost dispatchable power India can produce at scale without importing fuel — a strategic and economic anchor.
  • It's "baseload" — it runs all the time. Solar produces only in daylight, wind only when it blows. Coal plants run around the clock, which is what a grid needs to stay stable when renewables aren't generating. Until cheap, large-scale storage arrives, coal is the backbone that fills the gaps.
  • Demand keeps growing. India's electricity consumption rises with GDP, air-conditioning, EVs and industrialisation. Even as renewables are added fast, absolute coal demand has kept climbing because the overall pie is growing — new green capacity is meeting the extra demand rather than replacing coal.
The monsoon-and-renewables swing factor

Here's the subtlety that bit CIL in FY26. Coal demand from power plants is seasonal and weather-driven. A long, heavy monsoon does two things at once: it cools the country (cutting air-conditioning load and thus power demand) and it pushes more hydro and wind into the grid — so thermal plants run less and burn less coal. FY26 saw exactly this: extended monsoons, a rising share of renewables, and more coal coming from captive miners (power companies and the government auctioning their own blocks) all softened demand for CIL's coal, which is why offtake actually fell ~2% even though India's economy grew. In good years (hot summer, weak monsoon, strong industry) the reverse happens and CIL's coal flies off the shelf at high e-auction premiums.

04

The volume story: marching toward a billion tonnes

Production, offtake, and why the gap between them matters.

For a decade the headline ambition of India's coal policy has been a round, symbolic number: one billion tonnes a year. The country as a whole crossed that milestone (counting CIL plus captive and commercial miners) in March 2026 for a second consecutive year, and CIL itself targets close to 1 BT of its own. The volume story has two numbers you must keep separate.

Production vs offtake — why both numbers exist

  • Production = how much coal CIL physically mines out of the ground in a period.
  • Offtake = how much coal CIL actually moves out and sells — loaded onto trains and trucks and dispatched to customers.
  • The two diverge when coal piles up at pithead stocks (mined but not yet evacuated). If offtake lags production, inventory builds — money tied up in coal heaps, sometimes prone to quality loss or fire. In FY26 both fell ~2% together because demand was soft, so CIL deliberately throttled production to avoid stockpiling coal it couldn't sell.
Why "evacuation" is half the battle

Digging coal is the easy part for CIL; getting it to the customer is the chronic bottleneck. The overwhelming majority of CIL's coal travels by rail, so CIL's output is hostage to railway capacity, the availability of rakes (train sets), and dedicated freight corridors. A mine can be producing flat-out, but if the railways can't haul the coal away, it just sits at the pithead. This is why CIL spends heavily on "first-mile connectivity" — conveyor belts, silos, rapid loading systems and rail sidings that move coal from the mine face onto trains efficiently. Evacuation infrastructure is, quietly, one of the most important determinants of whether CIL hits its volume targets.

The honest read on FY26: CIL missed its production target, and output fell year-on-year, because demand was weak (monsoon, renewables, captive coal). That's not a capacity failure — CIL has the mines to dig more — it's a demand signal. The longer-run direction is still up: India needs more power, the national 1-BT milestone has been hit, and the FY27 national target was set even higher (~1.3 BT including all producers). For CIL, the question is whether its share of that growing pie holds as commercial and captive miners expand.

05

The cost stack: wages, overburden & evacuation

Why CIL's margins are decided as much by costs as by coal prices.

CIL's selling prices are largely set (FSA) or auctioned (e-auction), so on the cost side the company's margins hinge on a handful of big, mostly-fixed buckets. The defining one — unusual for a mining company — is wages.

The big cost buckets

  • Employee costs (wages) — the giant fixed cost. CIL employs hundreds of thousands of workers and is one of India's largest formal-sector employers. Wages are a huge slice of its cost base — and crucially, they are largely fixed (they don't fall when coal demand dips). Wages are reset every five years through the National Coal Wage Agreement (NCWA) for non-executive workers, negotiated with trade unions. When a new wage pact (or provision for one) lands, it can spike employee costs sharply — exactly what hit FY26, where a wage-revision provision pushed quarterly employee expense up ~22% and compressed margins.
  • Overburden removal (OBR) & the stripping ratio. Most of CIL's coal is opencast — you remove the soil and rock sitting on top of the coal seam (the "overburden") before you can dig the coal. The stripping ratio is how many cubic metres of overburden you must shift per tonne of coal. As mines age and seams go deeper, the stripping ratio rises, meaning more diesel, explosives and machinery per tonne — a structural upward creep in cost. OBR is a major, capital- and fuel-heavy cost line.
  • Contractual & transport / evacuation. Outsourced mining contractors, plus the cost of moving coal (rail freight, road, conveyors). Diesel prices feed directly into both OBR and transport.
  • Statutory levies. Coal carries heavy government charges — royalties, the GST Compensation Cess (a flat ₹400/tonne), the District Mineral Foundation contribution, and other cesses. These are largely passed through in price but are a big gross-to-net wedge.

Operating leverage — the double-edged sword

Because so much of CIL's cost (wages above all) is fixed, the company has heavy operating leverage. In a good year — high e-auction premiums, strong volumes, no wage reset — incremental revenue drops almost straight to the bottom line and margins fatten dramatically. In a bad year — a wage hike lands, premiums soften, demand dips — those same fixed costs don't budge and margins collapse fast (FY26's slide from ~33% to ~24% operating margin is the live example). This is why CIL's earnings can look lumpy: a year dominated by a wage revision will always look weak, even if the underlying business is fine.

06

The volatile lever: e-auction premium cyclicality

The single most-watched number each quarter — and the most cyclical.

Because the FSA price is sticky and regulated, almost all of CIL's quarter-to-quarter profit swing comes from the e-auction premium. It is the most market-sensitive lever CIL has — and it behaves like a commodity spread, moving with how tight the coal market is.

From ~69% a year earlier to ~36% in Q4 FY26: the premium nearly halved as high coal inventories, soft thermal demand and abundant imported/captive coal loosened the market. That swing — on a small slice of volume but at a high margin — is a big part of why CIL's profit fell in FY26.

What moves the premium

  • How tight the coal market is. When power demand is hot and coal is scarce (summer peaks, weak monsoon, supply disruptions), buyers bid e-auction prices up and the premium soars. When demand is soft and stocks are high (FY26's situation), the premium collapses.
  • Imported coal prices. E-auction coal competes with imported seaborne coal. When global coal prices spike (e.g. a geopolitical shock), domestic e-auction premiums tend to follow up; when global coal is cheap, the premium compresses.
  • How much CIL routes to e-auction. CIL controls the tap. If it allocates more coal to e-auction it captures more premium per tonne — but flooding auctions can itself soften the premium. Management's stated aim to lift e-auction share toward 15–20% is a deliberate realisation lever, balanced against not crashing the price.
Why analysts obsess over it: a few percentage points of e-auction premium, applied across ~100+ MT of e-auction coal, can be worth thousands of crores of profit. So when a CIL result comes out, the first thing the market checks isn't tonnage — it's the e-auction premium and volume. A high premium can make a flat-volume quarter look great; a collapsing premium (FY26) can sink an otherwise steady one. It is the company's most volatile, most-watched earnings driver.
07

The cash cow: dividend & valuation

A debt-light monopoly that hands most of its cash to shareholders.

For income investors, the dividend is a large part of the CIL story. The company generates enormous operating cash flow, carries almost no net debt (it's effectively net-cash), and has limited need for the kind of heavy reinvestment that a growth company swallows. So it returns the bulk of its profit as one of the market's most generous dividends.

Why CIL can pay so much

  • Monopoly economics + cheap mining. Most of CIL's coal is shallow opencast, cheap to dig, and sold into a chronically short market. That throws off high free cash flow consistently.
  • Net-cash balance sheet. CIL sits on large cash reserves and minimal net debt, so it doesn't need to retain earnings to service borrowings.
  • The government wants the cash. As ~63% owner, the state is the biggest dividend recipient — and it relies on CIL's payout (plus occasional stake sales like the June 2026 OFS) for budget revenue. That alignment keeps the payout policy generous and predictable.
How to read the cheap multiple

CIL typically trades on a single-digit P/E with a ~6% dividend yield — far cheaper than the broad market. Three reasons: (1) cyclicality — the e-auction-premium swing makes earnings lumpy and hard to forecast; (2) the PSU discount — government control raises the risk of value-diluting policy decisions (cheap FSA coal, social spending) and overhangs the stock; and (3) the big one, terminal-value fear — in a decarbonising world the market is reluctant to pay up for a coal monopoly whose product the world is officially trying to phase out. The bull rebuttal: a sub-9× P/E with a ~6% yield already prices in a lot of that pessimism, and as long as Indian coal demand keeps growing for another decade-plus, an investor is being paid handsomely to wait — with the dividend doing much of the work.

Eight regional miners, plus the move toward coking coal and washeries.

The coking-coal / washery push — substituting imports

India imports large volumes of coking coal (the high-grade coal steelmakers need), because domestic coking coal is scarce and high in ash. CIL is investing to change this: it has committed roughly ₹3,300 crore to build eight new coking-coal washeries (notably through CCL and BCCL) by ~FY30. A washery cleans raw coal — removing rock and ash — to lift its quality and energy content. The strategic logic is import substitution: cleaner domestic coking coal reduces India's reliance on expensive imports for its steel industry, and lets CIL earn more per tonne on a premium product. It's also a quality play on the thermal side — better-washed coal commands better prices and burns cleaner.

A structural reform under discussion: CIL's board has explored separately listing its strongest subsidiaries (MCL and SECL). The idea: list the crown-jewel miners individually so the market values them on their own merits rather than burying them inside the conglomerate discount — a potential value-unlock for CIL shareholders, similar in spirit to how a holding-company structure can mask the worth of its best parts.
09

The existential question: coal in an energy transition

Strong near-term demand vs the long-term phase-down — the debate that defines CIL's multiple.

No company embodies the energy-transition tension more starkly than a coal monopoly. The world has formally committed to reducing coal use to fight climate change. Yet India — the world's most populous nation, still electrifying and industrialising — keeps burning more coal each year. CIL lives inside that contradiction, and how an investor weighs it decides whether CIL looks cheap or dangerous.

"Peak coal" — the question with no agreed answer

The whole debate reduces to one question: when does Indian coal demand peak? Bulls argue it's a long way off — India's per-capita power use is still a fraction of the developed world, demand is racing ahead, and renewables (intermittent, storage-light) can't yet carry baseload. On this view CIL has a decade-plus of growing or stable volumes ahead, and the market's terminal-decline fear is overdone. Bears argue the peak is closer than it looks — solar is now the cheapest new power India can build, battery storage is falling fast, and once the crossover comes, coal demand could plateau and then fall, leaving CIL with stranded mines and a shrinking market. The truth is genuinely uncertain, which is precisely why CIL trades at a low multiple and a high dividend yield: the market is pricing in eventual decline while the cash keeps flowing in the meantime.

Diversification: solar, hydrogen & critical minerals

A coal company trying to build its next act before the transition arrives.

CIL's strategic answer to the transition question is to diversify its cash hoard into the businesses that will matter in a decarbonised world — using the coal-funded balance sheet to buy itself a future beyond coal. The push has three legs.

The most strategically interesting leg is critical minerals. The energy transition runs on lithium, cobalt, copper and rare earths — and India imports nearly all of them, heavily concentrated on a few foreign suppliers. CIL is a mining company with capital, technical depth and a government mandate; pivoting some of that capability into the minerals the world will need more of (rather than less of) is the cleanest hedge against coal's long-term decline. The Chile/Singapore subsidiaries are the vehicles for acquiring overseas mineral assets.

The honest caveat — small today, directional tomorrow

For now this is a directional story, not an earnings driver. Renewables and critical minerals are a tiny sliver of a group still ~99% defined by coal. The FY26 capex of ~₹16,000 crore is still overwhelmingly about coal — mines, evacuation, washeries — with diversification a minority slice. So the market gives CIL little credit for the pivot today. That's either an opportunity (if CIL successfully reinvents itself as a multi-commodity miner, that optionality is free in the current price) or a fair reflection of how early and unproven the diversification is. The competing claim on CIL's cash is the dividend — and the government, as majority owner reliant on that payout, may prefer cash returns over speculative new ventures, which limits how aggressively CIL can fund its own reinvention.

11

Financials & the FY26 results

A flat-revenue, lower-profit year — squeezed by wages and a softer premium.

FY26 was a weak year for profit despite steady revenue. Consolidated revenue held roughly flat at ~₹1.68 lakh crore, but net profit fell ~12% to ₹31,071 Cr as a wage-revision provision and softer e-auction premiums compressed the operating margin from ~33% to ~24%. The fourth quarter looked better on the surface — Q4 profit rose ~11% — but that was partly helped by higher other (non-operating) income and accounting adjustments rather than a true operational rebound.

How to read a CIL result in 30 seconds

When CIL reports, skip the headline tonnage and check four things in order: (1) the e-auction premium and volume — the real profit swing; (2) employee cost — is a wage revision distorting the quarter? (3) offtake vs production — is demand soft and inventory building? and (4) the dividend — is the cash cow still paying? FY26 fails on (1) and (2) — softer premium, big wage provision — which is the entire story of why profit fell while revenue held. None of that breaks the long-term franchise; it's a cyclical and wage-cycle dip, not a structural one.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, high-yield monopoly — printing cash today, with a question mark over tomorrow.

Coal India is a paradox: a cash-rich, debt-light, near-monopoly cash cow the market values cheaply because its product is one the world is officially trying to phase out. The investment debate is whether the deep value and ~6% dividend yield outweigh the cyclical earnings and the long-term decarbonisation overhang.

The bull case

  • Monopoly + cash cow — ~75–80% of India's coal, net-cash balance sheet, ~35% ROCE, ~6% dividend yield. A lot of pessimism already in the price.
  • Demand engine still running — coal is ~70%+ of power; absolute Indian coal demand keeps growing as the economy and grid expand; baseload need is real until storage scales.
  • Realisation levers — periodic FSA notified-price hikes, plus lifting e-auction's share toward 15–20%, grow realisation even with flat tonnage.
  • Cyclical recovery — a hot summer / weak monsoon / firm industry would re-tighten the market and snap the e-auction premium back up.
  • Free optionality — subsidiary listings (MCL/SECL), washeries, and the critical-minerals pivot are upside the market isn't paying for.

The bear case

  • Energy-transition terminal risk — the world wants less coal; if Indian demand peaks sooner than bulls think, CIL's core market shrinks and the multiple stays low.
  • Cyclical, lumpy earnings — the e-auction premium swings violently (FY26: ~69% → ~36%), making profit hard to forecast.
  • Fixed-cost squeeze — wages are huge and rising (NCWA resets); stripping ratios creep up; high operating leverage hurts in soft years.
  • Demand wobble — monsoons, renewables and captive/commercial miners can soften CIL's volumes (offtake fell in FY26).
  • PSU control — government can prioritise cheap FSA coal, social spend or its own cash needs (OFS) over minority holders; persistent discount.
The bottom line

Coal India is best understood as a deep-value, high-yield cyclical with an embedded long-term question mark rather than a growth stock. You are buying India's irreplaceable, lowest-cost coal franchise — and a near-certain ~6% dividend — at a single-digit P/E that assumes coal's days are numbered. Over the next few years the story turns on three things to watch each quarter: the e-auction premium (the profit lever), offtake (is demand growing or softening?), and the dividend (is the cash cow still paying?). Get those right and a sub-9× monopoly yielding ~6% is a sturdy income compounder for as long as India keeps burning coal; get the bigger call wrong — an early peak in coal demand, a faster transition, a failed diversification — and the "cheap" multiple proves cheap for a reason. The whole thesis is a bet on how long the cash machine keeps running, and whether CIL builds a second act before the music stops.
