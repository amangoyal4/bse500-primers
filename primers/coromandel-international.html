00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Coromandel International is the crop-solutions flagship of the Chennai-based Murugappa Group, and it does two big things: it makes and sells fertilisers — chiefly phosphate-based complex fertilisers (NPKs) and DAP — and it makes and sells crop-protection chemicals (agrochemicals — the pesticides, herbicides and fungicides that keep a crop alive). It is India's largest private-sector phosphatic-fertiliser producer, with roughly a 17.5% share of the country's phosphatic market and manufacturing anchored on the east coast at Kakinada, Visakhapatnam and Ennore.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Two engines, very different characters. The Nutrient (fertiliser) business is the big, low-margin, government-fed volume engine — about 85% of revenue in FY26 but only ~66% of EBITDA. The Crop-Protection (agrochemical) business is smaller but higher-margin and less subsidy-dependent — and in FY26 it grew revenue ~16% while lifting its own profit ~55%. The whole "quality of earnings" story is about tilting the mix toward the second engine.
  • The government is Coromandel's biggest customer-behind-the-customer. On P&K fertilisers, the farmer pays only part of the price; the rest arrives as a Nutrient-Based Subsidy (NBS) cheque from the Centre. That makes policy, subsidy rates and the timing of subsidy receipts as important to the P&L as tonnes sold — and it ties up serious working capital (the subsidy receivable was ~₹3,785 Cr at end-Dec 2025).
  • FY26 was record revenue but a dip in profit — for structural reasons. Consolidated income hit an all-time-high ~₹31,827 Cr (+30%) and EBITDA ~₹3,232 Cr (+23%), yet reported PAT slipped ~8% to ~₹1,898 Cr on exceptional items and NBS-linked timing effects, plus the first depreciation and interest from newly commissioned backward-integration plants. Understanding that gap — good operations, optically weaker headline profit — is most of understanding Coromandel today.
In plain terms

Think of Coromandel as a farmer's two shopping baskets rolled into one company. Basket one is the big, heavy bag of fertiliser — cheap-looking to the farmer because the government quietly pays most of the bill, but a thin-margin, capital-heavy, policy-driven business for the maker. Basket two is the small, expensive bottle of crop-protection chemical — no government subsidy, better margins, and the part that increasingly decides how the market values the whole company. Coromandel's strategy is to keep the volume basket humming (and cheaper to make, by owning more of its raw materials) while making the profitable basket a bigger and bigger part of the story.

01

Fertiliser economics 101: what actually earns the money

No chemistry degree required — just how a bag of NPK turns raw rock into revenue.

A fertiliser is, at heart, a packaged dose of three plant foods — Nitrogen (N), Phosphorus (P) and Potassium (K), the "N-P-K" you see printed on every bag — plus secondary nutrients like Sulphur. A "complex" or "NPK" fertiliser combines several of these in one granule; DAP (di-ammonium phosphate) is the workhorse phosphate product. Coromandel's job is to buy the raw ingredients, chemically combine them into these branded granules, and sell them to farmers through a vast rural dealer network.

The plant-food alphabet (and where it comes from)
NutrientWhat it does · and the raw material behind it
N — NitrogenDrives leafy, green growth. Comes from ammonia (made from natural gas) — mostly imported by Coromandel.
P — PhosphorusRoots, flowering, energy. This is Coromandel's core: made from phosphoric acid, which is made from rock phosphate — both largely imported, and the reason backward integration matters so much here.
K — Potassium (Potash)Overall plant health, water use. India has almost no domestic potash; 100% imported (MOP) — a permanent import dependency.
S — SulphurA secondary nutrient and an input to sulphuric acid (used to make phosphoric acid). Coromandel makes bentonite-sulphur fertilisers and its own sulphuric acid.
The realisation equation — who pays for the bag

For a phosphatic fertiliser, the price the maker actually receives — its realisation — comes from two pockets: Realisation ≈ Farmer's price (MRP) + Government subsidy (NBS). The farmer sees a low, affordable price at the dealer; the government tops it up with a fixed per-nutrient subsidy paid straight to the manufacturer. So a fertiliser maker's revenue rises or falls not just on tonnes sold, but on subsidy rates and global raw-material costs — and its margin is squeezed whenever import prices (phos-acid, ammonia, rock, potash) rise faster than the subsidy is topped up. That's the single most important mechanism in the whole business, and section 02 pulls it fully apart.

Why margins are thin — and why volume alone doesn't move the needle

  • It's a converter, not a brand-monopolist. A big chunk of the bag's cost is imported raw material whose price Coromandel doesn't control. When global phos-acid or ammonia spikes, the cost of goods jumps; the maker can only recover it if the government raises the subsidy or lets MRP rise. That's why fertiliser is a low-single-digit-to-low-teens EBITDA-margin business, not a 30%-margin one.
  • Seasonality is brutal and literal. Sales track the two crop seasons — Kharif (monsoon-sown, roughly Jun–Sep) and Rabi (winter, roughly Oct–Mar). A good monsoon means strong Kharif demand; a poor or delayed one dents volumes and leaves unsold stock in the channel. So the weather is, quite directly, a line item.
  • Working capital is a beast. The company ships fertiliser now but collects the government's share of the price weeks or months later, as the subsidy is processed. That gap becomes a large subsidy receivable on the balance sheet — money owed by the Centre — which the company must fund. Manage it well and returns are respectable; manage it badly, or let policy slip, and cash gets tight.
02

The subsidy machine: how the NBS regime really works

The single force an analyst leads with for any Indian fertiliser company — read this once and the whole sector clicks.

India keeps fertiliser cheap for ~140 million farming families by subsidising it — but it does so in two very different ways, and the difference is central to Coromandel. Urea (the nitrogen fertiliser) sits under a tightly controlled regime: the government fixes its retail price and reimburses makers for the gap. Phosphatic and potassic (P&K) fertilisers — Coromandel's home turf — sit under the Nutrient-Based Subsidy (NBS) scheme instead.

How NBS works, step by step

  • The government fixes a subsidy per nutrient, not per bag. Twice a year (for the Kharif and Rabi seasons) the Cabinet sets a rupee-per-kilogram subsidy for each of N, P, K and S. For Kharif 2026, for example, the rates were set at roughly ₹47.3/kg N, ₹52.8/kg P, ₹2.4/kg K and ₹3.2/kg S, inside a total P&K subsidy outlay of about ₹41,534 Cr for that season.
  • The subsidy is paid to the maker, not the farmer. There is no form for the farmer to fill; they simply buy the bag at the low dealer price, and the government pays the manufacturer the subsidy portion directly (via the DBT system, on proof of retail sale).
  • The MRP is (largely) decontrolled. Unlike urea, the maker has some freedom to set the farmer-facing price of a P&K product — so it can, in principle, pass on cost increases. In practice that freedom is limited by political sensitivity and competition, so the subsidy top-up does most of the heavy lifting.

Why this is the whole ball game

Because the subsidy is a fixed rupee amount per nutrient, the maker carries the risk of global raw-material prices moving between subsidy resets. If rock phosphate, phos-acid or ammonia spike (as they did during 2021–22 and again in bouts since), the cost of a bag rises immediately, but the subsidy only adjusts at the next seasonal reset — so margins get squeezed in between. Conversely, when the government raises NBS rates (as it did into Kharif 2026 to absorb higher global costs), it cushions the maker. This is exactly why Coromandel's FY26 profit dipped despite record revenue: strong volumes and prices, but cost inflation, exceptional items and NBS-linked timing pinched the reported bottom line. The lesson: for a fertiliser company, read the subsidy policy and the global cost curve, not just the sales line.

03

The company and who controls it

A Murugappa house, born of a 1961 joint venture, now the group's crop-solutions flagship.

Coromandel traces its roots to 1961, when it was formed as Coromandel Fertilisers — a joint venture between the Murugappa Group and the US chemicals major Chevron. Over six decades it became the fertiliser-and-agri-inputs flagship of Murugappa, one of India's oldest and most respected business houses. Its immediate parent and promoter is E.I.D. Parry (India) Ltd, itself a Murugappa company — so the ownership chain runs Murugappa → EID Parry → Coromandel.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter: the Murugappa Group via E.I.D. Parry (India) Ltd, holding roughly 56–57% of Coromandel (Screener records ~56.35% promoter holding as of Mar 2026). A stable, long-term promoter with a reputation for conservative, governance-first management is a real asset in a policy-sensitive sector.
  • Group pedigree: Murugappa is a diversified ~₹700-billion-plus revenue house with nine listed companies (Tube Investments, Cholamandalam Finance, CG Power, Carborundum Universal and others). Coromandel is its agri-inputs standard-bearer.
  • Balance sheet: modestly leveraged for a working-capital-heavy fertiliser business — Screener shows ~₹1,506 Cr of debt against ~₹12,474 Cr net worth at FY26, i.e. a strong, largely self-funding balance sheet that comfortably carries the subsidy receivable and the capex pipeline.
  • Footprint: ~13,000+ employees, manufacturing anchored on the east coast (Kakinada, Visakhapatnam, Ennore, Ranipet and others), and a rural retail network of ~1,100–1,200 Mana Gromor stores across the southern states, reaching around 20 million farmers.

Engine 1 — Nutrients: the volume base

The big, subsidy-fed fertiliser business — the foundation everything else is built on.

The Nutrient (fertiliser) segment is Coromandel's foundation: it is India's largest private phosphatic producer, with roughly a 17.5% share of the country's phosphatic market (Q4 FY26). In FY26 it grew phosphatic fertiliser sales ~7% to ~4.3 million tonnes, and — with crop-protection folded in — the subsidy business made up ~85% of total revenue.

What sits inside "Nutrients"

  • Complex fertilisers (NPKs) & DAP — the core. Multi-nutrient granules and DAP under brands led by the flagship "Gromor" family. This is the volume-and-subsidy heart of the company.
  • Single Super Phosphate (SSP) and other straight phosphatics — the legacy product line the group has made for over a century.
  • Specialty nutrients — water-soluble fertilisers, micronutrients, secondary nutrients and organic/bio products. A small but higher-value, faster-growing, non-subsidised adjacency where Coromandel is the domestic leader — a quiet margin-enhancer inside the nutrient umbrella.
  • Nano fertilisers — Gromor Nano DAP/Urea, discussed in section 07; a new, technology-led product line where Coromandel has grabbed leading share.
Why scale and brand still matter in a "commodity"

Fertiliser looks like a commodity, and in raw chemistry it is. But Coromandel earns an edge three ways. Scale & logistics: east-coast plants sit next to ports, cutting the cost of importing rock, acid and ammonia — a structural freight advantage in a business where a few dollars a tonne decides who wins a tender. Brand & distribution: the Gromor brand and ~1,100+ own stores plus a huge dealer network mean farmers ask for Coromandel by name, giving it pricing and share resilience. Product breadth: a farmer buying NPK can be sold specialty nutrients, nano products and crop-protection in the same visit — the cross-sell that lifts the value of every customer relationship. Commodity chemistry, branded execution.

05

Engine 2 — Crop Protection: the margin & quality driver

The smaller, subsidy-free, higher-margin business the market is increasingly paying up for.

If Nutrients is the volume engine, Crop Protection is the profitability engine. This is the agrochemicals business — the technicals (the active chemical ingredients) and formulations (the finished, branded, farmer-ready products) that protect crops from pests, weeds and disease. Crucially, it carries no subsidy: pricing is set by the market and by product quality, so margins are structurally higher and returns cleaner than the fertiliser base. In FY26 this segment grew revenue ~16% and lifted its own profitability ~55%.

Why crop protection is the "better" business

  • No subsidy, no receivable drag. Farmers (and export buyers) pay the full price; there's no waiting on a government cheque, so it's far less working-capital-hungry per rupee of profit than fertiliser.
  • Higher, more durable margins. Branded formulations and patented/off-patent technicals command pricing power that a subsidised commodity bag never will — and the moat is R&D, registrations and distribution, not just plant scale.
  • An export story. Coromandel exports technicals to global agrochemical majors and into world markets, diversifying away from the Indian monsoon and the domestic subsidy cycle — a genuinely different demand driver.
  • Moving up the value chain. The strategy is to shift from being a low-margin bulk supplier toward higher-value technicals, contract manufacturing for multinationals, and its own branded formulations — capturing more of the margin along the chain.
The NACL Industries acquisition — a step-change in ambition

In August 2025 Coromandel completed the purchase of a 53% controlling stake in NACL Industries for about ₹820 Cr. NACL is an established Indian crop-protection player with a branded domestic-formulations business, a technicals-export presence, and — importantly — contract-manufacturing relationships with global agrochemical multinationals. The logic is textbook value-chain climbing: bolt NACL's manufacturing capacity, product registrations and contract-manufacturing footprint onto Coromandel's vast distribution and balance-sheet muscle, and you get a materially larger, higher-margin crop-protection business almost overnight. It is the clearest single signal that Coromandel wants the quality engine, not just the volume one, to define its future — deliberately diluting its dependence on the subsidy cycle.

Why the mix-shift re-rates the story: a business that is 85% subsidy-fed commodity trades like a policy-and-commodity stock. As crop protection (plus specialty and nano) grows faster than the fertiliser base, a larger share of profit comes from cleaner, non-subsidised, export-diversified earnings — the kind the market awards a higher multiple. The NACL deal, the specialty-nutrient lead and the nano ramp are all facets of the same thesis: earn more of the profit outside the subsidy machine.
06

Backward integration: owning more of the bag

From a Senegal phosphate mine to new acid plants at Kakinada — the bet that lowers the cost of every tonne.

Coromandel's biggest structural vulnerability is its dependence on imported raw materials — rock phosphate, phosphoric acid and ammonia, all priced in dollars and all volatile. Its most important multi-year growth bet is therefore backward integration: making more of its own intermediates (and, upstream, mining more of its own rock) so that a larger share of the value in each bag is captured in-house rather than bought at global spot prices.

The pieces of the integration puzzle

  • Rock phosphate — the Senegal mine. Coromandel holds a controlling stake (~54%) in Baobab Mining & Chemicals (BMCC) in Senegal, a rock-phosphate miner, having raised its holding in stages from 45% (2022). The aim: secure up to one-third of its rock-phosphate requirement captively, diversify sourcing away from a handful of countries, and feed the new Kakinada phosphoric-acid plant with its own rock.
  • Phosphoric acid — the Kakinada plant. A new 650 TPD phosphoric-acid plant at Kakinada, commissioned in FY26, means Coromandel makes more of its own phos-acid rather than importing it — directly reducing exposure to one of the most volatile inputs in the whole chain.
  • Sulphuric acid & sulphur. A new 2,000 TPD sulphuric-acid plant at Kakinada (plus expanded sulphur capacity at Visakhapatnam) supplies the acid used to make phosphoric acid — integrating a step further back and feeding the bentonite-sulphur fertiliser line. Total Kakinada backward-integration outlay was roughly ₹1,000–1,100 Cr.
Why this matters — and why it hurt FY26 optics

Backward integration is a margin and resilience play: every intermediate Coromandel makes in-house is one it no longer buys at a volatile import price, so unit costs fall and the business becomes less hostage to global commodity swings and rupee weakness — a structurally better, more defensible fertiliser maker over time. But there's a J-curve: big new plants arrive with full depreciation and interest costs from day one, while the operating savings build up gradually as the plants ramp. That's a real part of why FY26's reported profit dipped even as revenue hit a record — the Kakinada plants started charging the P&L before they had fully paid back. The bet is that FY27 onward, as they run at scale, the cost savings show up in margins.

07

Retail, nano fertilisers & the new-vertical bets

The parts of Coromandel that look less like a fertiliser company and more like an agri-technology one.

Around the two core engines sit a cluster of newer businesses that give Coromandel optionality — and a different, more premium flavour to the story: a rural retail network, a fast-scaling nano-fertiliser line, drone and mechanisation services, and early forays into adjacent chemistries.

Mana Gromor retail — owning the last mile

Coromandel runs a network of ~1,100–1,200 "Mana Gromor" rural retail stores across the southern states — one-stop shops selling fertiliser, crop protection, seeds, specialty nutrients and advisory services directly to farmers. This matters more than it first appears: the stores are a captive distribution moat and a data/relationship asset, letting Coromandel cross-sell its higher-margin products, gather demand intelligence, and deepen brand loyalty in a market where trust and advice drive purchases as much as price.

Nano fertilisers — a technology-led land grab

  • Gromor Nano DAP & Nano Urea — liquid fertilisers carrying nano-scale nutrient particles that a plant absorbs far more efficiently, so a small bottle can replace part of a conventional bag. Lighter to transport, less subsidy-intensive, and better for soil and the fiscal bill.
  • Leadership already: management flagged that the nano business reached roughly 50% market share in FY26 on ~60% volume growth — an early lead in a product category the government is actively promoting as a way to cut the subsidy burden.
  • Why it's strategic: nano is a rare product where Coromandel can build a branded, higher-value, less-subsidy-dependent position inside the fertiliser franchise — aligning neatly with the same "earn outside the subsidy machine" theme running through crop protection and specialty nutrients.

Drones, mechanisation & adjacent chemistries

  • Drone spraying & farm mechanisation — Coromandel has invested in drone maker Dhaksha Unmanned Systems and partnered on farm-mechanisation services, positioning itself in precision agriculture (targeted spraying that saves chemical and labour). This is where "agri-inputs" starts to blur into "agri-technology."
  • New chemistries & verticals — the expanded sulphuric-acid capacity also supplies industrial and adjacent markets, and the group has explored higher-value speciality-chemical and new-vertical adjacencies off its acid and chemistry base. These are early-stage optionality rather than material earnings today, but they signal a company trying to widen beyond the subsidy-fed core.
The common thread

Retail, nano, specialty nutrients, drones, crop protection — read them together and a pattern emerges. Each is an attempt to reduce Coromandel's dependence on the low-margin, policy-driven, weather-exposed fertiliser commodity and to build branded, higher-value, farmer-loyal, less-subsidy-dependent revenue on top of a huge distribution base. The fertiliser engine funds and distributes; the newer engines lift the margin and the multiple. Whether they scale fast enough to genuinely re-shape the earnings mix is one of the two or three questions that decide the stock over the next five years.

08

The industry and the rivals

A policy-shaped market where a few large players and a giant cooperative compete for the same farmer.

India's fertiliser market is huge, essential and heavily shaped by government policy. Demand is set by acreage, the monsoon and cropping patterns; economics are set by the subsidy regime and global input prices. Within phosphatics, the field is a mix of a dominant cooperative, a handful of large private players and several regional producers. Coromandel is the largest private-sector phosphatic player, competing on scale, coastal logistics, brand and — increasingly — its crop-protection and integration edge.

What actually decides who wins

In a subsidised commodity, three things separate the strong from the also-rans. (1) Cost position — coastal plants near ports, captive intermediates, and backward integration lower the delivered cost per tonne; this is precisely where Coromandel's Kakinada/Vizag/Senegal build-out aims. (2) Distribution & brand — a dense dealer-and-store network and a trusted brand (Gromor) win the farmer's repeat purchase and give pricing resilience. (3) Diversification away from the subsidy commodity — the players building crop-protection, specialty and nano businesses (Coromandel foremost among the private names) earn cleaner, higher-multiple profit and are less hostage to policy and weather. Coromandel scores well on all three, which is why it is usually regarded as the best-run private name in the space.

The structural backdrop: India's farm sector is under-mechanised, soil health is a national priority, and the government is actively pushing balanced fertilisation, nano products and reduced subsidy leakage. That is broadly supportive of a diversified, technology-forward agri-inputs leader — but it also means the rules can change, and a single policy decision (subsidy design, DBT mechanics, import duties) can reshape sector economics overnight.

How to think about the valuation

At roughly ₹60,000 Cr market cap on ~₹1,898 Cr of consolidated FY26 profit, Coromandel trades around the high-20s× on consolidated earnings (Screener shows a P/E near ~30×; on standalone profit the multiple is lower, in the low-20s×) — a moderate multiple, neither cheap nor demanding, for a sector-leading, well-run, integrating agri-solutions company. The bull argues that as crop protection, specialty and nano grow the higher-quality share of profit, and as backward integration lifts fertiliser margins, the earnings deserve to compound and the multiple to hold or expand. The bear argues that ~85% of revenue still rides a government subsidy and the monsoon, that FY26's profit dip shows how quickly policy and commodity swings bite, and that a fertiliser commodity should not command a premium multiple. The valuation, in other words, is a live bet on whether Coromandel keeps graduating out of the commodity box.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A well-run leader graduating out of the commodity box — at a price that already assumes some of that graduation.

Coromandel is, on most measures, the best-run private agri-inputs company in India: sector-leading phosphatic scale, a strong promoter, a genuine crop-protection engine, and a credible, self-funded backward-integration programme. The investment debate is about how fast it can reduce its dependence on the subsidy-and-monsoon commodity core, and whether the moderate multiple already prices that in.

The bull case

  • Best-in-class integration — Senegal rock + new Kakinada acid plants structurally lower fertiliser unit costs as they ramp.
  • Mix-shift to quality — crop protection (turbocharged by NACL), specialty and nano grow the subsidy-free, higher-margin share of profit.
  • Scale & brand moat — largest private phosphatic maker, ~17.5% share, Gromor brand, ~1,100+ own stores, coastal logistics edge.
  • Strong owner & balance sheet — Murugappa/EID Parry governance; healthy ROCE (~23%), self-funded growth, moderate debt.
  • Policy tailwinds — government push on nano, balanced fertilisation and domestic integration aligns with exactly where Coromandel is investing.

The bear case

  • Subsidy dependence — ~85% of revenue rides the NBS scheme; a change in subsidy design, outlay or payout timing hits earnings and working capital.
  • Commodity & FX exposure — imported rock, phos-acid, ammonia and potash priced in dollars; input spikes squeeze margins between subsidy resets (as FY26 showed).
  • Monsoon & seasonality — a weak or delayed monsoon dents volumes and leaves channel inventory; results are weather-exposed.
  • Integration & M&A execution — new plants carry depreciation/interest before payback; NACL must be integrated well to justify the price.
  • Commodity multiple risk — if the mix-shift stalls, the market may keep valuing it as a policy-driven commodity, capping the re-rating.
The bottom line

Coromandel is a high-quality, well-owned leader of a policy-shaped sector, executing a coherent plan to become less commodity and more crop-solutions company: own more of the raw material, grow the margin-rich agchem and specialty lines, and lead in nano. FY26's record revenue with a dip in reported profit captures the tension perfectly — the operations are strong and the strategy is working, but the reported bottom line is still hostage to subsidy timing, global commodity prices and new-plant start-up costs. Watch two things each year: the crop-protection + specialty + nano share of profit (is the mix genuinely shifting?) and the subsidy receivable and NBS policy (is the commodity core behaving?). Get both right and a good company compounds; get either wrong and the commodity gravity reasserts itself.
