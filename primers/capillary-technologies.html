00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Capillary Technologies India Limited sells loyalty and customer-engagement software — the cloud platform that large consumer brands rent to run their points programs, membership tiers, reward catalogues, personalised offers and the customer data behind all of it. It is a pure product-SaaS business: brands sign multi-year subscriptions, Capillary hosts and continually upgrades one platform for all of them, and the revenue recurs. As of early 2026 that platform served 415+ brands, including 20 Fortune 500 customers, touching 1.9 billion+ end consumers across 49 countries from 16 offices — retail, fashion, food-and-beverage, airlines, fuel and financial brands among them.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a subscription platform, not an IT-services shop. Capillary does not sell developer-hours to build one-off systems. It sells access to a ready-made loyalty engine — ~80% of FY25 revenue was recurring subscription (~₹481 Cr of ~₹598 Cr), the rest implementation and services. That distinction is the whole point: a services firm re-earns its revenue project by project; a SaaS firm banks a contract and then keeps billing it, ideally for more each year.
  • The company only just turned profitable — and then listed. Capillary lost ~₹88 Cr in FY23 and ~₹57 Cr in FY24 before posting its first full-year net profit of ~₹13 Cr in FY25 on ~₹598 Cr of revenue, with EBITDA of ~₹78.5 Cr (a ~13% margin). It went public in November 2025 at ₹577 a share, raising ₹877.5 Cr. This is a business that reached the black at almost the same moment it reached the market — the path to durable profit is the live question, not a settled fact.
  • An Indian company that now earns most of its money in America. After acquiring the US loyalty firm Brierley+Partners, North America jumped to ~57% of FY25 revenue from ~48% a year earlier. Capillary was founded in Bengaluru; today its single largest market is the United States. That inversion — an Indian SaaS platform selling loyalty back to American enterprises — is the strategic core of the story.
In plain terms

A big retailer or airline wants a points-and-rewards program, but building and running one — the rules engine, the app, the reward fulfilment, the data science that decides who gets which offer — is expensive and never finished. Capillary rents them the finished machine: one platform, configured to the brand, billed as a yearly subscription and upgraded continuously for everyone at once. The brand gets a modern loyalty program without hiring an engineering army; Capillary gets a contract that renews and, when it works, grows. The entire investment case rests on two numbers a first-timer never thinks about: how much of last year's revenue stays and expands (retention), and how much of each new rupee of subscription actually drops to profit as the platform scales. FY25 was the first year both finally pointed the right way.

01

Why loyalty became a software category

No marketing degree needed — just why brands now buy retention as a platform.

Loyalty programs are old; loyalty software is not. For decades a "loyalty program" meant a plastic card and a spreadsheet of points. What turned it into a large, recurring software market is a shift in how consumer brands compete — and a change in the plumbing of the internet. Capillary sits exactly on that shift. To follow the company you need five ideas.

The only loyalty-tech terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
First-party dataInformation a brand collects directly from its own customers (purchases, app taps, preferences) rather than buying from ad networks. As browser cookies and third-party tracking die off, first-party data is the asset that survives — and a loyalty program is the best machine for gathering it. This is the tailwind under the whole sector.
Loyalty / rewards engineThe software that defines the rules — earn a point per rupee, unlock a tier at N visits, redeem for these rewards — and executes them in real time across app, web, store and email. Capillary's core product.
Customer engagement / CDPA customer-data platform unifies every touchpoint into one profile, then triggers personalised messages and offers. Loyalty and engagement have merged into a single stack — points are useless without the data and messaging around them.
Retention vs acquisitionWinning a new customer costs far more than keeping an existing one. As ad costs rose and growth-at-any-cost fell out of fashion, brands re-weighted budgets toward retaining and deepening customers they already have — which is precisely what loyalty software does.
SaaS & ARRSoftware-as-a-service: rented, hosted, multi-tenant software billed by subscription. ARR (annual recurring revenue) is the run-rate value of those subscriptions — the number that describes a SaaS business better than any single year's accounting revenue.
The structural shift that created the market: the end of the third-party cookie

For twenty years brands could rent knowledge about their customers from the open web — cookies, ad-network profiles, tracking pixels. That era is closing: privacy regulation, Apple's tracking limits and the phasing-out of third-party cookies mean a brand can no longer casually buy its way to customer insight. The only durable substitute is data a brand gathers itself, with permission — and a loyalty program is the most natural place to gather it, because the customer volunteers it in exchange for rewards. That single change reframes loyalty from a marketing nicety into critical infrastructure: the system of record for a brand's most valuable, most defensible asset. Capillary is selling shovels into exactly that gold rush.

Who buys, and why they rent rather than build

  • Large enterprises — retailers, fashion houses, quick-service and F&B chains, airlines, fuel networks, banks and consumer brands with millions of customers. They have the scale to justify a serious loyalty program but rarely the appetite to build and forever maintain the software themselves.
  • Why buy, not build: a loyalty platform is never "done" — it needs continuous new channels, fraud controls, reward integrations, data science and uptime at scale (Capillary advertises "five-nines" availability). Renting one shared platform that improves for everyone is cheaper and faster than each brand reinventing it. That "build-vs-buy" logic is the reason a SaaS category exists at all.
  • The seasonality is muted, the stickiness is high: unlike a fertiliser or a phone, a loyalty program isn't bought once a season — it's wired into a brand's daily operations, so ripping it out is disruptive. That switching cost is what gives good loyalty SaaS its retention.
02

What Capillary actually sells: the platform

One engine, several modules, sold as a subscription that lands and then expands.

Capillary is fundamentally a product-and-platform business: it builds one cloud loyalty-and-engagement stack, sells access to it under multi-year contracts, and layers implementation and managed services around the software. The revenue is dominated by the recurring piece — and understanding the modules explains where the growth and the stickiness come from.

The product stack

  • Loyalty+ — the flagship: the rules-and-rewards engine that runs points, tiers, membership, gamified challenges and redemption across every channel. This is the anchor product most brands buy first, and the hardest to displace once it is running.
  • Engage+ / customer engagement — the messaging and campaign layer: personalised offers, nudges and omnichannel communication driven off the loyalty data. Loyalty tells you who a customer is; engagement acts on it.
  • Insights+ / customer data — the CDP and analytics layer that unifies profiles and feeds the AI. It is what lets a brand see one customer across app, store and web, and target accordingly.
  • Rewards & managed services — reward-catalogue fulfilment, program design, and the human services (including the Brierley loyalty-strategy consulting practice in the US) that wrap the software. Lower-margin than pure subscription, but they win and deepen enterprise accounts.

The loop is the moat: the longer a brand runs on the platform, the richer its customer data and the more painful it is to switch — which is what turns a signed contract into a renewing, expanding one.
The land-and-expand mechanic that defines the revenue

Capillary rarely wins an enterprise all at once. It lands one brand, one region, one module — say the loyalty engine for a retailer's home market — proves it, and then expands: more of the retailer's brands, more countries, the engagement and data modules on top, more consumers under management. A single logo signed at a modest contract can, over a few years, become several times larger without a new sales battle. That is why a SaaS company's health is not captured by "how many customers did you win" but by "how much bigger did last year's customers get" — the retention-and-expansion number the next section is built around. The same logo, billed for three years and growing each one, is worth far more than three logos won and lost.

The SaaS scoreboard: ARR, retention and the rupee that stays

The most important idea in the case — and it is specific to a subscription software business.

Most people read a company by its revenue line. For a SaaS business that is the least informative number, because it lumps together three very different things: subscriptions that will recur, one-off services that will not, and the timing quirks of when a contract books. The right lens is a small set of gauges built to describe a subscription. Four of them run Capillary.

Now the gauge that matters most. Net Revenue Retention (NRR) asks a single question: take all the customers you had a year ago and ignore every new logo you won since — what is that same cohort worth today? Above 100% means your existing customers, as a group, spent more this year than last, even after some churned away. Capillary reported NRR of about 121% — meaning last year's book of business grew roughly a fifth larger on its own, before a single new customer was added.

The device that only fits a subscription platform: growth even if you sold nothing new

Here is what makes NRR the number to hold onto. A fertiliser maker who signs no new customers grows 0%. A SaaS company with 121% NRR who signs no new customers at all still grows ~21%, purely because last year's customers expand faster than others leave. Capillary's reported revenue growth is therefore two engines stacked: the existing book compounding on its own (the NRR engine), plus every new logo and acquisition on top. The quality of the business lives almost entirely in the first engine — because expansion revenue costs little to win, drops through at high margin, and signals that customers are getting real value. Watch NRR slip below ~100% and the whole model inverts: you would be running up a down escalator, needing ever more new sales just to stand still. At ~121%, the escalator is carrying Capillary up.

The company, its founders, and who controls it

A Bengaluru start-up that spent a decade, raised heavily, bought its way global, then listed.

Capillary is not a legacy corporate that IPO'd after decades of profit; it is a venture-built software company that took the long, expensive road typical of enterprise SaaS — years of investment and losses to build a platform and a global customer base, funded by private capital, before reaching profitability and the public market almost together.

  • 2013–2019

  • 2021

  • FY23

  • FY24
    Revenue jumps to ~₹525 Cr as acquisitions and contracts scale; loss narrows to ~₹57 Cr — approaching the inflection.
  • FY25
    First full-year profit: ~₹13 Cr net profit on ~₹598 Cr revenue; EBITDA ~₹78.5 Cr (~13% margin); NRR ~121%; North America ~57% of revenue.
  • Nov 2025

Ownership & structure

  • No single dominant family promoter: as a venture-built company, the cap table is a spread of founders, employees and institutional investors. Post-listing, promoter and promoter-group holding sits around ~51%, with domestic institutions (~19%) and public shareholders holding the rest. Investors should confirm the exact post-IPO shareholding in the latest filing.
  • Institutional venture backing over its life has included global growth investors; several used the IPO's offer-for-sale to partly monetise, which is normal for a decade-old VC-funded SaaS company reaching the public market.
  • Global operating footprint: the business is run as a genuinely international platform — 16 offices, engineering largely in India, and go-to-market weighted increasingly to the US and West Asia. The listed entity is the India-domiciled parent of that group.
Why the ownership shape matters: a venture-built SaaS company brings a professional, product-led management culture and no controlling-family overhang — a genuine positive. The flip side is a broad investor base with pre-IPO holders whose lock-ups will lapse, and a growth-by-acquisition history (Brierley, Rewards+) that means goodwill on the balance sheet and integration to keep executing. The governance question here is less "is the promoter fair to minorities" and more "can a professional team keep a bought-together global platform compounding profitably."
05

The American turn: an Indian platform selling loyalty back to the US

The strategic fact that surprises almost everyone — and the acquisition that drove it.

Here is the fact that upends the intuitive picture of an "Indian SaaS company": Capillary's single largest market is not India. It is the United States. After acquiring the American loyalty firm Brierley+Partners, North America became the biggest slice of revenue and kept growing — the reverse of the usual story where an Indian software firm services Western clients from an offshore back office. Capillary went the other way: it bought its way into the American enterprise front office.

North America went from under half to the majority of revenue in a single year — the clearest fingerprint of the Brierley acquisition. The home region, Asia-Pacific, actually shrank as a share. This is an Indian-founded company that now earns most of its revenue in dollars, from American brands.

Why does the US matter so much? Because it is the deepest, highest-spending loyalty market on earth — American airlines, retailers, hotels and restaurant chains run some of the largest, most sophisticated programs anywhere, and they pay accordingly. Brierley brought Capillary three things at once: marquee US enterprise relationships it could not have won cold, a loyalty-strategy consulting practice that opens doors ahead of the software, and local credibility in a market that is wary of buying critical systems from an unknown foreign vendor. Brierley's own contribution margin improved to ~42% in the half-year to September 2025, a sign the integration is turning the acquisition from a cost into a contributor.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with

The instinctive worry about an Indian SaaS firm is "can it sell to demanding Western enterprises?" Capillary's answer was not to try harder from Bengaluru — it was to buy an American loyalty house and inherit its client list, its consultants and its zip code. That is unusual and it cuts both ways. On the upside, it vaulted Capillary into the world's richest loyalty market and gave it Fortune 500 logos that anchor the whole business. On the downside, it means a large share of revenue, goodwill and execution risk now sits in acquired US operations that must be retained, integrated and grown — and a rising dollar-revenue base brings currency exposure the domestic story never had. The surprise isn't just that the US is the biggest market; it's that Capillary got there by acquisition, which is a different, harder thing to keep compounding than organic wins.

06

The AI question: loyalty's next battleground

Where AI genuinely helps a loyalty platform — and where it is a cost to be carried.

Every software company now claims to be "AI-first." For a loyalty and customer-data platform, though, AI is not marketing veneer — it touches the core job the software does, because a loyalty program is at heart a giant, real-time decision engine: which customer should get which offer or reward, and when. That is exactly the kind of problem machine learning is built for.

Capillary layers AI (branded around its aiRA intelligence and generative-loyalty features) across the stack in a few concrete ways:

  • Prediction: scoring each customer for churn risk, purchase propensity and the optimal timing of a nudge — so a brand spends its reward budget on the customers a well-timed offer will actually move, not on everyone.
  • Personalisation at scale: tailoring the offer, the reward and the message to the individual profile, across millions of customers, in real time — the thing no human marketing team can do by hand.
  • Generative assistance: letting a brand's marketers design campaigns, segments and program rules in natural language rather than through complex configuration — lowering the skill needed to run a sophisticated program.
Why AI is both the opportunity and the tax

AI is a genuine edge for a data-rich loyalty platform: the more consumer behaviour flows through Capillary's system, the better its models can predict and personalise, and the better the results, the stickier the customer — a flywheel a point-tool without the data cannot spin. That is the opportunity. The tax is that staying "AI-first" is not free: it means continuous spend on data science, model infrastructure and compute, and it invites competition from deep-pocketed platforms (Salesforce, SAP, Twilio) bolting AI onto their own marketing clouds. So AI is simultaneously the reason a modern loyalty platform can widen its lead and a permanent cost line that every player must fund out of the same gross margin. For a company that only just reached profit, how efficiently it converts AI investment into retention and expansion — rather than just into expense — is a live part of the margin question in section 09.

The prize in loyalty AI is not a cleverer chatbot — it is deciding, for a billion consumers, who gets which reward and when, and being right often enough that brands never leave.
07

From cash-burn to profit: the inflection, read plainly

The single defining financial event — and why one profitable year is a start, not a verdict.

Enterprise SaaS companies are built to lose money for years: you fund the platform, the sales force and the customer base up front, and the recurring revenue only overtakes the cost later, once the book is large enough. Capillary lived that arc in public view through its filings — deep losses, then a narrowing, then, in FY25, the crossover. Seeing that turn clearly is the heart of judging the business.

Two forces drove the turn. First, scale: as subscription revenue climbed toward ₹481 Cr, the largely-fixed cost of running one shared platform got spread over far more revenue — the operating-leverage that makes SaaS eventually profitable. Second, mix and integration: the acquired US business moved from a drag toward a contributor (Brierley's contribution margin rising past ~42%), and the recurring share of revenue held high. FY25 EBITDA of ~₹78.5 Cr on ~₹598 Cr of revenue is a ~13% margin — respectable for a company at this stage, but still a long way below the 25–35%+ margins mature SaaS platforms reach.

What the single profitable year does and does not prove

The crossover to profit is a real milestone — it shows the model can generate money rather than only consume it, which is the question that dooms many SaaS firms. What one year of ~₹13 Cr profit does not yet prove is durability: the margin is thin, part of the recent growth came from acquisitions rather than the base compounding, and Q1 FY27 slipped back to a small reported loss (largely a tax-and-timing effect, not a collapse in the business). So the honest reading is a company that has demonstrated the destination exists and taken its first steps into it — not one that has arrived. The next few quarters, showing whether margin widens as revenue grows, are where a start becomes a trend.

08

The rivals: a crowded, well-funded field

Capillary competes against marketing-cloud giants, loyalty specialists — and the in-house build.

Loyalty-and-engagement software is not a quiet niche. It is fought over by three kinds of competitor at once: the big marketing-cloud platforms that fold loyalty into a wider suite, the focused loyalty specialists, and — often the toughest rival of all — a brand's own decision to build and run its program internally. Capillary's positioning is as the enterprise-grade loyalty specialist, deeper than a point-tool but more focused and configurable than a giant suite.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Capillary is squeezed between two threats that pull in opposite directions. From above, the marketing-cloud giants have distribution and balance sheets it cannot match, and can afford to treat loyalty as a feature they give away to win the bigger suite deal. From the side, focused specialists like Comarch and Epsilon fight it on capability. Capillary's defence is to be deep enough in loyalty to out-feature a suite's add-on, yet integrated and global enough to out-scale a point tool — with AI and its billion-plus-consumer data as the widening moat. Whether that middle position holds is the strategic risk: get out-featured by specialists or out-bundled by giants, and the retention number that runs the whole model starts to leak.

09

Financials & valuation

A recently-profitable growth platform on a rich multiple — the debate is durability of both growth and margin.

Unlike a cheap cyclical, Capillary carries the valuation of a growth software company: at around ₹488 a share and a ~₹3,900 Cr market cap in September 2026, it trades on a trailing P/E in the mid-70s and several times its revenue. That is not a low-expectations multiple. The market is paying for the recurring-revenue quality, the ~121% retention and the runway of a young, profitable-at-last SaaS platform — which means the case rests on those continuing, not on the stock being cheap.

The scale of the challenge is easy to see in the FY25 shape: ~₹598 Cr of revenue produced only ~₹78.5 Cr of EBITDA (~13%) and ~₹13 Cr of net profit. This is a scaling platform whose margin is deliberately early and thin — the investment case is that those two lower numbers grow far faster than the top line as the largely-fixed cost of running one shared platform is spread over an ever-larger subscription base. The gap between the revenue and the profit today is the opportunity, if the operating leverage arrives.

The one number that settles the thesis: net revenue retention, read with margin

For a subscription platform priced this richly, the metric that proves or breaks the case is net revenue retention (the ~121%), tracked quarter by quarter, and read alongside two companions: EBITDA margin (is the ~13% widening toward the 20s as the platform scales, or stuck?) and the organic ARR growth rate stripped of acquisitions (is the base compounding on its own, or is growth being bought?). If NRR holds above ~115–120% while margin climbs and organic ARR keeps compounding, the platform is doing what elite SaaS does and the multiple has something to feed on. If NRR drifts toward 100% or margin stalls near breakeven, the company is buying growth at a rich price and the rating has nothing underneath it. Retention times margin, minus the cost of the next acquisition — that is the scoreboard, and it is checkable every quarter.

10

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A newly-profitable, US-heavy loyalty platform, asked to prove the profit compounds.

Capillary enters its life as a public company with real assets: a deep loyalty-and-data platform, ~121% net revenue retention, 20 Fortune 500 logos, a majority-US revenue base, a cash-rich balance sheet and its first profits behind it. What it has yet to prove is that the profit widens rather than merely appears — that a young SaaS margin can climb while growth stays high, against giants and specialists both. That tension frames the two cases.

The bull case

  • High-quality recurring revenue — ~80% subscription and ~121% NRR mean the base compounds on its own; new logos and modules stack on top.
  • Structural tailwind — the death of the third-party cookie makes first-party loyalty data critical infrastructure, expanding the whole market Capillary sells into.
  • Enterprise + US depth — 20 Fortune 500 customers and a majority-American revenue base give scale and pricing power the Brierley deal unlocked.
  • Operating leverage ahead — one shared platform over a growing subscription base is how SaaS margins go from ~13% toward the 20s and beyond.
  • Cash-rich, AI-led — fresh IPO capital and a data flywheel across 1.9 bn consumers to fund the AI edge and further tuck-in acquisitions.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — a mid-70s P/E on a thin ~13% margin needs both high growth and margin expansion to hold; either faltering hurts.
  • Growth partly bought — recent revenue leans on Brierley/Rewards+ acquisitions and goodwill; organic ARR compounding must carry the baton.
  • Formidable competition — Salesforce and SAP can bundle loyalty into bigger suites; Comarch/Epsilon fight on depth; the middle can get squeezed.
  • Thin, early profitability — one profitable year, and Q1 FY27 slipped to a small loss; durability of margin is unproven.
  • US & FX concentration — a majority of revenue and integration risk now sits in acquired dollar-earning US operations that must be retained and grown.
The bottom line

Capillary is one of the very few ways an Indian public investor can own a genuine, global product-SaaS platform — and it arrives with the loyalty market's best structural tailwind at its back, strong retention, marquee enterprise customers and, at last, a profit. The stock's price already assumes those strengths persist and deepen, so the reward is not in re-discovering the quality; it is in the quality proving durable and margin widening from a thin base. The next test is concrete and datable: whether, across the FY27 quarters, net revenue retention holds above ~115–120% while EBITDA margin climbs out of the low teens and organic ARR keeps compounding without leaning on the next acquisition. String together a few quarters of retention-plus-widening-margin and the young platform grows into a genuine SaaS compounder; let retention slip or margin stall near breakeven, and a richly-priced business will have bought its growth rather than earned it. For a company that reached profit and the public market in the same breath, that is the milestone that turns a promising first year into a proven one.
