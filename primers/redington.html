00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Redington is a technology-products distributor — the wholesale layer that sits between the world's technology brands and the tens of thousands of shops, resellers and system-integrators who actually sell to you and to businesses. It buys iPhones, laptops, servers, printers, software and cloud subscriptions in bulk from around 440 brands (Apple, HP, Dell, Lenovo, Cisco, Samsung, Microsoft, Google, Nvidia and hundreds more), warehouses them, extends credit to the resellers who buy them, and delivers across ~32 countries and ~40 markets spanning India, the Middle East, Turkey and Africa.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Enormous revenue, wafer-thin margin. FY26 revenue was ~₹1,19,347 Cr (up ~20%) — larger than most household-name Indian companies — yet it earns only a ~2% operating (EBITDA) margin and keeps roughly ₹1,284 Cr of net profit. Distribution is a volume-and-velocity business, not a pricing-power business.
  • A velocity machine, not a margin machine. Because it holds inventory briefly and turns its capital over many times a year, that ~1% net margin still produces a ~17% return on equity and an ~18% return on capital. The whole art is working-capital discipline — inventory days, receivable days, credit risk — not charging more.
  • The re-rating story is a slow climb up the value chain. Box-moving margins are structurally capped, so Redington is pushing into higher-margin cloud distribution (reselling AWS, Microsoft and Google cloud as recurring subscriptions), cybersecurity, AI infrastructure and value-added services — now past 20% of the India business. Whether that mix lifts the group's ~2% EBITDA margin is the single question the stock hinges on.
In plain terms

When a global brand like Apple or Dell wants to sell across a huge, fragmented geography, it cannot open accounts with, ship to, and collect money from every one of tens of thousands of small resellers itself. It hands that entire job — bulk buying, warehousing, financing, logistics, collections — to a distributor. Redington is that distributor at continental scale. It makes almost nothing on each transaction, but it does an astonishing number of transactions, and it gets its cash back fast enough to do them again. The business is less a shop and more a conveyor belt: the faster it runs and the fewer defaults fall off the end, the more it earns on the same small strip of capital.

01

Technology distribution 101: what a middleman actually does

No jargon — just why brands pay someone to stand in the middle.

A technology brand — say Lenovo — makes laptops. A corner computer shop in Coimbatore, or a system-integrator wiring up an office in Nairobi, wants to sell them. In theory they could deal directly. In practice, the gap between a global factory and a local reseller is enormous, and a distributor exists to close it. Redington performs five distinct jobs that a brand does not want to do market-by-market.

The value Redington adds is not on the product — it's the friction it removes between the two ends: one purchase order instead of thousands, one credit relationship instead of tens of thousands, one delivery network instead of none.

The five jobs, decoded

  • Aggregation & bulk buying — Redington places large orders with brands and breaks them into small lots the channel can absorb. A brand ships to a handful of distributors, not to 70,000 shops.
  • Inventory & warehousing — it holds the stock so the reseller doesn't have to, across 163 warehouses. That is a service (availability) and a risk (things it owns can lose value fast).
  • Credit & financing — this is the underrated one. Redington sells to resellers on credit — effectively lending them the money to stock up, then collecting later. It is part-distributor, part-financier to the channel.
  • Logistics & last mile — physically moving product across borders, customs, and dispersed geographies; its ProConnect arm runs warehousing and delivery.
  • Market coverage & enablement — feet on the street, ~62 sales offices, pre-sales support, and increasingly technical solutioning for complex enterprise and cloud deals.
Why the margin is thin on purpose

A distributor's price is almost entirely dictated at both ends. The brand sets the buy price and enforces it; the channel is fiercely competitive and shops on price. Redington cannot mark product up much without a rival distributor undercutting it or the brand objecting — so it earns a small, contractual margin measured in low single-digit percentages, topped up by volume rebates and incentives from brands for hitting targets. That is the trade: distribution swaps pricing power for certainty of volume. The skill is not in the markup — it is in doing gigantic volume without tying up much capital or eating losses on unsold stock and unpaid resellers.

02

The velocity machine: the real engine

The single most important idea in the whole business — and it isn't margin.

Here is the puzzle a first-timer must resolve. Redington keeps roughly 1.1 paise of net profit on every rupee of goods it sells. That sounds like a terrible business. Yet it earns a ~17% return on equity — better than plenty of high-margin companies. How? The answer is velocity: it recycles a small pool of capital many times a year, and a tiny margin repeated often becomes a healthy return.

A ~1% margin turns into a ~17% return only because the capital behind it is put to work over and over within the year.
The device that explains Redington in one line

Redington's shareholders' equity is around ₹10,000 Cr (book value ~₹130 a share on ~78 crore shares). In FY26 it pushed ₹1.19 lakh crore of other companies' hardware and software across that capital base — spinning its own book value nearly twelve times in a single year. That is the whole trick. A grocery store keeps more of each rupee than Redington does; almost none turns its capital anywhere near twelve times. Slow the conveyor belt down — through bloated inventory, stretched receivables, or a wave of reseller defaults — and the same 1% margin stops compounding into a 17% return. The margin is fixed and small; the speed is the variable that management actually controls.

The three numbers that run the belt

Distribution health lives in the cash conversion cycle — how many days elapse between paying for stock and getting paid for it. It has three moving parts:

  • Inventory days — how long product sits in the warehouse before it sells. Longer = more capital tied up, and more risk that a new model launch makes the old stock worth less.
  • Receivable days — how long resellers take to pay after buying on credit. This is where credit risk lives: a reseller who goes under owes money that may never arrive.
  • Payable days — how long Redington itself takes to pay the brands. The longer it can stretch this (against short inventory and receivables), the less of its own cash it needs.

This is why "working-capital days" is not back-office trivia for Redington; it is the business.

03

The company and who controls it

A Chennai distributor that grew across three continents — and, unusually, has no promoter at all.

Redington's technology-distribution business began in 1993, when R. Srinivasan built it out of the Indian operations of a Singapore-based firm and turned Chennai into the hub of a distribution network that eventually spanned India, the Middle East, Turkey and Africa. It listed in February 2007 and has since become a US$13.5 billion-plus company among the world's top-ten IT distributors.

  • 2000s

  • FY25

  • FY26
    Record ₹1,19,347 Cr revenue (+20%); India crosses ₹63,000 Cr; takes a ₹152 Cr non-cash impairment on Arena (Turkey); cloud/software/services pass 20% of the India book.

Ownership — the genuine surprise

Most large Indian companies have a promoter family or group holding a big anchor stake. Redington does not. After the early founders and private-equity backers exited over the years, the company today has a promoter holding of essentially zero — it is one of the rare large listed Indian firms that is, in effect, entirely publicly and institutionally owned.

  • Foreign institutions (FIIs): ~62% — the single largest ownership block, an unusually high figure that reflects how global investors view the META-plus-India distribution franchise.
  • Domestic institutions (DIIs): ~16% — Indian mutual funds and insurers.
  • Public & others: ~22% — retail and non-institutional holders.
Why "no promoter" cuts both ways: with no controlling family, decisions rest with a professional board and management answerable to institutions — governance by committee rather than by a founder's conviction. Bulls see clean, professional stewardship and no promoter-pledging or related-party risk. Skeptics note there is no deep-pocketed anchor to backstop the company in a crisis, and control is, in principle, contestable. Either way, it is a structural fact that makes Redington read differently from almost every peer on the exchange.

What Redington actually moves

Three engines: mobility (phones), enterprise IT, and a fast-growing cloud/services layer.

"Technology products" is a wide net. Redington's book breaks into three broad buckets, and their mix matters enormously because they carry very different margins and very different growth.

The three engines

  • Mobility — smartphones and related devices, roughly a third of the topline, and dominated by Apple. High-volume, brand-driven, low-margin, but the piece that makes Redington a genuinely huge-revenue company. In FY26 mobility grew ~19% in India as premium smartphones kept selling.
  • IT / enterprise (Technology Solutions) — personal computers, servers, networking, storage, printers and enterprise hardware from HP, Dell, Lenovo, Cisco and others. This is the traditional heart of distribution and includes the emerging AI-server demand that is now a real tailwind.
  • Cloud, software & services — reselling cloud subscriptions (AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud), cybersecurity, software licences and value-added services. Small in rupee terms but the margin future — recurring, less capital-hungry, and stickier than a one-off box sale.
Why the mix, not the revenue headline, is what to watch

A record revenue number is easy for a distributor to print — sell more phones, and the topline balloons even as margin flatlines. What actually changes the company's economics is a shift in the mix toward higher-margin lines. Moving another billion rupees of iPhones adds revenue and almost no margin; moving another billion rupees of cloud subscriptions or cybersecurity solutions adds far more profit per rupee and ties up less capital. So the interesting question for Redington is never "did revenue grow?" — it almost always does — but "did the share of cloud/software/services rise, and did that finally start nudging the blended margin up?"

05

The Apple question

The dependence a generalist should know about — one brand, roughly a third of sales.

Vendor concentration is the defining risk of any distributor, and Redington's is stark. Apple alone accounts for roughly 31% of total revenue — the single largest vendor by a wide margin, and a figure that has crept up as premium iPhone sales in India have boomed. Redington is, in plain fact, one of Apple's largest distributors in India, and a big slice of its entire ₹1.19-lakh-crore machine rides on that one relationship.

A concentration that is both a strength (deep partnership with the most sought-after brand) and a vulnerability (terms, allocations and channel strategy are Apple's to change).

This is the classic distributor's double-edged sword. On one edge, hosting Apple's India volume is a moat of sorts — the scale, credit lines, retail relationships and compliance needed to distribute Apple at this level are not easily assembled, and it makes Redington strategically important to a marquee vendor. On the other edge, the terms of that relationship are Apple's to set: a change in distribution structure, a tighter margin, a shift toward more direct or exclusive channels, or simply softer iPhone demand in a given year, all land disproportionately on a partner this exposed.

The structural fear behind vendor concentration: the ultimate distributor nightmare is disintermediation — a brand deciding it no longer needs a middleman and going direct-to-customer, or narrowing to fewer, captive channels. For most enterprise IT this remains impractical across Redington's sprawling geography. But it is the permanent tail risk that keeps distributors' valuations modest, and Apple's ~31% share is where that risk is most concentrated for Redington.

India vs META — and the Turkey problem

Two halves of the map, one of which quietly caused most of FY26's profit noise.

Redington earns across two big theatres: India (plus South Asia and Singapore), and META — the Middle East, Turkey and Africa, run largely through its overseas arm. Historically the overseas book was the bigger half; in FY26 the two are roughly balanced, with India crossing ₹63,000 Cr and growing ~30% — fast enough that the domestic business is now the larger, faster-moving engine.

Why geography is a swing factor, not a footnote

  • The META geography is a bigger deal than most realise. Redington is the largest IT distributor across the Middle East, Turkey and Africa — a genuinely differentiated position that few Indian companies hold, and one that diversifies it beyond the domestic cycle.
  • But foreign earnings arrive in volatile currencies. Selling across dozens of markets means holding and collecting in many currencies. A distributor's thin margin can be wiped out by an adverse forex move between buying stock in dollars and collecting in a depreciating local currency.
  • Turkey is the sharpest edge of that risk. The Turkish lira has endured years of extreme depreciation and hyperinflation, and Redington's Turkish subsidiary Arena operates squarely inside it.
The Arena story — a surprise buried in the FY26 print

Read the FY26 headline and you see a booming ₹1.19-lakh-crore, +20% company. Read the profit line and it looks like PAT fell — from ₹1,821 Cr to ₹1,284 Cr. Both are true, and the gap is almost entirely Turkey. FY25's profit was flattered by a one-off gain from selling the Turkish fintech Paynet; FY26's profit then absorbed a ~₹152 Cr non-cash impairment on Arena as Turkish conditions deteriorated. Strip Arena out and the underlying business grew handsomely — FY26 revenue up ~24% to ~₹1,12,523 Cr and PAT up ~21% to ~₹1,687 Cr. The reported drop is an accounting shadow cast by one troubled subsidiary, not a deterioration in the core distribution machine — a useful reminder that for a multi-currency distributor, the geography can be a louder swing factor than the operations.

07

Up the value chain: the cloud and services move

The deliberate answer to a structurally capped margin — annuity revenue instead of box-moving.

Management knows the box-moving margin is fixed and small. Its strategy is to migrate a growing share of the business toward lines that pay more per rupee and tie up less capital — above all, cloud distribution. This is the single most important strategic thread in the equity story.

Why cloud distribution is different from selling a laptop

  • It's recurring, not one-off. A laptop sale ends when the box ships. A cloud subscription (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud) bills every month and renews — an annuity that compounds as the customer's usage grows.
  • It's capital-light. There is no physical inventory to warehouse and no obsolescence risk; Redington aggregates cloud consumption for thousands of resellers and takes a slice, without funding a mountain of stock.
  • It carries a better margin and pulls in adjacent, higher-value work — cybersecurity, software licensing, professional services, AI enablement — that a pure hardware distributor never touches.

The honest limit of the re-rating case

The logic is sound, but the scale is the catch. Cloud and services are growing fast off a base that, in rupee terms, is still small against ₹1.19 lakh crore of mostly-hardware revenue. For the mix shift to move the group's ~2% EBITDA margin in a way investors can see, the high-margin lines have to grow for years while hardware keeps swelling the denominator underneath them. That is why the cloud story is best read as a slow, multi-year grind rather than a switch — and why the blended margin, not any single quarter's cloud growth rate, is the number that will decide whether the effort actually re-rates the stock.

08

The industry and the rivals

A scale game with global giants above and scrappy specialists below — and vendors themselves a competitor.

IT distribution is a scale-and-relationships oligopoly: a handful of large distributors handle the bulk of branded technology in any market, because the brands prefer few, deep, well-capitalised partners over many small ones. Redington competes on three fronts at once.

What actually keeps a distributor defensible

With no product margin to hide behind, a distributor's moat is unglamorous but real: the credit lines it can extend, the breadth of its brand roster, the density of its channel, and its working-capital discipline. A new entrant cannot simply undercut its way in — it would need the balance sheet to finance a huge reseller base, the trust of ~440 brands to carry their products, and the logistics to serve dozens of markets, all while surviving on ~2% margins. Redington's edge is not any one product; it is being one of the few players with the scale, credit and geography to be worth a global brand's while — and being the clear leader in the META region that global rivals find hardest to serve.

The structural backdrop: distribution is a low-margin, cyclical business tied to technology-refresh cycles, currency swings and enterprise capex. It rewards operational excellence and punishes working-capital sloppiness. That is precisely why the market caps distributors at low multiples — the sector's economics are understood, and outperformance comes from execution, not from a re-pricing of the product.

How to read a ~14× multiple on a company this size

At ~₹21,900 Cr market cap on ~₹1,284 Cr of reported FY26 profit (~₹1,687 Cr underlying), Redington trades in the low-teens on earnings and about ~2× book — cheap in absolute terms, and cheap for a company compounding revenue at double digits with an ~18% return on capital. It is not mispriced by accident: distributors deserve a modest multiple because the margin is thin, the earnings are cyclical and currency-exposed, and the business is working-capital-intensive. The re-rating case is narrow and specific — it rests almost entirely on whether the cloud/software/services mix can drag the blended EBITDA margin off its ~2% floor. Get that, and a low-teens multiple on a rising margin is a powerful combination; miss it, and the stock stays a cheap, high-turnover proxy for technology consumption across its geographies.

On the balance sheet: Redington carries roughly ₹2,800 Cr of borrowings, but these are overwhelmingly working-capital lines that fund inventory and receivables — the fuel of the conveyor belt — rather than long-term leverage, and they are largely covered by cash. The right lens is not "how much debt" but "how efficiently is the working capital being turned," which the ~26 net working-capital days answer well.

The growth runway

More devices, more enterprise IT, an AI-server surge — and the cloud mix on top.

Redington's growth does not depend on inventing anything; it depends on the sheer, rising volume of technology consumption across a young, digitising India and a broad emerging-market footprint — plus its own climb toward higher-margin lines. Four forces stack up.

The AI kicker, briefly

The AI build-out is not just an enterprise-software story for Redington — it is a hardware story flowing straight through its pipes. AI servers, GPUs and the memory and networking gear around them are big-ticket products that resellers and enterprises buy through distributors. Management has pointed to AI-driven demand (and even AI-driven component price rises) supporting continued 15–20% revenue growth. For a business whose whole model is moving more high-value boxes efficiently, a genuine new hardware cycle is exactly the tailwind it is built to catch.

The next few years are less about a new business and more about the same conveyor belt carrying richer cargo — AI servers on the hardware side, cloud subscriptions on the margin side.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, well-run velocity machine — with a thin margin that leaves little to absorb shocks.

Redington is a competently run, cash-generative, low-margin distributor with a differentiated META footprint, a strengthening India business, and a credible if slow climb toward higher-margin cloud and services. The debate is not whether it can grow revenue — it reliably does — but whether the mix and the discipline can lift returns without a working-capital or currency accident intervening.

The bull case

  • Cheap on cash earnings — low-teens P/E, ~2× book, ~2% dividend yield, for a company compounding revenue at double digits with ~18% ROCE.
  • Velocity economics — ~1% net margin turned into ~17% RoE by spinning capital ~12× a year; tight ~26 working-capital days.
  • Differentiated geography — the largest IT distributor in the Middle East, Turkey and Africa, plus a fast-growing India book crossing ₹63,000 Cr.
  • Mix upgrade underway — cloud/software/services past 20% of India; capital-light, recurring, margin-accretive.
  • AI hardware wave — servers, GPUs and components flowing through the channel it already owns.

The bear case

  • Structurally thin margin — at ~2% EBITDA, any inventory misjudgement, forex swing or reseller default hurts disproportionately.
  • Vendor concentration — Apple ~31% of revenue; terms, allocations and any move toward direct channels are the brand's to dictate.
  • Currency & Turkey — collections across many volatile currencies; the Arena/lira exposure has already dented reported profit.
  • Working-capital & credit risk — a large, thin-margin financing book to a fragmented reseller base; a slip in receivables is costly at this scale.
  • Capped profitability & no promoter anchor — the margin ceiling limits the re-rating, and there is no controlling owner to backstop a shock.
The bottom line

Everything about Redington reduces to one metric: the blended EBITDA margin, and whether the cloud-and-services mix can finally lever it off the ~2% floor where box-moving has pinned it for years. Revenue records and even the AI-server surge are, on their own, just more low-margin volume; the number that changes the company's worth is the profit it keeps per rupee. Watch the FY27 prints for two concrete signals — the share of cloud/software/services climbing well past 20% of India, and a group EBITDA margin that holds above ~2.2%, not just for a quarter but a year — alongside a cleaner Arena/Turkey line. If those bend the right way, a low-teens multiple on a rising margin does the rest; if they don't, Redington remains what it is today: a superbly efficient conveyor belt for the world's technology, priced accordingly.
