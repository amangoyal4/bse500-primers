00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

The Shipping Corporation of India (SCI) is India's largest shipping company — a government-owned operator of merchant ships that carry cargo across the world's oceans. It owns and runs a diversified fleet of roughly sixty vessels (approx.): oil tankers that move crude and refined fuels for India's refiners and importers, dry-bulk carriers hauling coal, iron ore and grain, gas carriers moving LNG and LPG, container/liner ships, and offshore-supply vessels that service oil rigs. It is a Navratna public-sector enterprise, majority-owned (~63.75%) by the Government of India, and it has been the country's designated national carrier — the state's own merchant marine — since the 1960s.

Three facts frame everything about SCI:

  • Its profit is a bet on freight rates it does not control. A shipping company earns freight — the price of moving a tonne of cargo one voyage, or the daily hire of chartering out a whole ship. Those rates are set by the global balance of ships versus cargo and can multiply or collapse within a year on a war, an OPEC decision, a Chinese-demand swing or a canal blockage. SCI's earnings therefore swing violently: FY26 consolidated net profit jumped ~60% to ~₹1,353 Cr on almost flat revenue — because rates, not volumes, did the work. This is a brutally cyclical, commodity-like business.
  • For years, the story was that the government wanted to sell it. In 2019 the Union Cabinet approved the strategic disinvestment of SCI — selling the government's controlling stake to a private buyer. To make it saleable, SCI carved its non-core assets (prime Mumbai real estate, a maritime training institute) into a separate demerged company, SCILAL, which listed in March 2024. The privatisation has been announced, delayed, and re-delayed for years — and, in a genuine turn, mid-2026 reports suggest the Shipping Ministry may now want to reverse that decision and keep SCI as a strategic national carrier.
  • It sits on a strong balance sheet and an ageing fleet. SCI ran FY26 with a record pre-tax profit (~₹1,423 Cr), healthy cash, and effectively net-cash at the group level (approx.). But much of its fleet is old, and the big looming spend is fleet renewal — ordering new tankers, bulkers and gas carriers to replace vessels nearing retirement. The stock trades as a cyclical PSU shipper on a low-teens-or-less earnings multiple (approx.), part freight-cycle bet, part asset-value-and-privatisation option.
In plain terms

SCI is a fleet of floating trucks for the sea, owned by the Indian state. It gets paid to move other people's cargo — oil, coal, gas, boxes — and the fare for that job (the freight rate) is set by a global market that nobody in Mumbai controls. When the world is short of ships, SCI mints money; when ships are plentiful, the same fleet can bleed. Layered on top of that ordinary cyclicality is something unusual: for most of the last five years, the single biggest thing moving the share price was not a freight index but a political decision — whether New Delhi would go through with selling the company. Owning SCI has meant owning a fleet, a real-estate spin-off you already banked, and a privatisation that keeps being promised and postponed.

01

Shipping 101: how an ocean carrier earns its keep

No maritime background needed — just the handful of ideas that drive every shipping company's profit.

A merchant ship is a very expensive machine that does one simple job: carry cargo from port A to port B. The owner spends a fortune to build or buy the ship, then earns money each time it moves goods. The price for that service is the freight rate, and understanding how it behaves is 80% of understanding a shipping company. Six ideas do the work.

The vocabulary that runs the business
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for SCI)
Freight rateThe price of moving cargo. It floats on the global balance of available ships versus cargo needing to move. When ships are scarce it spikes; when there is a glut it sinks. SCI does not set it — the world market does.
Spot / voyage charterHiring a ship for a single trip at today's market rate. Highly volatile — great in a boom, painful in a bust. The part of SCI's fleet on spot is a direct, unhedged bet on this week's rate.
Time charterRenting a ship to one customer for months or years at a fixed daily hire. Steadier, predictable income that rides through a downturn — but caps the upside in a boom. The mix of spot vs time charter is the single most important lever in the whole business.
DWT"Deadweight tonnage" — how much cargo (plus fuel and stores) a ship can carry. The industry's size unit. SCI's fleet is measured in millions of DWT; bigger DWT means more cargo per voyage.
BunkersThe ship's fuel. A huge, swingy operating cost — when oil prices jump, a voyage's fuel bill jumps with it, squeezing margins unless the freight rate compensates.
DrydockingEvery few years a ship must be hauled out for mandatory inspection and repair — weeks of downtime and heavy cost, during which it earns nothing. Older fleets drydock more.
The economics that decide everything: rate versus fixed cost

A ship's costs are mostly fixed — crew, insurance, financing, maintenance — whether it sails full or empty. Its revenue is almost entirely variable, riding the freight rate. That combination is what makes shipping so violently cyclical: above a certain rate, every extra dollar of freight drops almost straight to profit; below it, the ship loses money it cannot avoid. So a modest move in the daily charter rate — say, a tanker earning $30,000 a day instead of $18,000 — is the difference between a bumper year and a loss, on the exact same fleet, crew and voyages. A shipping company's profit is leveraged to a price it neither sets nor can predict.

Why freight rates whipsaw

Freight is a near-perfect commodity market: identical service, priced purely on supply and demand. Both sides are jumpy. Demand for ships tracks global trade — Chinese steel appetite for iron ore, refinery runs for crude, weather for grain. Supply of ships is slow and lumpy: a new tanker takes two-plus years to build, so when rates spike, owners over-order, the ships arrive years later all at once, and the glut crushes rates for years. Layer on shocks — a war that reroutes oil flows, an OPEC cut, sanctions that lengthen voyages, a blocked canal — and rates can double or halve in months. That is the sea SCI sails, literally and financially.

The counter-intuitive bit: a longer, more disrupted voyage can be good for a shipowner. When ships must sail the long way round (say, around the Cape of Good Hope instead of through a troubled canal), the same cargo ties up the ship for more days — which tightens the effective supply of ships and pushes rates up. Disruption that is bad for world trade can be a windfall for the carriers moving it. This is why geopolitical chaos cuts both ways for SCI.
02

The fleet: one company, five kinds of ship

SCI is not one business — it is five shipping businesses under one flag, each with its own cargo, customers and cycle.

Most shipowners specialise — a pure tanker company, a pure bulk company. SCI is unusual in running a mixed fleet spanning almost every merchant-ship type. That diversity is both a strength (it is never fully exposed to one collapsing rate) and a complexity (five cycles, five sets of economics). Here is the whole fleet at a glance, biggest arm first.

Why the mixed fleet matters

The five arms do not move together. When crude-tanker rates are hot, dry-bulk rates may be cold; when both are weak, the gas carriers on long charters keep paying. That internal diversification softens SCI's ride relative to a pure-play owner — but it does not remove the cyclicality, because tankers are so dominant (roughly seven of every ten revenue rupees) that a tanker-rate downturn still drags the whole company. The next five sections take each arm in turn, because you cannot value SCI without knowing which of its cycles is turning.

03

The company, the flag, and who controls it

A 1960s national carrier, a Navratna PSU, still majority-owned by the Government of India.

SCI is not a normal listed company — it is an arm of the Indian state that happens to trade on the exchanges. It was created to give India its own merchant marine so the country would not depend entirely on foreign ships to carry its trade, and that strategic mandate still colours everything: its ownership, its cargo, and its politics.

Timeline

  • 1961

  • 1970s–80s

  • 1992

  • 2008
    Conferred Navratna status — the elite tier of Indian PSUs granted greater operational and investment autonomy.
  • 2019

  • 2021
    SCILAL (SCI Land and Assets Ltd) is incorporated to hive off non-core assets — headquarters real estate, the Maritime Training Institute — a precondition for a clean sale.
  • 2023

  • Mar 2024

  • FY26

  • Jun 2026

Ownership & structure

  • Government of India: holds ~63.75% — the controlling stake earmarked (since 2019) for strategic disinvestment. This single line is the source of both the privatisation upside and the political uncertainty.
  • Public & institutions: the remaining ~36% floats freely, held by retail investors, mutual funds and FIIs who are, in effect, minority passengers alongside the state.
  • The SCILAL split: the demerged land-and-assets company is a separate listed entity that SCI shareholders received one-for-one. When you value SCI today, remember the prime real estate and training institute are no longer inside it — that value has already been carved out into SCILAL.
Why the ownership structure is the story: because the government owns two-thirds, SCI's biggest decisions — whether to sell it, how fast to renew the fleet, how much cargo support it gets — are made in New Delhi, not a boardroom. That is a double edge: it brings strategic cargo and sovereign backing, but it also means a minority shareholder's fortunes hinge on policy choices they cannot influence and whose timing keeps slipping.
04

Tankers: the crude-and-product engine

The largest arm — roughly seven in ten revenue rupees — and the single biggest driver of SCI's profit swings.

If you understand only one part of SCI, make it the tankers. India imports the overwhelming majority of its crude oil, and a lot of that oil arrives on ships. SCI's tanker fleet — split between crude oil tankers (moving unrefined crude to refineries) and product tankers (moving refined diesel, petrol, jet fuel) — is the workhorse that carries this cargo for Indian refiners, oil marketers and importers. When people talk about SCI's earnings booming or busting, they are usually talking about tanker rates.

How a tanker earns

A crude tanker is chartered either spot (one voyage at today's rate) or on time charter (fixed daily hire for a term). Spot rates for large tankers can run from the low thousands of dollars a day in a glut to well over $50,000–100,000 a day in a squeeze — a swing of several-fold on the same steel hull. The daily-earnings measure the industry watches is the time-charter-equivalent (TCE) rate: voyage revenue minus voyage costs (fuel, port charges), expressed per day. TCE versus the ship's fixed daily cost is the entire margin equation.

SCI's tanker earnings therefore move on forces far from Mumbai: OPEC+ production decisions (more oil shipped = more tanker demand), refinery runs, sanctions that reroute Russian and Middle-East barrels onto longer voyages (longer voyages soak up ship supply and lift rates), and the pace of newbuild tanker deliveries. In Q4 FY26, SCI's tanker revenue rose ~16% year-on-year to ~₹1,074 Cr — the arm doing the heavy lifting in a strong-rate environment.

The single lever: spot exposure versus locked-in charters

Two SCI tankers can be physically identical yet earn wildly differently, because one is on the spot market catching today's rate and the other is locked on a multi-year charter at yesterday's. In a rate boom, the spot ship is a lottery win and the chartered ship looks under-priced; in a bust, the chartered ship is a lifeline and the spot ship bleeds. Management's choice of how much of the tanker fleet to leave on spot is, in plain terms, how much of the company they are betting on the freight casino each year. That mix — not the number of ships — is what turns a flat-revenue year into a +60% profit year, as FY26 showed.

The India angle: SCI is not just a rate-taker on the open sea — it carries strategic and government-linked cargo, including crude for public-sector refiners and support for India's energy-security needs. That gives its tanker arm a base of relatively captive demand that a purely commercial owner lacks. It is one of the concrete ways the "national carrier" mandate shows up in the numbers.
05

Dry bulk: coal, ore and grain

The commodity-hauling arm — smaller than tankers, but arguably the most violently cyclical of all.

Dry-bulk carriers are the open-hold workhorses of the sea: giant floating boxes that carry loose, un-packaged commodities — coal, iron ore, grain, bauxite, fertiliser — poured straight into the hull. SCI's bulk fleet moves these cargoes for power utilities, steelmakers, traders and the government. It is a smaller slice of revenue than tankers, but it earns its keep in a different, and often even more volatile, cycle.

Dry-bulk freight is the market most directly tied to China. Chinese steel mills devour imported iron ore and coking coal; when Chinese construction and industry run hot, bulk rates soar, and when China slows or stockpiles, they slump. The benchmark most watched is the Baltic Dry Index, which has historically swung by multiples within a single year. SCI's bulk arm rides that index. In Q4 FY26, SCI's bulk-carrier revenue more than doubled year-on-year to ~₹218 Cr — a vivid illustration of how fast this segment can turn.

Why bulk is the sharpest cycle

Dry-bulk ships are cheaper and quicker to build than tankers, so supply responds faster to a rate spike — which means gluts form faster and rates crash harder. And demand is concentrated in a handful of commodity flows dominated by one buyer (China), so a single country's slowdown can gut the market. For SCI, the bulk arm is the high-beta corner of the fleet: it can deliver an outsized swing like the Q4 FY26 doubling, but it can just as quickly reverse. It is the part of the company that most rewards catching the cycle right — and most punishes catching it wrong.

06

Gas & LNG-LPG carriers: the steadier river

The quieter arm that behaves least like the rest — because it is largely locked into long contracts.

Gas carriers are specialised, expensive ships that move liquefied natural gas (LNG) and liquefied petroleum gas (LPG) — fuels kept liquid under deep refrigeration or pressure. These are technically demanding vessels, and crucially, they are often deployed on long-term time charters lasting many years. That single feature makes SCI's gas arm behave very differently from its tankers and bulkers.

Because a gas carrier is frequently signed to a multi-year charter at a fixed daily rate, its income is steady and visible — it keeps paying whether the spot market is booming or busted. For a company whose tanker and bulk earnings whipsaw with the cycle, this is ballast: a base of predictable revenue that does not evaporate in a downturn. SCI has participated in India's growing LNG-import logistics (India is building out gas infrastructure as a cleaner transition fuel), and its gas ships tie into that long-horizon demand.

Why the steadiness is worth paying for

In shipping, predictability is scarce and therefore valuable. A ship on a ten-year charter to a creditworthy energy buyer is closer to an infrastructure asset than a commodity trade — you can see the cash flows coming for years. The trade-off is symmetrical: that same locked-in ship cannot cash in when spot rates explode. So the gas arm gives up lottery upside in exchange for downside protection. In a portfolio of five shipping cycles, it is the segment doing the least dramatic thing — and that is precisely the point. It is the part of SCI a lender or a cautious buyer values most, because it pays through the storm.

Reading it right: the exact size of SCI's gas fleet and how much of it sits on long charters shifts over time and is reported within the broader tanker/bulk segment lines rather than broken out cleanly, so treat any single figure as approx. The structural point holds regardless: long-charter gas tonnage is the calm counterweight to the volatile spot exposure elsewhere in the fleet.
07

Liner, container & offshore: the smaller arms

The scheduled-service and rig-support businesses — plus the fee income from managing other people's ships.

Beyond the big bulk-liquid arms, SCI runs two smaller but distinct businesses. The liner / container arm operates scheduled cargo services — ships that sail fixed routes on a timetable, carrying containers and break-bulk cargo, including on coastal and strategic routes (such as services to India's island territories that a purely commercial operator might not run). The offshore arm operates offshore-supply vessels (OSVs) — the smaller workboats that ferry crew, equipment and supplies out to oil-and-gas rigs, chartered to explorers such as ONGC.

Alongside the ships it owns, SCI also earns technical and ship-management fees — it manages vessels on behalf of other owners and government bodies, and runs training and consultancy. This is fee-for-service income that is less rate-sensitive than owning and trading ships: you earn a management fee whether freight rates are high or low. It is a small line, but it is the most annuity-like, least cyclical revenue SCI has.

Why these arms exist at all

Some of SCI's smaller services are commercial; others are strategic obligations of being the national carrier — coastal and island routes, rig support for state energy firms, cargo the government wants an Indian flag to carry. A private tanker owner would simply not run a loss-making island liner service. SCI does, because part of its reason to exist is national capability, not just profit. For an investor that is a genuine tension: it means a slice of the fleet is deployed for policy reasons rather than the best available return — a cost of the PSU mandate that a future private owner might well trim.

08

The freight-rate cycle: the force that owns the earnings

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — and the reason SCI's profit is a bet it does not place itself.

Everything so far has circled one truth: SCI's earnings are governed less by how many ships it runs than by the freight rate those ships happen to catch — a price set by a global market that no shipowner controls. This section makes that concrete, because it is what makes SCI so different from an ordinary industrial company, where output and price are largely in management's hands.

Consider FY26. SCI's consolidated revenue rose only ~3% to ~₹5,780 Cr — barely moving. Yet net profit jumped ~60% to ~₹1,353 Cr, and pre-tax profit hit a record ~₹1,423 Cr. The fleet did not grow 60%. Volumes did not grow 60%. What changed was the rate environment: firmer tanker and bulk markets meant each voyage earned more above the ship's fixed cost, and because those costs barely move, the extra freight fell almost straight through to profit. That is operating leverage in its rawest form.

The device that only fits SCI: the spot-versus-charter dial, times five cycles

Here is the frame that makes SCI legible and would fit almost no other company in the index. SCI is not one bet — it is five semi-independent freight bets (tanker, bulk, gas, liner, offshore) running at once, and on each one management turns a dial between spot (catch today's volatile rate) and time charter (lock in a fixed rate for years). Turn every dial to spot and SCI becomes a pure, geared wager on this year's ocean freight markets — spectacular in a boom, brutal in a bust. Turn them to charter and it becomes a quasi-utility with capped upside. The company's profit in any year is the sum of where those five dials sat and where the five rates landed — almost none of which SCI controls. You are not buying a fleet so much as a portfolio of rate exposures, managed by how much of each cycle is left uncovered. That is why FY26's flat revenue could still throw off a record profit, and why next year's number is genuinely hard to forecast.

A genuine surprise: for a decade, the biggest single driver of SCI's share price was arguably not any freight index at all — it was a government decision about whether to sell the company, and a real-estate demerger that carved prime Mumbai land and a training institute out of a "shipping" stock. Investors spent years trading SCI partly as a privatisation-and-property event, not a pure freight play. The ships were almost the sideshow. The next section is about that other engine.
09

The disinvestment saga and the demerger

The catalyst that has driven the stock for years — announced, delayed, re-delayed, and now possibly reversed.

Running alongside the freight cycle is a second, entirely political engine: the government's plan to privatise SCI. This has been the recurring re-rating story — and the recurring disappointment — for half a decade. It comes in two moving parts: the disinvestment (selling the government's stake) and the demerger (cleaning up the company first so it can be sold).

Part one — the disinvestment

In 2019, the Union Cabinet approved the strategic disinvestment of SCI: selling the government's ~63.75% controlling stake, along with management control, to a private buyer. For investors, this dangled a classic privatisation upside — a new private owner might run the fleet more efficiently, and the sale process itself often surfaces a control premium. But the process has slipped repeatedly: valuations, the demerger precondition, market conditions and shifting government priorities have pushed the timeline back year after year. Every time it looks imminent, it stalls again.

Part two — the demerger (SCILAL)

A prospective private buyer wanted the ships, not SCI's incidental assets — prime Mumbai real estate (including the "Shipping House" head office) and a Maritime Training Institute. So before any sale, SCI carved these non-core assets — valued at ~₹2,392 Cr — into a separate company, SCI Land and Assets Ltd (SCILAL), incorporated in 2021. The demerger scheme became effective in 2023, and in March 2024 SCILAL listed separately, with SCI shareholders receiving one SCILAL share for each SCI share. That cleared the last big structural hurdle: SCI is now a cleaner, ships-focused company ready to be sold, with the property value already handed to shareholders in a separate stock.

The 2026 twist: in mid-2026, reports emerged that the Shipping Ministry may seek to reverse the 2019 Cabinet approval and retain SCI as a strategic national carrier — part of a broader push to build up Indian-flagged shipping for energy security and trade resilience. If that holds, the entire premise of the last five years — that you were buying a soon-to-be-privatised company — could invert: SCI stays a PSU. This is a live, unresolved policy question; treat the direction as uncertain, not decided.
What you are actually buying

Strip it back and SCI has been three things bundled into one ticker: a working mixed fleet earning freight, a privatisation possibility that could re-rate the shares if a sale finally happens (or deflate the premium if it is called off), and the leftover land that was already spun out into SCILAL and handed to you. The freight cycle you can at least analyse. The privatisation is a binary political event whose timing has repeatedly slipped and whose direction may now be turning. Anyone owning SCI for the disinvestment catalyst is, in effect, holding a position whose payoff depends on a decision made in a ministry — the hardest kind of catalyst to underwrite, because it answers to politics, not fundamentals.

Two structural points shape the valuation. First, the balance sheet is strong: SCI generates real cash at the top of the cycle and ran effectively net-cash in FY26 (approx.), which matters because the looming fleet-renewal capex — ordering new tankers, bulkers and gas carriers to replace an ageing fleet — is a large future call on that cash. A shipper that can self-fund renewal without drowning in debt is far better placed than one that cannot. Second, a chunk of SCI's worth is asset value: the fleet has a market value (ships can be sold second-hand), which puts a floor of sorts under the stock and is part of why it often trades near or not far above book. As a cyclical PSU shipper, SCI typically carries a modest earnings multiple (a low-teens-or-less P/E and roughly around book value — both approx. and moving with the share price and the cycle), precisely because the market knows peak-cycle profit does not repeat every year.

The one number that settles the thesis

SCI hangs on a metric with two halves. The first half is the through-cycle blended charter/freight realisation — the daily TCE its fleet actually earns, averaged across good years and bad, against its fixed operating cost. That single figure determines whether the fleet is a decent business or a value trap, and it is the number to track quarter after quarter (peak years like FY26 flatter it; a full cycle reveals the truth). The second half is the disinvestment outcome — a binary policy switch that either re-rates the shares on a sale or deflates the premium if the 2026 reversal talk hardens into a decision to keep SCI. Sitting behind both is fleet-renewal execution (does SCI order the right ships at the right point in the cycle without wrecking the net-cash balance sheet?). Freight realisation tells you what the fleet is worth; the disinvestment decision tells you whether there is a premium on top. You need a read on both — the analysable and the political — because SCI is genuinely priced as the sum of the two.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A strong fleet at a cyclical peak, a clean balance sheet, an ageing hull, and a privatisation whose direction is suddenly in question.

SCI enters the period ahead in unusually good financial shape — a record profit banked, effectively net-cash, and a diversified fleet — but facing three unresolved questions at once: where the freight cycle goes from here, whether it renews an ageing fleet well, and what New Delhi finally decides to do with it. Being clear-eyed about all three is the only way to hold this stock honestly.

The bull case

  • Strategic national asset — India's largest carrier with government cargo support and a maritime-security mandate that may now protect rather than sell it.
  • Freight-cycle leverage — a firm tanker/bulk rate environment can multiply profit on a fixed cost base, as FY26's +60% on flat revenue showed.
  • Clean balance sheet — effectively net-cash (approx.), able to fund fleet renewal internally without drowning in debt.
  • Diversified fleet — five ship types spread the cyclical risk; long-charter gas tonnage provides ballast through downturns.
  • Optionality — a completed demerger and an unresolved privatisation still leave a genuine re-rating path if a sale (or a strong policy backing) crystallises.

The bear case

  • Extreme cyclicality — freight rates can collapse as fast as they rose; FY26's record profit is a peak, not a run-rate, and a down-cycle can push a shipper into losses.
  • Disinvestment limbo — the privatisation has slipped for years and may now be reversed; a catalyst that keeps deferring can deflate the premium built into the price.
  • Ageing fleet & capex — old ships mean rising drydock costs and a large fleet-renewal bill ahead; mistime the newbuild cycle and returns suffer.
  • PSU constraints — government ownership brings slower decisions, non-commercial obligations (island/coastal routes) and dependence on policy choices minorities cannot influence.
  • Bunker & geopolitics — fuel-cost swings squeeze margins; Red Sea/canal disruptions and sanctions cut both ways and are impossible to forecast.
The bottom line

SCI is the Indian state's fleet on the world's oceans — a genuinely diversified carrier that just banked a record year because freight rates, not its own volumes, did the heavy lifting. That is the whole nature of the thing: a fixed cost base geared to a price the company cannot set, wrapped inside a government that keeps changing its mind about whether to own it. The freight cycle you can at least study; the two things that will actually decide the next few years are harder and more specific. Watch the ministry's ruling on disinvestment — a sale re-rates the shares, a formal decision to keep SCI recasts it as a permanent PSU — and watch the first big fleet-renewal orders: whether SCI commits its net-cash to new tankers, bulkers and gas carriers at a sensible point in the cycle, or over-orders into a peak the way the whole industry habitually does. Those two decisions, both made largely in Delhi and not on a freight screen, will tell you far more about SCI's next decade than any single quarter's rate print.
