00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Cholamandalam Investment and Finance (everyone calls it Chola) is a non-banking financial company (NBFC) — a lender that is not a bank — and it is the flagship financial-services company of the Murugappa Group, one of India's oldest and most respected business houses. For most of its life Chola has been, at heart, a vehicle financier: it lends money to people buying trucks, used commercial vehicles, tractors, cars and construction equipment — very often to first-time or thin-file borrowers in rural and small-town India whom banks find too small and too hard to assess. With an AUM of ₹2.43 lakh crore (March 2026) and a network of ~1,750+ branches — roughly 80% of them in rural and semi-urban areas — it is one of India's top three vehicle financiers and a top-tier diversified retail NBFC.

Three facts frame everything about Chola:

  • The moat is the branch-and-underwriting franchise, not just the money. Chola's real edge is distribution and know-how: decades of feet-on-the-ground presence in tier-3/4 towns, and the underwriting muscle to lend against used trucks and tractors to borrowers with no formal credit history — and still get paid back. That is genuinely hard to replicate, and it lets Chola earn a high yield (~15%) on a book others can't safely serve.
  • It is diversifying — deliberately and fast. Vehicle finance is now under half of the book. Chola has spent the last few years building new engines — loans-against-property (LAP), home loans, SME loans, and two newer arms, Consumer & Small Enterprise Loans (CSEL) and Secured Business & Personal Loans (SBPL). Several of these grew 25–46% in FY26. The prize is faster growth; the risk is scaling new (partly unsecured) books before they've been tested.
  • Fast, high-return growth — priced richly. FY26 profit rose ~23% to ₹5,233 crore, ROE was a strong ~19.7% (Q4 hit ~23%), and asset quality improved through the year (Stage-3 3.05%, down from 3.36% in Dec). For that quality-plus-growth combination the market pays a rich ~5× book. The whole investment question is whether Chola can keep compounding at 20%+ with clean books — and whether that premium is deserved.
In plain terms

Think of Chola as the finance company that shows up where the banks don't. A farmer in interior Tamil Nadu buying a second-hand tractor, or a small operator in a district town buying his first used truck to start a haulage business, often can't get a bank loan — no salary slip, no credit score, an asset the bank doesn't understand how to value. Chola's people do understand it: they've financed these vehicles for 30+ years, they know what a used truck is worth and what the borrower earns from it, and they have a branch a short ride away. That local knowledge lets Chola charge a healthy rate and keep losses manageable. The next chapter of the story is Chola using those same branches and that same trust to sell home loans, business loans and consumer credit — turning a truck-financier into a broad rural-and-semi-urban lending machine. The catch, as ever in lending, is doing that quickly without the new loans quietly going bad.

01

How an NBFC earns: the spread business, in six numbers

No finance degree required — just the handful of terms that drive everything.

A lender is, at its core, a spread business: it raises money at one cost and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap between the two — after the loans that go bad, and the cost of running the operation — is the profit. Six terms tell you almost the whole Chola story.

The only vocabulary you need (with Chola's FY26 numbers)

The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (Lending yield − Cost of funds) × Book − Bad loans − Operating costs − Tax. Every lever in that line is a strategy. You can widen the spread by lending to riskier borrowers at higher rates — but then bad loans rise. Chola sits at the higher-yield end of the quality spectrum: it earns a fat ~8% margin by serving borrowers a bank can't, and accepts a higher credit cost (~1.5–1.6%) than a prime lender's. The result is a strong ~3.5% return on assets and ~19.7% return on equity — returns that come from a wide spread, earned by reaching customers others can't, rather than from squeezing the safest borrowers. Its whole art is keeping that higher credit cost predictable through the cycle.

Why an NBFC is not a bank — and why that matters

  • A bank can take your savings deposit; an NBFC like Chola cannot. A bank funds itself with near-free current/savings (CASA) deposits. Chola must borrow almost all its money — from banks, the bond market (debentures), securitisation and other wholesale sources. Its cost of funds is therefore structurally higher than a bank's, which is the permanent handicap every NBFC lives with.
  • So NBFCs earn their keep where banks won't go. Because an NBFC can't out-fund a bank, it must out-reach and out-underwrite it — serving borrowers, assets and geographies banks find too small, too informal or too risky. Chola is the textbook example: used-vehicle and tractor finance for thin-file borrowers in small-town India is precisely the credit gap banks leave open.
  • The higher yield is compensation for that harder job. Chola charges ~15% not because it is gouging, but because these are genuinely higher-risk, higher-cost-to-serve loans. The whole business is the discipline of pricing that risk correctly and collecting relentlessly — which is the next section.
02

The vehicle-finance moat: branches, feet and 40 years of underwriting

The single most important idea in the entire Chola story — and the one hardest for a rival to copy.

For a prime lender, the moat is cheap money. For Chola, the moat is something harder to buy: a deep physical distribution network in small-town India, plus four decades of hard-won skill at underwriting used and commercial vehicles for borrowers no bank will touch. Understand this and you understand why Chola earns a wide spread that others cannot safely capture.

Why lending to a small-town truck buyer is so hard — and so profitable

Picture the customer. A man in a district town wants to buy a five-year-old used truck to start a goods-carrying business. He has no salary slip, no formal credit history, and often no bank account that tells a useful story. The asset — a used commercial vehicle — is hard to value and easy for a distant lender to misjudge. To a big-city bank, this is an un-lendable customer: the loan is small, the paperwork is thin, and the risk looks unknowable. So the bank passes. That's the gap Chola was built to fill — and filling it well requires three things a bank simply doesn't have:

The moat isn't any one step — it's that a rival has to build all four at once: the branch network, the trained underwriters, the collection machinery, and the decades of loss data that make the pricing right. That takes years and money, not a product launch.

Why this is genuinely hard to replicate

  • The branch network is a decades-long, expensive build. You cannot conjure ~1,750 branches in tier-3/4 towns overnight, or the local staff who know the region. Every branch is a fixed cost that only pays off once it has enough loans on the books — a chicken-and-egg problem Chola solved slowly over 40 years.
  • Underwriting used/commercial vehicles is a craft, not an algorithm. Valuing a used truck, judging whether a first-time operator will actually earn enough to repay, knowing which routes and freight markets are healthy — this is tacit, local knowledge baked into experienced field staff and years of loss data. A fintech with a slick app can't shortcut it; lending to this segment without the craft is how NBFCs blow up.
  • Collections are a physical, relationship business. When a small-town borrower falls behind, recovery isn't a call-centre script — it's a local officer who knows the customer, understands why the payment slipped (a bad monsoon, a broken axle), and can restructure or, in the worst case, repossess the vehicle. That on-ground presence is why Chola's losses on a "risky" book stay contained.
  • Trust and the Murugappa name lower acquisition cost. In small-town India, a customer choosing between an unknown lender and "Chola" — a Murugappa company that's been around for decades — often picks Chola. That trust brings customers in the door cheaply and improves the quality of who walks in.

Why "used vehicles" is the jewel in the crown

New-vehicle finance is competitive — banks and captive financiers (like a carmaker's own arm) fight over it, so margins are thin. Used-vehicle finance is different. The asset is hard to value, the borrower is often informal, and the loan is small — exactly the friction that keeps banks out and lets a specialist earn a fat spread. Chola has been doing this for 40 years; it has the loss data, the local valuers and the collection network to price and recover these loans. That is why used vehicles (~27% of the vehicle book) are both the highest-yielding and the most defensible part of Chola — the core of the core.

The honest caveat: a distribution-and-underwriting moat is powerful but not impregnable. Shriram Finance is the giant of used-vehicle lending and Bajaj, Mahindra Finance, Sundaram Finance and others fight hard in commercial vehicles and tractors. Chola's edge is being among the very best at a genuinely hard craft, with a first-rate branch network — not a monopoly on it. The moat is real; it is a "hard-to-join club" advantage, not a fence no one can cross.
03

The company and the Murugappa Group

From a 1978 equipment-financier to a ₹2.4-lakh-crore diversified retail NBFC.

Chola was incorporated in 1978 in Chennai by the Murugappa Group — one of India's oldest and most conservatively-run business houses (founded 1900, with interests spanning abrasives, fertilisers, cycles, engineering and finance). It began financing equipment and commercial vehicles for small operators, pivoted decisively into retail vehicle finance in the early 1990s, and over the last decade has broadened into a diversified retail lender.

Timeline

  • 1978
    Incorporated in Chennai by the Murugappa Group to serve the credit gap for equipment and commercial vehicles — built from the start on conservative, relationship-based, collateralised lending to small operators.
  • 1991–92
    Pivots decisively into retail vehicle finance — the business that becomes its identity. Expands the used-vehicle, LCV/HCV, tractor and construction-equipment franchise across South and West India.
  • 2007–10
    Enters home loans and loans-against-property; a brief, ill-fated personal-loans/JV foray in the 2008 crisis is exited — teaching a lasting lesson about scaling unsecured credit carelessly.
  • 2010s
    Rebuilds around secured retail — vehicle finance + LAP + home loans — and compounds the book steadily to become a top-3 vehicle financier, with a branch network pushing deep into rural and semi-urban India.
  • 2022
    Launches the new growth engines — Consumer & Small Enterprise Loans (CSEL), Secured Business & Personal Loans (SBPL) and a dedicated SME arm — to diversify beyond vehicles and lift growth.
  • FY26
    AUM crosses ₹2.43 lakh Cr (+21%); PAT ₹5,233 Cr (+23%); ROE ~19.7%. Vehicle finance is now under half the book; the new engines (SME +41%, SBPL +46%) scale fast while credit costs are managed with a ₹200 Cr precautionary overlay.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter — the Murugappa Group. The group holds roughly 49–50% of Chola, largely through the listed holding company Cholamandalam Financial Holdings (CFHL) and Murugappa family entities. That is a genuine, engaged promoter with skin in the game — but note the balance (~50%+) is a real public float of institutions and retail, unlike the tightly-held stocks with a token free float.
  • Governance pedigree. The Murugappa Group is known across corporate India for conservative, professional, long-term management and clean governance — a reputation that matters enormously for a lender, because it lowers the cost of borrowing and reassures the investors, depositors and rating agencies who fund the book.
  • The CFHL holding structure. Public investors can own Chola directly (CHOLAFIN) or via the holding company CFHL, which owns a stake in Chola plus the group's general-insurance JV (Chola MS). The two report side by side each quarter — useful to know so you don't confuse CFHL's consolidated numbers with Chola's standalone lending results.
  • Capital position. Chola is well-capitalised — CRAR ~19.21% with Tier-1 ~14.73% (March 2026), comfortably above the regulatory minimum — giving ample room to keep growing the book without an urgent capital raise.

Why the segment mix is the whole strategy

Read the book as a barbell. On one end sit the big, secured, proven engines — Vehicle Finance, LAP and Home Loans — which are ~90%+ of the book, secured against real assets, and carry Chola's through-cycle track record. On the other end sit the newer, faster, riskier engines — SME, CSEL, SBPL — small today but growing 40%+, some unsecured, and not yet tested by a downturn. Chola's game is to let the proven core provide the stability and the funding relationships, while the new engines add the growth that keeps a 20%+ AUM CAGR alive. The tell that management is being careful: when CSEL (the unsecured piece) started showing stress, they throttled it back to +4% rather than chasing growth — exactly the discipline the next sections probe.

05

The new growth engines: diversifying beyond the truck

How Chola is broadening its book — and the execution risk of scaling new lines fast.

A vehicle-finance business, however good, has a ceiling: it grows roughly with vehicle sales, which are cyclical and tied to freight, farm income and the economy. To sustain the 20%+ growth the market prizes, Chola has spent the last few years bolting new lending lines onto its existing distribution — using the same branches, the same brand, and the same rural-and-semi-urban customer base to sell more than just vehicle loans.

The four new levers

  • Loan Against Property (LAP) — the most successful diversification. Chola lends to small businesses and self-employed borrowers against their property. It is secured (so lower-risk), higher-yielding than a home loan, and taps a huge under-served market. It has grown into a ₹52,000-crore-plus second pillar and, in FY26, the fastest-growing profit contributor.
  • Home Loans — affordable and self-employed housing finance in the same geographies. Lower-yield, but sticky, secured and low-loss — the "ballast" segment that improves the book's quality mix as it grows (+23% in FY26).
  • SME Loans — dedicated small-business lending (working capital, term loans), growing ~41% off a small base. India's SME credit gap is enormous; this is Chola's bid to be a broad small-business lender, not just a vehicle one.
  • CSEL & SBPL — the newest, most digital arms. CSEL (Consumer & Small Enterprise Loans) is largely unsecured, originated through digital channels and partnerships; SBPL (Secured Business & Personal Loans) is its secured cousin. These are the highest-yield, highest-risk pieces — and the ones that demand the most caution.

Why bolting-on works — and where it can bite

Why it works: the expensive part of a lending business — the branches, the brand, the customer relationships — is already built and paid for. Selling a home loan or a business loan to a customer who's already in a Chola branch for a truck loan is cheap incremental growth: the acquisition cost is near-zero and you already know the customer. That "cross-sell on existing rails" is the single best way an NBFC can grow without stretching.

Where it bites: new products are new risk. Underwriting a truck loan is a craft Chola has honed for 40 years; underwriting an unsecured consumer loan or a fresh SME book is a different skill, learned in real time — and the losses show up only after 12–24 months, by which point you've written two more years of loans. This is exactly why the industry-wide unsecured blow-up of 2024–25 is a warning: growing a new unsecured book at 40%+ feels great until the seasoning arrives. Chola's decision to slow CSEL to +4% suggests it saw the risk and pulled back — but the execution risk of scaling new books fast is the central worry on the growth side of the story.

The lesson from history: Chola has been here before. A personal-loans push around 2007–08 went badly and had to be exited, teaching the group a hard lesson about scaling unsecured credit into a downturn. The current caution on CSEL — slowing it deliberately, keeping the new engines mostly secured (SBPL, SME, LAP) — reads like a company that remembers that scar. Watch whether that discipline holds if growth elsewhere slows.
06

The two swing factors: cost of funds vs credit cost

The classic NBFC tension — and Chola's through-cycle record on both.

Strip a lender down to its essence and its profit swings on two numbers: how cheaply it can borrow (cost of funds), and how many loans go bad (credit cost). Everything else — yield, fees, operating costs — moves slowly. These two move with the cycle, and they can move against you at the worst possible time. How an NBFC manages this tension through good years and bad is the truest test of its quality.

Swing factor #1 — cost of funds

Chola borrows almost everything it lends — from banks, debentures (bonds), securitisation and other wholesale sources. Its cost of funds is therefore set partly by its own credit rating (Chola is a top-rated NBFC, helped by the Murugappa name) and partly by where interest rates are in the cycle. When the RBI is cutting rates, Chola's borrowing cost falls and — because its lending rates re-price more slowly — its margin expands. That tailwind was real in FY26: as rates softened, Chola's cost of funds eased and NIM rose ~40 bps to ~7.9–8.0%. When rates rise, the opposite happens, and the margin compresses until lending rates catch up. A high credit rating is the shock-absorber: it keeps Chola's funding available and relatively cheap even when the market tightens — which is when weaker NBFCs get shut out entirely.

Swing factor #2 — credit cost

Chola lends to higher-risk borrowers, so it expects a meaningful chunk of loans to go bad — a credit cost of ~1.5–1.6% of the book each year. The danger isn't that credit cost exists; it's that it can spike when the economy turns, a monsoon fails, freight rates collapse, or an unsecured book seasons badly. A vehicle-finance NBFC is especially exposed to rural income and freight cycles: when farmers and truckers earn less, they fall behind on EMIs. The whole art is keeping credit cost predictable and provisioned-for, not letting it surprise you.

Chola's through-cycle record — the reassurance and the caveat

The reassurance: Chola has navigated multiple downturns as a large lender — the 2008 crisis, the 2018–20 NBFC liquidity crunch (which killed weaker rivals), demonetisation, and COVID — and come through each with its funding intact and credit costs that spiked but then normalised. That is a genuine track record: a lender that has repeatedly kept the taps open and the losses contained when the sector was in crisis. The Murugappa governance and top credit rating are a big part of why funding never dried up. The caveat: FY26 is a benign year — falling rates, easing credit costs, both swing factors favourable. The real test of any lender is the bad year, not the good one, and the newer unsecured/SME books have never seen a serious downturn. A cheap-funding, low-loss year flatters every NBFC; the quality shows in the year both swing factors turn against you.

The number to watch each quarter: not the AUM growth (which will impress) but the credit-cost trend and the cost-of-funds trajectory. If Chola keeps growing ~20% while credit cost stays near ~1.5% and cost of funds holds or falls, the model is humming. If credit cost creeps up — especially in CSEL/SME — while growth stays high, that's the early warning that the new books are being grown by loosening standards. The ₹200 Cr precautionary overlay Chola set aside in FY26 is management flagging that it, too, is watching the macro clouds.

Asset quality and the cycle

Reading the bad-loan numbers — and why two different "bad loan" figures exist.

For a lender, asset quality is the story — a fast-growing book means nothing if the loans quietly go bad. Chola's numbers improved through FY26, but there's a wrinkle worth understanding: there are two different "bad loan" measures, and they look very different.

Why there are two "bad loan" numbers — and it's not a red flag

Chola's GNPA (4.36%) looks scarier than its Stage-3 (3.05%) — but they're measuring the same loans with two different rulers. "Stage-3" is the international accounting standard (Ind-AS): a loan is Stage-3 once it's 90+ days overdue. The "RBI GNPA" is the regulator's stricter definition — it tags overdues daily and has tougher rules about when a recovered loan can be marked healthy again. Since 2021, the RBI has required NBFCs to report both, and the RBI number is almost always higher. For a vehicle-finance NBFC — where borrowers routinely pay a few days or weeks late because farm and freight income is lumpy, then catch up — the gap is normal and expected. The number that reflects real, provisioned risk is the net figure and the credit cost; the headline gross numbers just depend on which ruler you use.

The encouraging signal in FY26 is direction: Stage-3 improved sequentially (3.36% → 3.05%), credit costs eased ~20 bps (before overlay), and management still chose to top up provisions with a ₹200 Cr overlay — the posture of a lender getting more conservative even as its numbers improve. The worry, as always, lives in the newer books: CSEL's loan losses ran ~5.2% in Q4 — far above the group average — which is precisely why Chola slowed that segment. Vehicle-finance losses ticked up modestly (to ~1.6% from ~1.2% a year earlier), a reminder that even the core is cyclical.

The context that matters: a ~3% Stage-3 on a high-yield, thin-file, rural book is normal and priced-for — it is not comparable to a prime bank's sub-1% number. The right lens isn't "Chola's NPAs are higher than HDFC Bank's" (of course they are — different customers); it's "does the ~8% NIM more than pay for a ~1.5% credit cost, through the cycle?" So far, emphatically yes — but that verdict was earned mostly in good years and one COVID stress, not a prolonged rural downturn.

A crowded field of vehicle financiers, diversified NBFCs, and banks pressing in.

Chola competes on several fronts at once: against specialist vehicle financiers for the truck-and-tractor customer, against diversified retail NBFCs for LAP, home loans and consumer credit, and against banks increasingly chasing the same borrowers. Its position is "top-tier vehicle financier, rapidly diversifying" — differentiated by its distribution reach and underwriting craft rather than by the lowest funding cost.

The competitive tension in one line

Chola is squeezed from two directions, like most NBFCs: banks above it, with cheaper deposit funding, chasing the same borrowers wherever the roads are paved; and specialist rivals beside it (Shriram, Sundaram, M&M Finance) who know the vehicle-finance craft just as well. Its answer is to win where the banks won't go and out-execute the specialists — the deepest, best-run branch network in rural and semi-urban India, paired with clean Murugappa governance and a top credit rating. That is a defensible middle ground, not a monopoly: Chola must keep out-executing a dozen well-funded rivals to keep taking share. The clearest peers to benchmark it against are Shriram (cheaper, higher-yield, riskier) below, and Bajaj Finance (pricier, higher-return) above — which is exactly why the valuation debate is framed against both.

Reading the returns — why they look so good

Chola's ~19.7% ROE and ~3.5% ROA are genuinely strong, and the FY26 improvement came from a happy alignment of three things: (1) NIM expansion — falling interest rates cut the cost of funds while lending yields held, widening the spread ~40 bps; (2) easing credit costs — asset quality improved, so less profit leaked to provisions; and (3) operating leverage — a bigger book spread over a largely-fixed branch cost base. All three tailwinds blew the right way at once. That is the model working beautifully in a benign year. The honest reader's job is to remember that the same three levers can reverse: if rates rise, credit costs spike, and growth slows, ROE compresses just as cleanly as it expanded. The strength of FY26 is real; the question is its durability.

10

The valuation debate: a premium price for a premium franchise

For an NBFC, price-to-book is the master lens — and Chola's is rich.

Valuing a lender is different from valuing a normal company. Because an NBFC is essentially a pile of loans funded by a pile of borrowings, the key metric is price-to-book (P/B) — how much you pay for each rupee of the lender's net worth (equity). A high P/B says the market expects that equity to earn high, growing returns (high ROE) for years. Chola trades at a rich ~5× book — well above most NBFCs — and the entire bull/bear argument is about whether it deserves it.

The bull vs bear of the multiple

The bull: Chola is a rare thing — a large NBFC that combines a genuinely defensible distribution-and-underwriting moat, a wide ~8% NIM, a strong ~19.7% ROE, clean Murugappa governance, a long runway to keep diversifying, and a decades-long record of surviving every downturn with funding intact. Quality and consistency like that, bulls argue, deserve a Bajaj-adjacent premium; Chola has compounded book value at ~20%+ for years and shows no sign of stopping. The bear: ~5× book already prices in years of flawless 20%+ growth and high returns. Any wobble — a rural downturn that spikes vehicle-finance losses, an unsecured mishap in CSEL/SME, a rate cycle that compresses NIM, or growth simply decelerating — and a richly-valued NBFC can de-rate hard, because the price leaves almost no margin for error. You are paying a near-Bajaj multiple for a business that, unlike Bajaj, is more cyclical (rural/freight-linked) and is still learning its newest, riskiest products.

You are not buying today's clean quarter; you are buying the promise that a rural-and-semi-urban lender with a real moat can keep compounding its book at 20%+ with contained credit costs through a full cycle. Get that, and ~5× book is fair. Miss it — in a bad rural year, or an unsecured stumble — and it isn't.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely high-quality franchise — priced for it to keep executing.

Chola is one of the cleaner large-lender stories in India: a top-tier vehicle financier with a real distribution moat, a diversifying secured book, strong returns, clean Murugappa governance, and a through-cycle survival record. The investment debate is less about business quality — which is high — and more about valuation and the durability of clean, fast growth as the newer books scale and the cycle turns.

The bull case

  • Real, hard-to-copy moat — ~1,750+ branches (~80% rural/semi-urban) plus 40 years of used/CV underwriting craft = a wide ~8% NIM others can't safely capture.
  • Strong, improving returns — ~19.7% ROE (Q4 ~23%), ~3.5% ROA, NIM expanding as funding costs fall; a well-oiled compounding machine.
  • Diversifying with discipline — LAP, home loans, SME and SBPL growing 25–46%, mostly secured; CSEL deliberately slowed when it got risky.
  • Through-cycle survivor — came through 2008, the 2018–20 NBFC crunch and COVID with funding intact and losses that spiked then normalised.
  • Murugappa governance + top rating — clean, conservative promoter; well-capitalised (CRAR ~19.2%); trusted brand lowers funding and acquisition costs.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~5× book and ~29× earnings price in years of flawless execution; little margin for error.
  • Cyclical, rural-linked book — vehicle finance rides freight, farm income and monsoons; a rural downturn can spike credit costs fast.
  • Untested new engines — SME, CSEL and SBPL are growing 40%+ off small bases and have never seen a serious downturn; CSEL already ran ~5.2% losses.
  • FY26 flattered by a benign cycle — NIM, credit cost and operating leverage all moved the right way at once; they can reverse together too.
  • Structural bank competition — banks with cheaper deposit funding keep pressing into vehicle, LAP and home loans on Chola's turf.
The bottom line

Chola is a structurally advantaged, high-quality lender — the Murugappa Group's flagship NBFC, with a genuine moat in the hard craft of small-town vehicle finance, strong and improving returns, a diversifying secured book, and a rare record of surviving every crisis with its funding and reputation intact. The business risk is moderate and well-managed. The valuation risk is the real one: at ~5× book on a more-cyclical, rural-linked model than Bajaj's, the stock already pays for the promise of clean 20%+ compounding through a full cycle. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter — AUM growth (is the runway intact?), credit cost / Stage-3 (does quality hold as the new books season and if the rural cycle turns?), and NIM (does the spread survive a rate cycle?). Get those right and a premium franchise justifies its price; stumble on any of them, and a richly-valued NBFC has room to fall.
