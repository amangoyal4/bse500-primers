00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're actually buying, before we go deep.

Alivus Life Sciences is one of India's leading makers of Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients (APIs) — the pharmacologically active drug molecule that does the work inside a medicine, before it is pressed into a tablet or filled into a capsule by someone else. It sells those molecules to pharmaceutical companies worldwide, in chronic-disease areas like cardiovascular, diabetes, central-nervous-system, anti-infective and oncology, and it runs a growing CDMO (contract development & manufacturing) arm that makes molecules to a customer's contract. It is not a maker of finished, branded medicines you would recognise on a pharmacy shelf — it operates one step upstream, as an ingredient supplier.

Three facts frame the whole company:

  • It is Glenmark Life Sciences under a new name and new owner. The company was born in 2001 as the API division of Glenmark Pharmaceuticals, hived off into a separate company in 2019, and listed on the market in 2021. In 2023–24 the Nirma group — the household-name detergents-and-chemicals conglomerate — bought a 75% controlling stake from Glenmark, and in January 2025 the company was renamed Alivus Life Sciences. The BSE code (543322) never changed; only the identity did.
  • Its edge is regulatory, not just chemical. Making an API is hard, but the durable moat is that Alivus has spent decades getting its molecules approved and designed into its customers' own drug filings in strict markets — the US, Europe, Japan — via more than 611 regulatory dossiers (DMFs and CEPs). Once a customer builds your API into their approved product, switching to a rival supplier is slow, costly and risky. That stickiness is the business.
  • The plot is a supplier weaning itself off its former parent. Historically a meaningful slice of revenue was captive sales back to Glenmark Pharma. The post-Nirma strategy is to grow the non-Glenmark ("non-GPL") and CDMO business faster — and it is working: external (non-GPL) revenue has climbed from about 59% of sales in FY22 to roughly 71% in FY26, so the Glenmark captive share has fallen to around 30%. Alivus is deliberately trading a related-party umbilical cord for open-market customers, while pushing up the value chain into complex, higher-margin molecules (peptides included).
In plain terms

When you swallow a cholesterol tablet, the branded pill is made by a finished-drug company — but the specific molecule that lowers your cholesterol had to be synthesised by someone, to exacting purity, in a regulator-inspected plant. Alivus is one of the companies that makes that molecule and ships it by the tonne to drug-makers around the world. It earns a steady, unglamorous margin on chemistry and compliance rather than on brands or marketing. The investment question is not "is this a good drug company?" — it does not sell drugs to patients at all — but "can this ingredient supplier grow beyond its old parent, climb into harder chemistry, and keep its margins while a new owner funds the expansion?"

01

What an API actually is — and why the maker isn't a drug company

The one distinction you must grasp before the rest of the primer makes sense.

Every medicine has two kinds of ingredient. There is the Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) — the molecule that actually treats the condition (the metformin in a diabetes tablet, the atorvastatin in a cholesterol pill) — and there are the excipients, the inactive fillers, binders and coatings that turn that molecule into a swallowable, stable, correctly-dosed tablet. Alivus makes the active part: the pure chemical, usually a fine powder, produced in bulk in a chemical plant to pharmaceutical-grade purity.

The crucial point for an investor is that an API maker sits one rung upstream of the companies whose brands you know. A finished-drug or "formulations" company (a Cipla, a Sun Pharma, a Glenmark) buys or makes the API, formulates it into a tablet, brands it, gets the finished product approved, and sells it to pharmacies, hospitals and patients. Alivus almost never does that last mile. Its customers are those formulations companies. That single structural fact changes everything about how the business behaves:

Why "boring ingredient supplier" is the point, not the flaw

An API business does not chase blockbusters or advertise to doctors. It wins by making a known molecule more purely, more cheaply, more reliably, and with cleaner regulatory paperwork than the next supplier — and then embedding itself so deeply in a customer's approved product that swapping it out is a headache the customer would rather avoid. The revenue is less spectacular than a branded drug but more repeatable: chronic-disease APIs get reordered every quarter for years, because patients take those medicines for life. Alivus deliberately concentrates on chronic, non-commoditised APIs — molecules that are technically fiddly enough to keep casual competitors out, but large enough in volume to matter.

So when you read that Alivus is a "pharma stock," resist the reflex to value it like a drug company hunting the next patent. It is closer to a speciality-chemicals manufacturer with a pharma-grade compliance overlay: its fortunes ride on chemistry, capacity, cost and regulatory approvals, not on prescriptions written or brands built.

02

The qualification moat — why a customer can't just swap suppliers

The single most important reason API relationships are sticky and slow to lose.

The instinct is that a commodity chemical has no moat — anyone can undercut on price. For regulated-market APIs, that instinct is wrong, and understanding why is understanding the whole business. When a drug company files to sell a medicine in the US, Europe or Japan, it must name the specific API and the specific supplier, and back it with a detailed regulatory dossier proving the ingredient is made to a controlled, reproducible, inspected process. Alivus provides that dossier — a DMF (Drug Master File) in the US or a CEP (Certificate of Suitability) in Europe — and once the customer's finished product is approved referencing Alivus's file, the two are regulatorily bound together.

The moat is Step 3 → Step 4: once your molecule is written into an approved product, the customer is reluctant to switch, because a new supplier means new stability data, new filings and regulatory delay on a product already earning revenue.

This is why an API maker's count of regulatory filings is watched as closely as its revenue. Each DMF or CEP is a molecule Alivus is qualified to supply into a strict market — a permission slip that took years and money to earn, and that a competitor must replicate from scratch. Alivus carries over 611 DMF and CEP filings globally (as of March 2026), one of the deeper regulatory libraries among Indian API makers. That library is the closest thing the business has to a patent estate: not legal monopoly, but accumulated qualification.

The compliance overlay that separates winners from commodity shops

There is a hard line in this industry between suppliers qualified for regulated markets (US FDA, EU, Japan — demanding, inspection-heavy, but higher-value and stickier) and those serving only "rest of world" markets on price. Alivus lives substantially on the regulated side, which is what lets it hold ~30–34% EBITDA margins rather than the thin margins of a pure commodity chemical trader. The flip side: a single adverse regulatory inspection at a key plant can freeze shipments and dossiers overnight, which is why a maker like Alivus obsesses over plant compliance. The moat and the single biggest operating risk are the same wall, viewed from two sides.

03

Two businesses in one: generic-API supply and CDMO

They look similar from outside; economically they are quite different — and the second is the growth story.

Alivus does two related things that are worth keeping firmly apart. The larger, established business is generic-API supply: Alivus develops a molecule for an off-patent drug, files its own dossier, and sells that API to many customers who each make their own version of the finished medicine. The faster-growing business is CDMO — Contract Development and Manufacturing Operations — where Alivus makes a molecule to a specific customer's contract and specification, often for that customer's own proprietary or patented product, rather than off its own catalogue.

Why does the CDMO piece matter so much to the equity story? Because it is the part of the business that can grow value rather than just volume. A generic API tends to see its price grind lower over time as rival suppliers qualify against it; a CDMO relationship, by contrast, is co-developed, scarce and priced on capability — so it defends and can lift group margins. Today the split is lopsided: CDMO is only around 7% of revenue versus roughly 93% from the API business, but management's stated ambition is to grow CDMO toward 12–15% over the coming years. In FY26 the CDMO arm delivered the second-half turnaround management had guided to and closed the year with roughly 18% growth — small base, but an early sign the strategy is converting.

Why the margin already moved before the revenue did

Here is the tell that this is a mix story, not a volume story. Between FY24 and FY26, Alivus's revenue grew only in the mid-single digits — yet its EBITDA margin climbed from ~30% to 33.6%, a fresh high, and PAT grew ~16% in FY26 alone. A commodity supplier growing 5–7% a year does not usually expand margins 350+ basis points; that gap is the fingerprint of a deliberate shift toward higher-value molecules and CDMO, where Alivus earns more per kilo of chemistry. The near-term revenue line is sleepy on purpose while the company re-tools what it makes; the margin line is where the strategy shows up first.

04

Glenmark → Nirma → Alivus: the carve-out and change of hands

Who built it, who owns it now, and why the sequence matters.

Alivus has changed its name, its owner and its parentage in barely five years, so it pays to walk the sequence carefully. The business began inside Glenmark Pharmaceuticals as its captive API division, was separated into its own company, floated on the market, and then sold wholesale to a buyer from an entirely different industry. Each step reshaped the investment case.

How it got here

  • 2001
    Founded as the API business unit of Glenmark Pharmaceuticals — an in-house molecule shop supplying Glenmark's own drug-making needs and, over time, external customers.
  • 2019
    Carved out into a separate company, Glenmark Life Sciences Limited (GLS), to run and be valued as a standalone API business.
  • Aug 2021
    IPO on BSE & NSE — a ~₹1,514 Cr issue priced at ₹720/share; Glenmark Pharma remained the majority owner. (Note: the public listing was 2021, not 2023 — 2023 is when the Nirma sale was struck.)
  • Sep 2023
    Glenmark Pharma agrees to sell a 75% stake to Nirma Limited at ₹615/share — roughly ₹5,650 Cr, valuing GLS near ₹6,700 Cr — to pay down Glenmark's own debt.
  • May 2024
    Deal completes: Nirma becomes the controlling promoter (~75%); Glenmark exits control. The company is now a Nirma-group business.
  • Jan 2025
    Renamed Alivus Life Sciences; NSE symbol changes GLS → ALIVUS (20 Jan 2025). BSE scrip code 543322 unchanged. A new independent identity, distinct from Glenmark.
  • FY26
    First full stretch under Nirma as "Alivus": revenue ₹2,552 Cr, PAT ₹565 Cr, EBITDA margin 33.6% (a high), CDMO up ~18%; heavy capex phase underway.

Ownership today

  • Promoter — the Nirma group: ~74.9%. Nirma holds the controlling block bought from Glenmark. This is a strategic owner, not a financial flipper — Nirma is building a pharma/life-sciences pillar and is expected to hold and invest, not exit.
  • Institutions & public — the balance (~25%). Foreign and domestic institutions hold high-single-digit percentages each, with the remainder in public hands. The genuine free float is modest, since three-quarters sits with the promoter.
  • Glenmark — now a customer, not an owner. The critical shift: Glenmark Pharmaceuticals went from being both parent and largest captive customer to being, post-2024, simply one (still important) external customer. The ownership umbilical cord is cut; the commercial one is being deliberately loosened.
Why the change of owner is more than cosmetic: under Glenmark, Alivus was partly a captive supplier serving a parent that competed with Alivus's other potential customers — an awkward structure that could cap who would buy from it. Under Nirma, an owner with no pharmaceutical-formulations business of its own, Alivus is a cleaner, neutral supplier that rival drug-makers can deal with without feeding a competitor. The change of hands didn't just move shares; it removed a conflict that sat at the heart of the old structure.
05

Weaning off the parent — the "GPL vs non-GPL" split

The single metric the whole thesis turns on — and the company reports it every quarter.

If there is one number to obsess over with Alivus, it is not revenue growth or even margin in the abstract — it is the mix between "GPL" and "non-GPL" business. GPL is Glenmark Pharmaceuticals, the former parent and long-time captive customer; "non-GPL" is everyone else — the external, third-party customers that Alivus has to win in open competition. Management reports this split precisely because it is the truest measure of the transition from a captive supplier into an independent one. And the transition is visibly under way: external (non-GPL) revenue has risen from about 59% of sales in FY22 to roughly 71% in FY26 — meaning the Glenmark captive share has fallen from around 41% to about 30%, with the non-GPL book growing double-digits (up ~14.5% year-on-year in an early FY26 quarter) while the GPL base lags.

The molecule that once counted as an internal transfer to its own parent now has to be won, quarter after quarter, on the open market. That shift — from captive to competitive — is the entire second act of this company.

This is what makes Alivus genuinely different from a typical Indian API maker that was born independent. Alivus is unwinding a dependency built into its DNA. A large captive customer is a comfort and a trap at once: it guarantees baseline volume, but it caps the multiple the market will pay (related-party revenue is discounted by investors), it deters rival drug-makers wary of enriching a competitor, and it leaves the supplier exposed to the parent's own ups and downs. Cutting that dependency is the value-creation lever — and it is measurable in real time through the GPL/non-GPL disclosure.

The non-transferable device: read three lines together, not one

Alivus's progress can be judged on a single composite that would fit no other company in its sector: the non-GPL revenue share, the CDMO growth rate, and the EBITDA margin — read as one signal. If the non-GPL share is rising, CDMO is compounding double-digits, and margin is holding above ~33%, then the company is genuinely diversifying into higher-value chemistry and the re-rating case is intact. If instead non-GPL stalls, CDMO growth fades, or margin slips as capex ramps, then Alivus is still, underneath the new name, a Glenmark-dependent commodity API house — and should be valued as one. The find-replace test: could this paragraph describe Divi's, Aarti or Aether? No — none of them carries a legacy captive-parent line to shed. This is Alivus's alone.

On the exact figure: the non-GPL mix disclosed by the company moves each quarter (the ~71% for FY26 and ~59% for FY22 are approximate, drawn from investor materials and broker notes), so treat the precise percentage as an approximate, verify-against-latest number rather than a fixed truth. What is not in doubt is the direction and pace — a clear, multi-year climb in external revenue share — and that trajectory, tracked over the coming quarters, is the truest read on whether the independence strategy is working.
06

Why a detergents-to-cement empire now owns an API maker

The genuinely surprising part of the story — and what Nirma actually brings.

Here is the fact that stops most people short: the controlling owner of one of India's larger API businesses is Nirma — the group whose name is synonymous with a decades-old washing-powder jingle, and which today spans detergents, soaps, soda ash and other bulk chemicals, and cement. A consumer-and-chemicals conglomerate buying a pharmaceutical-ingredients maker sounds like a mismatch. It is less of one than it looks, and the logic is worth spelling out.

  • APIs are, at heart, complex chemistry — Nirma's home turf. Nirma is fundamentally a chemistry and process-manufacturing group (soda ash, surfactants, speciality chemicals). An API plant is a highly-regulated cousin of the chemical plants Nirma already runs at scale. The buyer is not a tourist in manufacturing; it understands throughput, yields, raw-material sourcing and capital-heavy chemistry.
  • A deep-pocketed, patient owner for a capex-heavy phase. Alivus is entering an intensive investment period — new capacity, a new site, brownfield expansions. A promoter with the balance sheet and appetite to fund that (Nirma is a large, cash-generative private group) matters more than a listed formulations parent that was itself paying down debt. The owner change swapped a financially-stretched parent for a well-capitalised one.
  • A clean, conflict-free owner. As covered above, Nirma sells no branded finished drugs, so Alivus can serve any drug-maker without the awkwardness of enriching a competitor — widening, in principle, the universe of customers willing to design Alivus in.
The surprise that reframes the risk profile

A newcomer instinctively worries that a detergent group has no business running a pharma company. The more useful frame is the opposite: Alivus's biggest needs right now are capital, process-chemistry scale, and a neutral parentage — and those are precisely the three things a large, chemistry-native, non-pharma conglomerate can supply better than the debt-laden drug company that used to own it. The real Nirma question is not competence but capital allocation and stewardship: will a new owner from outside pharma keep investing behind regulatory quality and R&D with the discipline the business demands, or treat it as a cash cow? That is a governance judgement to watch, not a competence dismissal to make.

Because Nirma is a privately-held group and now owns ~75% of Alivus, minority shareholders should watch related-party dealings, capital-allocation choices and dividend policy closely — the usual caveats for any tightly-held, promoter-controlled company apply here, and the free float is modest.
07

The climb up the complexity curve — plants, capex and peptides

Where the growth actually comes from: more capacity, harder molecules, and CDMO.

Alivus's forward story is a capacity-and-complexity build-out. It is spending to make more (new reactor volume) and to make harder, higher-value molecules (complex APIs, speciality chemistry, peptides) — the two levers that turn a mature generic-API base into a growth business. This is a physically real, checkable programme, not a slogan: the milestones are plant commissionings and reactor volumes.

The near-term catch: capex now, revenue later

A capacity build is spending before it is earning. New reactors depreciate and consume cash from day one but only fill up over several years as customers qualify products onto them. That is why Alivus's revenue can look sleepy (mid-single-digit growth) while capex runs hot — the two are out of phase by design. The concrete thing to watch is utilisation of the new capacity: does Solapur and the doubled reactor volume actually fill with higher-value, non-GPL and CDMO work, or sit half-idle dragging on returns? Commissioning a plant is the easy, visible milestone; loading it profitably is the one that decides whether the capex was wise.

A reminder that plant risk is real and current: Alivus reported a fire at its Dahej facility on 14 Feb 2026, confined to one of the site's seven blocks (an intermediate unit), with API production resuming around 1 March and a phased restart thereafter. Dahej is roughly 30% of total installed API capacity, so even a contained, single-block incident delayed some shipments — a live example of the operating fragility that comes with concentrated, regulated manufacturing.

The competitive set — where Alivus sits among Indian API makers

Comparison sharpens what Alivus is, and is not.

Alivus competes in a crowded, capable field of Indian API and fine-chemicals companies, plus low-cost Chinese suppliers. Placing it against the field clarifies its identity: a large, regulated-market API house with a growing CDMO arm and a distinctive captive-parent legacy to shed — neither the biggest nor the most specialised name, but a scale player transitioning its mix.

The peer set is not just competition; it is a map of where the value is, and Alivus has told you which direction it intends to walk.

09

The risk stack

The concrete things that can go wrong — ranked by how much they matter.

Alivus's risks are the classic API-maker stack, sharpened by its specific history. None is exotic; all are worth weighing before the growth story seduces.

  • Customer concentration — including the legacy Glenmark line. A B2B ingredient supplier serves a limited set of large customers, and Alivus additionally carries its former parent as a significant one. Losing or shrinking a major account — Glenmark included — would hit revenue directly. The whole non-GPL strategy is, in part, a hedge against this concentration; until it plays out, the concentration remains.
  • Regulatory / inspection risk (the sharpest tail). Alivus's markets and moat depend on plant compliance. An adverse USFDA or EU inspection outcome at a key site can halt shipments, freeze new filings and dent the very stickiness that justifies the margin. The recent Dahej fire is a reminder that single-site disruption is a live hazard.
  • Raw-material / China dependency. Like most Indian API makers, Alivus relies on China for many key starting materials (KSMs) and intermediates. Price spikes, supply shocks or geopolitics in that supply chain squeeze margins and can disrupt production — a structural vulnerability the whole industry shares.
  • Pricing pressure & competition. Generic APIs face relentless price erosion as more suppliers qualify, from both Indian peers and low-cost Chinese producers. If the mix-shift up the value chain stalls, the base business is exposed to this grind.
  • Capex execution. The near-doubling of reactor capacity and the new Solapur site must be commissioned on time and filled with profitable work. Delays, cost overruns or under-utilisation would turn the investment phase into a return drag rather than a growth engine.
  • FX exposure. A large share of sales is exported and dollar-linked while many costs are rupee/China-linked, so currency swings move reported margins.
  • Ownership & governance. A ~75% privately-held promoter (Nirma) and modest free float mean minority holders rely on the controlling group's capital-allocation and related-party discipline; the new owner's pharma stewardship is still being demonstrated.
  • Valuation. The stock trades around the low-20s P/E on a business growing mid-single-digit at the top line — the multiple already anticipates the mix-shift and CDMO ramp succeeding, so a stumble is not fully cushioned.
10

Financials & valuation

A steady, cash-generative base — with the whole debate riding on mix and margin, not top-line pace.

Alivus's financials describe a solid, profitable, cash-generative API business growing slowly at the top line but expanding where it counts — margins. FY26 (year ended March 2026) revenue was about ₹2,552 Cr, up ~6.9%; net profit (PAT) about ₹565 Cr, up ~16.2%; and the EBITDA margin reached 33.6%, a fresh high, up roughly 360 basis points year-on-year. That combination — modest revenue growth, faster profit growth, rising margin — is the numerical signature of the mix-shift story.

On valuation, Alivus trades around a low-20s price/earnings multiple (roughly ₹13,800 Cr market cap on ~₹565 Cr of profit), with healthy returns on capital (ROCE ~25%, ROE ~19%) and a low dividend yield. That is neither cheap nor extravagant for a quality API franchise — it is a multiple that already assumes the strategy works: that CDMO keeps compounding, the mix keeps shifting up, and margins hold as the new capacity fills. The debate is whether the mid-single-digit revenue line accelerates enough, and the margin holds high enough, to justify paying for a growth re-rating on a business whose base is a mature generic-API book.

The one number that settles the thesis

For Alivus the deciding metric is not a single quarter's revenue or even margin in isolation — it is the trajectory of the non-GPL / external-customer revenue mix and CDMO growth, read against the EBITDA margin and regulated-market share. If external and CDMO revenue keep taking share while margin stays north of ~33% as the ₹450 Cr-a-year capex converts, then Alivus is genuinely re-rating from a captive commodity supplier into an independent, complex-molecule and CDMO franchise, and the low-20s multiple is defensible. If the mix stalls or margin gives way as new plants sit under-utilised, the multiple is expensive for what would then be a slow-growing, parent-dependent generic API house. The scoreboard is legible every results season: mix up, CDMO up, margin held — or not.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A quality ingredient supplier in mid-transition, priced for the transition to work.

Alivus is a rare kind of pharma story: not a drug-discovery gamble, but a steady, profitable ingredient supplier deliberately remaking its own identity — shedding a captive-parent legacy, backed by a deep-pocketed new owner, climbing toward harder chemistry and contract work. The assets are real, the margins are good and improving, and the strategic direction is clear. What is unproven is the pace: whether the mix-shift and the capex convert fast enough to earn the growth multiple the market already assigns.

The risks, in one line each

  • Concentration & the Glenmark tail — a B2B supplier with a legacy captive customer still to be diluted.
  • Regulatory / plant risk — an adverse inspection or a site incident (cf. the Dahej fire) can freeze the very stickiness that justifies the margin.
  • China input dependency — KSMs and intermediates leave margins hostage to a supply chain Alivus doesn't control.
  • Execution — the reactor-capacity doubling and Solapur must be built and profitably filled, not just commissioned.
  • Valuation — a low-20s multiple on a mid-single-digit top line already prices in success.

The bull case

  • Deep regulatory moat — 611+ DMF/CEP filings and designed-in status make the base revenue sticky and hard to dislodge.
  • Margin already inflecting — EBITDA margin ~30% → 33.6% in two years on flat-ish sales; mix-shift is visibly working.
  • CDMO turning — ~18% growth in FY26 after the guided H2 turnaround; the higher-value engine is engaging.
  • A cleaner, richer owner — Nirma removes the competitor-parent conflict and can fund the capex a debt-laden Glenmark could not.
  • Complexity optionality — peptides/speciality positioning sits near the industry's hottest demand wave, with capacity being built to serve it.

The bear case

  • Still parent-tethered — until the non-GPL mix clearly dominates, this is a supplier leaning on its former parent.
  • Slow top line — mid-single-digit revenue growth is unremarkable for the multiple being paid.
  • Capex before returns — new capacity depresses cash and returns until it fills; under-utilisation would sting.
  • China & price grind — input dependency and generic price erosion cap the base business if the mix-shift stalls.
  • Tightly held, unproven steward — modest float and a non-pharma promoter whose long-run capital discipline is still being shown.
The bottom line

Alivus is Glenmark Life Sciences grown up and out on its own — a quality API and CDMO franchise finishing the awkward job of no longer needing the parent it was born inside, with a chemistry-native conglomerate now paying for the next chapter. The chemistry, the compliance library and the customer relationships are genuinely good; the question is entirely about pace and mix. The concrete milestones to watch are specific and dated: the Solapur commissioning and the reactor-capacity climb toward ~2,650 KL by FY28, the quarterly non-GPL and CDMO mix, and whether the EBITDA margin holds above ~33% as the ₹450 Cr-a-year capex converts to loaded plants. If those three move the right way together, a name-change becomes a genuine re-rating. If they don't, the market will eventually price Alivus for what its base still is — and the new name won't change that.
