Balkrishna Industries, India's global off-highway tyre champion

A complete walk through the company the market calls "BKT" — a rare Indian exporter that quietly became a top-five global maker of the giant, specialised tyres that go on tractors, harvesters, diggers and mining trucks. How a low-volume, thousands-of-sizes niche works, why ~75% of the tyres are sold in Europe and the Americas rather than at home, the low-cost-India moat that lets it undercut Michelin in their own backyard, and why a high-quality exporter earning ~23% margins now faces a punishing US tariff wall.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Balkrishna Industries — universally known by its brand BKT — makes off-highway tyres (OHT): the big, specialised, deeply-treaded tyres for farm equipment (tractors, harvesters), construction and earthmoving machines, mining, industrial handlers, all-terrain vehicles and gardening kit. It does not compete with MRF or Apollo in ordinary car, bike and truck tyres for Indian roads. It is a niche global specialist — a top-five player in a ~$20 billion worldwide OHT market, holding roughly 6–7% global share and aiming for ~10%.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is an export machine, not a domestic tyre brand. Around ~70% of revenue comes from outside India — Europe is the single biggest market (~43% of sales), the Americas next (~16%). BKT is that rare Indian manufacturer that competes head-on with Michelin, Trelleborg and Titan International in their home markets, and wins share on price and specialist service.
  • The moat is cost arbitrage plus SKU complexity. BKT makes thousands of low-volume tyre specifications from low-cost Indian plants, sells them mainly into the high-margin replacement market, and even makes its own carbon black (a key raw material) to protect margins. That combination lets it price ~20–30% below the Western majors and still earn a ~22–23% EBITDA margin.
  • It is deeply cyclical — and just came through a downcycle. Demand tracks global farm incomes, crop prices and the construction/mining cycle, amplified by dealer de-stocking and re-stocking. FY26 was a flattish, margin-pressured year (revenue roughly flat at ₹10,656 Cr, PAT down ~25% to ₹1,222 Cr) as a global OHT destocking hangover, a strong rupee-vs-euro swing and a brand-new 50% US tariff all bit at once — even as volumes hit a record 3.17 lakh tonnes.
In plain terms

Think of BKT as the Indian value-brand specialist for "everything except the road." When a European farmer needs a new tractor tyre, or a quarry needs a fresh set for its loader, BKT offers a tyre engineered for that exact machine — in one of thousands of odd sizes the big global brands find uneconomic to stock — at a meaningfully lower price, delivered through a dealer network BKT has spent 30 years building across 160+ countries. Because it manufactures in India, its costs are far below Michelin's or Trelleborg's, so it can undercut them and still earn strong margins. The catch: it sells cyclical kit to farmers and miners across the world, so its fortunes rise and fall with harvests, commodity prices, exchange rates — and, now, trade tariffs.

01

What is an "off-highway tyre", really?

No engineering degree required — just the idea that "not-a-road tyre" is a completely different business.

The world's tyre market splits into two very different halves. On-highway tyres go on cars, motorcycles, buses and trucks that drive on paved roads — huge volumes, standardised sizes, brutal competition, thin margins. Off-highway tyres go on machines that mostly don't use public roads: farm tractors, combine harvesters, backhoe loaders, mining dumpers, forklifts, ATVs and ride-on mowers. BKT lives entirely in this second half — and it is a fundamentally more attractive place to be.

The economics flip because of that "low volume × thousands of sizes" combination. A giant like Michelin is optimised for long production runs of standard sizes; it finds it uneconomic to keep re-tooling for a size that sells a few thousand tyres a year. BKT built its whole factory system around exactly that flexibility — short runs, frequent size changes, a vast catalogue. That is why it can serve the "long tail" of specialist sizes the majors under-serve, and why an off-highway tyre earns a better margin than a commodity car tyre.

Why "replacement" demand is the quiet superpower

Tyres wear out and get replaced. In off-highway, the replacement (aftermarket) market is far bigger and more profitable than the OEM market (selling to the tractor/digger maker to fit at the factory). OEM sales are low-margin, lumpy and tied to new-machine sales; replacement sales are higher-margin, recurring, and driven by the huge installed base of machines already working in fields and quarries worldwide. BKT sells the large majority of its tyres into replacement — which makes its revenue stickier and more profitable than a business dependent on new-equipment cycles. Every tractor already ploughing a field in France or Iowa is a future BKT customer when its tyres wear through.

02

The business model: many sizes, low cost, sold far away

Three design choices explain how a Mumbai-based company out-competes Europe's tyre giants.

BKT's model rests on three deliberate bets: an ultra-wide product catalogue (thousands of SKUs), low-cost Indian manufacturing, and a global dealer-and-brand distribution network that sells almost everything abroad. Read this once and the strategy clicks: BKT engineers a specialist tyre for nearly any off-road machine, builds it cheaper than anyone in the West can, and delivers it through its own branded distribution across 160+ countries.

The value chain — from carbon black to a farmer's field

BKT is unusually vertically integrated for a tyre company. It even makes one of its own key raw materials, which is why the chain below starts inside BKT, not with an external supplier.

Three engines of the model

  • Product breadth — a catalogue of 3,000+ specifications across agriculture, OTR (off-the-road / earthmoving), industrial, ATV, and gardening. Breadth is a moat: a dealer can source almost his entire off-highway range from one supplier.
  • Cost leadership — Indian labour, scale and integration give BKT a structural cost advantage over European and American majors, so it can price below them and still earn ~22–23% EBITDA margins.
  • Brand + distribution — BKT is not an anonymous white-label maker; it has spent 30 years and heavy marketing (football, motorsport — see §8) turning "BKT" into a trusted value-brand in Europe and America, sold through its own dealers, mostly into replacement demand.
Why selling abroad — not at home — is the whole point

India's own off-highway market is small and price-sensitive. The money is where the machines and the incomes are: the large, mechanised, high-value farms and construction/mining fleets of Europe and North America. BKT's insight, decades ago, was to skip the crowded Indian on-road tyre war entirely and instead attack the Western off-highway aftermarket as a lower-cost challenger. That is why ~70% of revenue is exports and Europe alone is ~43% of sales — and why BKT is best thought of as a global off-highway business that happens to manufacture in India, rather than an "Indian tyre stock."

03

The company and who controls it

A three-generation Poddar-family business that bet on a niche the world ignored.

Balkrishna is a promoter-run, family-controlled company. It traces to a Poddar-family group founded in the 1950s; the off-highway tyre business began in 1987, and it grew into today's global specialist under the second and third generations. The Poddar family — Chairman & Managing Director Arvind Poddar and his son, Joint Managing Director Rajiv Poddar — controls roughly 58% of the shares, giving a long-term, owner-operator character to strategy.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Poddar family hold ~58.3% — a controlling, aligned, multi-generational stake. Arvind Poddar (CMD) and Rajiv Poddar (JMD) run the business day to day. Owner-operator control is generally a positive for a long-cycle capital-heavy business, though it also concentrates key-person and governance dependence in one family.
  • Brand as strategy: unusually for a B2B tyre maker, management treats the BKT brand as a core asset — hence the football and motorsport sponsorships aimed squarely at building recall among Western farmers, contractors and dealers.
  • Balance sheet: broadly conservative but not net-cash during heavy expansion — at FY26 BKT carried modest net debt (~₹900 Cr) against a large multi-year capex programme; it is comfortably serviceable given the cash the business throws off in good years.

Why the geography matters so much

Because BKT sells mostly abroad, it is really a play on three things a domestic investor can't see on Indian roads: (1) global farm income and crop prices — when European and American farmers are prosperous, they buy and replace tyres; (2) the construction and mining cycle in those regions; and (3) the currency — BKT earns in euros and dollars but spends largely in rupees, so a stronger euro/dollar helps and a stronger rupee hurts (FY26 saw an unfavourable forex swing dent profits). This is what makes BKT unusual among Indian companies: its report card is written more in Iowa cornfields and German farm shows than in Mumbai.

05

The cost-arbitrage moat — how an Indian firm undercuts Michelin

The competitive edge in one idea: same specialist tyre, built far cheaper.

BKT's durable advantage is cost. It manufactures technically demanding, low-volume off-highway tyres from low-cost Indian plants and sells them into premium Western markets at prices meaningfully below Michelin, Trelleborg or Titan — while still earning a healthy margin. That price gap, sustained for decades, is how a challenger from Mumbai has taken share in France and Ohio.

Where the cost advantage comes from

  • Labour & overheads — Indian manufacturing labour and fixed costs are a fraction of European or American equivalents. In a still-labour-intensive process like tyre building, this is a large, structural gap.
  • Scale & specialisation — BKT concentrates purely on off-highway, running big, efficient plants (notably Bhuj) tuned for flexible short-run production. It doesn't dilute focus across car and truck tyres.
  • Backward integration — making its own carbon black (and mould/tooling capability in-house) removes a supplier's margin and a layer of price volatility (see §6).
  • Lean, promoter-driven capital discipline — a family owner watching every rupee of capex per tonne of capacity.
Why the moat is real but not permanent

The cost gap is genuine and hard to close — a European major can't move its cost base to India without huge disruption, and a new low-cost entrant would need years to build BKT's catalogue, brand and dealer trust. That combination (cost + breadth + brand) is the true moat; cost alone would be copyable. But the edge is not invulnerable. It can be eroded by (1) currency — a persistently strong rupee shrinks the price gap; (2) trade barriers — tariffs (see §10) can wipe out the cost advantage at a stroke in a given market; and (3) rising Indian input and freight costs. The moat is a spread, and spreads can compress.

The whole company is, at its core, a bet that "specialist tyre, made in India, sold in the West" stays cheaper than the alternative — for long enough to keep taking share.
06

Margins: rubber, carbon black, crude & forex

What actually moves BKT's ~23% EBITDA margin up and down.

BKT's margin is one of the best in the global tyre industry, but it is not fixed — it swings with raw-material prices, freight and exchange rates. Understanding the levers explains why FY26 margins slipped even as volumes hit a record.

The four big margin levers
LeverWhat it does to the margin
Natural rubberThe largest raw material. Rubber price spikes squeeze the margin until BKT can pass them through in price (with a lag).
Carbon black & crudeCarbon black (crude-derived) is a major input. BKT makes its own — insulating it from the merchant price and even letting it sell surplus. A crude spike hurts less than it would a rival buying carbon black on the market.
Freight / logisticsExporting heavy tyres across oceans, container and shipping-rate spikes (as in FY22) directly hit the margin.
Forex (EUR/USD vs INR)Earns in euros/dollars, spends in rupees. A stronger foreign currency helps; a stronger rupee hurts. FY26 saw an adverse forex swing (including a Q4 realised forex loss) that pressured profit.

Carbon black — the quiet margin weapon

Most tyre makers buy carbon black (the black reinforcing filler that gives a tyre its strength and colour). BKT manufactures its own, with capacity expanding from ~265,000 MTPA toward ~360,000 MTPA. This does three things: it removes a supplier's profit margin, it dampens the impact of crude-oil-linked price swings, and it opens a new revenue stream — BKT plans to sell third-party carbon black, targeting roughly ~10% of revenue from carbon black by FY30. It is a rare example of a raw-material cost centre being turned into a profit and stability lever.

Why FY26 margins fell despite record volumes

FY26 is the perfect illustration of the model's cyclicality. BKT sold more tyres than ever (3.17 lakh MT) — but revenue was roughly flat and profit fell ~25%. The culprits were price and cost, not demand: a still-soft pricing environment after the destocking downcycle, an unfavourable rupee-vs-euro/dollar move, and the drag from the US tariff. Record volume with lower profit is the signature of a company whose top line depends on realisations and forex as much as on units shipped. It's why watching only "volumes up" can badly mislead — margin and currency do the real talking.

07

The cycle: destocking, restocking, and why it whipsaws

The channel-inventory cycle is the force that makes BKT's earnings lumpy — understand it and the last three years make sense.

BKT sells mostly through dealers and distributors who hold inventory. That distribution layer amplifies the underlying demand cycle in a way that catches investors out. When end-demand softens, dealers don't just buy less — they de-stock, running down their warehouses instead of ordering, so BKT's sales fall far more than actual farmer/contractor consumption. When demand recovers, dealers re-stock, and BKT's sales snap back faster than end-demand. This is the "bullwhip" effect, and it defines BKT's recent history.

The FY24 destocking hangover is why FY24–FY25 looked weak; the FY25–26 restock (plus a European rebound) is why volumes recovered to a record even as pricing stayed soft.

What drives the underlying cycle beneath the whipsaw

  • Farm incomes & crop prices — the biggest driver, since agriculture is ~59% of sales. High grain/soft-commodity prices → prosperous farmers → more tyre buying and replacement. Weak farm economics → deferral.
  • Construction & mining activity — drives the ~37% OTR segment; tied to infrastructure spend, commodity prices and machine utilisation.
  • Replacement rhythm — the big, stable base under it all: the worldwide installed fleet of machines needing new tyres regardless of new-equipment sales.
  • Weather & season — harvests, planting cycles and even droughts move regional demand quarter to quarter.
The investor takeaway: BKT is a quality business but a cyclical stock. Its best entry points historically come when the cycle looks worst (destocking, weak farm income, soft pricing) — precisely when headline numbers disappoint. Reading BKT means separating the structural share-gain story (up and to the right over a decade) from the cyclical noise (down and up every few years). Confusing the two — extrapolating a boom or panicking in a destock — is the classic mistake.
08

Market-share gains, capacity & the brand

The structural growth story: taking share in a $20bn market, one dealer and one football match at a time.

Beneath the cycle sits a genuine, multi-decade market-share-gain story. In a global OHT market worth roughly $20 billion, BKT holds only about 6–7% — leaving a long runway. It has grown share steadily by being the cost-effective specialist, and management targets roughly ~10% global share over time. Getting there rests on three planks: capacity, brand, and new adjacencies.

The three planks of the growth story

  • Capacity — BKT runs five plants (Aurangabad/Waluj, Bhiwadi, Chopanki, Dombivali and the flagship Bhuj in Gujarat, one of Asia's largest tyre facilities). Achievable capacity is ~360,000 MTPA, being expanded (via new lines and debottlenecking) toward ~425,000 MTPA. A large multi-year capex programme — around ₹6,800 Cr committed through FY29 — funds tyre, carbon-black and automation expansion.
  • Brand-building — the eye-catching part: BKT sponsors LaLiga (Spanish football, through 2028), Italy's Serie BKT, France's Ligue 2 BKT, US Monster Jam (through 2031) and, at home, multiple IPL teams. For a B2B tyre maker this looks flashy, but the logic is real — it builds recall and trust among the Western farmers, contractors and dealers who buy tyres, helping BKT punch above a challenger brand's weight.
  • New adjacencies — BKT is cautiously widening beyond pure OHT: it has entered truck-bus radials, relaunched two-wheeler tyres, and plans passenger-car radials in India, plus the third-party carbon-black business. These are optionality on top of the core, targeting a ~2.2x revenue jump to ₹23,000 Cr by FY30.
Why share gains matter more than the cycle, long-term

A cyclical business that is also gaining share compounds through the cycle: each downturn's trough tends to sit above the last, and each recovery reaches a new high, because BKT keeps winning customers from higher-cost rivals. That is the bull's core argument — that over a decade the share-gain slope dominates the cyclical wobbles. The FY30 ₹23,000 Cr target (a ~17% revenue CAGR) is management's articulation of that: capacity + brand + adjacencies compounding on a still-small global share. The bear's reply is that targets are easy and tariffs, currency and competition make a straight line unlikely.

09

The industry and the rivals

A consolidated global oligopoly of specialists — and why BKT is emphatically not "the Indian MRF."

The global off-highway tyre market is a concentrated field of specialists, quite separate from the mass on-road tyre giants. BKT's competitors are the Western off-highway majors — and crucially, not MRF, India's on-road leader, which barely overlaps with BKT's niche.

The one comparison people get wrong: BKT vs MRF

It's tempting to file BKT next to MRF as "Indian tyre stocks," but they are almost opposite businesses. MRF is the on-road Indian champion — cars, bikes, trucks, mostly sold in India, competing with Apollo, CEAT and global majors on Indian roads. BKT is an off-highway global exporter — tractors, diggers and mining trucks, mostly sold in Europe and America, competing with Michelin, Trelleborg and Titan. MRF's cycle is Indian auto demand and monsoons; BKT's is global farm income, mining and forex. They share a country and a raw-material basket (rubber, crude) — and very little else. Treating BKT as "an Indian tyre play" misses that it is really a global agri/industrial-equipment aftermarket play that happens to be listed in Mumbai.

The competitive edge, restated: against the Western majors BKT wins on price and catalogue breadth; against a hypothetical cheaper Asian entrant it wins on brand, dealer relationships and 30 years of specification know-how. The risk is a two-front squeeze — majors defending share with pricing/financing, and lower-cost rivals attacking from below — but no competitor today combines BKT's specific mix of cost, breadth and brand in this niche.
10

The US question — opportunity meets a 50% tariff wall

The Americas were meant to be BKT's next big growth leg — then trade policy changed the maths overnight.

The United States is simultaneously BKT's biggest untapped opportunity and, right now, its sharpest near-term risk. BKT's US OHT share is low (~below 5%), so on paper America is the natural place to keep taking share from Titan and the majors. But in 2025 US trade policy imposed steep tariffs on Indian imports — reaching a 50% tariff — which lands directly on BKT's cost-advantage model and, for a time, made selling into the US effectively unviable.

What happened

  • A reciprocal tariff of 25% on Indian goods took effect in early August 2025, then was raised to 50% later that month — a punitive level for a price-sensitive, cost-arbitrage exporter.
  • BKT indicated it was effectively not selling into the US under the 50% regime, because the tariff erases the price advantage that is the whole point of the model. The US drag showed up in FY26 volumes and margins.
  • The company's response has been to lean into recovering Europe, a resilient India, and other markets while the US situation plays out — and to keep the US as a share-gain opportunity for when the tariff environment normalises.
Why a tariff is uniquely dangerous to this business

BKT's edge is a price spread — it sells below Michelin/Titan because it makes tyres more cheaply in India. A tariff is precisely the tool that neutralises that spread: a 50% duty can more than erase a 20–30% price advantage, flipping BKT from "cheaper" to "not competitive" in that market overnight, with no operational fix available. Unlike a rubber-price spike (temporary, industry-wide) or a currency move (mean-reverting), a tariff is a policy variable — it can persist, and it targets India specifically. That's why the US tariff is the one macro risk that strikes at the heart of the moat rather than merely the margin.

The honest read: the US tariff is a real, current headwind, but not an existential one — the US is a minority of a business whose core is Europe, and BKT has other growth legs (European recovery, India, carbon black, new tyre categories). The bull frames it as a temporary lid on one market and a deferred opportunity; the bear notes that if 50% duties persist, a whole growth leg is closed and BKT's cost moat is shown to be vulnerable to policy it cannot control. Either way, US trade policy is now a first-order variable to watch for this stock.

How to think about the ~34× multiple

At ~₹42,900 Cr for ~₹1,222 Cr of FY26 profit, BKT trades around 34× trailing earnings — a clear premium for a cyclical manufacturer. That price reflects the market treating BKT as a high-quality, structural share-gainer rather than a plain commodity cyclical: best-in-class margins, a genuine cost moat, decades of share gains and a family owner. But note the trap: a P/E on trough-ish earnings can look high yet be reasonable if profits are near a cyclical low and set to recover. Bulls argue you're buying a compounder in a soft patch; bears argue the multiple already prices a recovery that tariffs, currency and competition could delay. The number to watch alongside P/E is where you think BKT sits in its cycle — the multiple means very different things at a trough versus a peak.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely world-class niche business — cyclical, currency-exposed, and now tariff-tested.

BKT is one of the most impressive niche-champion stories in Indian industry: a family firm that skipped the crowded on-road tyre war and instead became a top-five global specialist in off-highway tyres, exporting from India into the West on a durable cost advantage. The investment debate is about the cycle, the currency, the tariff and the price — not the quality of the franchise.

The bull case

  • Structural share-gainer — only ~6–7% of a $20bn market, targeting ~10%; a decade-long runway of taking share from higher-cost majors.
  • Real, durable moat — low-cost India + a 3,000+ SKU catalogue + a trusted value brand + in-house carbon black; hard to replicate all at once.
  • Best-in-class margins — ~22–23% EBITDA is elite for a tyre maker; the replacement-heavy, specialist mix keeps it there.
  • Record volumes & recovering Europe — the destocking downcycle is passing; FY26 hit record tonnage; the ₹23,000 Cr FY30 roadmap adds carbon-black and new-category optionality.
  • Aligned owner-operators — the Poddar family's ~58% stake and long-term horizon.

The bear case

  • Deep cyclicality — earnings whipsaw with farm income, mining and dealer de-/re-stocking; FY26 profit fell ~25% despite record volume.
  • US 50% tariff — strikes directly at the cost moat in a key growth market; if it persists, a whole leg of expansion is closed.
  • Forex exposure — earns in EUR/USD, spends in INR; a strong rupee compresses margins (as in FY26).
  • Raw-material swings — natural rubber and crude-linked costs can outrun price hikes; margins are not guaranteed.
  • Premium valuation — ~34× (arguably on soft earnings) leaves limited room if recovery or share gains disappoint.
The bottom line

BKT is a high-quality, globally-competitive niche champion — a rare Indian company that is a genuine world player, built on a durable cost-plus-catalogue-plus-brand moat, run by aligned owners. The business quality is not really in doubt. What is in doubt is timing and price: it is a cyclical, currency-and-tariff-exposed exporter trading at a premium multiple through a soft patch. The story over the next few years will be written in a handful of variables to watch each quarter — European and US demand, the rupee-vs-euro/dollar, natural-rubber prices, and the fate of the US tariff. Get a cyclical recovery plus continued share gains, and the premium is justified; get a prolonged tariff/currency squeeze, and a 34× cyclical has room to correct.
