Industry & Company Primer NSE: TMPV · BSE: 500570 Built for a non-specialist reader

Tata Motors (Passenger Vehicles), a British luxury carmaker wearing an Indian badge

A complete walk through the company that, since the 2025 demerger, holds three very different car businesses under one share: Jaguar Land Rover — the UK luxury-SUV maker that is by far the biggest part (~85% of revenue); Tata's Indian passenger cars (Nexon, Punch, Harrier, Safari); and Tata's electric-vehicle arm (TPEM), India's #1 EV maker. This is not the trucks-and-buses company — those became a separate listed entity (NSE: TMCV). This primer explains why the earnings of this stock live and die with JLR's luxury cycle, forex and US tariffs; how a cyberattack, tariffs and a China slump made FY26 a rough year; why Tata's India SUV/EV story is a genuine bright spot; and how you value a JLR + India-PV + EV combination.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep — and what you're not looking at.

This company is best understood as a British luxury-car maker with an Indian mass-market carmaker and an EV champion bolted on. Since late 2025 it is a separate, stand-alone listed company — the passenger-vehicle half of the old Tata Motors, spun off in a demerger that split the group in two. Under the single ticker TMPV sit three businesses: Jaguar Land Rover (JLR), the UK maker of Range Rover, Defender, Discovery and Jaguar — by far the largest part; Tata's domestic passenger cars (Nexon, Punch, Harrier, Safari, Tiago, Altroz); and Tata Passenger Electric Mobility (TPEM), India's leading electric-car business.

Read this first — what this primer is about. After the 2025 demerger there are two Tata Motors on the exchanges. This primer covers only the passenger-vehicle entity — Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles Ltd (NSE: TMPV, BSE: 500570), which holds JLR + Tata cars + the Tata EV arm. It is not about the trucks, buses and pickups — those sit inside the other listed company, confusingly renamed "Tata Motors Limited" (NSE: TMCV). Every figure below is for the PV business — and because that business is ~85% JLR by revenue, its earnings are dominated by the fortunes of a UK luxury-SUV maker. We never mix in the CV/trucks numbers.

Three facts frame everything:

  • JLR is the whole story — and it had a bad year. Roughly 85% of revenue and almost all the profit swing comes from Jaguar Land Rover. When JLR is healthy (as in FY25), the group earns billions; when JLR stumbles (as in FY26), the group's profit near-vanishes. FY26 revenue fell to ₹3.36 lakh Cr (−8.3%) and consolidated EBITDA margin collapsed to 6.8% (from 13.4%) because JLR was hit by a cyberattack, US tariffs and a China slump all at once (§06). This is the single most important thing to understand about the stock.
  • The India business is the bright spot. While JLR struggled, Tata's Indian PV arm grew revenue +20.7% to ₹58,465 Cr on record volume (6.4 lakh+ units, +15%), holding #3 in India behind Maruti and Mahindra, powered by a design-led SUV line-up and a dominant (but eroding) EV position (§07–§08).
  • Read the profit line very carefully. Reported FY26 net profit looks enormous — ~₹82,645 Cr — but that is almost entirely a one-off notional accounting gain of ~₹82,616 Cr from the demerger itself. Strip it out and the underlying profit-before-tax-and-exceptionals fell to just ₹2,519 Cr (from ₹28,650 Cr a year earlier). The operating reality is a weak year, not a bumper one (§10).
In plain terms

When you buy this stock, you are mostly buying Jaguar Land Rover — a maker of expensive British SUVs sold to wealthy buyers in the US, China, Europe and the Gulf. That business is lucrative but volatile: luxury demand swings, the pound/dollar/euro move its profits around, and it now faces a US import tariff on UK-made cars. Riding along are two Indian businesses that are the opposite in character — a growing mass-market car brand that got the SUV wave right, and an EV leader that is now being crowded by rivals. The demerger's whole point was to let investors own this combination — global luxury + Indian growth + EV — cleanly, separate from the very different truck business. What you get is a company whose headline earnings are a JLR weather report, wrapped around a genuinely improving India story.

01

The demerger: what actually happened in 2025

The single most important piece of context — get this right and everything else clicks.

In 2024–25 the old Tata Motors — one company that contained both the Indian commercial-vehicle business and the passenger-car / electric-car / Jaguar Land Rover businesses — split itself into two separately-listed companies. Understanding the mechanics matters, because it determines exactly what you own when you buy the ticker TMPV — and it explains the huge, confusing one-off gain in FY26's profit line.

Why split at all?

The old Tata Motors was a strange animal: under one share sat a stable, cash-generating Indian truck-and-bus business and a volatile, capital-hungry, globally-exposed passenger and luxury-car business. Markets tend to apply a "conglomerate discount" to businesses this mismatched — the whole traded for less than the sum of its parts, because no single investor wanted both risk profiles. Splitting them lets each attract its natural shareholder base, run its own balance sheet, and be valued on its own merits. This PV entity is the "high-beta" half: global luxury + Indian growth + EV, all more volatile and more capital-hungry than the steady truck maker.

Why "unlocking value" is a real thing, not jargon

Imagine a fruit basket priced at ₹100 that contains ₹70 of apples and ₹70 of oranges. Apple-lovers won't pay ₹140 because they'd be forced to buy oranges they don't want; orange-lovers feel the same. So the basket languishes at ₹100 — a "discount to parts". Split the basket and each fruit finds its true buyer, so the parts together fetch more than the old whole. That's a demerger. Here, the "apples" are a steady, net-cash Indian CV leader (now TMCV) and the "oranges" are this — a high-growth, high-risk global luxury + PV + EV play. A truck-cycle investor and a luxury-auto investor want very different things; separating them lets each buy exactly the risk they want.

The mechanics — dates, names and the 1:1 swap

  • Announced: August 2024. Appointed (effective) date: 1 October 2025, after NCLT (court) approval. Record date: 14 October 2025 — the cut-off that decided who received the new shares.
  • The swap: for every 1 share of the old Tata Motors, holders received 1 share of the new commercial-vehicle company (face value ₹2), while keeping their original share, which became the PV company. A clean 1:1 mirror — every old shareholder ended up owning both resulting companies in the same proportion.
  • The naming twist (important): the original listed company — the one that already held JLR, Tata cars and the EV arm — was simply renamed "Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles Limited (TMPV)" on 24 October 2025, and kept its original stock-exchange listing and codes. So TMPV trades under the same BSE scrip code as the old Tata Motors — 500570 — with its ticker changed from TATAMOTORS to TMPV. The commercial-vehicle business was the piece carved out and separately listed (as "Tata Motors Limited", NSE: TMCV, BSE: 544569, listed 12 Nov 2025).
  • The accounting one-off: because of how the demerger was structured (the CV business demerged out, and the PV holding company reorganised), TMPV booked a large notional, non-cash exceptional gain of ~₹82,616 Cr in FY26. This is why reported "net profit" looks huge — it is an accounting artefact of the restructuring, not operating earnings (§10).
The name trap to avoid: "Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles Ltd (TMPV)" = the cars + EV + JLR company, ticker TMPV, BSE 500570 (this primer). "Tata Motors Limited (formerly TML Commercial Vehicles Ltd)" = the trucks-and-buses company, ticker TMCV, BSE 544569. Because the CV arm reclaimed the plain "Tata Motors Limited" name and TMPV kept the old code, a headline that says "Tata Motors" can mean either entity — always check whether JLR/cars (this primer) or trucks/buses (not this primer) are meant.

02

What the passenger-vehicle company actually is

Three businesses, one share — and their sizes are wildly unequal.

Strip away the corporate history and TMPV is a three-part car company: a British luxury-SUV maker (JLR), an Indian mass-market carmaker, and an Indian EV specialist (TPEM). The crucial point — the one that governs the whole investment case — is that these three are nowhere near equal in size. JLR is roughly five times the revenue of the entire India car business. So while the India story is the more exciting growth narrative, the earnings and the share price are overwhelmingly a JLR story.

The three parts, in one paragraph each

  • Jaguar Land Rover (JLR) — the crown and the risk. A UK-based maker of premium and luxury SUVs — the Range Rover, Range Rover Sport, Defender and Discovery under Land Rover, plus the Jaguar brand (mid-relaunch as an all-electric ultra-luxury marque). It sells globally, earns in pounds/dollars/euros/yuan, and its profits swing hard with luxury demand, model cycles and currencies. Tata bought it from Ford in 2008. At ~85% of TMPV revenue, JLR is the company for practical purposes (§04–§06).
  • Tata India passenger vehicles — the growth story. A mass-market Indian carmaker with a design-led, SUV-heavy line-up: the Nexon (compact SUV), Punch (micro-SUV, a best-seller), Harrier & Safari (mid-size SUVs), plus the Tiago, Altroz and the new Sierra. #3 in India by volume, with a reputation for safety (5-star crash ratings) and styling (§07).
  • Tata Passenger Electric Mobility (TPEM / Tata.ev) — the EV leg, sitting inside the India PV business. Tata was India's first-mover and long-time leader in electric cars — the Nexon EV, Punch EV, Tiago EV and Curvv EV. Still #1 by share, but that share has fallen sharply as MG and Mahindra pile in (§08).
Why "85% JLR" is the number that matters most

Here is the mental model to carry through the whole primer: TMPV's profit is JLR's profit, plus or minus a modest India contribution. Because JLR is so large and its margins are so much more volatile than the India business, a good or bad year at JLR completely overwhelms whatever India does. In FY25, JLR earned ~£2.5bn of pre-tax profit and the group looked like a strong performer. In FY26, JLR's pre-tax profit collapsed to £14 million — and the whole group's operating profit near-vanished, even though India grew 20%+. So when you read this stock, read JLR first. The India business changes the growth story and the long-run optionality; JLR changes the earnings this year.

03

The company and who controls it

A Tata-group flagship, nearly eight decades old, now the "global-cars" half of a split.

The passenger-vehicle business traces back to the same storied Indian industrial name as the truck business — but its modern identity was forged by two big bets: entering passenger cars in the 1990s (the Indica, India's first indigenous car), and the 2008 acquisition of Jaguar Land Rover from Ford for ~US$2.3bn. It sits inside the Tata group, India's largest and most respected business house, controlled through Tata Sons and chaired at group level by N. Chandrasekaran, the architect of this demerger.

Timeline

  • 1945
    Founded as Tata Engineering and Locomotive Co. (TELCO) — commercial vehicles first; passenger cars would come decades later.
  • 1998
    Launches the Tata Indica — India's first indigenously developed passenger car, entering the mass car market.
  • 2008
    Buys Jaguar Land Rover from Ford — the transformative deal that made Tata a global luxury-car owner, and the piece that now dominates this entity.
  • 2020
    Launches the Nexon EV and builds an early, dominant lead in India's nascent electric-car market — the foundation of TPEM/Tata.ev.
  • 2021
    Jaguar unveils its Reimagine strategy — Jaguar to relaunch as an all-electric ultra-luxury brand; JLR begins a multi-year electrification and premiumisation push.
  • Aug 2024
    Tata Motors announces the demerger — splitting commercial vehicles from passenger vehicles + JLR into two listed companies.
  • Sep–Oct 2025
    A cyberattack halts JLR production for ~5 weeks (from 1 Sep 2025) — later reported as UK's costliest hack, materially denting FY26 volumes.
  • Oct–Nov 2025
    Demerger completes. The original company is renamed Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles Ltd (24 Oct); trades as NSE: TMPV / BSE: 500570. FY26 ends with a JLR-hit profit and a big one-off demerger accounting gain.

Ownership & balance sheet

  • Promoter (Tata group): ~42.6% held by Tata Sons and group entities — clear, stable Tata control, mirrored 1:1 from the old company via the demerger swap.
  • Institutions & public: roughly institutions ~34% (a broad FII + DII base) and public ~23% — a large-cap, index-weight, highly liquid register; TMPV is a bluechip and an index constituent.
  • Balance sheet: the group carries net debt of ~₹30,700 Cr at March 2026 — a big change of character from the net-cash CV business. JLR had deleveraged hard (eliminating ~£5bn of debt since 2022 and briefly turning net-cash), but FY26's cash-flow hit from the cyberattack, tariffs and heavy EV capex pushed JLR back to a modest net-debt position (£2.6bn). The India PV business, by contrast, is net cash (~₹6.7K Cr).
Why the balance sheet is a JLR story too

Notice the contrast with the truck company: the CV entity is net cash, while TMPV carries net debt. That's because JLR is a capital-devouring business — it is spending an announced ~£18bn multi-year investment programme on new models and the shift to electric, and a rough patch in cash generation (like FY26's) quickly swings it from net-cash back to net-debt. So JLR's balance sheet is a swing factor of its own: in good years it deleverages and could even fund buybacks; in bad years, capex plus weak profit eats the cash and debt creeps up. The India business helps, but it's too small to move the group's net-debt needle much. Watch JLR's free cash flow and net-debt trajectory as closely as its margin.

04

The JLR engine: how a luxury-car business earns

The core of the whole company — how Range Rovers and Defenders make (or lose) money.

Because JLR is ~85% of the company, you cannot understand the stock without understanding how a luxury carmaker earns. It is a different animal from a mass-market carmaker: it sells fewer vehicles at far higher prices and margins, so its economics turn on brand strength, model desirability and the price mix — not raw volume. JLR's strategy over the last few years has been a deliberate move up-market: fewer, richer, more profitable models.

Even as total volumes fell ~23% in FY26, JLR pushed the mix toward its highest-margin models. This "shrink to premium" strategy is why JLR's underlying margins can recover faster than its volumes — the cars it does sell are the profitable ones.
Why luxury-auto economics are "high-margin but high-drama"

A mass-market carmaker earns a thin margin on huge volume; a luxury maker like JLR earns a fat margin on modest volume. In a good year (FY25) JLR ran an ~8.5% adjusted EBIT margin and threw off £2.5bn of pre-tax profit — genuinely strong for a carmaker. But the flip side of high margins on desirability is high sensitivity: luxury demand is discretionary (rich buyers defer when confidence dips), the model cycle is lumpy (an ageing model line droops until the refresh arrives), currencies swing the numbers, and a single shock — a China tax change, a US tariff, a cyberattack — can hammer a year. That's the JLR bargain: when it's good, it's very good; when it's bad, the whole company's profit disappears. FY26 was the bad kind (§06).

The Jaguar "Reimagine" reinvention — a bet within the bet

The Jaguar brand (as opposed to Land Rover / Range Rover) is being completely relaunched as an all-electric, ultra-luxury marque — pausing its old model range entirely and returning with a small number of radical, ~£100,000+ EVs (an electric GT first). The logic: Jaguar had become an also-ran; rather than compete in the crowded premium segment, JLR is repositioning it far up-market against Bentley/Porsche-tier rivals. This is high-risk, high-reward — the wind-down of old Jaguar models cost volume in FY26 (a deliberate air-pocket), and the reinvented Jaguar must now prove that buyers will pay six figures for an electric Jaguar. Land Rover / Range Rover / Defender remain the profit core and are getting their own electric versions (Range Rover Electric) on flexible platforms.

05

JLR the swing factor: why this stock is a luxury-cycle + forex + tariff bet

The defining force — read this once and the share-price behaviour makes sense.

If you learn only one thing here, learn this: TMPV's earnings are a leveraged bet on JLR, and JLR's earnings are themselves a leveraged bet on three volatile forces — global luxury demand, currencies, and trade policy. Layer a small-margin, capital-heavy business (autos) on top of a discretionary, global, forex-exposed product (luxury SUVs) and you get an earnings stream that can swing violently year to year. That is the essential character of this stock.

The three forces that move JLR (and therefore the stock)

  • Luxury demand & the model cycle. Range Rovers and Defenders are discretionary purchases by affluent buyers. When wealth, confidence and stock markets are strong, order books swell; when they wobble (especially in China, a key luxury market), demand softens fast. On top of the macro sits the model cycle: a fresh Range Rover launch drives a wave of demand and pricing power; an ageing line-up between refreshes droops. Both cycles are lumpy and hard to forecast.
  • Currency (forex). JLR earns a structural volatility a domestic carmaker never faces. It builds mostly in the UK (cost base in £) and sells in dollars, euros, yuan and pounds. A stronger pound hurts translated profit; a weaker pound helps. Currency moves alone can swing hundreds of millions of pounds of profit in a year — noise the underlying business can't control.
  • Trade policy — the new US-tariff risk. The US is one of JLR's largest and most profitable markets, and JLR ships US-bound cars from the UK. In 2025 the US imposed an import tariff on UK-made cars, forcing JLR to pause US shipments in April 2025 and then absorb higher costs. Tariffs directly tax JLR's most valuable export flow — a structural headwind that didn't exist a few years ago and is now a permanent line item in the risk assessment.
Why JLR's profit is so much more volatile than its revenue — operating leverage

Here's the amplifier. JLR has enormous fixed costs — factories, tooling, engineering, the £18bn investment programme. When volumes are high, those fixed costs are spread over many cars and margins are fat; when volumes fall (as they did ~23% in FY26), the same fixed costs are spread over fewer cars, and margin collapses far faster than revenue. That's why JLR's revenue fell ~21% in FY26 but its adjusted EBIT margin fell from 8.5% to 0.7% — a near-total wipeout of operating profit on a "only" one-fifth drop in sales. This operating leverage cuts both ways: a recovery in volumes should snap margins back up sharply. It is the mathematical reason the stock is a leveraged bet on JLR's fortunes — small changes in luxury demand produce big changes in profit.

Owning this stock means owning a UK luxury-SUV maker's exposure to Chinese wealth, the US dollar, British trade policy and its own model calendar — with an Indian growth business riding shotgun.
The India business is the shock-absorber that isn't (yet) big enough. In theory, a growing, net-cash Indian car business should smooth JLR's swings. In practice, at ~15% of revenue it is simply too small to offset a JLR downturn — FY26 proved it: India grew 20%+ and the group's profit still collapsed. Over time, if India (and especially EV) scales, it could genuinely diversify the earnings. For now, though, the India story changes the long-term case far more than it cushions the current-year earnings. Don't let a good India headline distract from a bad JLR quarter.
06

The FY26 storm: three shocks at JLR in one year

A case study in why this is a swing stock — and how to read a "bad" year.

FY26 (year to March 2026) is the perfect illustration of the JLR swing factor: three separate shocks hit JLR at once, and the group's operating profit near-vanished even as India boomed. Understanding what happened — and how much of it was one-off vs structural — is essential to reading the stock, because the market's whole debate is "how much of FY26 was temporary?"

The three shocks, unpicked

  • The cyberattack (mostly one-off). On 1 September 2025 a cyberattack — later attributed to a Russia-linked group and called the costliest hack in UK history (~£1.9bn to the UK economy) — halted JLR's global production for ~5 weeks, taking out ~5,000 vehicles a week at its UK plants (Solihull, Halewood, Wolverhampton). This directly destroyed a big chunk of H2 volume. It is a one-time event — Q4 FY26 showed a strong bounce-back — but it dominated the year's shortfall.
  • US tariffs (structural). New US import tariffs on UK-made cars forced JLR to pause US shipments in April 2025 and then absorb higher costs on its most profitable export market. Unlike the cyberattack, this is a persistent headwind — it re-rates JLR's US economics downward until (and unless) it is renegotiated or offset.
  • China weakness + luxury tax (cyclical/structural). China wholesales fell ~29.8% — the worst regional drop — hit by a soft luxury market and a Chinese luxury-consumption tax. Part cyclical (China's slowdown), part structural (tax + local-EV competition eroding foreign luxury).
  • The Jaguar wind-down (deliberate). JLR intentionally stopped selling most old Jaguar models ahead of the all-electric relaunch — a self-inflicted volume air-pocket that will reverse as new Jaguars arrive.
How to read a "bad" year at a swing business — one-off vs structural

The analyst's job in FY26 is to separate the temporary damage from the permanent. The cyberattack and the Jaguar wind-down are one-off/deliberate — they should reverse, and indeed Q4 already showed JLR back to a ~9% EBIT margin. The US tariff and (partly) the China luxury tax are structural — they lower JLR's normalised earning power going forward. So the bull reads FY26 as a trough distorted by transient shocks, arguing the "real" JLR still earns a mid-single-digit-plus margin once the noise clears. The bear reads it as evidence of fragility — a business so exposed to external shocks (hacks, tariffs, China) that a single bad year can erase all its profit, and whose structural headwinds (tariffs, China EV competition) are only growing. The truth is somewhere between, and the FY27 recovery — how fast volumes and margins rebound — is the number that settles the argument.

07

The India PV franchise: the design-led SUV comeback

The smaller but faster-growing half — and a genuine turnaround story.

If JLR is the earnings, the India passenger-vehicle business is the growth. A decade ago Tata's car business was a struggling also-ran; today it is India's #3 carmaker (behind Maruti Suzuki and Mahindra, having overtaken Hyundai in FY26) with a design-led, SUV-heavy, safety-first line-up that turned the brand around. In FY26 it grew revenue +20.7% to ₹58,465 Cr on record volume of 6.4 lakh+ units (+15%), holding ~13.6–14% market share.

Why the India business works now — the SUV + safety + design formula

  • SUVs, done right. Like the whole industry, India has shifted to SUVs — and Tata rode it with the right products. The Punch (micro-SUV) has been its best-seller two years running; the Nexon is a compact-SUV mainstay; the Harrier and Safari anchor the mid-size end (now with petrol variants added to widen appeal); the new Sierra revives an iconic nameplate. A richer SUV mix lifts realisations and margin.
  • Design & brand transformation. Tata's "Impact" design language turned the brand from utilitarian to desirable — a genuine perception shift that let it command better pricing and win younger buyers.
  • Safety leadership. Tata leaned hard into 5-star crash-safety ratings (Global NCAP), making "the safest cars in India" a marketing pillar — a real differentiator with family buyers.
  • Multi-powertrain strategy. Tata sells petrol, diesel, CNG (~27% of its mix) and EV (~12–14% of its mix) — hedging fuel-price and policy shifts and widening its addressable buyer base.
Why the India business matters more than its size suggests

At ~15% of revenue, India can't rescue a bad JLR year — but it is the part of TMPV with the clearest long runway. India's car market is under-penetrated (few cars per 1,000 people vs developed markets) and growing structurally as incomes rise; SUVs and EVs are the fastest-growing slices; and Tata has a credible top-3 franchise with its own factories, a wide dealer network, and a captive-finance/charging ecosystem via the Tata group. So the India business is the optionality in the stock: if it keeps compounding volume and lifts its still-thin margins, it gradually becomes a bigger, steadier share of group profit — diversifying away from JLR's drama. The bull's long-term case leans heavily on India (and EV) growing into a much larger slice over the next decade.

08

EV leadership under pressure: the moat that's eroding

Tata built India's EV market — and is now watching rivals crowd in.

Tata was India's electric-car pioneer and long-time runaway leader — for years it was the Indian EV market, with a share above 60–70% at its peak. That first-mover dominance is a real asset. But FY26 laid bare a new reality: Tata's EV share is eroding fast as well-funded rivals (MG, Mahindra) launch competitive electric SUVs. The EV story is now "still #1, but the moat is under siege" — a genuine strategic challenge, not a footnote.

The EV moat — what Tata has, and what's under attack

  • What Tata built (the moat): a first-mover lead, the widest EV line-up (Nexon EV, Punch EV, Tiago EV, Curvv EV), 250,000+ cumulative EVs sold, an early charging-infrastructure push via the Tata group (Tata Power), and the manufacturing/cost advantage of scale in a young market. The Nexon EV remains India's best-selling electric car.
  • What's attacking it: MG's Windsor (a smartly-priced EV with a battery-subscription model) and Mahindra's BE 6 / XEV series (bold, well-reviewed electric SUVs) have grabbed share fast; more launches from Maruti, Hyundai and others are coming. As the market grows, every carmaker now wants in — so Tata's early-mover pond is filling with competitors.
  • Tata's response: new models (Curvv EV, Harrier EV, and a dedicated Sierra EV), a new EV-only platform, lifetime battery warranties, and a stated ambition to keep EVs a growing share of its mix (targeting ~30% EV mix by ~FY31). The question is whether it can defend leadership on product and price now that rivals are credible.
Growth in a bigger pie vs a shrinking slice — why both can be true

The EV numbers look contradictory until you separate volume from share. India's EV market is growing rapidly (industry EV car sales jumped ~84% in FY26), so Tata's EV volumes rose ~43% — absolute growth is healthy. But because the market is growing even faster and new rivals are launching aggressively, Tata's share fell from ~53% to ~39% — its relative dominance is eroding. For a first-mover, this is the classic transition: from "owning a nascent market" to "competing in a maturing one". The bull says a ~39% share of a fast-growing market, with the widest range and the Tata brand, is still a powerful position. The bear says the trajectory matters more than the level — a leader losing 14 points of share in a year is a moat visibly weakening, and defending it will cost margin (price cuts, more incentives). The EV story shifts from "unassailable leader" to "leader that now has to fight."

09

Cyclicality & how you value a three-part car company

Why the "right" multiple for this stock is genuinely hard to pin down.

Autos are cyclical, capital-heavy, forex-exposed businesses, and this one is unusually hard to value because it is really three businesses with different economics, cycles and geographies stapled together. The market's core challenge — and the source of the debate over the stock — is figuring out what "normalised" earnings look like when the biggest part (JLR) just had an abnormal year.

Why it's cyclical — and cyclical in several ways at once

  • Operating leverage. Both JLR and India carry heavy fixed costs (plants, R&D, the £18bn programme), so profits swing far more than revenue — margins soar when volumes are high and collapse when they fall (FY26 was the collapse).
  • Multiple overlapping cycles. JLR rides the global luxury cycle (US, China, Europe) and the model cycle; India rides the domestic car cycle; the EV business rides the EV-adoption + subsidy cycle. These don't move together, which both diversifies and complicates the earnings.
  • Forex & policy overlay. On top of the demand cycles sit currency swings and trade policy (US tariffs, China tax) that can move profit independently of underlying demand.
Why trailing P/E is useless here — and what to look at instead

You cannot value this stock on its reported trailing P/E. FY26's reported "net profit" of ~₹82,645 Cr is dominated by a one-off non-cash demerger gain of ~₹82,616 Cr, so a screen showing a P/E of "~1.5×" is meaningless — it's dividing the price by a distorted, one-time number. The real operating picture was a weak year: PBT-before-exceptionals of just ₹2,519 Cr. So the market values TMPV on a "through-the-cycle" or "normalised" basis instead: it asks what JLR + India + EV can earn in a normal year (not FY26's trough, not FY25's peak), and applies a modest cyclical multiple to that — often cross-checked with a sum-of-the-parts (value JLR on its normalised margin/EV-to-sales, value the India PV + EV business separately, add net debt). The single hardest judgement is JLR's normalised margin: bulls pencil in a recovery toward mid-single-digit-plus EBIT as FY26's shocks fade; bears assume tariffs and China keep it structurally lower. That one assumption drives most of the valuation.

The mental checklist for valuing TMPV: (1) Ignore reported PAT this year — it's an accounting artefact. (2) Normalise JLR — what EBIT margin and volume is realistic post-tariff, post-cyber, mid-model-cycle? (3) Value India + EV separately — a growing top-3 franchise with a still-thin but rising margin, deserving a growth multiple. (4) Subtract net debt (~₹30,700 Cr). (5) Sanity-check against the price (~₹1.28 lakh Cr market cap, ~₹345/share). The stock's cheapness or dearness lives almost entirely in step (2): your view on JLR's normalised earning power.

How to think about the valuation

At roughly ₹1.28 lakh crore of market value (~₹345/share) the stock trades at about 1.15× book — a subdued multiple that tells you the market is not paying up for FY26's reported profit (which it correctly ignores as an accounting one-off) and is instead pricing in a cautious, normalised view of the future. The bull case: JLR's FY26 was a shock-distorted trough — the cyberattack and Jaguar wind-down reverse, Q4 already showed a 9%+ margin, and a recovery in volumes snaps operating leverage back the right way; meanwhile India + EV keep compounding, so normalised earnings are far above FY26 and the stock is cheap on through-cycle profit. The bear case: JLR faces structural headwinds that won't reverse — US tariffs, China luxury tax and local EV competition, an ageing model cycle, and a costly, uncertain Jaguar reinvention — while its India EV moat erodes; so "normalised" JLR earnings are permanently lower than the past, and a modest multiple on lower earnings is fair, not cheap. As with any cyclical, the whole debate reduces to one number: JLR's normalised margin — which is exactly why §05 and §06 are the master variables.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A global luxury swing stock with an Indian growth engine — priced for a JLR recovery that must actually arrive.

The demerger delivered a clean, investable pure-play on the "global cars" half of Tata Motors: a British luxury-SUV maker (JLR) that is the earnings engine, wrapped around a genuinely improving Indian car-and-EV business. The India story is real and growing; the JLR story is powerful but volatile, and FY26 was a bruising reminder of how much can go wrong at once. The debate is about two things: JLR's normalised earning power (is FY26 a trough or the new lower normal?) and whether India + EV can scale into a real second pillar.

The whole thesis is one question: was FY26's JLR collapse a shock-driven trough that recovers — or proof that tariffs, China and its own reinvention have permanently lowered what this business can earn?

What to watch each quarter

  • JLR volumes & margin recovery — the master signal; does EBIT margin climb back toward mid-single-digits-plus as the cyber/tariff/Jaguar noise clears (bull), or stay stuck low (bear)?
  • US tariff & China — any tariff relief/renegotiation, and whether China luxury demand stabilises.
  • JLR net debt & free cash flow — with £18bn of capex, does JLR generate cash and deleverage, or does debt keep creeping up?
  • India PV share & margin — can Tata hold #3, lift its still-thin margins, and keep the SUV momentum?
  • India EV share — does the slide from ~53% to ~39% stabilise, or keep eroding as MG/Mahindra/others attack?
  • The Jaguar relaunch — do the radical ~£100k+ electric Jaguars actually sell?

The bull case

  • FY26 was a shock-distorted trough — the cyberattack and Jaguar wind-down are one-off/deliberate and reverse; Q4 already snapped back to a ~9% JLR EBIT margin.
  • Operating leverage cuts both ways — a recovery in JLR volumes should lift margins sharply, so normalised earnings are far above FY26's trough.
  • Premiumised, richer mix — Range Rover/RR Sport/Defender at 76.5% of volume means the cars JLR does sell are its most profitable; JLR had been deleveraging strongly pre-shock.
  • India is a genuine growth engine — #3 and rising, +20.7% revenue, a design-led SUV franchise with a long, under-penetrated runway.
  • Still India's #1 EV maker — widest range, best-seller (Nexon EV), Tata-group charging ecosystem; EV volumes still grew 43%.
  • Cheap on normalised earnings — market ignores the distorted reported PAT and prices a cautious view; a JLR recovery re-rates the stock.

The bear case

  • JLR is the whole company and it's fragile — ~85% of revenue in a discretionary, forex-exposed luxury business that a single hack/tariff/China shock can wipe to breakeven.
  • Structural headwinds won't reverse — US tariffs on UK cars, China luxury tax + local-EV competition, and an ageing model cycle may keep normalised JLR margins permanently lower.
  • The Jaguar reinvention is a big, unproven bet — a total relaunch as ~£100k+ EVs, with the old range already killed off — it could flop.
  • Net debt & heavy capex — £18bn programme against weak cash flow pushed JLR back to net debt; the group carries ~₹30,700 Cr net debt.
  • EV moat eroding — India EV share fell from ~53% to ~39% in a year as MG and Mahindra surged; defending it will cost margin.
  • India can't rescue a bad JLR year — at ~15% of revenue it's too small to offset a JLR downturn, as FY26 proved.
The bottom line

Tata Motors Passenger Vehicles is, in practice, a bet on Jaguar Land Rover — a lucrative but volatile British luxury-SUV maker — with a genuinely improving Indian car-and-EV business riding alongside. The India story is the more attractive growth narrative (a top-3, design-led SUV franchise with a long runway and EV leadership), but at ~15% of revenue it can't yet steer the ship. The ship is JLR, and FY26 showed how rough the seas can get: a cyberattack, US tariffs and a China slump combined to erase almost all the group's operating profit in a single year — even as reported profit was flattered by a giant one-off demerger accounting gain that you should ignore entirely. The quality of JLR's luxury brands and India's franchise is not in doubt. What is in doubt is JLR's normalised earning power — whether FY26 was a shock-driven trough that recovers (the bull) or evidence that tariffs, China and its own reinvention have permanently lowered the bar (the bear). Get that right and this is a cheap, recovering global-luxury-plus-India-growth story; get it wrong and it's a fragile, capital-heavy, single-asset bet dressed up as a diversified carmaker. The two numbers that decide it: JLR's margin recovery (§05–§06) and whether India + EV scale into a real second pillar (§07–§08).
