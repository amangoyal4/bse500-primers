Zensar Technologies, the mid-cap that fixed its margins and still has to find its growth

A complete walk through the RPG Group's Pune-based IT services company — what "application development and modernisation" actually means when you strip the jargon, why a firm doing $644 million of revenue is a minnow in a market where the whales do twenty times that, and why the single most interesting fact about Zensar is that it nearly doubled its profit over three years in which its dollar revenue barely moved at all.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Zensar Technologies is an Indian IT services company: it rents out engineering and software talent to large Western enterprises to build, modernise and run their business software. A bank needs its 20-year-old lending system rewritten to run in the cloud; an insurer wants a new claims portal; a manufacturer wants its warehouse data pulled into one analytics platform. Zensar wins that piece of work, staffs it with engineers in Pune, Hyderabad, London, New Jersey and Johannesburg, and bills the client either by the hour or for a fixed deliverable. It is headquartered in Pune and controlled by the RP-Sanjiv Goenka (RPG) Group, the Kolkata/Mumbai industrial house better known for tyres and carbon black.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • It is small — genuinely small — in a market of giants. FY26 revenue was $643.7 million. TCS does more than that in a fortnight. Accenture does it in under four days. Zensar competes for the same enterprise technology budgets as firms twenty to a hundred times its size, which shapes everything: which deals it can bid for, how much it can charge, and how badly one lost client hurts.
  • The margin repair is done; the growth is not. Zensar's operating margin went from roughly the high single digits in FY23 to a steady 16.0% EBITDA in FY26 — a genuine, hard-won turnaround under a new chief executive. Over the same stretch, dollar revenue crawled: FY26 grew 3.1% in reported currency and just 1.7% in constant currency. The company got much better at converting a rupee of revenue into profit, without getting much better at winning more rupees.
  • The balance sheet is a fortress and the stock is cheap-ish. Net cash of $319.5 million — roughly ₹2,900 Cr, about a quarter of the entire market capitalisation of ~₹11,800 Cr (approx.) — sits against a P/E of around 15×, well below where large-cap Indian IT trades. That discount is the market's opinion on the growth problem, expressed as a number.
In plain terms

Zensar sells hours of skilled engineering, wrapped in project management and delivered against a contract. That is a fundamentally different business from the other technology-services names already in this library: eClerx sells analytical and data operations (a KPO), Firstsource runs entire business processes such as mortgage servicing (a BPM), and Intellect Design licenses its own banking software (a product company). Zensar does none of those. It writes and maintains the client's software, on the client's roadmap, for as long as the client keeps paying. There is no product to license, no process to own — only people, skills and the ability to keep winning the next statement of work.

Which is why size matters so much here. In a talent-arbitrage business, scale buys you cheaper delivery, deeper bench strength, the credibility to bid for a $200 million transformation, and the ability to absorb the loss of a big client without flinching. Zensar has none of that cushion. Its entire investment case rests on whether a well-run small firm can grow inside a market that structurally rewards the big.

01

IT services 101: what these companies actually do

Strip the jargon and the model is startlingly simple — which is both its charm and its curse.

Every large company on earth runs on software it did not buy off a shelf. A retailer's pricing engine, a bank's loan-origination workflow, an insurer's policy-administration system, a manufacturer's plant-scheduling tool — these are custom systems, often decades old, patched by hundreds of hands. Someone has to build them, upgrade them, move them to the cloud, connect them to each other and keep them running at 3 a.m. on a Sunday. That "someone" is the IT services industry, and India supplies most of it.

The commercial logic is arbitrage plus specialisation. A senior software engineer in New Jersey costs an American bank a multiple of what an equivalently skilled engineer costs in Pune. An Indian services firm hires that Pune engineer, trains them on the bank's technology stack, bills the bank a rate that is still far below the American cost, and keeps the spread. Layer on process discipline, 24-hour coverage across time zones, and the ability to scale a team from five people to five hundred in a quarter, and you have the model that built a multi-hundred-billion-dollar export industry.

Why this is a good business and a hard one at the same time

The good part: almost no capital is required. There is no factory, no inventory, no plant to depreciate. Zensar's balance sheet is essentially people, receivables and a very large pile of cash. Returns on capital in Indian IT services are among the highest of any industry in the country, and the cash converts almost fully — which is why these firms pay out generous dividends rather than reinvest.

The hard part: nothing you build is yours. Every rupee of revenue must be re-won. A client can move a project to a rival at the end of a contract, insource it, or simply cancel it when budgets tighten. There is no patent, no installed base, no switching cost beyond the pain of transition. The industry's growth is therefore hostage to somebody else's spending decisions — and, increasingly, to whether an AI tool can do in ten minutes what previously took a billable engineer a week.

Where Zensar sits in the pecking order

Indian IT is conventionally read in three tiers. The large caps — TCS, Infosys, HCLTech, Wipro — run $10bn to $30bn of revenue and hundreds of thousands of employees. The upper mid-tier — LTIMindtree, Mphasis, Coforge, Persistent, Hexaware — sit between roughly $1bn and $5bn. Then there is Zensar's cohort: firms below $1bn, which the market watches for growth rate rather than heft. Above all of them sit the global consultancies, Accenture and Cognizant, which bid for the same work with far stronger front-end consulting muscle.

02

What Zensar actually sells: two segments, one delivery machine

The service catalogue, decoded — and how a deal turns into revenue.

Zensar reports its business in two operating segments. Digital & Application Services (DAS) is the dominant one — the engineering work: designing digital products and customer experiences, building and maintaining applications, data engineering and analytics, and the newer AI-engineering work. Cloud Infrastructure & Security Services (CIS) is the smaller partner: running the plumbing — cloud environments, networks, endpoints, security operations. DAS is the growth and margin engine; CIS is stickier, more annuity-like, and often the wedge that gets Zensar into an account before the bigger application work follows.

The service lines, in plain language

  • Experience services — research, strategy and design for how a customer actually uses a bank app or a retailer's checkout. This is the closest Zensar gets to consulting, and it sits at the front of the funnel: whoever designs the thing usually gets to build it.
  • Advanced engineering — the actual software construction, now increasingly badged as generative-AI engineering: building AI agents and copilots into a client's own systems rather than merely writing code by hand.
  • Data engineering & analytics — pulling a client's scattered data into one governed platform so it can be reported on and, latterly, fed to models. A near-universal enterprise project of this decade.
  • Cloud infrastructure & security — migrating workloads to AWS/Azure, then running and defending them. Lower-margin than engineering, but it renews.
  • Application management — the unglamorous, indispensable core: keeping thousands of existing applications patched, compliant and available. Multi-year contracts, predictable revenue, and the base on which everything else is cross-sold.
The bit outsiders always miss: revenue is a headcount equation

For a services firm, revenue is roughly billable people × utilisation × realised rate. That identity explains almost every quarterly surprise in the sector. Revenue can fall because a client cut the team size (fewer people), because engineers came off one project before the next started (lower utilisation), or because a contract was renewed at a discount (lower rate). Margin, meanwhile, is driven by a different set of dials — the mix of juniors to seniors, how much work is done offshore in India versus expensively onsite in the client's office, subcontractor spend, and the fixed cost of sales and administration.

The uncomfortable implication: margin and revenue are driven by largely separate levers. A management team can improve one while the other deteriorates. Zensar has spent three years proving exactly that — and it is the reason the rest of this primer keeps circling back to a single asymmetry.

How the work is priced

Roughly speaking, a maintenance contract might run for three to five years at a fixed monthly fee; a modernisation programme might be a fixed-price engagement lasting twelve to eighteen months; a staff-augmentation arrangement is billed by the hour and can be switched off with thirty days' notice. The blend matters enormously for stability. A firm heavy on hourly staff augmentation looks great in a boom and evaporates in a downturn; one heavy on multi-year managed services grows slowly but sleeps well. Zensar's push toward larger, longer, outcome-oriented contracts — visible in that record Q4 order book — is an explicit attempt to shift the blend toward the second kind.

03

The company, the RPG group, and who controls it

An industrial house's software arm — with a corporate birthday that will surprise you.

Zensar Technologies is a Goenka family company, part of the RPG Group chaired by Harsh Goenka. That places it in unusual company: its sibling businesses make tyres (CEAT), carbon black (PCBL), power cables and transmission equipment (KEC International), and run business processes for Western clients (Firstsource Solutions). RPG owns an IT services firm the way other Indian houses own a cement plant — as one line in a diversified industrial portfolio rather than as the family's defining asset.

The surprise most investors miss: Zensar's corporate entity was incorporated in 1963, as International Computers Indian Manufacture — a joint venture with Britain's ICL to make computers in India. That makes the legal shell older than TCS (1968) and far older than Infosys (1981) or Wipro's IT arm. India's oldest listed computing company is not one of its giants; it is a sub-$1bn mid-cap in Pune that changed its name to Zensar in 2001. Longevity, in this industry, has conferred remarkably little advantage.

Ownership and governance

  • Promoters (RPG / Goenka family entities) hold roughly 49% of the equity, with no pledged shares reported. That is a controlling but not overwhelming stake — enough for unambiguous control, small enough that the remaining half of the register is genuinely public float.
  • The rest is spread across domestic and foreign institutions and retail holders. For a company of this size, institutional attention is thinner than for a large-cap, which means the stock can move sharply on a single quarter.
  • Leadership matters more than usual here. The margin turnaround is closely identified with the current chief executive and the operating team assembled since 2023. In a business whose only assets walk out of the building each evening, a leadership change is a genuine risk event, not a footnote.
  • Capital allocation is shareholder-friendly and conservative: no debt, a very large cash balance, and a meaningful dividend (₹12.60 per share proposed for FY26). The open question is whether that cash pile eventually funds an acquisition — the classic mid-cap route to buying growth that cannot be won organically.
Four RPG companies, one library

Anyone reading across this primer collection will notice something odd: the Goenka industrial house now appears four separate times. CEAT makes tyres. PCBL makes carbon black — the black powder that goes into tyres. Firstsource runs mortgage servicing and healthcare claims processing for American clients. And Zensar writes enterprise software. The group's cash flows therefore straddle rubber commodity cycles, Indian auto demand, US mortgage rates and global enterprise technology budgets — four almost entirely uncorrelated drivers under one family. For an investor, the practical consequence is that "RPG exposure" is not a theme; the four businesses share a controlling shareholder and essentially nothing else.

Where the money comes from: four verticals, one dominant

Banking pays the bills. Everything else has been shrinking.

Zensar organises itself around industry verticals, because enterprise buyers value domain knowledge — an engineer who already understands claims adjudication is worth more to an insurer than one who does not. Four verticals carry the business, and their recent trajectories diverge sharply enough that the mix itself has become part of the investment argument.

Banking, financial services and insurance — the engine

Close to half of Zensar's revenue comes from banks, capital-markets firms, payments companies and insurers. It is also the only vertical that grew properly in FY26: BFSI rose roughly 12.5% year-on-year in Q4, and about 2.2% sequentially. Financial services is the natural home for application modernisation — these are institutions with the oldest systems, the deepest regulatory obligations and the largest technology budgets in the corporate world. An insurer running policy administration on a mainframe cannot simply buy its way out; it has to hire engineers to rebuild it.

The flip side is cyclicality of a particular kind. Bank technology budgets are set annually and slashed quickly when rates, credit losses or capital ratios turn against them. A vertical that is 46% of revenue and concentrated among a handful of large accounts is a source of both the company's recent growth and its sharpest single risk.

Manufacturing and consumer services — the drag

Roughly a quarter of revenue comes from manufacturers, retailers and consumer-facing businesses. This block declined in FY26 — about −2.2% year-on-year and −3.3% sequentially in Q4. Discretionary technology spending in retail and manufacturing has been among the weakest pockets of the global market: when a consumer business is defending margin, the store-experience redesign and the supply-chain analytics project are exactly what get deferred.

Telecom, media and technology — the sharp decline

The hi-tech and telecom vertical fell hardest: roughly −16% year-on-year in Q4 FY26. Selling engineering services to technology companies is a double-edged trade. When they are expanding, they outsource enormous volumes of product engineering; when they cut — as much of the sector has, aggressively — they cut vendors first and their own staff second. An 18% vertical shrinking at that rate subtracts roughly three percentage points from group growth on its own, which is most of the reason group constant-currency growth was 1.7% rather than something respectable.

Healthcare and life sciences — flat, and strategically interesting

At around 11% of revenue and roughly flat year-on-year (−0.3% in Q4), healthcare is Zensar's smallest named vertical. It is also where the structural demand argument is strongest — payer and provider systems in the United States are famously fragmented, heavily regulated and overdue for modernisation. That this vertical has not grown for Zensar, in a market where several peers have expanded healthcare books aggressively, is a fair thing for a sceptic to point at.

Read the verticals as a single sentence

In FY26, one vertical grew at double digits and three did not grow at all. Group revenue rose 1.7% in constant currency because banking carried manufacturing, hi-tech and healthcare on its back. That is a fragile construction. It means the growth question for Zensar is not abstract — it resolves into two very concrete sub-questions: can BFSI keep compounding at low double digits, and can even one of the other three stop shrinking? If the answer to both is yes, group growth mechanically moves to mid-single digits without anything heroic happening. If BFSI merely normalises to the group average, FY27 looks like FY26.

05

The South Africa anomaly

A Pune software firm whose fortunes are partly set in Johannesburg — and why that is stranger than it sounds.

Almost every Indian IT services company has the same geographic shape: a large majority of revenue from North America, a meaningful slice from the UK and continental Europe, a token line for "Rest of World", and that is that. Zensar breaks the pattern. Roughly one rupee in eight comes from South Africa — a market most Indian technology companies barely acknowledge, and one that has essentially no presence in the peer set's disclosures.

The South African business is a legacy of decades of relationship-building with local banks, retailers, telecoms operators and public-sector bodies — a franchise Zensar built when few Indian firms bothered, and which it has held. It is a genuinely different business from the American one: smaller contracts, more onsite delivery, local currency billing, and a client base that values a long-standing incumbent over a new global logo.

In FY26 this odd corner of the portfolio was one of the better performers, with Africa growing roughly 14.4% year-on-year in Q4 even as the United States declined about 2.2%. Europe grew around 4.3%. In other words, the two geographies most investors ignore both outgrew the one they focus on.

What the South Africa line really buys — and costs

The benefit is diversification of a useful kind: a revenue stream whose drivers (South African bank IT budgets, local telecom competition, rand-denominated contracts) have almost nothing to do with US enterprise spending cycles. When American discretionary budgets froze, this book kept growing. That is worth something in a business as cyclical as this one.

The cost is threefold. First, currency: the rand is among the more volatile emerging-market currencies, and reported growth from this geography can swing on translation alone. Second, margin: a market with more onsite delivery and smaller deals is typically less profitable than a large offshore-leveraged American contract. Third, narrative: analysts modelling Indian IT have no framework for a South African line item, so it tends to be ignored in good times and treated as a discount factor in bad ones. It is a real, cash-generating business that the market has never quite decided how to price.

A useful way to hold this: Zensar's geographic mix is not the standard Indian IT export template with a rounding error attached. It is roughly two-thirds a US business, one-fifth a European one, and one-eighth an African domestic services company that happens to be consolidated into a Pune-headquartered listco. Those three parts respond to different economies, different currencies and different competitive sets — which is why the group's revenue line has been steadier than its US line alone would suggest.
06

The lever that's been pulled: margin without growth

The single most important idea in this company's investment case — and it is arithmetic, not opinion.

Here is the fact that defines Zensar Technologies, stated as plainly as it can be stated. Between FY23 and FY26, the company's dollar revenue went from roughly $620 million to $643.7 million — a total of about 4% over three years, or a little over one percent a year. Over the same three years, its EBITDA margin went from roughly 9% to 16.0%. Profit after tax rose several-fold. Nearly all of the improvement in this company's earnings came from doing the same amount of work more cheaply.

Why this happened — and why it was the right thing to do

The margin repair was not an accounting trick. It came from a specific, identifiable list of operating actions taken from 2023 onward: reducing expensive subcontractors and replacing them with own employees; recruiting more freshers and rebalancing the pyramid so that fewer costly senior engineers sit on work a junior can do; lifting utilisation so fewer people sit on the bench between projects; moving work offshore from expensive onsite locations; and walking away from contracts that could not be made profitable. Attrition fell to 9.8%, which quietly helped everything else — replacing a departing engineer costs real money and real client goodwill.

Those actions were correct and overdue. A company earning single-digit operating margins in an industry where competent operators earn the high teens was leaking value. Fixing it created a large amount of genuine, permanent earnings.

The asymmetry, and why it is the whole thesis

Cost is a lever the management team controls. Growth is not. A chief executive can decide, unilaterally, to hire more freshers, cut subcontractors and raise utilisation — and the margin will respond within a few quarters. No such decision exists for revenue. Winning share from TCS, Infosys, Accenture and Cognizant requires thousands of other people — client CIOs, procurement committees, budget cycles — to choose Zensar over a firm they will never be criticised for choosing. That is why margin turnarounds in IT services arrive quickly and growth turnarounds arrive late, if at all.

Which brings us to the arithmetic that settles this stock. Management's stated ambition is to move the operating margin from the current mid-teens toward the high teens. Suppose that succeeds completely and the EBITDA margin goes from 16% to 18%. That is roughly 12–13% more profit — once. It is a step, not a slope. By contrast, revenue compounding at 8% a year delivers that same 12% of extra profit every eighteen months, forever, and keeps delivering it. The cost lever has perhaps 200 basis points of travel left in it. The growth lever has no ceiling and has barely been touched.

This is why "Zensar improved its margins again this quarter" is, at this point, close to a non-event, while "Zensar grew 5% in constant currency" would be a genuine re-rating trigger. The market has already paid for the margin story — that is what took the stock from its FY23 lows. It has explicitly declined to pay for a growth story, which is what the roughly 15× earnings multiple represents against large-cap Indian IT trading materially higher.

The uncomfortable version: a services firm that holds its margin while its revenue is flat is, in real terms, shrinking. Wage inflation runs a few percent a year; so does client-side price pressure on renewals. Holding 16% while growing 1.7% means the operating machine has to run faster each year merely to stand still. The margin cannot be defended indefinitely from a static revenue base — eventually the pyramid is as steep as it can get, utilisation is as high as it can safely go, and the only remaining source of profit growth is more work.
07

Sub-scale in a giant's game

What being a $644M firm actually costs you when the tender is open to everyone.

Scale in IT services is not vanity. It changes what a company can sell, at what price, and with what resilience. When a Fortune 200 insurer puts out a five-year, $300 million application-estate transformation, it draws up a shortlist. Zensar is structurally unlikely to be on it — not because its engineers are worse, but because a contract of that size would represent nearly half of its annual revenue, and no procurement committee awards that concentration risk to a vendor who would be transformed by winning it.

That final row is not a consolation prize — it is the entire commercial rationale for mid-cap IT existing at all. Coforge, Persistent and Hexaware have all, at various points, grown considerably faster than the large caps precisely by being nimble, deeply specialised, and willing to treat a mid-sized enterprise as a priority client rather than an afterthought. Being small is not automatically a disadvantage. Being small and undifferentiated is.

The mid-cap's only two winning strategies

There are exactly two ways a sub-$1bn IT services firm has historically outgrown the giants, and neither is "be generally good at everything". The first is vertical depth: know one industry so well — insurance platforms, say, or payments — that a client will hire you ahead of a firm ten times your size because you have already solved their exact problem eleven times. The second is account mining: land a mid-sized client, then grow inside it relentlessly until a $2 million relationship becomes a $30 million one, at which point renewal is near-automatic and the economics of scale arrive one account at a time.

Zensar's BFSI concentration and its record Q4 order book suggest it is attempting both. The verticals that shrank in FY26 suggest the attempt is not yet uniformly working. Whether the strategy is real or merely stated is answerable — not by rhetoric, but by watching whether the number of clients above the $5 million and $10 million annual revenue thresholds keeps rising, quarter after quarter, in the company's own disclosure.

The competitive set, named

Zensar meets different rivals in different rooms. Against TCS, Infosys, Wipro and HCLTech it loses on scale and wins, occasionally, on attention. Against LTIMindtree, Mphasis, Coforge, Persistent and Hexaware it competes on comparable terms — these are the true peers, and they set the benchmark for what growth a well-run mid-cap should be delivering. Against Accenture and Cognizant it faces firms with far deeper consulting front-ends that shape the client's strategy before the engineering work is even scoped. And in South Africa it competes against a largely local field on entirely different terms.

The uncomfortable benchmark is the middle group. When peers of similar size grow high single digits or better in constant currency and Zensar grows 1.7%, the gap cannot be explained away by macro — every one of them faces the same client budgets, the same rate environment and the same AI questions.

08

The AI question, both ways round

The technology that could shrink Zensar's core product — and the reason clients are calling.

Every firm that bills for software engineering hours now faces the same two-sided question. AI coding assistants demonstrably let a competent engineer produce more working code, faster. If a project that once needed twelve engineers for nine months now needs seven for six, then the revenue attached to that project falls — even though the work still gets done, and gets done by Zensar. That is the deflationary side. On the other side, every large enterprise now wants AI built into its own systems, which is a large, new, urgent category of engineering work that did not exist three years ago.

Zensar's public posture is aggressively on the second side. The company reports that roughly 85% of its workforce is AI-certified, positions itself as building "AI-native" offerings, and attributes a meaningful share of its record Q4 order book to AI-led engagements. Its advanced-engineering service line is explicitly framed around generative-AI engineering rather than conventional coding.

Why the deflation argument is not hypothetical

The deflation is easiest to see in exactly the work that has historically been Indian IT's ballast: routine application maintenance, testing, first-line support, code translation from one language to another. These are the tasks that AI tooling handles best, and they are typically the lowest-rate, highest-volume, most people-intensive part of a services contract. A client that renews a maintenance contract and asks for a 15% price reduction "because you're using AI now" is not making an unreasonable argument, and that conversation is happening across the industry.

The counter-argument runs like this: enterprise technology estates are so vast, so old and so poorly documented that the binding constraint has never been the raw speed of writing code. It has been understanding what the existing system does, what the regulator requires, who owns which data, and how to change any of it without breaking a business that runs 24 hours a day. AI compresses the typing. It does not, yet, compress the understanding. If that holds, productivity gains get absorbed into doing more projects rather than doing the same projects for less money — and the backlog of enterprise modernisation work is effectively bottomless.

How this actually resolves — and how you'll know

Both effects are real and they run simultaneously. The question is never "does AI help or hurt", it is which effect is larger for this particular firm's revenue mix. A company whose revenue is skewed toward high-volume, low-complexity maintenance is more exposed to the deflation. A company skewed toward design, domain-heavy modernisation and regulated industries is more exposed to the expansion.

The tell will not be found in any press release, because every services firm in the world is issuing the same one. It shows up in two places over several quarters: revenue per employee (if AI is expanding the pie, this rises as smaller teams deliver larger contracts; if AI is deflating pricing, it stagnates while headcount falls), and whether headcount and revenue decouple at all. Zensar's ~10,550 employees producing $643.7M works out to roughly $61,000 of revenue per head. Watch that number for three or four consecutive quarters. It is a far more honest reading of AI's effect on this business than any certification statistic.

The specific risk for a mid-cap: if AI compresses the value of routine application work, the firms best placed to survive are those with consulting-led front-ends that shape what gets built, and those with proprietary intellectual property. Zensar has neither in quantity — it is a pure services firm without a product portfolio and without Accenture's strategy practice. It must climb the value chain using capabilities it is building now, in a window that will not stay open indefinitely.
09

The order book, the large deal, and client mining

Where the evidence for a growth inflection would first appear — if it is going to appear.

Revenue is a lagging indicator in this industry. A contract signed today staffs up over one or two quarters and then delivers revenue for years. So the place to look for a turn is the order book — and in the fourth quarter of FY26, Zensar's order book did something it has not done before. It reached $401.8 million, up 122.9% sequentially, and included what the company describes as the largest deal in its history.

Put that against the revenue base for context: a single quarter's signings equal roughly 62% of an entire year's revenue. Even allowing that order books are multi-year and lumpy, and that one giant contract distorts a quarter, this is a materially different signing pattern from anything in the company's recent record.

Mining beats hunting

For a firm of this size, the cheapest growth is inside accounts it already has. Landing a new enterprise logo costs a fortune in pre-sales effort, proof-of-concept work and discounted first contracts, and takes years. Expanding an existing relationship — from the application-maintenance contract into the data platform, then into the cloud migration, then into a second business unit, then into a second country — costs almost nothing by comparison and carries far better margins because the delivery team already understands the estate.

This is why the disclosures worth tracking are the client-band counts: how many accounts generate more than $1 million, $5 million, $10 million and $20 million a year. Growth in the upper bands means mining is working. A rising count of small accounts with a static count of large ones means the firm is hunting hard and mining badly — an expensive way to run flat.

The mid-cap's structural client problem

Concentration cuts both ways and it cuts sharply at this size. Zensar's largest handful of clients account for a substantial share of revenue, which is normal for the tier — but it means a single top-ten client consolidating vendors, insourcing a function or being acquired can remove two or three percentage points of group growth in one announcement. Nothing management does can prevent it, and no amount of margin discipline offsets it.

That is also why the growth line has been so stubborn. Zensar has, in several recent years, won new business at a perfectly respectable rate — and then given most of it back through ramp-downs elsewhere. Net growth is the residual of two large, opposing flows, and for a company with only a few hundred meaningful clients, the residual is volatile. Gross wins tell you the sales engine works; only net constant-currency growth tells you the company is getting bigger.

Reading the balance sheet

There is essentially nothing to worry about here, which is itself worth stating. Zensar carries no meaningful debt and holds $319.5 million of net cash — around a quarter of its entire market value. Strip that out and the operating business is being valued at roughly eleven times earnings. For a debt-free, cash-generative, dividend-paying exporter with a 16% operating margin, that is not an expensive number by any conventional measure.

The cash also poses a strategic question the company has not yet answered. Roughly ₹2,900 Cr is enough to buy a business of real consequence — a specialist in a vertical Zensar lacks, or a European or American firm with the client relationships it cannot win organically. Mid-cap IT has a long history of buying growth this way, with results ranging from transformative to value-destroying. A large acquisition would be the loudest possible statement that management has concluded organic growth will not come fast enough.

Why the multiple is where it is

The discount to large-cap Indian IT — which typically trades at a substantial premium to 15× — is not a market oversight. It is a precise judgement: investors will pay a premium multiple for durable growth, and Zensar has not demonstrated any. Cost discipline, however excellent, earns a market multiple rather than a premium one, because it is finite and largely already banked.

The corollary is what makes this interesting. If constant-currency growth moved from 1.7% to, say, 6–7% and stayed there for a few quarters, the earnings would rise modestly — but the multiple applied to those earnings would likely move far more, because the company would have changed category from "well-managed but stagnant" to "growing mid-cap". Most of the potential return in this stock sits in that re-classification, not in the incremental profit itself.

The metric that settles it

Everything about Zensar reduces to one line in one table: constant-currency revenue growth, reported every quarter, with the 16% EBITDA margin still intact beside it.

Not reported-currency growth, which the rupee can flatter. Not rupee revenue, which grew 7.7% in FY26 while the underlying business grew 1.7%. Not the order book, which is a promise. Constant-currency growth is the one number that cannot be dressed up, and it is the number that has been the company's persistent failure for years.

The specific test has two halves, and both must pass. First, constant-currency growth crossing and holding mid-single digits — call it 5% or better for three consecutive quarters, which would be the first sustained stretch of real expansion in years. Second, the EBITDA margin staying at or above 16% while it happens. Growth bought by discounting the record order book, showing up as revenue at a 13% margin, would not be a turnaround completed; it would be the turnaround traded away. The two numbers have to move in the same direction at the same time, and the company has never yet shown that it can make them.

11

Future, risks, and the bull/bear case

What has to go right, what could go wrong, and where a reasonable person could land.

Zensar enters FY27 in the best operational shape it has been in for a decade — margins restored and stable, attrition low, balance sheet immaculate, and a record order book behind it. It also enters FY27 having grown 1.7% in constant currency, with three of its four verticals shrinking and its largest geography in mild decline. Both of those sentences are true, and the whole debate is about which one describes the future.

The case for

  • The record order book is real evidence. $401.8M in one quarter, including the largest deal ever signed, is the leading indicator that would precede an inflection — and it lands 18–36 months of revenue that has not yet been recognised.
  • Margins are proven, not promised. Three consecutive years around the mid-teens, with 16.1% in the most recent quarter, establishes a floor. Any revenue growth from here drops through at attractive incremental economics.
  • BFSI is compounding. A 46% vertical growing 12.5% year-on-year is a genuine franchise, in the industry with the deepest modernisation backlog and the largest technology budgets.
  • The valuation asks very little. ~15× trailing, around 11× excluding net cash, with a dividend. Modest growth plus multiple normalisation toward the mid-cap peer group is a decent outcome without heroics.
  • ₹2,900 Cr of optionality. The cash can buy a vertical capability or a client base outright — a lever most sub-scale peers do not have.
  • Geographic diversification works. Africa +14.4% and Europe +4.3% offset a declining US in FY26. The odd-shaped map is a stabiliser, not a liability.

The case against

  • The growth problem is chronic, not cyclical. Roughly 1% a year of dollar revenue growth across three years, through both weak and recovering markets, points at competitive position rather than macro.
  • The cost lever is nearly exhausted. From 16%, perhaps 200bps remain. After that, profit growth requires revenue growth, and there is no substitute.
  • Sub-scale in a consolidating market. Enterprise clients keep shrinking vendor lists. A $644M firm is precisely the size that gets rationalised off a preferred-supplier panel.
  • Client and vertical concentration. BFSI at 46% and a concentrated top-client list mean one budget freeze or one consolidation can erase a year of hard-won growth.
  • AI cuts at the core product. Routine application development and maintenance — the industry's ballast — is the work most exposed to price deflation, and Zensar has no product IP or large consulting practice to fall back on.
  • Currency and macro. US and European enterprise budgets, the rupee, and the rand all sit outside management's control and all move the reported line.
  • Key-person dependency. The turnaround is closely identified with the leadership installed since 2023. Continuity is an unquantified but material risk.

What to actually watch, in order

  • Constant-currency revenue growth, every quarter. The single number. Anything sustained above 5% changes the story; anything below 3% confirms it.
  • EBITDA margin held at or above 16% while that growth arrives. Growth purchased with margin is not progress.
  • Conversion of the Q4 order book. Watch for the ramp appearing in reported revenue over FY27 — and for management commentary on start dates slipping.
  • The non-BFSI verticals. Manufacturing/consumer, hi-tech and healthcare all declined in FY26. One of them turning positive would do more for group growth than another 50bps of margin.
  • Client-band counts. Rising numbers of $5M+ and $10M+ accounts are the fingerprint of successful mining.
  • Revenue per employee. Around $61,000 today. The cleanest available read on whether AI is expanding or deflating this business.
  • What happens to the cash. A large acquisition would materially change the risk profile in either direction.
The bottom line

Zensar spent three years fixing the half of the business a determined management team can fix by itself. It cut costs, rebuilt the pyramid, held on to its people and doubled its operating margin without needing a single new client to say yes. That work is finished, and it was worth doing — it is why the company earns roughly ₹800 Cr today instead of a fraction of it, and why the balance sheet holds ₹2,900 Cr with nothing owed against it.

What remains is the half no chief executive can order into existence. Winning a larger share of enterprise budgets from firms twenty times its size, in three verticals that shrank last year, in a market where the tool that writes the code is getting cheaper every quarter. The record fourth-quarter order book is the first serious piece of evidence that it might be possible — and it is only evidence, not proof, because signings become revenue only when a client actually staffs the ramp. The verdict arrives on a fixed schedule, four times a year, in a single line of a press release. When Zensar reports its first quarter of FY27, look past the margin, past the dollar-rupee, past the AI certification percentage, and find the constant-currency growth figure. Three consecutive prints above 5%, with 16% margin still standing, and this is a different company. Another year of 1.7%, and the margin repair will have been the whole story rather than the first chapter of one.
