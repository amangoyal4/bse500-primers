00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Adani Power is India's largest private-sector thermal (coal-fired) power producer. It owns a fleet of big coal power stations — 18,150 MW across 13 plants in nine states — that burn coal to make electricity, which it sells either under long-term contracts to state electricity distribution companies ("discoms") or into the open "merchant" market. It is the listed power-generation arm of the Adani group, controlled by the family of Gautam Adani.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A genuine cash machine — at scale. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) it generated ~105 billion units (BU) of electricity, sold ~99 BU, and earned ~₹55,600 Cr of revenue, ~₹21,300 Cr of (continuing) operating profit and ₹12,971 Cr of net profit. It runs a ~38% EBITDA margin and earns a ~17% return on capital — strong numbers for a heavy-industry utility.
  • The earnings engine is the tariff mix. About 95% of operating capacity is tied up in long-term power-purchase agreements (PPAs) that give predictable, regulated returns; the rest is sold "merchant" at volatile spot prices. In FY26 ~20.9 BU (~21% of sales) was merchant. That contracted base is the floor; merchant is the swing factor that makes good years great and bad years ordinary.
  • The story is a capacity-doubling bet. Adani Power plans to grow from ~18 GW today to ~41.9 GW by FY32 — adding ~23.7 GW with roughly ₹2 lakh crore (₹2 trillion) of capex, partly funded by debt. That aggressive build-out, on top of an Adani-group leverage and governance overhang, is exactly what the bulls and bears argue about.
In plain terms

Think of Adani Power as a landlord of giant electricity factories. Each plant has a fixed size (its MW capacity), a fixed-ish cost base (the plant, the staff, the financing) and one big variable cost — coal. It makes money by running those plants as many hours as it can and selling the power. Most of that power is pre-sold on 25-year contracts at agreed prices, which is boring and safe; a slice is sold day-to-day on the exchange at whatever the market pays, which is exciting and risky. The two perennial threats are (1) coal getting expensive and (2) the buyers — cash-strapped state discoms — not paying on time. And the whole company is now pointed at one enormous goal: building twice as many plants over the next six years.

01

Power-plant economics 101: how a coal plant earns

No engineering degree required — just five numbers that drive everything.

A thermal power plant is, financially, a capacity business with one big input cost. It has a fixed number of megawatts and a largely fixed cost base (the plant, the staff, the interest on the loans that built it), and it earns by running those megawatts as many hours as possible and selling the electricity for more than the coal cost. Five numbers tell almost the whole story.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and Adani Power's number)
Capacity (MW / GW)The physical ceiling — how much power the plant can produce at full tilt. Adani Power has 18,150 MW (~18 GW) of operating capacity.
PLF / utilisation (%)"Plant Load Factor" — what share of that maximum the plant actually produced over the year. Higher = more units on the same fixed cost. Adani Power ran ~66.5% in FY26 (Q4 peaked at ~74%).
Units sold (BU / kWh)The electricity actually sold, in billion units (BU). Adani Power sold ~99.2 BU in FY26 (and generated ~105 BU).
Tariff (₹/kWh)The price per unit of electricity. Split into a fixed capacity charge (covers the plant & financing) and a variable energy charge (covers fuel) under PPAs — or a single volatile price in the merchant market (~₹3.86/unit average exchange price in FY26).
Fuel cost (₹/kWh)Mostly coal — the dominant variable cost. Under PPAs it is largely passed through to the buyer; in the merchant market the plant eats it. This is why coal prices matter so much.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Gross spark spread ≈ Units sold × (Tariff − Fuel cost), and Units sold ≈ Capacity × PLF × 8,760 hours. So Adani Power grows in three ways — add capacity (more MW), run it harder (higher PLF), and widen the gap between tariff and fuel cost. The crucial twist: the plant and its loans are fixed costs, so once a plant covers them, every extra unit sold drops largely to profit. That's operating leverage — and it's why PLF and the merchant tariff are the numbers to watch each quarter.

Why "fixed + variable" tariffs change the risk

  • Capacity charge (the fixed leg) — under a PPA, the discom pays this just for keeping the plant available, whether or not it draws power. It covers the plant's cost and financing and earns a regulated return. This is the predictable, bond-like part of earnings.
  • Energy charge (the variable leg) — covers the actual fuel burnt; under most PPAs the coal cost is passed through to the buyer, so the generator is insulated from coal-price swings on contracted volume.
  • Merchant price (no contract) — for uncontracted capacity, the plant sells at the day's market price, which captures fuel and margin in one number. Upside in a power-short summer; painful when prices and coal both move the wrong way.
02

The business model: fuel in, contracted power out

Two design choices explain how Adani Power earns — read this once and the strategy clicks.

Adani Power's model rests on two deliberate decisions: contract most of the fleet on long-dated PPAs (for predictability and pass-through), and grow chiefly by buying distressed plants cheaply through the bankruptcy courts (for low-cost capacity). Together they turn a commodity business into a relatively defensive cash-flow machine — with a merchant slice on top for upside.

The value chain — from coal to a paid invoice

Electricity isn't a stock you hold; it's made and sold in the same instant. The chain runs from fuel procurement, through generation, to a contracted or merchant sale, and finally — the part that actually matters for cash — to getting paid.

Growth by distressed acquisition — the Adani Power signature

  • Buy cheap, through the IBC. Much of the fleet was assembled by acquiring stressed thermal assets via India's bankruptcy process (NCLT/IBC) at a fraction of replacement cost — Mahan Energen (ex-Essar), Raigarh (ex-Korba West), Raipur (ex-GMR), Lanco Amarkantak and others. Buying a built plant for cents on the rupee is far cheaper than greenfield construction.
  • Re-contract and ramp. Acquired plants are tied into PPAs (or run merchant), turned around operationally, and folded into a single fuel-and-operations machine — lifting utilisation and stripping cost.
  • Now adding greenfield too. With distressed supply thinning, the next leg is large ultra-supercritical greenfield builds (Mahan, Raipur, Mirzapur, Korba phases) — bigger, more efficient, and more capital-hungry than the bargain acquisitions that came before.
Why the PPA-heavy mix matters so much here

By locking ~95% of capacity into long-term contracts, Adani Power converts a notoriously cyclical commodity business into something closer to an infrastructure annuity: the capacity charge is paid regardless of demand, and the fuel cost is largely the buyer's problem (pass-through). That predictability is what lets the company carry tens of thousands of crores of debt and still earn steady returns. The trade-off: the contracts are only as good as the counterparties — and India's state discoms are chronically loss-making and slow to pay. So Adani Power swaps commodity-price risk for counterparty / receivables risk. That swap is the heart of the business.

From one Gujarat plant to India's largest private thermal fleet.

Adani Power is the power-generation flagship of the Adani group, tightly controlled by the promoter family of Gautam Adani. It listed in 2009, scaled through a decade of building and (especially) buying plants, and is today run as one arm of a sprawling infrastructure conglomerate spanning ports, airports, transmission, green energy and more.

Ownership & management

  • Group context: Adani Power sits inside the wider Adani conglomerate. That brings scale, fuel-sourcing reach (the group mines and trades coal, incl. the Carmichael mine in Australia) and capital access — but also ties the stock's fortunes and perception to the whole group's leverage and governance reputation.

The group overhang: In early 2023 a short-seller report (Hindenburg) triggered a sharp sell-off across Adani-group stocks on debt and governance concerns, and a US legal matter later kept the names in the headlines. The operating businesses, Adani Power included, kept performing — but the episode is a reminder that this stock carries a group-level sentiment risk on top of its own fundamentals.

05

Merchant vs PPA — the real earnings engine

The single most important idea in the investment case: which units are pre-sold, and which ride the market.

Every unit Adani Power sells lands in one of two buckets, and the split between them decides how predictable — and how cyclical — the profits are. About 95% of operating capacity is locked into long- and medium-term PPAs; the remaining slice is sold "merchant" at spot prices. In FY26, ~20.9 BU (~21% of the ~99 BU sold) was merchant. That ~21% is small in volume but outsized in swing.

The two buckets, decoded

PPA volume = the floor. Pre-sold to discoms on 25-year contracts at agreed tariffs, with the fixed capacity charge paid for merely being available and the fuel cost passed through. This is the bond-like base that covers the plant, the staff and the debt — predictable, defensive, but capped on upside.

Merchant volume = the swing. Sold day-to-day on the power exchange at whatever the market clears. When India is power-short (a hot summer, a coal crunch), merchant prices spike and this slice earns fat margins; when supply is easy, merchant prices sag (the FY26 average exchange price fell ~14% to ~₹3.86/unit) and the slice earns little. It is small in size but high in beta — the difference between a record year and an ordinary one.

06

Coal, fuel cost and the receivables problem

The two perennial threats to a thermal generator's cash — and Adani Power lives with both.

Two forces decide whether a coal generator's reported profit turns into actual cash: what the coal costs, and whether the buyer pays. Adani Power has structural protection on the first (pass-through on contracted volume) but real exposure on both — most visibly through cash-strapped state discoms and the high-profile Bangladesh export contract.

Coal & fuel cost — mostly passed through, but not entirely

  • Coal is the dominant variable cost. Adani Power burns a mix of domestic coal (cheaper, via linkages and the SHAKTI policy) and imported coal (for coastal/import-designed plants like Mundra, and to top up when domestic supply is short). The group's own mining and trading reach — including the Carmichael mine in Australia — helps secure supply.
  • Pass-through cushions the PPA book. Under most PPAs the energy charge passes the actual fuel cost to the discom, so on contracted volume Adani Power is largely insulated from coal-price spikes. The cushion is imperfect — pass-through can be contested, capped or delayed by regulators (the imported-coal tariff fight ran for years).
  • Merchant volume eats fuel risk. On uncontracted units there is no pass-through: if coal rises and merchant prices don't, that slice's margin is squeezed directly.
Historically, the pricing of imported coal has drawn scrutiny (allegations of over-invoicing inflating pass-through fuel costs to customers — which Adani denies). Whatever the merits, it underlines that fuel-cost pass-through in a regulated market is a contested mechanism, not a guarantee.

Receivables — the sale that hasn't turned into cash

The customers are state electricity distribution companies, most of which are structurally loss-making and chronically slow to pay. So Adani Power can book strong revenue while a large pile of it sits in receivables — money owed but not yet collected. Collecting it (and the late-payment surcharge on overdue bills) is a recurring working-capital battle.

The Bangladesh / Godda case study

The 1,600 MW Godda plant exports 100% to Bangladesh under a 25-year PPA — strategically prized, but a textbook receivables risk. After Bangladesh's 2024 political turmoil, the Bangladesh Power Development Board (BPDB) fell badly behind, and dues ballooned to roughly US$0.8–1.0 billion at the peak. Adani invoked the PPA's default clause and cut supply to one unit to force payment. Bangladesh then cleared the bulk — including a ~US$437m catch-up in mid-2025 — and full supply resumed. The episode shows both the leverage a hard contract gives Adani Power and how a single counterparty's finances can put a chunk of cash flow at risk.

Net: receivables don't usually threaten the profit-and-loss (revenue is still booked), but they strain cash flow and working capital, and a true counterparty default would force write-offs. It is the quiet, ever-present risk under the headline numbers.

07

The capacity-doubling pipeline & the capex bet

The most aggressive thermal build-out in India — and the company's defining wager.

Adani Power's clearest growth lever is sheer capacity addition. It has sharply raised its ambition: from ~18 GW today toward ~41.9 GW by FY32 — i.e. more than doubling the fleet in six years, adding ~23.7 GW with an estimated ~₹2 lakh crore (₹2 trillion) of capital expenditure. That makes it the single largest thermal-capacity build-out in the country at a time when most of the world is building renewables.

The bet — and the catch

The bull logic is clean: India's power demand is growing fast, renewables can't yet provide reliable round-the-clock baseload, and Adani Power can build large, efficient coal plants quickly and pre-sell them on long PPAs — so each new GW is a new annuity. The catch is capital and timing: ~₹2 lakh crore of capex over six years means more debt on top of today's ~₹45,000 Cr net debt, big execution risk, and a wager that thermal power stays needed and well-paid for the 25-year life of plants being commissioned in the 2030s. Double the fleet on schedule and the earnings compound; over-build into softening demand or tightening climate policy, and the leverage bites.

08

The industry and the rivals

A state-dominated power market where Adani is the private heavyweight.

India's power-generation market is large, demand-growing and dominated by the public sector — led by the state giant NTPC. Among private thermal players, Adani Power is the clear number one by capacity, competing for PPAs, fuel and increasingly for merchant share against a handful of large groups and the ever-present state utilities.

The competitive tension to watch

Adani Power's edge is speed and scale in firm, dispatchable coal capacity — exactly what a fast-industrialising, power-short grid still needs at peak and at night. Its bet is that for the next decade, India can't keep the lights on with renewables alone, so reliable coal baseload commands long PPAs and decent merchant prices. The risk is that the ground is shifting under thermal: every year, solar-plus-storage gets cheaper and policy gets greener. The strategic question is whether Adani Power can earn back its ~₹2 lakh crore coal investment before firmed renewables make new coal plants the expensive, politically awkward option.

The regulatory backdrop: generators operate under state and central electricity regulators (CERC/SERCs) who set and contest tariffs, pass-through and surcharges; PPAs, coal linkages (SHAKTI policy) and discom payment rules are all government-shaped. Regulatory and political risk — over tariffs, fuel pass-through and emissions norms — is a permanent feature of the business.
09

Coal vs the energy transition

The structural question hanging over every rupee of the capex plan.

Adani Power is making a multi-decade, ₹2-lakh-crore bet on coal at the very moment the world is trying to move off it. That tension — coal's near-term necessity versus its long-term decline — is the defining structural force for this company, the equivalent of the demographic question for a maternity hospital. The bull and bear cases are really two views on how long coal stays king of India's baseload.

Why this is the crux — but not an immediate kill-switch

Four points keep the coal thesis alive despite the transition. (1) Baseload reality. Renewables are intermittent; until cheap, large-scale storage exists, the grid needs firm coal capacity at night and at peak — and demand is growing ~6–7% a year. (2) Long PPAs lock the cash. A plant commissioned with a 25-year PPA earns its capacity charge regardless of where the energy mix drifts. (3) Pre-contracted build. Much of the new capacity is being pre-sold, reducing the risk of stranded merchant plants. (4) India's own timeline. India's net-zero target is 2070, not 2050 — giving coal a longer policy runway than in the West.

The bear's reply: stranded-asset and ESG risk. If firmed renewables (solar + batteries) undercut new coal economically before 2035, the back-end of this build could earn far less than modelled; global capital is increasingly reluctant to fund coal, raising the cost and difficulty of financing; and carbon pricing or tighter emission norms could lift costs later in the plants' lives. A 25-year asset started in 2030 has to stay competitive into the 2050s — a long time to bet on coal.

The honest tension: Adani Power is, in effect, betting that India needs a lot more firm coal power for the next 15–20 years — and pricing that need into PPAs and merchant margins before renewables-plus-storage take over. Bulls see a decade-plus of well-paid, contracted baseload; bears see the last great coal build-out, financed with debt, into a structurally declining fuel. Both can be right at different points in the asset's life — which is why PPA tenure, fuel-cost discipline and the pace of storage are the things to track.

How to think about the valuation

At a ~₹4.3 lakh crore market cap on ~₹13,000 Cr of FY26 profit, Adani Power trades around 33× trailing earnings (and ~6–7× book) — a striking premium for a coal generator, which would historically fetch a low-teens multiple. Bulls argue a doubling fleet with long PPAs deserves a growth multiple, not a utility one. With the stock already up multi-fold from its lows (52-week range ~₹105–254), the valuation leaves limited room for stumbles.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cash-rich utility riding a power-hungry economy — priced for a flawless build-out.

Adani Power is a genuinely large, profitable, contracted-cash-flow business levered to India's structural power demand — but it is also a leveraged, coal-concentrated company inside a scrutinised conglomerate, valued well above the multiple coal utilities usually earn. The debate is about execution, fuel/counterparty risk and the long-term coal question more than about today's earnings.

The bull case

  • Structural demand — India's power consumption is growing ~6–7%/yr and still needs firm, dispatchable coal baseload renewables can't yet replace.
  • Contracted visibility — ~95% of capacity on long-term PPAs with fuel pass-through; a bond-like cash-flow floor under a merchant kicker.
  • Capacity-doubling growth — a clear path from ~18 GW to ~42 GW by FY32, much of it pre-contracted; each GW a new annuity.
  • Cheap-asset playbook — proven ability to buy distressed plants below replacement cost and ramp them.
  • Scale & group support — largest private generator, group fuel-sourcing reach, ~17% ROCE, ~38% margins.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~33× earnings for a coal utility leaves little room for error and leans on merchant-boosted, partly one-off profits.
  • Debt-funded build — ~₹2 lakh crore capex adds to ~₹45,000 Cr net debt; execution and financing risk on a huge scale.
  • Receivables / counterparty risk — loss-making discoms and the Bangladesh/Godda saga show how a buyer's finances can choke cash flow.
  • Coal & transition risk — stranded-asset, ESG-financing and future carbon/emission risks on 25-year assets started in the 2030s.
  • Group overhang — leverage, governance and headline risk at the Adani-group level sit on top of company fundamentals.
The bottom line

Adani Power is a large, cash-generative bet on India staying power-short and coal staying essential for the next 15–20 years — wrapped in long PPAs, run by an aggressive builder, and priced for that build-out to go right. The business risk is real but mostly slow-moving: fuel cost (cushioned by pass-through), receivables (a working-capital grind, occasionally acute), execution of the capex (the big one), and the long-arc coal question. The story over the coming years will be written in a few numbers each quarter — PLF and merchant prices (is the existing fleet earning?), net debt and capex pace (is the build-out affordable?), and receivable days (is the revenue turning into cash?). Get those right and a doubling fleet compounds; stumble, and a 33× coal stock has a long way to fall.
