00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

PhysicsWallah (PW) sells exam coaching — cheaply, at massive scale. It began as a Hindi-language physics channel on YouTube, grew into an app selling online "batches" for a few thousand rupees, and now also runs 353 physical coaching centres ("PW Vidyapeeth") across India and the UAE. Its core market is test-prep for India's two great gateway exams — JEE (engineering) and NEET (medical) — plus government-job exams (UPSC), professional upskilling ("PW Skills"), books and more. In November 2025 it became the first Indian edtech unicorn to list publicly.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The whole thesis is "dramatically cheaper." Where premium rivals charged ₹25,000+ for online prep and offline institutes charge lakhs, PW priced online batches at roughly ₹3,000–5,000 and offline courses at ~₹35,000–40,000. It reaches students that pricier brands simply cannot, and — because its founder is a genuine YouTube-famous teacher — it acquires them for a fraction of what rivals spend on marketing.
  • High growth, thin-to-negative profit — for now. Revenue rose from ~₹1,940 Cr (FY24) to ~₹3,900 Cr (FY26), but PW is loss-making / near-breakeven: a ₹1,131 Cr loss in FY24 narrowed to ₹243 Cr in FY25 and just ~₹24 Cr in FY26, with EBITDA turning positive (~14% margin). The cause of the losses is deliberate: it is burning cash to build offline centres and buy smaller edtechs to grab the market while it can.
  • You value it on sales, not earnings. With profits near zero, a P/E ratio is meaningless. At a ~₹36,000 Cr market cap on ~₹3,900 Cr of revenue, PW trades around 9× sales — the market is paying for future profits from a business that is still proving it can be consistently profitable at scale. Whether it can is the single defining question of the stock.
In plain terms

Think of PW as the low-cost airline of Indian coaching. Byju's was the luxury carrier — glossy app, celebrity ads, aggressive selling, huge losses; it crashed. PW went the other way: one trusted teacher, honest low prices, enormous volume. That model was so lean it was profitable early — rare for an edtech. Then PW chose to spend that advantage building hundreds of physical classrooms in small towns and acquiring rivals, which pushed it back into losses. The bet is that once those centres fill up and the land-grab ends, the underlying cheap-and-massive model earns real money. The market has already crowned it India's most-valuable edtech; now it has to earn the crown.

01

Edtech economics 101: how cheap coaching makes money

No jargon — just the handful of numbers that decide whether an edtech lives or dies.

A coaching business is, financially, a volume-and-margin game. You pay a big fixed cost to make a course once (record the teacher, build the app, or rent a classroom and hire faculty), then you try to sell that same course to as many students as possible. Five numbers tell you almost the whole story.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and PW's number)
Paid usersStudents who actually pay (not free viewers). PW had ~5.3 million paid users in FY26 (+20%) — ~4.9M online, ~0.47M offline. Total registered/enrolled base is far larger (48 lakh+).
ARPU"Average Revenue Per User" — how much each paying student is worth. PW's is very low online (~₹3,500) but much higher offline (~₹36,600/yr). The whole model is low ARPU × huge volume.
CAC"Customer Acquisition Cost" — what it costs to win one paying student. PW's is roughly 1/5th of rivals', because a famous teacher's YouTube audience is (almost) free marketing.
Gross marginRevenue left after the direct cost of delivering the course. PW's rose to ~35% in FY26 (from ~26%) — improving as scale spreads fixed content costs.
EBITDA marginOperating profit as a share of revenue. PW turned positive (~14% in FY26) after huge losses — the number that shows the model can work at scale.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (Paid users × ARPU) − content & delivery cost − marketing (CAC × users) − centre/tech overhead. An edtech has two ways to win: keep CAC tiny (so it doesn't bleed money buying students) and keep content costs fixed (record once, sell to millions). PW is unusually strong on both for its online business — which is exactly why it was profitable when almost every rival was burning cash. Its offline centres flip the maths: they cost real money to run (rent, local faculty), earn higher ARPU per student, but only make money once each classroom is full. That tension — cheap-and-scalable online vs expensive-but-premium offline — runs through the entire company.

Why "affordable" is a strategy, not just a price

  • It unlocks a bigger market. Most Indian families cannot afford lakhs for coaching. By pricing at a fraction, PW converts a huge, previously-priced-out audience into paying customers — volume that premium brands can never reach.
  • It is defended by low costs, not high prices. A rival can always undercut a premium brand; it is very hard to undercut the cheapest credible player who also has the biggest free audience. Cheapness plus trust is the moat.
  • It is emotionally sticky. The founder-teacher relationship (students call him "Alakh Sir") converts a commodity — exam facts — into loyalty. That loyalty lowers CAC and raises retention, the two things that make the unit economics work.
02

The business model: a teacher, a funnel, and two channels

Read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

PW's model rests on one asset and one funnel. The asset is trusted, teacher-led content — made cheaply and centrally, then sold everywhere. The funnel takes a student from a free YouTube video at the top down to a paid online batch, then (increasingly) into a paid offline classroom, and out into adjacent products like skills courses and books. Each step costs the student more and earns PW more.

The further right you go, the higher the revenue per student — but also the higher the cost to serve. The free top of the funnel is what makes the whole thing cheap to fill.

The two channels, side by side

  • Online (app & YouTube) — record a batch once, stream it to millions. Low price, low cost, high margin, infinitely scalable — but also low revenue per student and easy for rivals to copy on price. This is the profitable core.
  • Offline / "phygital" (Vidyapeeth & Pathshala centres) — physical classrooms in tier-2/3 towns, often blending live and recorded teaching ("phygital"). Higher price, higher cost, capex-heavy — but far higher revenue per student, stickier, and the way to reach families who want a real classroom. This is the growth-and-burn engine.
Why go offline at all if online is more profitable?

Because most of the money in Indian coaching is still offline. Parents in smaller towns trust a physical classroom, a local teacher and a fixed timetable far more than an app. Offline students pay ~10× the online price and drop out less. The old giants (Allen, Aakash, FIITJEE) built empires on exactly this. So PW is doing something bold: taking its cheap, brand-heavy online model into the offline arena and undercutting the incumbents on price — ₹35–40k where they charge lakhs. The prize is huge; the catch is that every new centre is a fixed-cost bet that only pays off once it fills, which is precisely why aggressive centre-building has pushed PW into losses. Online funds the brand; offline is where PW is spending to win the bigger, slower, richer market.

03

The company and who controls it

From a ₹0 YouTube channel to a newly-listed ₹36,000 Cr company.

PW is a founder-teacher business. Alakh Pandey — the original "Physics Wallah" ("the physics guy") — began posting free physics lectures on YouTube around 2016 while working as a coaching teacher. In 2020, with co-founder Prateek Maheshwari (also known as Prateek Boob), he turned the channel into a company and an app. Five years and a wave of acquisitions later, it is a publicly-listed, multi-category education platform — but Alakh Pandey is still, to millions of students, the brand.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: co-founders Alakh Pandey (Founder & CEO) and Prateek Maheshwari held about 80% together before the IPO, diluting to roughly ~72% after listing. This is an extremely founder-controlled company — a strength (aligned, decisive) and a risk (key-man dependence on Alakh, discussed in §10).
  • Institutional backers: the pre-IPO cap table carried strong venture names — WestBridge Capital (~6%), Hornbill Capital (~3%), GSV Ventures (~2%) and Lightspeed (~2%) — long-term edtech and growth investors rather than short-term flippers.
  • Balance sheet: flush with cash after the fresh-issue IPO — cash/treasury of roughly ₹5,000 Cr at end-2025 — giving PW ample runway to fund centre expansion and acquisitions without borrowing.

The categories, decoded

  • JEE & NEET (the core) — coaching for India's engineering and medical entrance exams, the two highest-stakes tests in the country. At end-FY25 PW had roughly 5.7 lakh JEE and 9.3 lakh NEET paid students — the biggest single funnels, and where the "affordable coaching" story was born.
  • Government & competitive exams — UPSC (civil services), SSC, banking, GATE, defence and state exams. A vast, price-sensitive market of aspirants; PW acquired specialists (e.g. Sarrthi IAS) to build depth here.
  • PW Skills — professional upskilling in data science, analytics, coding, banking and finance (built largely on the acquired iNeuron). Targets college students and working professionals — a different, higher-ticket customer than a school-age aspirant.
  • Books & test series — physical books, practice papers and test series. Low-tech, but a natural, trusted, cash-generative extension of a coaching brand.
  • International — an early push into the UAE / Middle East (large Indian-origin student population), PW's first step beyond India's borders.
Why so many categories?

Two reasons. First, the free audience is category-agnostic: once you have tens of millions of trusting students and a cheap way to reach them, adding a new subject is far cheaper for PW than for a rival starting from scratch. Second, diversification de-risks the core: JEE/NEET demand is huge but cyclical and fiercely fought, so UPSC, skills and international spread the bets. The danger is the opposite of focus — spreading management attention and capital across too many categories (some, like the brief K-12 school push, were later dialled back). The discipline test is whether PW expands into categories where its cost-and-brand advantage actually travels, or chases growth for its own sake.

05

Online vs offline: the Vidyapeeth growth-and-capex engine

The most important strategic choice PW has made — and the reason it is in losses.

If online is PW's profitable heart, offline is its growth story and its cash drain. PW ended FY26 with 353 physical centres across India and the UAE — up from 198 a year earlier and just a handful three years ago. These "PW Vidyapeeth" (and "Pathshala"/"phygital") centres charge far more per student than the app, and they are how PW attacks the huge offline coaching market long owned by Allen, Aakash and FIITJEE — but each one is a fixed-cost bet.

PW has guided to opening at least ~70 new centres a year. Each new centre opens near-empty and only earns money once it fills — so a rapid build-out temporarily depresses profitability even as it grows revenue.
The classroom "J-curve" — where the losses come from

A new coaching centre is like a new hospital or a new store: it opens with full fixed costs (a leased building, local teachers, staff) but an empty room — so at first it loses money, then climbs to profit as enrolments build over a couple of years. Plotted over time, each centre traces a "J": down first, then up. When you're opening 70–150 centres a year, you always have a big batch of young, loss-making centres dragging the average down. That is the honest explanation for PW's losses: not a broken model, but a chosen land-grab. Management's own signal is telling — by FY26, 72 of the ~116 mature Vidyapeeth centres were already profitable. The bull case is that as the newer centres age into that same profitability, offline flips from cash drain to profit engine. The bear case is that the ramp takes longer, or competition forces prices down before it happens.

Unit economics and the path to profitability

The defining debate: can PW be reliably profitable at scale?

This is the question the entire stock hangs on. Unusually for an edtech, PW was profitable early — its cheap, low-CAC online model actually made money when Byju's and Unacademy were torching billions. Then it chose to spend that edge on offline centres and acquisitions, and slid into losses. FY26 shows it clawing back toward breakeven. The debate is whether that recovery is durable — or whether the costs of scaling (centres, teachers, marketing, AI) keep eating the profits the model theoretically generates.

The loss shrank ~90% in FY26 to near-breakeven. But note: the full-year figure hides a wobble — early quarters were profitable, then Q4 FY26 slipped back to a ~₹75 Cr loss as PW ramped AI and marketing spend. The path to profit is real but not yet smooth.
The bull case in one mechanism

PW's cost advantage is structural. Its CAC is roughly a fifth of rivals' because a famous teacher's free YouTube audience does the marketing that competitors pay hundreds of crores for. Its content is made once and sold to millions, so gross margin rises with scale (it went from ~26% to ~35% in two years). Layer on the offline centres maturing (72 of 116 mature ones already profitable) and the maths is compelling: if PW simply stops aggressively opening new loss-making centres and lets the existing base fill, EBITDA — already ~14% — should expand meaningfully. That is why management frames the story as "we are choosing to invest, not failing to profit." The near-breakeven FY26, EBITDA turning solidly positive, and a Q3-FY26 quarter with positive net profit are the evidence they point to.

The bear's counter: the same story can be read as an edtech that keeps finding reasons to spend — first offline centres, then acquisitions, now a big AI push — such that profit is always "next year." The Q4-FY26 relapse into loss, and offline ARPU actually falling ~9% (as shorter, cheaper courses mix in), are the warning signs. A low-price model has thin margins by design; if PW must keep spending on marketing, faculty and AI to defend its lead, sustained double-digit net margins may prove elusive. Watch whether losses stay gone quarter after quarter, not just on a full-year average.
07

The Byju's lesson: why "cheap and honest" beat "premium and loud"

To understand PW, you have to understand the giant it replaced.

PW cannot be understood without Byju's — once India's most-valuable startup ($22bn+), now in insolvency. Byju's built the opposite model: a premium-priced product sold through an aggressive, high-pressure sales force, funded by enormous marketing (celebrity ambassadors, World Cup sponsorships) and even bigger losses. When funding dried up, the debt, the lawsuits and the burn brought it down. Its collapse is the backdrop against which PW's entire strategy makes sense.

Why the affordable model proved more durable

Three structural reasons. (1) Low CAC is survivable. Byju's needed constant fresh capital to buy customers; when funding stopped, so did growth. PW's audience comes free with the content, so it doesn't depend on the next funding round. (2) Affordable is recession-proof. When money is tight, families trade down to PW, not away from it — the cheap option gains in hard times. (3) Trust compounds. A teacher students genuinely like generates word-of-mouth and retention that no ad budget can buy. Byju's optimised for selling; PW optimised for teaching cheaply — and in a downturn, the second turned out to be the sturdier business.

The cautionary half of the lesson: PW's leadership has openly worried aloud about "becoming another Byju's." The risk isn't the pricing model — it's the behaviours that sank Byju's: over-expansion, too many acquisitions, chasing categories for growth, and spending ahead of profit. PW's offline land-grab and acquisition spree rhyme, faintly, with that pattern. The whole bull case rests on PW keeping the discipline that made it different — and not drifting into the excess that destroyed its predecessor.

The industry and the rivals

A giant, fragmented coaching market — online challengers and offline incumbents.

India's test-prep and coaching market is enormous, deeply cultural and highly fragmented — millions of students chase a few thousand elite college seats every year, and families spend heavily to improve the odds. PW competes on two fronts at once: against online edtech challengers (its native turf) and against entrenched offline institutes (the bigger, older market it is now invading).

The competitive tension to watch

PW's online dominance is relatively secure — it is the cheapest player with the biggest audience, and the two rivals that could have challenged it (Byju's, Unacademy) have imploded or retreated. The real contest is offline. There, PW is the disruptive newcomer attacking incumbents (Allen, Aakash, Narayana) who have decades of brand, results and local relationships. PW's weapon is price and digital polish; the incumbents' weapon is a proven offline track record and the trust of results-obsessed parents. Whether a low-price app-brand can win in the high-trust, results-driven offline arena — city by city, against determined local competition — is the open question of the next five years. Consolidation cuts both ways: rivals' weakness (Byju's/Aakash mess, FIITJEE closures) is PW's opportunity, but it also invites price wars for students and teachers.

The IPO and how to value a loss-making platform

Why you use price-to-sales, not price-to-earnings — and what that means.

PW's November 2025 IPO was a landmark: the first Indian edtech unicorn to go public, in a market that had soured badly on the sector after Byju's. It raised ₹3,480 Cr (mostly a ₹3,100 Cr fresh issue to fund growth, plus a small ₹380 Cr offer-for-sale) at ₹109/share, valuing the company around ₹28,000 Cr. Shares jumped ~30–45% on debut and have since traded in a wide band; the market cap sits near ₹36,000 Cr.

Why P/E doesn't work here — and P/S does

A price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio divides the share price by profit per share. But PW's profit is roughly zero (a tiny loss) — divide by ~zero and you get a meaningless or infinite number. So for a high-growth, near-breakeven business you value it on price-to-sales (P/S): market cap ÷ revenue. PW's ~₹36,000 Cr market cap on ~₹3,900 Cr of revenue is about 9× sales. That is a rich multiple — you only pay ~9× revenue for a company that barely breaks even if you believe two things: that revenue keeps growing fast (it grew 35–50% recently), and that today's near-zero margin will widen into a healthy profit margin later. In effect, the market is pricing in the future profitable PW, not the current near-breakeven one. If either belief slips — growth slows, or margins stay thin — a 9× P/S stock can fall a long way.

The IPO's use of proceeds tells you management's own priorities: the fresh capital is earmarked chiefly for new offline/hybrid centres, existing-centre leases, branding & marketing, and technology (including AI) — i.e. more of the same growth-and-scale spending that has kept profits suppressed. This is a company explicitly raising money to keep investing, not to bank profits. That is legitimate for a land-grab phase — but it means the "when does it make real money?" clock is the one investors will watch every quarter.

Reading the multiple honestly: at IPO, some analysts flagged PW's EV/sales and (thin) EV/EBITDA as well above sector benchmarks — a premium justified only by continued hyper-growth and margin expansion. Bulls see a category leader with a structural cost edge and a long runway; bears see a low-margin coaching business priced like a high-margin tech platform. Both views ride on the same two numbers: revenue growth and net margin.
10

Regulation and the key-man question

Two risks that are specific to a founder-led coaching company.

Beyond the usual competitive and financial risks, PW carries two that are unusually pointed: coaching-sector regulation (a market under growing government scrutiny) and key-man dependence (a brand fused to one person). Neither is fatal, but both are structural, and both deserve a clear-eyed look.

Coaching-sector regulation

  • The backdrop is real distress. India's high-pressure coaching culture — epitomised by Kota, the coaching capital — has drawn sustained public and political concern, including over student mental health and suicides. That has produced regulation.
  • Central guidelines (Jan 2024): the Ministry of Education issued rules — no enrolment of students below 16, no misleading claims or guaranteed-rank promises, minimum tutor qualifications, and mandatory mental-health/counselling support.
  • State laws: states such as Rajasthan and Assam have passed their own Coaching Centres Control & Regulation Acts (2025) — fees, registration, facilities and student-welfare rules for physical centres.
  • The edtech gap — for now: much of this regulation targets physical centres; online coaching is less covered. That partly shields PW's app business but not its fast-growing offline arm — and the regulatory net over online edtech (advertising standards, data, consumer protection) is likely to tighten over time.
Why it matters to PW specifically: PW is now one of the largest offline coaching operators too, so state coaching laws apply directly to its Vidyapeeth centres — on fees, facilities and enrolment age. Tighter rules could raise compliance costs or cap certain practices. On balance PW's affordable, welfare-friendly positioning fits the spirit of the reforms better than premium hard-sell rivals — but regulation is a permanent feature of this sector to keep on the radar.

Key-man dependence on Alakh Pandey

PW's brand is, to a remarkable degree, one man. Millions of students chose PW because they trust Alakh Pandey as a teacher; the founder and the brand are nearly synonymous. That is a genuine asset — it drives the near-free customer acquisition that makes the whole model work — but it is also a concentrated risk: reputational damage, health, departure, or simply the natural limits of one person's time and attention could hurt the brand in a way that a more institutional company would shrug off.

How PW is trying to de-risk the founder

PW's answer is to build a stable of teachers and sub-brands rather than rest on one face — many popular educators now teach under the PW umbrella, and acquisitions (Xylem, Sarrthi IAS, others) bring their own established faculty and followings. The goal is to convert "Alakh's channel" into "PW the platform," where no single teacher is irreplaceable. Progress is real but incomplete: Alakh Pandey remains the emotional centre of the brand and the promoter holding ~72% of the company. Investors are, to a meaningful extent, backing him — his judgement, his discipline, and his ability to institutionalise what he built.

What the numbers really say

Revenue doubled in two years; EBITDA swung from a ₹829 Cr loss to a ₹549 Cr profit; the net loss collapsed ~98% from ₹1,131 Cr to ~₹24 Cr. That is a genuine, fast operating turnaround. Two cautions keep it honest. First, employee cost is the dominant expense (roughly ₹1,700 Cr+ in FY26) — this is a people-heavy teaching business, and margins live or die on managing that. Second, the FY26 net figure is a full-year average: PW was net-profitable in the middle of the year but slipped back to a ~₹75 Cr loss in Q4 as it ramped AI and marketing spend — a reminder that the last mile to sustained profit isn't yet locked in. Post-IPO cash of roughly ₹5,000 Cr means the company can fund its expansion comfortably; it does not need to reach profit next quarter — which is both a comfort and, for impatient investors, a frustration.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine disruptor — priced for it to become a durable, profitable platform.

PW is the rare edtech that got the model right: cheap, trusted, teacher-led, and reaching a mass market its rivals priced themselves out of. It survived the sector's brutal shakeout, emerged as India's most-valued edtech, and became the first to list. The investment debate is not whether the business is good — it is whether a low-price, people-heavy, land-grabbing company can turn its scale into consistent profit fast enough to justify a ~9× sales valuation.

The bull case

  • Structural cost moat — near-free customer acquisition via a famous teacher; ~1/5th the CAC of rivals. Very hard to copy.
  • Affordability wins the mass market — reaches a vast, price-sensitive audience premium brands can't, and gains share in downturns.
  • Operating turnaround is real — revenue doubled in two years; EBITDA swung positive; losses down ~90%; gross margin rising.
  • Offline optionality — Vidyapeeth centres attack the huge, higher-ARPU offline market; mature centres already profitable.
  • Cleared field & cash pile — Byju's gone, Unacademy retreated; ~₹5,000 Cr post-IPO to fund growth debt-free.

The bear case

  • Still not durably profitable — near-breakeven only, and Q4 FY26 slipped back to a loss; thin-margin model by design.
  • Rich valuation — ~9× sales prices in years of growth + margin expansion; little room for disappointment.
  • Offline is capex-heavy & contested — a land-grab against entrenched incumbents; ARPU already dipping; ramp risk.
  • Key-man & "next Byju's" risk — brand fused to Alakh Pandey; over-expansion / acquisition sprawl could repeat the mistakes that sank the giant it replaced.
  • Regulation — tightening coaching-sector rules (fees, age, welfare) touch its offline arm; edtech oversight likely to grow.
The bottom line

PhysicsWallah is a genuinely differentiated, mission-aligned disruptor — it made quality exam-prep affordable to millions and built a real cost advantage doing it. The business risk is lower than most edtechs; the valuation-and-execution risk is high. The story over the next few years will be written in two numbers each quarter — revenue growth (is the scaling engine still humming?) and net margin (does the profit finally show up, and stay?). Get both right and PW compounds into the durable, profitable education platform its price implies; stumble on either, and a stock valued at 9× sales for a barely-profitable coaching business has a long way to fall. It is the opposite bet to Byju's — and PW's job is to make sure it stays that way.
