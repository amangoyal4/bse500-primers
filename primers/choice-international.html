00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Choice International is a diversified financial-services group from Mumbai that does three quite different things at once: it runs a retail stock-broking and distribution business (Choice Equity Broking — a discount-plus-full-service hybrid strong in small-town India), a lending NBFC (Choice Finserv, doing MSME, secured and solar loans), and — the part that surprises people — a large government & infrastructure advisory practice (Choice Consultancy) that advises ministries, state governments and public agencies on projects, schemes and digital governance for a fee.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is growing fast off a small base. Revenue rose from ₹759 Cr (FY24) to ₹922 Cr (FY25) to ~₹1,145 Cr in FY26 (+24%), and profit jumped 46% to ₹238 Cr — a five-year sales CAGR near 45%. Margins are wide: a ~37% EBITDA margin in FY26.
  • Three engines, three kinds of money. FY26 revenue split roughly 59% broking & distribution, 28% advisory, 13% NBFC. Broking earns brokerage plus interest on client funding; the NBFC earns a lending spread; the advisory arm earns fee income from government mandates — a sticky, contracted, order-book-style revenue that a pure broker simply does not have.
  • The surprise is the consulting arm. A company most screens file next to Angel One earns roughly a quarter of its revenue writing Detailed Project Reports for highways and government schools, digitising farm-credit societies, and building an AI-powered media-monitoring centre for a state government. That advisory business carries a ₹698 Cr order book dated two-to-three years out — visibility a broker's revenue never offers.
In plain terms

Picture three shops sharing one signboard. The first is a busy online broking shop — millions of retail investors buying and selling shares, paying small fees and a little interest. The second is a lending counter that borrows wholesale and lends to small businesses at a spread. The third — the odd one — is a consulting desk that works for the government, drawing up project blueprints and running scheme rollouts for a fee. The market-linked shops (broking, lending) swing with sentiment and rates; the government desk grinds out contracted fees for years regardless of where the Nifty is. Choice's pitch is that the three together compound more smoothly than any one alone. The catch: a small-cap juggling three regulated businesses at once has three places to trip.

One correction worth stating up front: Choice's promoter family is the Patodia / Poddar group (founder Sunil Patodia; Chairperson Vinita Sunil Patodia; MD Kamal Poddar; CEO Arun Poddar) — not the "Kamdar" family sometimes attached to the name. Ownership and control sit with the Patodia/Poddar promoters, who hold roughly 54% (see §5).
01

The three-legged model, in plain English

No finance degree required — just how each of the three legs turns activity into revenue.

Most listed financial companies do one thing well: a broker brokes, an NBFC lends, a consultancy consults. Choice deliberately does all three, and the reason to understand each separately is that they earn money in structurally different ways — and respond to completely different forces. Here are the three revenue machines, decoded.

The three legs — how each one actually earns
LegWhat it doesHow it earnsWhat drives it
Broking & distributionRetail stock broking (Choice Equity Broking) + selling insurance, mutual funds and wealth productsBrokerage on trades, interest on margin funding, distribution commissions, platform feesMarket volumes, retail-investor sentiment, active clients, cross-sell
NBFC / lendingChoice Finserv — MSME, secured retail, loans-against-securities, rooftop-solar loansNet interest spread (lend rate minus borrow cost) on the loan bookLoan book growth, cost of funds, asset quality (bad-loan rate)
Advisory / consultingGovernment & infrastructure consulting (Choice Consultancy) + investment banking / SME IPOsFees on mandates — project reports, programme management, e-governance, IPO advisoryGovernment spending, order-book wins, execution/collection on mandates
The one distinction that matters

Two of these legs are market-linked — brokerage rises and falls with trading volumes; a lending book is exposed to rates and defaults. The third is mandate-linked — advisory revenue is contracted in advance, disclosed as an order book, and recognised as work gets done over two-to-three years. That single difference is the whole design thesis: when markets go quiet and brokerage dips, the government-advisory fees keep arriving on their own schedule. A diversified group is only worth more than the sum of its parts if the parts don't all fall together — and broking, lending and government consulting genuinely march to different drums.

Why "financial services" undersells it

Screeners tag Choice under Financial Services, which nudges a reader to compare it with Angel One or a small NBFC. That misses the point. The closest thing to a fair description is a holding company for three separately regulated businesses — a SEBI-regulated broker, an RBI-registered NBFC, and a consulting firm that bids for public-sector work. Each has its own regulator, its own economics and its own risk. The rest of this primer takes them one at a time, then puts them back together.

02

Leg 1 — broking & distribution

The biggest leg (~59% of revenue) and the one that rides India's retail-investing boom.

The broking arm — Choice Equity Broking, marketed as Choice Broking and delivered through the FinX app and web platform — is a hybrid: cheaper than a traditional full-service broker, but with more advice and hand-holding than a pure discount app. Its edge is reach into tier-2 and tier-3 India, where a branch-and-partner network still helps first-time investors open an account and start.

How a broker earns — the three streams

  • Brokerage — a fee per trade. Volume-driven, so it swings with how actively clients trade (and with market mood).
  • Interest / margin funding — when clients borrow to trade or take positions, the broker earns interest on that funding. In a healthy market this is a large, steadier slice.
  • Distribution — commissions from selling insurance, mutual funds and wealth products to the same clients. Choice Insurance Broking alone reported ₹93 Cr of premium in a single quarter (Q4 FY25, +49% YoY). This is the cross-sell engine (see §6).
Why the "cash delivery" tilt matters

Choice's management stresses that a large share of its broking revenue comes from the cash-delivery segment — investors buying shares to hold — rather than from hyperactive intraday and options traders. That matters because India's regulator (SEBI) has been tightening the screws on futures-and-options (F&O) trading, where most small traders lose money. A broker whose revenue leans on genuine investors, not on churn in weekly options, is less exposed to the regulatory axe that has been shrinking industry F&O volumes. It is a lower-octane, more durable revenue base than a pure derivatives-driven discount broker — a deliberate positioning choice, not an accident.

The client base is real and growing — ~1.5 million+ active clients, 12.63 lakh demat accounts, and client assets up ~28% in a year. But the broking leg is also the group's most cyclical: in a bear market, both brokerage and margin-funding income fall together, which is precisely why the other two legs exist.

03

Leg 2 — the lending NBFC

Small today (~13% of revenue) but the leg that turns a broker into a lender.

Choice Finserv is the group's non-banking financial company (NBFC) — a lender that borrows wholesale and lends to small businesses and retail borrowers, earning the spread between what it pays for money and what it charges. The loan book reached ~₹800 Cr in FY26, up from ~₹768 Cr the year before, with the bulk in retail and MSME lending.

What it lends, and the deliberate caution

  • Secured lending & MSME finance — loans to small enterprises, often against collateral, plus loans-against-securities that dovetail with the broking clients.
  • Rooftop solar loans — financing solar installations, a fast-growing green-lending niche riding India's rooftop-solar push.
  • The avoidance: management has been explicit about staying out of unsecured personal lending — the segment where RBI has repeatedly warned on froth and where asset quality can turn ugly quickly.
Reading an NBFC's health in two numbers

For any lender, watch two things. First, the spread — Choice Finserv runs a NIM around 11%, wide because MSME lending prices in more risk than a home loan. Second, asset quality — the share of loans going bad. Choice reports gross NPAs of ~2.74% and net NPAs of ~1.86%, stable and unremarkable for MSME lending, sitting on an unusually thick ~46% capital cushion (CRAR). The thick capital is a double-edged sword: it means the book is safe and has huge room to grow, but it also means capital is under-deployed — returns will look modest until the book scales up. This is a lending business still early in its ramp.

The NBFC's real job in the group: at ~13% of revenue it is not the profit driver — it is the connective tissue. A broking client who needs a loan-against-securities, or a small-business owner the advisory team already knows, becomes a Choice Finserv borrower. The NBFC monetises relationships the other two legs originate. Its value is as much strategic (completing the flywheel) as financial.
04

Leg 3 — the government & infrastructure advisory practice

The genuinely unusual leg — and the reason Choice is not just another broker.

This is the section that makes Choice worth a second look. Bolted onto the retail broker is Choice Consultancy — a fee-based government and infrastructure advisory business that works for central and state governments, ministries, and public agencies. It advises on infrastructure projects, prepares Detailed Project Reports (DPRs), runs programme and project management, delivers e-governance and digital transformation, and supports public financial management and social-sector schemes. It is, plainly, a business you would never expect to find inside a discount-broking app company.

The genuine surprise, stated plainly

A smart generalist who hears "Choice International — NSE-listed broker" would never guess that the same company wrote the blueprint to renovate 800 government schools, is building a state government's AI media-monitoring room, and has done project reports for over 3,000 km of highways. This is not a token side hustle: the advisory arm delivered ~₹330 Cr of revenue in FY26 (≈28% of the group) and carries a ₹698 Cr order book. A retail stock-broker earning a quarter of its money advising ministries on public infrastructure is, in Indian listed markets, close to one of a kind.

The economics — why fee income behaves so differently

  • Contracted, not transactional. A mandate is signed, then executed over 2–3 years, with revenue recognised as milestones are met. That gives forward visibility — the ₹698 Cr order book plus a ₹400 Cr+ pipeline of pending bids tell you a lot about next year's advisory revenue before the year even starts.
  • Counter-cyclical to broking. Government spending on infrastructure and schemes runs on political and budget cycles, not stock-market cycles. When brokerage softens in a bear market, advisory fees keep coming.
  • Asset-light and high-margin. Consulting sells expertise and people, not balance sheet — no loan book to fund, no capital at risk, so incremental margins are high once the team is built.
  • Plus investment banking. The same advisory umbrella houses SME/IPO investment banking — Choice had 34 ongoing IPO mandates in FY26, a fee business that also feeds the broking and distribution machine.
The catch that makes it hard: the government is a wonderful client for visibility and a difficult one for timing. Public-sector receivables can be slow; payments lag; mandates are lumpy and tender-driven. The order book tells you revenue is coming, not exactly when — and a heavy concentration in one or two states (historically Maharashtra) adds execution risk. Choice's stated fix is geographic spread: it is pushing the advisory practice across 22 states to de-risk the single-geography dependence.

The company and who controls it

A Mumbai promoter house that grew from a 1990s broking outfit into a three-legged group.

Choice was incorporated in Mumbai on 12 March 1993 by Sunil Patodia, and remains a promoter-led family business. Control sits with the Patodia / Poddar group — Chairperson Vinita Sunil Patodia, Managing Director Kamal Poddar, and CEO Arun Poddar — who together with related promoter entities hold roughly 54% of the company.

Ownership & governance

  • Promoters (~54%): the Patodia/Poddar family and related entities. Vinita Sunil Patodia, Kamal Poddar, Arun Poddar and Suyash Patodia are among the largest individual holders; the group has historically carried some promoter share pledging (a governance point worth tracking).
  • Institutions: FIIs hold ~11% and are a meaningful presence for a company this size — a sign the diversified-growth story has drawn institutional attention despite the small-cap profile.
  • No dividend. Choice retains its earnings to fund growth across all three legs rather than paying them out — consistent with a company still compounding fast, but a point income-focused investors should note.
The small-cap governance reality: a promoter-concentrated group scaling three regulated businesses quickly is exactly the profile where governance, related-party dealings and disclosure quality deserve extra scrutiny. The flip side of founder control is aligned incentives and fast decision-making; the risk is the classic small-cap one — rapid growth outrunning controls. Nothing here is a red flag, but the standard applied to a ₹17,000 Cr promoter-led compounder should be a demanding one.

The cross-sell flywheel — how the legs feed each other

The real argument for keeping three businesses under one roof.

A diversified group only earns its keep if the pieces help each other, not just sit side by side. Choice's case is that a single client relationship, once won, can be monetised across all three legs — and that the advisory and broking arms feed customers to each other. Traced out, it looks like this:

The broking client base feeds distribution and lending; the SME/IPO clients feed advisory; and government mandates give the whole group a fee-income spine independent of markets. FinX is the digital surface that ties the retail side together.

Where the flywheel is real — and where it is a slide

  • Broking → distribution → lending is a genuine, proven loop: management explicitly credits "increased engagement per client supported by cross-selling of insurance and wealth products through the FinX platform." A client acquired once pays multiple times.
  • SME IPO ↔ broking also connects: an SME the bankers take public becomes a broking/distribution relationship, and vice versa.
  • The government-advisory leg is the loosest link. Advising the Maharashtra government on a media centre does not obviously cross-sell a demat account. Its value to the group is diversification and cash-flow stability, not cross-sell — and it is honest to say the "one flywheel" story works better for the retail legs than for the public-sector one.
The digital layer doing the tying

The glue is FinX, Choice's retail super-app (3 million+ downloads), plus a growing set of partnerships — most notably a tie-up with India Post Payments Bank (IPPB) to push digital investment products through the postal network's vast rural reach. That partnership is the clearest expression of the strategy: use a low-cost distribution rail to acquire small-town clients cheaply, then run them through the cross-sell loop. Whether IPPB-sourced customers actually convert into paying, cross-sold relationships is one of the more interesting things to watch over the next two years.

A test you can't run on a normal broker

Run a find-and-replace: swap "Choice" for "Angel One" and one sentence collapses instantly — "the company's ₹698 Cr advisory order book, dated two-to-three years out, is worth roughly 60% of a full year's group revenue." A pure broker has no order book at all; its revenue resets to zero every morning the market opens and has to be re-earned tick by tick. Choice starts each year already knowing where a large chunk of its advisory revenue will come from. That contracted-revenue cushion is the single feature that separates it from every discount-broking peer — and it is why the group's earnings should, in theory, wobble less than a broker's through a market downturn.

The mix is also the answer to the most important question about the stock: is this a diversified compounder, or a cyclical broker with two side businesses? If the advisory (and NBFC) legs keep gaining share of profit — moving the mix toward that 50/40/10 target — the compounder case strengthens. If broking stays two-thirds of profit, the group's fortunes stay chained to market cycles, whatever the revenue chart says. Watch the profit mix, not just the revenue mix.

08

The industry and the rivals

Three legs means three different competitive arenas — Choice is small in each.

Because Choice competes in three markets, it has no single peer group. It is a mid-tier player in broking, a small player in NBFC lending, and a niche challenger in government consulting — a specialist in none, a generalist across all three. Here is who it lines up against in each ring.

The double-edge of being diversified

Diversification cuts both ways competitively. The upside: Choice is never fully exposed to a single price war — when discount brokers race brokerage to zero, its advisory and lending fees are untouched. The downside: a focused rival can out-invest it in any one arena. Angel One can spend more on broking technology; a dedicated infra-consultancy can field deeper engineering teams; a large NBFC has a cheaper cost of funds. Choice's bet is that the combination — cross-sell, shared brand, a fee spine plus a market-linked base — beats being the best at any one thing. That is a genuine strategy, but it asks management to run three different games well at once, from a small base.

The regulatory backdrop, tripled: each leg answers to a different regulator, and each faces its own live risk. Broking sits under SEBI, whose F&O curbs and margin rules directly shape volumes. The NBFC sits under the RBI, with its evolving stance on lending, provisioning and cost of funds. The advisory arm depends on government tendering and payment cycles. A rule change in any one arena can dent a leg — the price of playing three games.

How to hold the ~80× multiple in your head

At ~₹17,300 Cr market cap on ₹238 Cr of FY26 profit, Choice trades near 80× trailing earnings and ~15–16× book — expensive even for a fast grower. That price makes sense only if the 30%-plus profit growth management targets keeps compounding for several years, and if the advisory and NBFC legs de-risk the earnings enough to deserve a premium over a plain broker. The bull view: a diversified, order-book-backed compounder with a 45% sales CAGR and expanding margins is scarce and worth paying up for. The bear view: this is still a small-cap where two of three legs are cyclical, the consulting arm's revenue is lumpy and government-payment-dependent, and ~80× is a lot to pay for a business that could de-rate sharply on a single weak year. Both readings start from the same numbers.

The one metric that settles it: the advisory order book's conversion into revenue — and with it, the non-broking share of group profit. If the ₹698 Cr book keeps compounding and converting on schedule, dragging the profit mix toward the 50/40/10 target, Choice earns the compounder multiple. If the order book stalls, or converts slowly because government payments lag, the market re-rates it back toward what a cyclical broker-plus-lender is worth. That conversion rate is the number to track, quarter after quarter.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A fast, diversified grower — carrying a small-cap's risks and a rich price.

Choice is one of the more interesting diversified small-caps in Indian financials: a genuine three-legged model, a scarce government-advisory spine, and a record of compounding revenue and profit at 20–45% a year. The investment argument is a tug-of-war between that quality-of-growth and the twin drags of small-cap execution risk and an ~80× price.

The bull case

  • Real diversification — three legs on different cycles; a fee-based advisory spine most brokers lack.
  • Order-book visibility — ₹698 Cr advisory book + ₹400 Cr+ pipeline give revenue foresight a pure broker cannot have.
  • Fast, profitable compounding — 45% five-year sales CAGR, +46% FY26 PAT, margins rising to ~37%.
  • Long runway — India's retail-investing boom, public-infrastructure spend and MSME credit all structural tailwinds; IPPB reach on top.
  • Aligned promoters — a ~54% family-owned group reinvesting all earnings into growth.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~80× earnings on a small-cap; vulnerable to a sharp de-rating on any single weak year.
  • Broking cyclicality — ~59% of revenue rides market volumes; a bear market and SEBI's F&O curbs both bite the biggest leg.
  • Government-payment & execution risk — advisory revenue is lumpy, tender-driven and exposed to slow public-sector receivables.
  • Small-cap scaling & governance — three regulated businesses grown fast from a small base; promoter concentration and past pledging warrant scrutiny.
  • NBFC credit risk — an early-stage loan book whose asset quality is untested through a full down-cycle.
The bottom line

Choice International is a rare thing on an Indian screen: a retail broker whose second act is advising governments on highways, schools and digital public services — a fee spine that could, over time, turn a cyclical broking house into a steadier diversified compounder. That transformation is not yet proven; it lives in one number. The milestone to watch is not this quarter's brokerage but the moment the advisory leg's converted revenue and profit pull the group mix toward management's 50/40/10 target, with the ₹698 Cr order book turning into cash on schedule. Reach that, and the ~80× multiple has a story to grow into; miss it — through slow government payments, a broking downturn, or a scaling stumble — and a small-cap priced for a compounder gets re-rated as the cyclical broker it partly still is.
