00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Central Bank of India ("Central Bank" or "CBI") is one of India's oldest and a mid-size public-sector bank — a 114-year-old, government-controlled lender that gathers deposits from savers across the country and lends to households, farmers, small businesses and corporates. Founded in 1911 as the first Indian commercial bank fully owned and managed by Indians, headquartered in Mumbai, it runs a genuinely national branch network of ~4,585 branches — about two-thirds of them in rural and semi-urban India — and carries a ~₹8.13 lakh crore total business (deposits + advances).

Three facts frame everything:

  • A long, painful clean-up — now finished. Central Bank was the most damaged of the big PSU banks: it was put under the RBI's Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework in June 2017 — effectively banking's intensive-care ward, which restricted its ability to lend — because of towering bad loans and negative returns. Its net NPAs peaked above 10%. It was the last public-sector bank to escape PCA, finally exiting in September 2022. FY26 is a different institution: net profit of ₹4,369 Cr (+15.4%), gross NPAs of just 2.67%, net NPAs of ~0.49% and a fortress ~96.7% provision coverage.
  • Its edge is cheap, sticky deposits. Central Bank is a classic old-PSU franchise: deposit-rich, loan-light. It has a very high CASA ratio of ~47% — nearly half its deposits sit in low- or no-interest current and savings accounts — one of the best low-cost funding mixes in the sector. For years it gathered far more deposits than it lent out; now, free of PCA, it is finally growing loans fast (advances +18.9% in FY26) to put that cheap money to work.
  • The government has started selling down a very large stake. New Delhi once owned ~93% of Central Bank — among the highest state stakes of any listed company in India — and by rule must cut it to 75%. That sell-down is now live: an offer-for-sale (OFS) in May–June 2026 shed ~8%, taking the government to roughly 81%. The tiny public float and the certainty of more selling to come make the stock technically volatile — and both a re-rating candidate and a dilution risk.
In plain terms

Think of Central Bank as a huge, low-cost deposit machine — a ₹4.7-lakh-crore pool of cheap savings gathered over a century through thousands of small-town branches — that spent the 2010s unable to fully use it because a mountain of bad corporate loans had crippled its capital and the regulator had it on a lending leash. That leash came off in 2022. The bad loans are now cleaned up (below 3%), profit is back to a record ~₹4,400 crore, and the bank is finally lending aggressively again. Two open questions define the stock: can it turn its cheap-deposit advantage into durable, higher-quality profit — and will the market ever stop discounting it for the near-certain, ongoing government share sales still to come?

01

Bank economics 101: how a bank actually earns

No finance degree required — just the numbers that drive everything.

A bank is, at its core, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors) and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap between what it earns on loans and pays on deposits — scaled to its assets — is the engine. A handful of terms tell you almost the whole story, and they matter doubly for a bank like Central Bank, whose entire recent history is a story about a bad-loan book that had to be cleaned up and a cheap deposit base it is only now learning to lend against.

The only banking units you need
TermWhat it means (and Central Bank's number)
NIINet Interest Income — interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings. The core revenue line. Central Bank: ~₹4,002 Cr in Q4 FY26 (+17.7% YoY) — growing strongly as the loan book expands.
NIMNet Interest Margin — NII as a % of interest-earning assets. The "spread", standardised. Central Bank: ~3.4% in FY26 — respectable for a PSU bank, helped by its cheap deposits.
CASACurrent + Savings Account deposits — money in everyday accounts that pays little or no interest. The cheaper your funding, the fatter your margin. Central Bank: ~47% — one of the highest in the sector, its single biggest strength.
GNPA / NNPAGross / Net Non-Performing Assets — the % of loans that have gone bad. The single best gauge of lending discipline. Central Bank: 2.67% / ~0.49% — a world away from the double-digit net NPAs of the crisis years.
PCRProvision Coverage Ratio — how much of the bad loans the bank has already set money aside against. Central Bank: ~96.7%, meaning almost all recognised bad loans are pre-funded — one of the highest PCRs anywhere.
CRAR / CET-1Capital Adequacy — the equity cushion versus loans, the buffer against losses (and the very thing PCA polices). Central Bank: CRAR 17.91%, CET-1 15.61% — comfortably above the regulatory floor.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. So a bank gets richer four ways — lend more (grow advances), fund cheaper (more CASA, lower deposit cost), lose less (low NPAs and low credit cost), and earn fees (cards, insurance, forex, distribution). Central Bank's story touches every lever: for years, "loan losses" were so enormous they pushed the bank into deep losses; fixing them brought profit back. Now the growth lever is "lend more" — using its exceptional cheap-funding advantage to finally expand a book that was frozen for half a decade.

Why "loan losses" and "capital" are the swing factors for this bank

  • Provisioning is the hidden lever. When a loan turns bad, the bank must set aside money to cover the expected loss — a provision, charged straight against profit. In the crisis years Central Bank was provisioning so heavily it wiped out its entire spread. As the book cleaned up, that charge fell and profit reappeared. This is why credit cost (provisions as a % of loans) is the number that made — and can unmake — the turnaround.
  • Capital is the leash. A bank can only lend a multiple of its equity capital; if bad loans eat that capital, the regulator forces it to stop growing. That is exactly what PCA did to Central Bank for five years (§04). Rebuilding capital — via government infusions and retained profit — is what let the lending engine restart.
  • Slippages are the leading indicator. A "slippage" is a loan freshly turning bad. Watching fresh slippages each quarter tells you whether the clean book is staying clean — the single most important early-warning gauge for a bank with Central Bank's history.
02

How the money flows: deposits → loans → margin

Read this once and the whole bank clicks into place.

A bank is a conveyor belt for money. Savers' deposits come in one end; loans go out the other; the spread between the two is harvested as net interest income. Two things govern how much profit drips out: how cheap the deposits are (the CASA mix) and how much more the loans yield than the deposits cost (the margin). For Central Bank the interesting twist is the shape of the belt itself: for years it gathered far more deposits than it lent — the money piled up at the deposit end and only trickled out as loans, dragging returns down. The whole recent story is that belt finally speeding up on the loan side.

Central Bank's edge sits at Stage 1: a ~47% CASA mix means unusually cheap fuel. Its historical weakness sat at Stage 3: it under-lent, so a lot of that cheap money earned only low-yield investment returns instead of loan yields. Speeding up Stage 3 — safely — is the entire growth thesis.

Why the CASA mix is the whole game — and Central Bank's is superb

A current account pays 0% and a savings account ~3%; a bulk fixed deposit can cost 7%+. The more of your funding that sits in low-cost CASA, the cheaper your money — and the wider your margin on the same loan. Central Bank's CASA of ~47% is among the highest of any large Indian bank, a legacy of its century-old network of small-town savings-account customers. This is a genuine, hard-to-replicate asset: gathering cheap, sticky deposits is the difficult, valuable half of banking, and Central Bank has been quietly good at it even through its worst years. The problem was never the funding — it was what the bank did (or couldn't do) with it.

Why "under-lending" quietly capped returns

Net interest income = volume × spread. Central Bank always had cheap deposits (good spread on each loan) but too little loan volume — its credit-deposit ratio (loans as a share of deposits) sat well below peers, meaning a large chunk of its cheap deposits sat in low-yielding government bonds rather than higher-yielding loans. That is why its returns lagged even as its funding cost was low: it wasn't using the machine at full capacity. FY26's ~19% advances growth lifts the credit-deposit ratio toward the mid-70s and puts more of that cheap money into higher-yielding loans — the single clearest lever it has to raise return on assets from a low base. The risk, of course, is lending too fast and re-importing the bad loans it just spent a decade clearing.

03

The company and who controls it

A 1911 pioneer — the first "swadeshi" bank — now majority-owned by the state.

Central Bank of India was founded on 21 December 1911 by Sir Sorabji Pochkhanawala, a Parsi banker, with Sir Pherozeshah Mehta as its first chairman. It holds a special place in Indian history as the first commercial bank wholly owned and managed by Indians — Pochkhanawala reportedly called it "the property of the nation." Nationalised along with India's other large banks in 1969, it is today a professionally-run public-sector bank headquartered in Mumbai, controlled by the Government of India, with roughly 4,585 branches spanning all 28 states — about 65% of them in rural and semi-urban India.

Timeline

  • 1920s–60s

  • 1969
    Nationalised by the Government of India alongside 13 other major banks — becoming a fully state-owned public-sector bank.
  • 2007
    Comes to market with an IPO, listing on the exchanges while the government retains a dominant majority.
  • Jun 2017
    Placed under PCA. The RBI puts Central Bank in the Prompt Corrective Action framework as net NPAs top ~10% and returns turn negative — restricting fresh lending and expansion.
  • 2017–2021
    Repeated government capital infusions recapitalise the bank; bad loans are recognised and provisioned; the book is slowly cleaned.
  • Sep 2022
    Exits PCA — the last public-sector bank to do so — after net NPAs fall below 4% and capital ratios recover. The lending leash comes off.
  • Jun 2025
    Enters insurance via the Generali partnership — acquiring ~25% stakes in Future Generali's life and general-insurance arms (later "Generali Central").
  • FY26
    Record net profit ₹4,369 Cr (+15.4%); total business ₹8.13 lakh Cr; GNPA 2.67% / NNPA ~0.49%; PCR ~96.7%; CRAR 17.91%. The government sells ~8% via OFS, cutting its stake from ~89% toward ~81%.

Ownership & management

  • Government of India — the dominant owner. The state's stake was ~93% as recently as end-2024 — one of the highest of any listed company in India — and stood at ~89.3% in March 2026 before the May–June 2026 OFS cut it to roughly 81%. This anchors the stock's identity as a "PSU bank" and drives both its funding safety and its valuation discount (§07).
  • A very thin public float. Even after the OFS, non-government holders own under a fifth of the bank — domestic institutions (notably LIC, which raised its stake past 6% during the OFS), mutual funds, a sliver of foreign investors and retail. Because the government must still fall to 75%, further dilution is not a possibility but a scheduled certainty.
  • Board-driven, government-appointed leadership. Like all PSU banks, top management (MD & CEO, executive directors) is appointed via the government's process on fixed tenures — a governance model that differs sharply from the founder- or professional-CEO continuity of private banks.
  • An emerging financial-services footprint. Alongside the core bank sit its new insurance associate stakes (Generali Central life & general insurance, §08), sponsored regional rural banks, and a large network of business correspondents reaching deep into rural India — small relative to the bank, but a source of fee income and cross-sell.
04

The PCA years: banking's intensive-care ward

Why Central Bank spent five years unable to lend freely — and what PCA actually is.

To understand Central Bank you have to understand the five years it lost. From June 2017 to September 2022, it was held under the RBI's Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework — and it was the last of India's public-sector banks to get out. PCA is the single most important fact in the bank's modern history, because it explains both why its loan book stayed small for years and why the recovery, once it came, was so pronounced.

What PCA is, in plain English

PCA is the RBI's early-warning-and-restraint regime for weak banks. When a bank breaches thresholds on three vital signs — capital adequacy, net NPAs, and profitability/leverage — the regulator places it under PCA and imposes restrictions to stop it digging a deeper hole. Those restrictions can include curbs on fresh lending (especially to risky borrowers), limits on branch expansion, dividend bans, and tighter oversight of costs and hiring. It is, in effect, being put on a ventilator: the patient is stabilised and stopped from doing further harm, but it also can't run. For a bank, "can't lend freely" means "can't grow its main engine" — which is why time under PCA is so costly.

Why PCA both hurt and helped

PCA is punishing in the short run — a bank frozen out of full-throated lending cedes market share to healthier rivals and earns less on its assets, which is a big reason Central Bank's returns lagged for years and why it became a deposit-heavy, loan-light franchise (it kept gathering deposits but couldn't fully lend them). But PCA is also curative: it forced the bank to recognise its bad loans honestly, provision heavily against them, and rebuild capital — supported by repeated government injections. By the time it exited in September 2022, its net NPAs had fallen to ~3.9% (from ~10.2% in 2017) and its CRAR had recovered to ~13.3%. The ventilator did its job: a stabilised, recapitalised, honestly-provisioned bank walked out — and could finally start lending again.

Why this section matters most: almost everything good in Central Bank's numbers today — the return to record profit, the sub-3% GNPA, the ~19% loan growth — is the mirror image of the PCA years. It was so damaged it needed the intensive-care regime longer than any peer; the flip side is that its recovery is more recent and, arguably, has more room to run off a lower base. The risk that shadows the whole story is simple: a bank that just spent five years in intensive care must not re-injure itself by lending recklessly now that the leash is off.
05

The turnaround and the re-rating story

How a loss-making, PCA-bound bank became a record-profit lender — and why the market is warming to it.

Rewind to the late 2010s. Central Bank of India was posting losses, its net NPAs were in double digits, and it was locked under PCA. Understanding how a bank climbs out of that hole is the key to the entire investment case, because a bank's earnings are governed less by how much it lends than by how little of what it lent goes bad — and Central Bank's recovery is a textbook example of a bad-loan clean-up flowing straight through to profit.

The mechanism: why cleaning bad loans creates profit

A bank's profit is roughly net interest income + fees − operating costs − loan-loss provisions − tax. In the crisis years, the provisions line was so vast — money set aside against a mountain of bad corporate loans — that it wiped out the entire spread the bank earned, pushing it into losses. Why this drives the "re-rating"

A "re-rating" means the market pays a higher multiple for the same earnings because it trusts them more. For years, investors valued Central Bank at a deep discount because its profits were non-existent or fragile — a PCA-bound bank with double-digit bad loans. Now that GNPA is under 3%, net NPA is a negligible ~0.49%, and provision coverage is an exceptional ~96.7%, those earnings look far more durable. The bull thesis is straightforward: a bank now earning a positive (if still modest) return on assets, with a clean book and its best asset — cheap deposits — finally being lent out, should trade closer to book value than the deep discount it sat at through the crisis. The bear counter is that Central Bank's returns are still lower than better PSU peers, its recovery is younger, and part of any given quarter's profit can lean on volatile treasury gains and lumpy recoveries rather than core spread.

06

The deposit-heavy franchise — cheap money looking for a job

The defining feature of this particular bank — a superb funding base attached to a historically under-used lending engine.

Every PSU bank has a turnaround story; what makes Central Bank distinctive is the shape of its balance sheet. It is unusually deposit-rich and loan-light: a very high CASA ratio (~47%) and a huge, sticky, low-cost deposit base, married for years to a loan book that was too small relative to those deposits. That combination is both the bank's greatest asset and the clearest explanation for why its returns have lagged — and why they can now improve.

Why a ~47% CASA ratio is a genuine moat

Anyone can lend money; the hard, valuable part of banking is gathering deposits cheaply and reliably. A current account pays 0% and a savings account ~3%, while a bulk term deposit can cost 7%+. A bank whose funding is nearly half CASA has structurally cheaper money than a rival that must "buy" deposits at high rates — and can therefore lend profitably through almost any cycle. Central Bank's ~47% CASA — built over a century of small-ticket savings relationships across rural and semi-urban India, and reinforced by its status as an ultra-safe government bank — is one of the best low-cost funding profiles among large Indian lenders. This is the quiet, durable strength that survived even the crisis years.

Why cheap deposits + more lending = higher returns

Here is the elegant part of the Central Bank story. Its return on assets has been low (~0.9% in FY26) not because its funding is expensive — it is cheap — but because too little of that funding was earning full loan yields. Money parked in government bonds earns less than money lent to a homebuyer or a small business. So as the bank lifts its credit-deposit ratio — putting a bigger share of its cheap ~47%-CASA deposits into higher-yielding retail, agri and MSME loans — its net interest income rises without its funding cost rising much. That is the cleanest path to a higher return on assets from a low base. The catch, and it is the whole risk, is credit quality: growing a loan book at ~19% is exactly when banks plant the seeds of the next bad-loan cycle. Watch slippages and credit cost alongside the loan growth — safe growth re-rates the stock; reckless growth repeats history.

07

The government float and the dilution overhang

Why a ~93%-then-~81% government stake is the single biggest technical driver of the stock.

Central Bank has an ownership quirk sharper than almost any other listed bank: for years the Government of India owned ~93% of it — leaving barely 7% in public hands. India's market rules (SEBI's Minimum Public Shareholding, or MPS, norm) require every listed company to have at least 25% public float — meaning the government must eventually cut its stake to 75%. For Central Bank, that is not a distant technicality; it is an active, ongoing process that shapes how the stock trades week to week.

What "minimum public shareholding" means, and why it's an overhang

An "overhang" is a known future supply of shares that hangs over the price. When the government still needs to sell a large block to meet the 25% float rule, investors know that more stock is coming — usually via an Offer for Sale (OFS) (the government sells existing shares into the market, often at a discount to the current price) or a Qualified Institutional Placement (QIP) (the bank issues new shares to institutions, raising capital but diluting existing holders). Either way, the extra supply tends to cap the price and add volatility around each sale.

Why the overhang cuts both ways

The bad news for a holder: a huge government stake still to be sold means recurring supply. Each OFS is typically priced at a discount to the market, dragging the shares down around the event (the stock fell ~6% when the FY26 OFS opened at its ₹31 floor). And because the float has been so tiny, the price can whip around on relatively small trades — the stock is technically volatile. If future dilution comes via a QIP of new shares, it also increases the share count, spreading the same profit over more shares.

The good news: the same forced sell-down is a genuine re-rating catalyst. A larger public float means more liquidity, more index and institutional eligibility, and more analyst coverage — all of which can lift the multiple over time. The government also wants a higher share price, because it makes each stake sale more valuable, aligning New Delhi's interest with minority holders'. And the OFS demand (2.4× subscribed, with LIC buying) shows real appetite. Net: the overhang is a headwind on any given day but, handled well, the transition from a 7%-float curiosity to a properly-floated bank can be part of the bull case.

The honest tension: a very high government stake is exactly why Central Bank can be both cheap and a value trap. Until the float normalises, the stock trades with a "supply hanging over it" discount; each sale is dilutive or price-suppressing in the short term even as it improves the long-term liquidity story. Watch for the timing and pricing of the next OFS or QIP — it is the most reliable near-term driver of the share price, arguably more than any single quarter's earnings.
08

The Generali entry — a toe-hold in insurance

A new diversification and bancassurance move — modest in size, but a genuine change of direction.

In 2025 Central Bank made its most notable strategic move in years: it entered the insurance business by partnering with the global insurer Generali. This is a diversification and "bancassurance" play — a way to earn fee income and cross-sell insurance to its vast deposit base — and it is worth understanding precisely, because it is easy to overstate.

What Central Bank actually bought

  • General insurance: in June 2025 Central Bank acquired a ~24.91% stake in Future Generali India Insurance (the non-life / general-insurance arm) for up to ~₹451 crore, picked up through an insolvency process involving the former Future group.
  • Life insurance: it also acquired a ~25.18% stake in Future Generali India Life Insurance for ~₹57 crore.
  • Rebranded and rising: the two entities were renamed under a unified "Generali Central" identity, and in March 2026 the Competition Commission cleared Central Bank to lift each stake to ~26%. Generali remains the majority owner (~74%).
An important clarification: despite occasional loose reporting, these are significant minority (associate) stakes of ~25–26%, not majority acquisitions. Generali is the controlling shareholder. Central Bank is a strategic minority partner and, crucially, the bancassurance distributor — it does not control the insurers.
Why a bank buys into insurance — "bancassurance"

"Bancassurance" simply means selling insurance through a bank's branches. For a bank with ~4,585 branches and tens of millions of deposit customers, that distribution reach is worth a lot to an insurer — and the bank, in turn, earns fee/commission income for every policy it sells, plus a share of the insurers' profits via its ~26% stake. It is an attractive adjacency: fee income is capital-light (it doesn't consume the bank's scarce lending capital) and it diversifies revenue away from the pure interest spread. For Central Bank specifically, it is a way to monetise its greatest asset — its customer base and branch network — beyond lending, and to build optionality in fast-growing life and general insurance. The caveats: the stakes are small relative to the ₹8-lakh-crore bank, the investment ties up some capital, and turning a distribution relationship into real, sustained fee income takes execution. It's a sensible strategic seed, not (yet) a needle-mover for group profit.

09

The PSU-bank re-rating and where Central Bank sits

A sector-wide theme — and an honest placement of Central Bank within the PSU pack.

Central Bank is not an island; it rides a broader theme. After a decade in the wilderness, India's public-sector banks have been re-rating — cleaning up bad loans, returning to strong profits, and finally being rewarded by the market off very low valuations. Understanding that theme, and being honest about where Central Bank sits within it, is essential: it is a smaller, mid-tier PSU franchise, below the sector's leaders on scale and returns.

The PSU re-rating theme, in brief

  • Cheap on price-to-book. PSU banks trade at a fraction of private-bank valuations. The re-rating bet is that as their asset quality and returns prove durable, that discount narrows.
  • Government backing. Sovereign ownership makes their deposits ultra-safe and their funding cheap — and repeated capital infusions mean they rarely fail. That safety is worth something the market long ignored.
  • Improving return ratios off a low base. Return on assets across the pack has climbed toward ~1%+; loan growth has picked up; credit costs have collapsed. Even modest improvement from a low base is a big percentage change.
  • But real handicaps remain — lower RoA/RoE than private banks, weaker technology and efficiency, priority-sector lending mandates, and government-appointed management. The re-rating narrows the gap; it rarely closes it entirely.

Where Central Bank really fits

Be clear-eyed: within the PSU universe, Central Bank is not a leader — it sits below SBI, Bank of Baroda, PNB, Canara and Indian Bank on both scale and return ratios, and its ~0.9% RoA trails the ~1%+ that the stronger PSU banks now earn. What it does have is a distinctive combination: an outstanding low-cost deposit base, a very late-stage but genuine turnaround (more recovery runway off a lower base), and a heavy government-dilution story that can drive re-rating as the float normalises. Its closest analogue is UCO Bank — another small PSU that emerged from the PCA/high-government-stake mould. The investment question is whether a cheap, deposit-rich, improving-but-still-sub-par bank re-rates toward its better peers as it lends more and floats more stock — or stays a perennial discount name because its returns never quite catch up.

How to think about the valuation

Central Bank trades around a ~₹29,500 crore market cap (~₹32–33/share) on FY26 earnings — roughly ~6.5× earnings, ~0.7–0.8× book value, with a dividend yield around ~3.7%. For a bank, the multiple that matters most is price-to-book against return on equity: a bank earning a ~13% ROE would, in a "normal" world, trade at or above 1× book. Central Bank sitting below book is the market applying a meaningful discount for three reasons — a still-modest ~0.89% RoA (below the best PSU peers), a younger and less-proven recovery, and the near-certain further government dilution (§07). The bull case is that a durable clean book plus a rising credit-deposit ratio lifts returns and closes some of the discount, with the float-normalisation as an added catalyst; the bear case is that the returns stay sub-par, part of the profit leans on treasury and recoveries, and repeated OFS/QIP supply keeps a lid on the price. Unlike a private bank, the debate here is as much about the multiple, the float and dilution as about the earnings.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A healed, cheap, deposit-rich PSU bank — the debate is returns, durability and dilution.

Central Bank of India has done the hard part: it has climbed out of a near-existential bad-loan crisis and five years in the RBI's PCA ward — the last PSU bank to escape — to a clean book, record ~₹4,400 Cr profit and a fortress ~96.7% provision coverage. The investment debate now is almost entirely about how far its returns can rise (can it lend its cheap deposits out fast and safely to lift a sub-par RoA?), how durable and repeatable the profit is (core spread vs treasury and recovery gains), and how the ongoing government sell-down plays out (re-rating catalyst or persistent overhang?).

The bull case

  • Exceptional cheap funding — a ~47% CASA ratio and a huge, sticky, low-cost deposit base, one of the best in the sector; the hard half of banking, already done.
  • Clean, de-risked book — GNPA 2.67%, NNPA ~0.49%, PCR ~96.7%; the crisis-era risk is recognised and provided-for.
  • Under-lent, with room to grow — a historically low ~74% credit-deposit ratio and +18.9% advances growth mean cheap deposits are finally being put to higher-yielding work, lifting returns off a low base.
  • Cheap valuation — below book value on a ~13% ROE, ~6.5× earnings, ~3.7% dividend yield; a modest re-rating is a solid return.
  • Float normalisation & optionality — the forced government sell-down improves liquidity and coverage over time; the Generali bancassurance seed adds fee-income optionality.

The bear case

  • Sub-par returns — ~0.89% RoA lags SBI, BoB, PNB, Canara and Indian Bank; Central Bank is a mid-tier PSU, not a leader.
  • Dilution overhang — the government still holds ~81% and must reach 75%; recurring OFS/QIP supply is dilutive and price-suppressing, and the thin float makes the stock volatile.
  • Growth-quality risk — lending at ~19% right after a bad-loan crisis is exactly when future NPAs are seeded; the recovery is younger than peers'.
  • Profit quality — quarterly results can lean on lumpy treasury gains and recoveries; Q4 FY26 already showed a one-time ₹632 Cr tax hit dent the number.
  • PSU handicaps — government control, priority-sector mandates, a tech/efficiency gap versus private banks, and slower structural growth.
The bottom line

Central Bank of India is a genuinely healed, exceptionally well-provisioned public-sector bank with a superb cheap-deposit franchise — trading below its own book value. The business risk is far lower than it was during the PCA years: the book is clean, capital is strong, and profit is at a record. But it is honestly a smaller, lower-return member of the PSU pack, and its stock is unusually shaped by a technical force — a giant government stake still being sold down toward the 75% floor. The story over the next few years will be written in a handful of numbers: the credit-deposit ratio and RoA (is the cheap money being lent out to lift returns?), slippages and credit cost (is the fast loan growth staying clean?), CASA and NIM (is the funding edge holding?), and the timing and pricing of the next government stake sale. Get the lending-quality and returns right while the float normalises, and a cheap, clean, deposit-rich bank can re-rate toward book and beyond; let returns stagnate or dilute at a bad price, and it stays what the market fears — a cheap bank that stays cheap.
