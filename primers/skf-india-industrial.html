00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

SKF India (Industrial) Ltd makes bearings — the precision-machined rings of steel and rolling balls or rollers that let a shaft spin smoothly under load. It is the Indian arm of Sweden's SKF, the world's largest bearing manufacturer, and it sells far more than the bearing alone: seals, lubrication systems, condition-monitoring sensors and a services/aftermarket business that keeps industrial machinery running. Its customers are heavy industry, railways, cement, mining, metals, renewables and the machine builders who put bearings inside the equipment they sell — plus a large replacement market of factories buying spares to keep existing machines alive.

Three facts frame everything:

  • This is a brand-new listed company, born from a split. In 2025 the old SKF India Ltd demerged its Industrial business from its Automotive business into two separately listed companies. The Industrial half became SKF India (Industrial) Ltd — SKFINDUS, BSE 544572; the residual Automotive half kept the old name and the old code (BSE 500472). The demerger completed on 1 October 2025 at a 1:1 share ratio. Because it is so recent, clean standalone-Industrial audited full-year figures are limited — several numbers here are carve-out, first-year or "held".
  • The industrial half was the bigger half. Reconciling the two post-demerger entities, Industrial was roughly 62% of the old combined business and Automotive roughly 38%. In its first reported year (FY26) the Industrial entity did about ₹3,440 Cr of revenue. Its reported profit of ~₹218 Cr was knocked down by a one-time ~₹196 Cr demerger-related charge; strip that out and normalised PAT was closer to ~₹410 Cr. The cleanest single datapoint is Q3 FY26 — the first full quarter as a standalone firm — with revenue of ~₹861 Cr at an underlying pre-tax margin of about 13%.
  • It is a scarce, high-return MNC pure-play. Parent AB SKF owns 52.58%. The business runs at roughly 27% return on equity and ~30% return on capital, funds itself, and now offers investors a clean, listed bet on Indian industrial bearings alongside only Schaeffler and Timken. The market values it near ₹13,550 Cr — about 62× reported earnings, or ~35× once you normalise for the demerger charge.
In plain terms

What you are buying here is the freshly-carved-out industrial half of a global bearings leader — an aftermarket annuity on India's rotating machinery, now free of its slower, more cyclical automotive twin. Bearings are the definition of a boring-but-essential product: almost nobody thinks about them, yet one sits inside nearly every motor, pump, gearbox, train axle and wind turbine in the country, and every one of them eventually wears out and needs replacing. SKF sells that first bearing to the machine maker and the replacement bearing to the factory owner for the rest of the machine's life. The whole investment question is whether this newly independent company can grow that industrial-plus-replacement revenue while holding its margins on a fresh, standalone cost base — and whether a rich valuation already assumes it will.

01

Bearings 101: the part you never think about, in almost everything

No engineering degree needed — just why this humble ring of steel is everywhere.

A bearing is a machine component that lets one part rotate against another with as little friction as possible while carrying a load. In its most common form it is two concentric steel rings with a set of hardened balls or rollers running between them; the inner ring turns with the shaft, the outer ring stays fixed in the housing, and the rolling elements let the two spin past each other almost frictionlessly. Take the bearing out and a spinning shaft grinds metal on metal, overheats, and destroys itself in seconds. Put a good one in and the machine runs quietly for years.

That is why bearings are one of the most universal industrial products in existence. Anywhere something rotates — and almost every machine has something rotating — there is a bearing doing the work you never see.

The handful of terms that run the whole business
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
BearingThe core product: rings + rolling elements that carry a rotating load. Comes in families — ball bearings (light, fast), tapered/spherical roller bearings (heavy loads, railways, mining), and more. SKF makes the full range for industry.
OE / OEM"Original Equipment (Manufacturer)" — bearings sold to the firm building a new machine (a pump maker, a rail-coach factory, a turbine builder), fitted at the factory. Lower-margin, volume-driven, tied to industrial capex.
Aftermarket / replacementBearings sold as spares to keep existing machines running. Every bearing eventually wears out, so this is a recurring, higher-margin annuity sold through a distributor network. The stickier half of the business.

Condition monitoringSensors and software that "listen" to a bearing's vibration to predict failure before it happens. The digital, services-led layer SKF sells on top of the hardware — turning a spare-parts sale into an uptime contract.
Why "boring and essential" is the point

A bearing is cheap relative to the machine it sits in — often a rounding error on the cost of a pump or a locomotive — but if it fails, the whole machine stops. That asymmetry is the entire commercial logic of a premium bearing brand. A cement plant will not risk a multi-crore kiln on a bargain bearing to save a few hundred rupees; it buys the branded one it trusts. So bearings behave less like a commodity and more like an insurance policy the customer pays willingly — which is why a name like SKF can hold pricing and margin in a product that, on paper, looks like plain machined steel.

A genuine surprise: the reason this company exists as a separate listed stock at all is that SKF India split into two — and the part investors now buy is precisely the one nobody notices. You will not find an SKF industrial bearing on a shelf or in an ad, yet one is very likely turning inside the fan cooling the room you are in, the pump in your building's water system, the train you took this week and the wind turbine on the horizon. A product this invisible is rarely the headline of a listed company; here it is the whole company.
02

What SKF Industrial actually sells: hardware plus the service around it

Four product families, one deep customer relationship — read this once and the model clicks.

SKF Industrial is not just a bearing box-shifter. It sells an integrated package of rotating-equipment technology: the bearing, the seal that protects it, the lubrication that feeds it, the sensor that watches it, and the engineering service that ties them together. Each product deepens the same relationship with the same industrial customer — and each is a step up the value ladder from plain hardware toward recurring, service-like revenue.

The product families, decoded

  • Bearings — the core. Ball, cylindrical, tapered and spherical roller bearings across sizes from a fist to a metre wide, engineered for specific duties: high speed, heavy load, dirty environments, high temperature. This is where the SKF name earns its premium — the metallurgy, tolerances and design that make a bearing last are genuinely hard to copy.
  • Seals — the guardian. A bearing dies from contamination as often as from load, so SKF sells the seals that keep grit and water out. Low glamour, but they attach naturally to every bearing sale and lift the value of each machine position.
  • Lubrication systems — the feed. Automatic lubricators and central lubrication systems that deliver the right amount of grease at the right time, which is the single biggest driver of how long a bearing survives. Another adjacency that turns a component sale into a small system sale.
  • Condition monitoring & services — the brain. Vibration sensors, analytics and reliability engineering that predict a failure weeks before it happens. This is SKF's move from selling parts to selling uptime — the highest-value, most defensible layer, because it embeds SKF into the customer's maintenance planning, not just its purchase order.
Why the whole stack matters, not just the bearing

Any low-cost rival can undercut SKF on a single generic bearing. What is far harder to undercut is the bundle: the bearing plus the seal plus the lubrication plus the sensor plus the SKF engineer who tells a plant which bearing is about to fail and swaps it during planned downtime. Sold together, these turn a commodity component into a reliability service — and a reliability service is priced on the cost of unplanned downtime (which can be enormous), not on the cost of a steel ring. That bundling is how a bearing maker defends margin against cheaper competition, and it is the strategic reason the demerger created a focused industrial company that can sell the full stack rather than being half a car-parts business.

The demerger: what the split did, and what each half kept

The defining corporate event — and the reason to read every number carefully.

For decades, SKF India was a single listed company that sold bearings to both industry and the auto sector. In 2025 it deliberately cut itself in two. Understanding exactly what went where is essential, because it determines which historical figures belong to the company in this primer — and which quietly belong to its twin.

04

OE versus the aftermarket: where the real money hides

The single most important economic idea in an industrial-components business.

A bearing maker sells the same physical product into two very different economies. One is original equipment (OE) — bearings fitted into new machines at the factory, bought in bulk by manufacturers who negotiate hard on price and rise and fall with the industrial capital-spending cycle. The other is the aftermarket — replacement bearings bought by the owners of existing machines when the old one wears out. They look similar on a shelf. They could hardly be more different as businesses.

Illustrative, not a reported split — SKFINDUS does not disclose an exact OE/aftermarket ratio. The point is directional: aftermarket revenue is more recurring and higher-margin; OE revenue is larger in bursts but swings with the industrial cycle and prices harder.

The aftermarket is the annuity. A machine sold today with an SKF bearing inside becomes an SKF customer for the next fifteen or twenty years, because that bearing will eventually fail and be replaced — often more than once. The owner, mid-production, is not shopping on price; they want the exact part that fits, available fast, from a brand they trust. That is why aftermarket bearings sell at better margins than OE bearings, through a distribution network rather than a procurement tender, and why the size of the installed base of machines running SKF parts matters as much as this year's new sales.

Why the aftermarket is the ballast under a cyclical business

OE demand is hostage to the capital-spending cycle: when factories stop building new lines, OE bearing orders dry up fast. The aftermarket does not behave that way — the machines already installed keep running, keep wearing, and keep needing spares even in a downturn (arguably more, as owners stretch the life of old equipment rather than buy new). So the replacement business is the counter-cyclical keel that steadies an otherwise capex-driven company. For a firm that has just been separated specifically to focus on industry, the depth of its aftermarket and distribution reach is one of the truest measures of its quality — and one of the things SKF's decades-old dealer network genuinely provides that a new entrant cannot quickly replicate.

What SKFINDUS does not tell you (yet): the company does not publish a clean OE-versus-aftermarket revenue split, so anyone claiming a precise number is guessing. As standalone reporting matures, the disclosure worth watching most is exactly this mix — because a rising aftermarket share is the clearest sign the demerged industrial company is compounding an annuity rather than just riding the capex cycle.
05

The Swedish parent, the brand, and the moat

Why "made by SKF" is a moat — and where the parent both helps and takes.

SKFINDUS is majority-owned and technologically fed by AB SKF of Sweden, founded in 1907 and the largest bearing manufacturer on earth (group revenue around SEK 99 bn, roughly US$9 bn, in 2024). That parentage is the company's biggest single asset — and, in the fine print, a modest recurring cost.

ElementWhat it isWhy it matters
BrandSKF is a century-old global reliability standard — a byword for "the bearing you trust on critical equipment."Lets the Indian company hold premium pricing and win the aftermarket, where trust beats price.
TechnologyAccess to the parent's global R&D in bearing metallurgy, seals, lubrication and condition-monitoring software.Keeps the product a generation ahead of low-cost rivals without India funding all the research itself.

The localization story is genuinely important. India still imports a large share of its high-end bearings, and a domestically manufactured SKF product — same technology, same brand, made locally — is exactly what both customers and policy want: shorter delivery times, rupee pricing, and a hedge against import disruption. As Indian industry scales, an established local maker of premium bearings is positioned to take share from imports rather than merely grow with the market.

The two-edged nature of a controlling parent

The parent relationship is mostly a gift and partly a tax. On the upside, SKFINDUS gets a global brand, a century of R&D and a proven product without carrying the full cost of any of them — an enormous head start over a domestic challenger. On the downside, a controlling foreign parent that also charges royalties and technology fees means a slice of every rupee of profit flows to Sweden by design, related-party pricing warrants scrutiny, and minority Indian shareholders are along for the ride on decisions made in Gothenburg. The moat is real; just remember the tollgate is owned by the majority holder, not by the minority investors who buy the stock.

Worth verifying yourself: the ~4%-of-sales royalty figure comes from an older era of the company's disclosures and may not reflect the current arrangement for the newly demerged entity. Treat the exact rate as held and check the latest related-party notes; the direction — a permanent parent fee on Indian sales — is what matters for the margin analysis.
06

The industrial-capex tailwind — and its cyclical shadow

Why now, and why "when the capex cycle turns" cuts both ways.

A pure-play industrial bearings company is, at heart, a leveraged bet on Indian industry running and expanding. Every new factory line, every kilometre of railway rolling stock, every wind turbine and every pump installed adds bearings on the way in and a stream of replacement bearings for years after. The bull case rests on a genuine, multi-year industrial and infrastructure build-out; the bear case is that the very same capex cycle can go into reverse.

The honest shape of the cycle

The tailwind is real, but it is a capex tailwind, and capex is cyclical by nature. When industry is investing, OE bearing orders surge and the installed base grows; when investment pauses — a slow year, a rate shock, a global downturn — new-machine orders fall away quickly. What steadies SKFINDUS through that is the breadth on the chart above (no single sector carries it) and the replacement business running underneath all of them. This is not a company that escapes the industrial cycle; it is one built to be less violently thrown by it than a single-end-market supplier, with an aftermarket keel that keeps paying while OE waits for the next upswing.

07

The company, the parent, and who controls it

A Swedish-controlled MNC with a heavy institutional register and a thin retail float.

SKFINDUS is controlled by AB SKF of Sweden, which holds 52.58% (routed since December 2025 through an intermediate holding entity, SKF Interim AB, in an intra-group transfer). The rest of the register is unusually institution-heavy for a mid-cap, which shapes how the stock trades.

Ownership & structure — what to keep straight

  • Parent: AB SKF (Gothenburg, Sweden), the world's largest bearing maker, holds 52.58% via SKF Interim AB. The same stake mirrors across both demerged Indian entities because of the 1:1 split.
  • Management: the standalone industrial company is led by managing director Mukund Vasudevan, who has framed the early standalone quarters as a "stable start" with robust underlying performance beneath the one-off demerger noise.
  • Don't confuse the siblings: SKFINDUS / BSE 544572 is the industrial company in this primer. SKF India (SKFINDIA / BSE 500472) is now the automotive company — the old ticker, a different business. Any figure you pull must be matched to the right one of the two.
  • Balance sheet: the industrial entity is self-funding, with high returns on capital (~30% ROCE, ~27% ROE) and no dependence on debt to run its working capital — a fundamentally sound starting position for a newly listed company.
The related-party lens: a 52.58% foreign parent that also supplies technology, brand and (historically) royalties means SKFINDUS is run for the benefit of a controlling shareholder in Sweden as much as for minority Indian investors. That is not unusual for an MNC subsidiary and is not a red flag by itself — but transfer pricing, royalty rates and any parent-favouring capital decisions deserve ongoing attention, because the minority owns a business it does not control.

The rivals: a premium MNC cluster, plus cheap imports

Who else makes bearings in India — and where the real pressure comes from.

Indian bearings is a concentrated, quality-gated market: making precision bearings at scale needs metallurgy, capital and a track record, so it is a game of a few serious players rather than a fragmented crowd. On the listed side, the premium industrial conversation is really SKF Industrial, Schaeffler and Timken, with domestic specialists and low-cost imports competing at the edges.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two forces matter. First, at the premium end this is a rational oligopoly — SKF, Schaeffler and Timken compete on engineering, availability and service rather than a price war, and a rising industrial tide can lift all three. Second, the genuine threat is not another premium brand but the low-cost import at the bottom of the aftermarket, where a cost-conscious buyer of a non-critical bearing may not pay up for the SKF name. SKF's defence is to keep selling reliability, not steel — the seal, the lubrication, the sensor, the fast local availability and the guarantee that the part is genuine — so that even a price-sensitive customer on a machine that matters comes back to the branded product.

The one number that settles the thesis

At ~35× normalised earnings for a business barely two quarters into standalone life, the case turns on a single, checkable thing: can SKFINDUS grow industrial-plus-aftermarket revenue while holding — ideally lifting — its margin on the new, unbundled cost base? A demerged company inherits fresh standalone costs (its own head office, listing, corporate overhead) that a combined entity spread across a bigger base; the fear is margin slips as those costs land, the hope is that focus and aftermarket mix push margins the other way. So watch the quarterly print of revenue growth and underlying pre-tax margin together — near that ~13% clean-quarter level — with an eye on any drift in the aftermarket share. If the top line compounds and the margin holds as the standalone cost base settles, the premium is earned; if margin leaks while growth is ordinary, a 35× multiple on a two-quarter-old company has a lot to reprove.

On the scarcity premium: part of why SKFINDUS trades richly is simply that it is now one of only three clean, listed ways to own premium Indian industrial bearings. Scarcity is a real reason a stock can stay expensive — but it is a reason of the market, not of the business. Underwrite the operating metrics above; treat the scarcity re-rating as a tailwind you did not pay for, not a thesis you rely on.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality franchise, newly freed — asked to prove its standalone economics.

SKFINDUS starts life as one of the best-positioned industrial franchises on the Indian market: the SKF brand, a broad end-market spread, an aftermarket annuity, high returns and a committed parent building capacity. What it has not yet proven is the one thing a demerger always puts on trial — that the standalone company earns the margins the combined one did, once its own costs are fully loaded.

The bull case

  • Premium brand, real moat — a century-old global leader's technology and reliability reputation, hard to displace on critical equipment.
  • Aftermarket annuity — a large installed base generating recurring, higher-margin replacement demand across every end-market.
  • Industrial + infra tailwind — railways, heavy industry, renewables and import substitution give multi-year demand breadth.
  • High returns, self-funding — ~30% ROCE / ~27% ROE with no reliance on debt for working capital.
  • Scarce clean pure-play — one of only three listed premium industrial bearings names, with a parent committing fresh capacity (new Pune plant by 2028).

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~35× normalised (62× reported) earnings on a company with barely two clean quarters of history.
  • Capex cyclicality — OE demand swings with industrial investment; a slow capex year hits the new-machine half fast.
  • Import & peer pressure — cheap Chinese bearings squeeze the low end; Schaeffler and Timken compete hard at the top.
  • Parent leakage & control — royalties/technology fees flow to Sweden, AB SKF holds 52.58%, and minorities don't set the agenda.
  • Standalone execution & thin data — fresh corporate costs, an unproven margin on the new base, and a thin retail float that can amplify moves.
The bottom line

SKF India (Industrial) is a rare thing: a genuinely high-quality, high-return franchise selling a product so essential it hides in plain sight, handed to public investors as a clean, focused entity for the first time. The business quality is not really in doubt — the brand, the returns and the aftermarket annuity are all real. What is in doubt is the price and the newness: a market that has already re-rated the stock is asking a two-quarter-old company to demonstrate that a demerged, independently-costed industrial bearings business can compound revenue at its premium margin. The next few quarters answer it in one recurring figure — standalone revenue growth alongside the underlying pre-tax margin holding near that clean ~13% level as the new cost base settles in. Track that pair each quarter; it will tell you whether the split created a better company or merely a more expensive one.
