00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Avenue Supermarts runs DMart — a chain of large, no-frills value supermarkets selling groceries, packaged foods, household products, and a growing range of general merchandise and apparel, all at visibly lower prices than the shop next door. At 500 stores and ~20.6 million sq ft as of March 2026, it is not India's biggest retailer by store count, but it is comfortably the most profitable and most valuable — a ~₹2.8 lakh crore company built on a single, ruthlessly executed idea: be the cheapest, and make money on volume.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a low-margin, high-turns machine — and that's the point. DMart earns a thin ~14–15% gross margin and a ~7–8% EBITDA margin — deliberately giving most of its cost advantage back to shoppers as lower prices. It makes up for thin margins by selling enormous volume per store and turning its inventory ~13 times a year. FY26 consolidated revenue was ~₹68,900 Cr (+~16%) with consolidated net profit of ~₹2,970 Cr.
  • It owns its stores and grows slowly on purpose. Unlike rivals who lease, DMart mostly buys or builds its real estate and expands cluster-by-cluster, saturating one region before moving on. This is a genuine cost moat — but it caps how fast the chain can grow. FY26 saw a record 85 new stores, yet that is still measured expansion versus a quick-commerce player's pace.
  • The whole debate is now about disruption and price. DMart trades at a premium multiple (~80–95× earnings) that assumes years of compounding. The threat hanging over it is quick commerce — Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart delivering groceries in 10 minutes — which in FY26 grew to several times the size of DMart's own online arm. The bull/bear fight is whether 10-minute convenience can erode a value model built on the customer driving to a big store and buying in bulk.
In plain terms

Think of DMart as the Indian cousin of a Costco-meets-Walmart discount store, run by one of India's most respected stock-market investors. The trick is a loop: pay suppliers fast and in cash, own the building so there's no rent, run a spartan store with low overheads — all of which lets DMart buy cheap. It passes that saving on as permanently low prices, which pulls in crowds, which means it buys even more, which makes it cheaper still. That loop has made it the highest-quality business in Indian retail. The open question for the next decade is whether shoppers will keep driving to a big-box store to save money, or pay a little more to have it delivered to their door in ten minutes.

01

Value retail 101: how a discount supermarket earns

No retail background required — just the handful of numbers that drive everything.

A supermarket is, financially, a thin-margin, high-throughput business. It buys goods from suppliers, marks them up by a small amount, and sells huge quantities. Almost everything that matters can be captured in a few numbers — and DMart's edge is that it optimises every one of them.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and DMart's number)
Gross marginWhat's left after paying suppliers for the goods, before the store's own costs. Supermarkets run thin: DMart is around ~14–15% — low by design, because it deliberately sells cheap.
EBITDA marginOperating profit after running costs (staff, power, logistics) but before interest/tax/depreciation. DMart's ~7–8% is exceptional for grocery — most peers struggle near break-even.
Revenue / sq ftSales squeezed out of each square foot of store — the key productivity gauge. DMart did ~₹34,000/sq ft (FY25), far above most organised grocers.
Inventory turnsHow many times a year it sells through and replaces its stock. DMart turns inventory ~13× a year — fast turnover is how a thin-margin shop earns a healthy return.
SSSG / LFL"Same-store" (or "like-for-like") sales growth — how much existing stores grew, stripping out new openings. The cleanest read on underlying health.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (thin margin per item) × (huge number of items sold). A discounter can't earn much on any single packet of atta — so it must sell enormous volume and keep its own costs rock-bottom. The genius of the model is that low price and high volume reinforce each other: cheaper prices pull in more shoppers, more shoppers mean bigger buying volumes, bigger volumes mean better supplier deals, which fund still-lower prices. Get that loop spinning and a 14%-gross-margin business can out-earn a 40%-gross-margin one — because it sells so much more, so much faster, on so little capital. That loop is the subject of the next section.

Why margin is the wrong thing to admire here

A newcomer often assumes "higher margin = better business." In value retail it's the reverse. A high gross margin would mean DMart is over-charging — surrendering its only weapon. The right scorecard is return on the capital invested: thin margin × rapid turns × low overhead × no rent can produce a return on capital that a high-margin, slow-turning, debt-heavy rival cannot touch. DMart's job, every single day, is to be the cheapest place to buy a basket of staples — and to still make money doing it.

02

The EDLC→EDLP flywheel: the idea that explains DMart

Read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

DMart's entire strategy compresses into two abbreviations: EDLC ("everyday low cost") feeding EDLP ("everyday low price"). The order matters. DMart first does everything possible to lower its own cost of doing business; then it hands almost all of that saving back to shoppers as permanently low prices — no flash sales, no "70% off" theatrics, just a basket that is reliably cheaper week after week. Those low prices generate volume, and volume regenerates the cost advantage. It is a self-reinforcing loop — a flywheel.

…and the loop repeats: cheaper buying → cheaper prices → more volume → cheaper buying. The longer it spins, the wider the gap to any rival who must charge more to cover rent and thinner volumes.

The four levers of "everyday low cost"

  • Pay suppliers fast. DMart famously settles supplier bills quickly — often within days, far quicker than the weeks-to-months norm in Indian retail. In exchange it extracts better purchase prices and extra cash discounts. This is the single most distinctive lever: it converts a strong balance sheet into a procurement edge competitors with stretched payables can't match.
  • Own the store, kill the rent. Most DMart stores sit on land/property the company owns. No landlord means no rent line and no lease-renewal risk — a permanent structural cost saving baked into every store, year after year (more on this in §04).
  • Run lean. Spartan fit-outs, tight SKU ranges focused on fast-movers, low staff-to-sales ratios, efficient regional warehousing. DMart spends on price, not on marble floors or heavy advertising.
  • Concentrate the buying. Because expansion is clustered (§04), DMart buys deeply in a region rather than thinly everywhere — denser logistics, fuller trucks, and more leverage with suppliers per rupee of stock.
Why "everyday low price" beats "discount events"

Most retailers run promotions: high list prices punctuated by sales. EDLP is the opposite philosophy — structurally low prices, all the time. The advantage is twofold. For the shopper, it builds trust: you don't have to wait for a sale or hunt for the best deal, DMart is simply cheap. That trust converts into habit and loyalty without spending on advertising. For DMart, steady low prices mean predictable, smooth demand — which makes buying, stocking and staffing far more efficient than the boom-bust of promotion-led retail. EDLP is not a pricing tactic; it's the operating system the whole company is tuned around.

The flywheel is simple to describe and brutally hard to copy: a rival can match any one lever, but matching all four at once — and doing it for 20 years — is what built the moat.
03

The company and who controls it

From one store in Powai to India's most valuable retailer — and a once-in-a-generation founder.

DMart is the creation of Radhakishan Damani — one of India's most successful and famously low-profile stock-market investors, who turned his understanding of businesses into building one. He incorporated Avenue Supermarts in 2000 and opened the first DMart in Powai, Mumbai, in 2002, then spent two decades compounding it into a ~₹2.8 lakh crore company.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter group ~74.5%. The Damani family controls roughly three-quarters of the company — a very high, deliberately stable promoter stake. Radhakishan Damani himself holds ~23%, with the rest across family members (e.g. Gopikishan Damani) and family trusts. This concentration means the founder's value-retail philosophy is firmly in the driver's seat.
  • The founder's culture. Damani is famously frugal, long-term and publicity-shy — the antithesis of growth-at-any-cost. DMart reflects him: it grows only as fast as it can do so profitably, avoids debt-fuelled land-grabs, and treats every rupee of cost as the shopper's money. That discipline is a real asset — and, to bears, a constraint in an era of blitzscaling rivals.
  • The CEO transition. Neville Noronha's 20-year tenure was central to execution; his exit in early 2026 and the arrival of Anshul Asawa (three decades at Unilever) is the most-watched management change in the company's history — continuity of the model versus a fresh hand, just as the competitive landscape shifts.
  • Balance sheet. Historically near-debt-free and cash-generative; the owned-real-estate strategy ties up capital in property but funds expansion largely from internal cash, keeping leverage modest even as the store count climbs.
04

Own the real estate, grow in clusters

The most consequential strategic choice DMart ever made — a moat that is also a speed limit.

Two deliberate decisions shape DMart's physical growth: it owns most of its stores rather than leasing them, and it expands cluster-by-cluster — saturating one region before opening up the next. Both choices make DMart structurally cheaper and harder to copy. Both also make it grow more slowly than a leasing, scatter-everywhere rival. Understanding this trade-off is essential to the whole story.

Why owning beats leasing

  • No rent, forever. Rent is one of a retailer's biggest fixed costs. By owning, DMart removes that line entirely — a permanent margin advantage on every store, compounding for decades. A leasing rival must charge more just to cover the landlord.
  • No renewal risk, no rent inflation. Leased retailers face rising renewals and the threat of losing a prime spot to a higher bidder. DMart's owned stores are immune — its best locations are locked in permanently.
  • A growing property asset. The land and buildings appreciate over time, giving DMart a hard-asset balance sheet behind the retail business — collateral and optionality a lease-light rival lacks.

Why clustering beats spraying stores everywhere

  • Logistics density. Many stores close together means short supply runs, fuller trucks and a single regional warehouse serving many outlets — far cheaper per store than scattered locations.
  • Brand saturation. Blanketing a region builds top-of-mind awareness and habit without heavy advertising; DMart becomes the place to buy staples in that area.
  • Buying leverage. Concentrated volume in a region strengthens negotiating power with regional suppliers and distributors — feeding the EDLC lever from §02.
The moat-versus-speed trade-off, plainly

Owning real estate and clustering give DMart a cost edge no leasing chain can erase — that's the moat. But buying or building a store takes far more time and capital than signing a lease: you must find the right plot, acquire it, navigate approvals, and construct. So DMart can only add stores as fast as it can deploy capital into property — which is why even a record year (85 stores in FY26) looks measured next to a quick-commerce app launching hundreds of dark stores in months. The same discipline that makes each store wildly profitable also caps the growth rate. Bulls call it patient, high-quality compounding; bears call it too slow for a market being reshaped by speed. Both are describing the same trade-off.

05

Unit economics: thin margins, fast turns, high returns

How a 14%-gross-margin business becomes the best-returning retailer in India.

The numbers that look weak in isolation — a ~14–15% gross margin, a ~7–8% operating margin — are exactly what make DMart strong. The magic is in velocity and capital efficiency: sell a vast amount through each store, turn the stock over ~13 times a year, carry little working capital, and earn a high return on the money invested. The "low margin, high turns" magic, with numbers

Imagine ₹100 of stock. DMart marks it up modestly and sells it — earning, say, a few rupees of net profit. Thin. But it then refills and resells that shelf ~13 times in a year. So that same ₹100 of capital generates ~13 cycles of profit annually. A high-margin boutique that turns its stock only 3–4× a year may earn more per sale, but DMart earns more per rupee of capital per year — and that, not headline margin, is what builds shareholder value. Add owned real estate (no rent leaking out) and quick supplier payment (cheaper buying), and the return on capital pulls clear of the field.

Same-store sales growth (SSSG) — the cleanest health check

New stores always boost total revenue, so the number analysts watch most closely is same-store (like-for-like) growth — how fast existing stores grow. It tells you whether DMart is genuinely getting more out of its established base or merely buying growth by opening doors. In FY26 the signal was mixed-to-improving: mid-year LFL growth had moderated to ~5.5–7% (a worry, especially in metros where quick commerce bites), before Q4 LFL re-accelerated to roughly double digits (~10–11% for stores 24+ months old). That swing — soft middle, strong finish — is at the heart of the FY26 debate: is the slowdown structural (quick commerce eating share) or just a temporary, deflation-and-mix soft patch?

What to watch: SSSG in metro stores specifically (where quick-commerce overlap is highest), gross-margin stability, and revenue per sq ft. If LFL growth holds double digits while margin is steady, the flywheel is intact. Persistent mid-single-digit metro LFL would be the first hard evidence that convenience is denting the value proposition.

06

The product mix and the GM&A margin lever

What DMart sells — and why the apparel-and-merchandise slice matters more than its size suggests.

DMart's basket splits into three families: Foods (staples, packaged food, dairy), Non-Foods / FMCG (soaps, detergents, personal & home care), and General Merchandise & Apparel — "GM&A" (clothing, footwear, utensils, plastics, luggage, toys, small appliances). Foods dominate the revenue, but GM&A is the margin lever — the higher-profit category that disproportionately drives DMart's profitability.

Why GM&A is the lever that matters

Groceries are the traffic-builder: cheap staples bring shoppers in every week. But the margin on a sack of rice is wafer-thin. General merchandise and apparel — a t-shirt, a set of steel utensils, a school bag — carry much higher margins. So the mechanism is: use cheap foods to pull the crowd, then convert some of those visits into higher-margin GM&A purchases. Because GM&A is so much more profitable per rupee, even a couple of percentage points of mix shift toward it can lift DMart's blended margin noticeably. That is why management and analysts obsess over the GM&A share — it is the single biggest internal margin dial DMart has.

The catch — and where quick commerce intersects

The GM&A lever has a vulnerability. Apparel and general merchandise are discretionary — when household budgets are squeezed (or inflation runs hot), shoppers cut back on a new shirt long before they cut back on rice. So GM&A volumes are more cyclical, and in soft consumption patches the high-margin mix can stall, dragging blended margin. Worse, the discretionary, branded-FMCG end of the basket is precisely where quick-commerce apps compete hardest — a shopper grabbing an impulse non-food item on a 10-minute app is a sale DMart's store didn't make. Protecting and growing the GM&A mix while defending the FMCG basket is therefore the crux of DMart's margin story for the next few years.

The private-label angle: DMart also runs in-house brands (e.g. across staples, home care and apparel) that typically carry higher margins than third-party brands and deepen the EDLP price gap. Growing private label is a quiet, parallel margin lever alongside the GM&A mix shift.

07

DMart Ready: the cautious bet on online

A deliberately conservative e-commerce arm — and the front line against quick commerce.

DMart's online business, DMart Ready (run through subsidiary Avenue E-Commerce Ltd), is everything the parent is: cautious, store-anchored, and unwilling to torch cash chasing growth. Customers order online and collect from a pickup point or get next-day / slot-based home delivery — fulfilled largely from DMart's own stores and small warehouses rather than a sprawling network of 10-minute dark stores. It is a hedge and a learning vehicle, not a blitzscaling land-grab.

The FY26 story for DMart Ready was one of disciplined retrenchment. Rather than spreading thin to look big, it exited five smaller cities (Amritsar, Belagavi, Bhilai, Chandigarh, Ghaziabad) and concentrated on large metros where order density makes the unit economics viable. The parent continued to fund it with periodic capital infusions, but on a tight leash — the message is "grow online where it can pay, don't subsidise growth for its own sake."

Why DMart won't blitzscale online

The quick-commerce playbook — hundreds of dark stores, deep delivery subsidies, customer-acquisition spending — burns enormous cash for years. That is fundamentally against DMart's DNA: a company built on cost discipline and profitability recoils at funding losses to buy market share. So DMart Ready stays modest and store-leveraged, accepting slower online growth in exchange for not blowing up the parent's economics. Bulls see prudence (why subsidise a customer who'd otherwise shop your profitable store?); bears see a company structurally unwilling to fight on the battlefield where the next decade's grocery share is being decided — the very tension the next section tackles head-on.

08

The quick-commerce question: disruption, or noise?

The single biggest debate hanging over the stock — laid out fairly from both sides.

The defining strategic question for DMart is whether quick commerce — Blinkit, Zepto and Swiggy Instamart delivering groceries in 10–30 minutes from neighbourhood "dark stores" — structurally erodes a value model built on the shopper travelling to a big store and buying in bulk. This is not a small skirmish: in FY26 the quick-commerce players grew explosively and, on revenue, are now multiples the size of DMart Ready, while DMart's own metro same-store growth wobbled mid-year. It is the reason a stellar business can still trade nervously.

The bear thesis — convenience beats price

  • Speed is the new value. For top-up shopping — the daily milk, bread, snacks, an impulse item — many urban customers now value 10-minute delivery over a few rupees saved. That erodes the frequency of DMart trips, even if the big monthly stock-up survives.
  • The overlap hits the profitable basket. Quick commerce competes hardest in FMCG and impulse general-merchandise — exactly the higher-margin, discretionary slice (§06) that powers DMart's profitability, not the wafer-thin staples.
  • Metros are softening first. DMart's metro same-store growth came under visible pressure in FY26 — the markets with the densest quick-commerce coverage. If that's structural, the long-term growth rate resets lower.
  • DMart won't fight on speed. Its cost-discipline DNA (§07) makes it unwilling to burn cash on a dark-store war — so it may cede the convenience battlefield by choice.

The bull rebuttal — the value moat holds

  • Quick commerce isn't cheap — it's convenient. Instant delivery is funded by delivery fees, smaller pack sizes and (for now) investor subsidies; on a true price-per-unit basis for a large basket, DMart remains structurally cheaper. Value-seeking, price-sensitive India is a vast, durable customer base.
  • Different missions. The big monthly stock-up (rice, oil, detergent, a bag of clothes) is a different job from a 10-minute top-up. DMart owns the high-volume, planned, value basket; quick commerce owns the small, urgent one. Both can grow.
  • Subsidies aren't forever. The quick-commerce model still burns cash; as investors demand profitability, delivery becomes pricier and the price gap to DMart widens again — re-validating the value proposition.
  • Q4 FY26 re-acceleration. Same-store growth bouncing back to ~double digits late in the year is the bulls' evidence that the mid-year softness was a deflation/mix patch, not the start of structural decline.
How to think about it

The honest answer is that both can be partly right. Quick commerce almost certainly captures a meaningful slice of high-frequency top-up grocery in dense metros — that share is probably gone. But it is far less obvious that it disrupts the large, planned, value-driven basket in price-sensitive India, especially once delivery subsidies normalise. DMart's bet is that being much cheaper on a big basket remains a durable reason to visit. The verdict will show up over several years in two numbers: metro same-store growth (is the value trip still worth taking?) and gross margin (is DMart having to cut price to defend the basket?). Until those clearly break, the disruption is a real risk — not yet a proven fact.

The bottom line of the debate: DMart's quality is not in question — its terminal growth rate is. The premium multiple (§09) assumes the value moat survives the convenience wave largely intact. If quick commerce structurally caps DMart's metro growth, the stock is expensive; if value endures, today's nervousness is an opportunity. This single question explains most of the volatility in the shares.

How to think about the ~80–95× multiple

At a market cap around ₹2.8 lakh crore on ~₹2,970 Cr of FY26 consolidated profit, DMart trades at roughly 80–95× trailing earnings — a premium even among quality Indian consumer names, and a multiple of global retail peers. That price only makes sense if you believe DMart compounds revenue and profit at a high rate for many years: more stores, the GM&A mix lever, owned-real-estate cost advantage and best-in-class returns, all while the value moat survives quick commerce. Bulls argue a debt-light, founder-disciplined, structurally-advantaged category leader with a decades-long runway deserves a scarcity premium. Bears argue the multiple bakes in near-perfect execution and leaves no room for a structural growth reset — so if metro growth keeps fading, even a great business can de-rate hard. Both can be right at different points in the cycle, which is why same-store growth and margin are the numbers to track each quarter.

10

The growth runway

A long road of store additions, a margin-mix lever, and an under-penetrated market.

DMart's growth comes from three stacked engines — and the runway is long because organised value retail is still a small slice of a huge, fast-formalising Indian grocery market.

Three growth engines, stacked

  • New stores (capacity). FY26's record +85 takes the chain to 500; with most of India still under-served by large-format value retail, the white space for many hundreds more owned stores — especially deepening existing clusters and methodically entering new ones — is the primary lever.
  • Same-store growth (density). Existing stores keep growing as clusters mature and baskets deepen — growth with little extra capital. The key to watch is whether metro LFL recovers durably.
  • Margin mix (GM&A + private label). Lifting the higher-margin general-merchandise-and-apparel share, plus growing in-house brands, raises profitability without needing a single new store (§06).

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely elite business — priced for the value moat to survive the convenience wave.

DMart is one of the highest-quality businesses in Indian retail: a disciplined, founder-driven, cash-generative value leader with a real cost moat and a long store runway. The investment debate is almost entirely about two things — valuation and disruption, not whether the business is good.

The bull case

  • Structural cost moat — EDLC→EDLP flywheel, owned stores (no rent), fast supplier payment and clustering combine into a price edge rivals can't easily match.
  • Best-in-class economics — ~8% EBITDA margin and ~17% ROCE in a notoriously thin business; high returns on a modest, mostly self-funded balance sheet.
  • Long runway — organised value retail is under-penetrated; hundreds of potential owned stores ahead, plus the GM&A and private-label margin levers.
  • Founder discipline — a frugal, long-term promoter group (~74.5%) that grows only where it's profitable and avoids value-destroying land-grabs.
  • Q4 FY26 re-acceleration — same-store growth bouncing to ~double digits suggests the value trip still pulls crowds.

The bear case

  • Quick-commerce disruption — Blinkit/Zepto/Instamart erode high-frequency, high-margin metro baskets; DMart's terminal growth rate may reset lower.
  • Very rich valuation — ~80–95× earnings leaves no room for error; any structural growth slowdown invites a sharp de-rating.
  • Growth-speed limit — the owned-store model is slow and capital-heavy versus dark-store-led rivals; FY26 PAT grew only ~10%.
  • Metro softness — like-for-like growth visibly wobbled mid-FY26 in the most competitive markets.
  • Execution & transition risk — a once-in-two-decades CEO change (Noronha → Asawa) and an online arm still losing money amid intensifying competition.
The bottom line

DMart is a structurally advantaged, elite-quality value retailer with a genuine moat, a long runway and a balance sheet and culture built to compound patiently. The business risk is low; the valuation and disruption risks are real and intertwined. The story over the next few years will be written in two numbers each quarter — metro same-store growth (is the value trip still worth taking against 10-minute delivery?) and gross margin (is DMart having to sacrifice price to defend the basket?). Hold those, and a near-100× multiple eventually grows into itself; lose them, and a magnificent business is still a vulnerable stock.
