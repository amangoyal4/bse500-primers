00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

CG Power and Industrial Solutions makes the heavy electrical equipment that moves and uses electricity — the giant transformers and switchgear that step voltage up and down across the grid, and the motors, drives and alternators that turn power into work inside factories, railways and infrastructure. It is a nearly 90-year-old brand (the old "Crompton Greaves"), but its story is really about the last five years: it was a fraud-hit, near-bankrupt company that the Murugappa Group's Tube Investments rescued in 2020 and turned into one of Indian capital goods' best performers. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) it earned ₹12,418 crore of revenue and ~₹1,232 crore of pre-exceptional net profit, it sits on a record ₹17,107 crore order book, it is essentially debt-free — and it is making an audacious, capital-heavy bet on semiconductor packaging.

Four facts frame everything about this company:

  • It is a turnaround, not a start-up. The distressed shell Murugappa bought in 2020 already had real factories, real products and real customers — it had just been looted by its previous promoter. The genius of the story is that the underlying business was sound; the rescue was about restoring governance, clearing debt and running it well. That arc — from ₹300 crore of market value to ~₹1.5 lakh crore — is the backbone of the investment case.
  • Its profit runs on two core engines. Power Systems (transformers, switchgear, reactors — riding the grid build-out) and Industrial Systems (LT & HT motors, drives, alternators, plus a railways business). Power Systems is the fast, high-visibility growth engine; Industrial Systems is the steady cash cow.
  • One structural force is supercharging it: the grid. India is spending lakhs of crores rewiring its power grid to absorb 500 GW of renewables and surging demand from data centres, EVs and electrification. That is a multi-year demand wave for exactly the transformers and switchgear CG makes — visible in an order book that grew 61% in a single year.
  • It is priced for perfection — and then some. The stock trades near 115× earnings at a ~₹1.5 lakh crore market cap — extreme for a capital-goods company. Bulls say a debt-free, high-return, structurally-growing franchise plus a semiconductor optionality deserves it; bears say a huge chunk of that price is a promise, not a profit.
In plain terms

Picture the electricity system as a vast machine. Power is made in one place (a solar park, a coal plant) and used somewhere far away (a factory, a city, a data centre), and getting it there needs transformers to change the voltage and switchgear to route and protect it — the "plumbing" of the grid. Then, wherever electricity is actually used to do work — pumps, fans, conveyor belts, trains — you need motors. CG Power makes both the plumbing and the motors. That's a good, boring, essential business. What makes CG interesting is three layers stacked on top: (1) it was left for dead after a massive fraud and was brought back to life by a famously clean-governance owner, so it's a rare "quality restored" story; (2) India is in the middle of a once-in-a-generation grid rebuild, so demand for its core products is booming; and (3) it is spending thousands of crores to enter semiconductor manufacturing — a completely new, high-stakes business the market is betting could be a future gold mine. The share price today is paying for all three of those at once.

How an electrical-equipment maker actually earns

No engineering degree required — just the handful of forces that drive the profit.

At its core, CG Power is a project-and-product manufacturer: it wins orders to build electrical equipment, manufactures it in its plants, delivers it, and books revenue as it goes. Understanding it means understanding three things — what it sells, how orders convert to profit, and why the order book is the number that matters most.

The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (orders won × execution pace × margin), run across two segments with different speeds. The trick to reading CG is that revenue this year is largely decided by orders won in prior years — a company with a ₹17,000 crore backlog on ~₹12,400 crore of sales has, in effect, already sold more than a year of future revenue. So the two things to watch are order intake (is the future pipeline still filling?) and margins (is it executing profitably, or has it bid too cheaply for growth?). In FY26 both moved the right way: order intake jumped 33% and standalone EBITDA margin expanded ~130 bps to 16.7% — growth and profitability at once, which is exactly what the market pays a premium for.

The three levers of the business

  • Order intake — how much new work it wins. Driven by the grid capex cycle, export demand, and railway/industrial spending. This is the leading indicator; it fills the backlog that becomes tomorrow's revenue.
  • Execution & mix — how fast it converts backlog to sales, and toward which products. A shift to higher-voltage transformers or export orders lifts realisation and margin; competitive railway pricing or commodity spikes squeeze it.
  • Operating leverage — because plants and engineering are largely fixed costs, once volumes rise the extra revenue drops disproportionately to profit. This is exactly what drove FY26's margin expansion as Power Systems scaled.
02

The Murugappa turnaround — from fraud to franchise

The single most important thing to understand: how a looted, near-bankrupt company became a debt-free compounder — and why that arc matters for trust today.

You cannot understand CG Power without understanding what happened to it. This is a company that was defrauded to the brink of collapse, then rescued and rebuilt by one of India's most conservatively-governed business houses. The turnaround isn't background colour — it is the investment thesis, and it explains why the market grants CG a "trust premium" it would never give a company with this recent a scandal under different owners.

The fall: a great company, looted

CG Power traces to 1937 and the storied Crompton Greaves electrical franchise. By the 2010s it sat inside Gautam Thapar's Avantha Group, which pursued an aggressive, debt-funded global acquisition spree that stretched the balance sheet to breaking. Then, in 2019, the company's own board dropped a bombshell: it disclosed that large sums — reported figures run into thousands of crores — had been diverted through unauthorised transactions to promoter-linked entities. Money that should have funded the business was parked outside it; liabilities had been understated; the company had, in effect, been used as a piggy bank. Auditors flagged it, SEBI, the Serious Fraud Investigation Office and the CBI moved in, and by 2020–21 Gautam Thapar was arrested under money-laundering law.

The result was near-death. CG Power's market value collapsed from roughly ₹10,000 crore (2015) to about ₹300 crore (early 2020). It owed banks over ₹2,000 crore, had essentially no working capital, and carried the toxic "fraud account" tag that freezes a company out of the banking system. It was, on paper, a wreck.

The rescue: Murugappa's bet

Here is where it turns. In mid-2020, Tube Investments of India — the flagship engineering company of the Murugappa Group, a ~120-year-old Chennai house famous for clean, conservative governance — stepped in and invested about ₹700 crore for a ~56% controlling stake. Their thesis was elegantly simple: strip away the fraud, and the underlying business was fundamentally sound — real factories, real products (transformers, motors), real blue-chip customers, real engineering talent. The company hadn't failed commercially; it had been robbed. Fix the governance and the debt, run it competently, and a genuinely good business would re-emerge.

Why the turnaround matters for trust today

Ordinarily, a company caught in a multi-thousand-crore fraud carries a permanent governance discount — investors simply don't trust the numbers. CG is the rare exception, and the reason is the identity of the owner. The Murugappa Group's whole reputation is built on old-fashioned, boring, straight-dealing governance; when they took control, cleaned the books, cleared the debt and put their own people in charge, the market didn't just re-rate the earnings — it re-rated the trust. In practice that means: audited accounts you can believe, no promoter pledging games, disciplined capital allocation, and a controlling owner whose interests are aligned with minority holders. The turnaround, in other words, didn't just save the company financially — it replaced the thing that was actually broken, which was governance. That restored trust is a real, if intangible, part of why CG earns a premium multiple. It is also why any hint of the old problems recurring would be punished far harder here than elsewhere.

Murugappa didn't buy a business that had failed. It bought a good business that had been robbed — and the entire value creation since is what happens when you give a sound company honest management.

04

Power Systems and the grid supercycle

Why India's rewiring of its power grid is a multi-year demand wave — and CG's fastest, most visible growth engine.

The single biggest force on CG's growth is India's transmission & grid build-out. To connect hundreds of gigawatts of new solar and wind (often generated far from cities), and to feed booming demand from data centres, EVs, air-conditioning and electrification, India has to massively expand and upgrade its power grid — and that means buying enormous quantities of exactly what CG's Power Systems division makes: high-voltage transformers, switchgear and reactors.

Why the grid build-out becomes a transformer order

Electricity loses energy as it travels down a wire, and the loss is smaller at higher voltage — so power is pushed onto the grid at very high voltages (up to 765,000 volts) for the long journey and stepped back down near where it's used. Every one of those voltage changes needs a transformer; every junction, connection and fault-protection point needs switchgear; long lines need reactors to manage the electrical "pressure." So when India decides to build thousands of kilometres of new high-voltage lines to carry clean power from a Rajasthan solar park to a Mumbai data centre, that decision converts, very directly, into years of orders for CG's core products. It's a picks-and-shovels play: CG doesn't sell the electricity or build the solar farm — it sells the essential grid hardware that the whole build-out depends on, and it gets paid regardless of which renewable developer wins.

What's driving the demand

  • The renewable connection wave. India is targeting 500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030; connecting it requires a vast new transmission network. Utilities like PowerGrid are tendering aggressively — CG won a ₹641 crore order for 765 kV transformers from PowerGrid in FY26, its largest domestic win.
  • The export supply-shortage windfall. Western markets (US, Europe) face a severe shortage of transformer capacity as they electrify and rebuild aging grids — and lead times have stretched to years. CG is uniquely positioned to fill that gap, and landed a landmark ₹900 crore export order to supply transformers for a US hyperscale data-centre project — its largest single order ever. Management is expanding transformer capacity partly to serve this export opportunity.
  • Data centres & electrification at home. AI-driven data centres, EV charging, metro rail and factory electrification all add grid load and, therefore, demand for CG's equipment on the domestic side too.
  • Capacity is being built to meet it. CG is expanding power-transformer capacity (from ~45,000 MVA toward ~85,000 MVA by around FY28) and approved a ₹748 crore greenfield switchgear plant — signalling management's confidence that this demand is durable, not a spike.
The other side: a grid supercycle is real but it is still a capex cycle — order intake can be lumpy, tenders can slip, and margins can be squeezed if CG bids aggressively to win share. The export opportunity is large but comes with long qualification timelines (utility approvals take 1–3 years), currency risk and geopolitical/tariff exposure. The direction is clearly up; the pace won't be a straight line.
05

Industrial Systems & railways — the steady cash cow

Motors, drives and alternators that turn power into work — plus a railway modernisation angle.

If Power Systems is the exciting growth engine, Industrial Systems is the dependable base — the larger segment by revenue (~₹6,197 crore in FY26) and the one that keeps the cash flowing through the cycle. It makes the machines that use electricity to do physical work: motors, drives, alternators and industrial electronics, sold in volume to industry, plus a growing railways business.

What a motor business actually is

An electric motor converts electricity into rotational force — it's what spins a factory's pumps, fans, compressors, conveyor belts and machine tools; a train's wheels; a building's HVAC. Almost every piece of industrial equipment has a motor inside it, so a motor maker sells into virtually the entire economy. CG is a leading brand here, with a mix skewed roughly 80:20 toward LT (low-tension) motors over HT (high-tension) motors — LT being the smaller, high-volume workhorses; HT being the large, high-power units for heavy industry. Because motors are essential, standardised and constantly being bought and replaced, this is a steadier, more predictable business than lumpy grid projects — a reliable cash cow that funds the more exciting bets elsewhere. Its swing factors are industrial capex, commodity (copper, steel) prices, and product mix.

The pieces of Industrial Systems

  • Motors — LT & HT. The core: low- and high-tension motors for industry. In FY26 motors grew on both volume and price (a healthy sign of demand and pricing power), though commodity-cost pressure weighed on margins.
  • Drives & industrial electronics. The electronic controls and variable-speed drives that govern motors — CG is developing new drive products to move up the value chain and capture more of the automation trend.
  • Alternators & generators. Machines that convert mechanical energy into electricity — the complement to the motor line.
  • Railways. A modernisation play: propulsion systems, transportation equipment, and signalling. CG deepened this with a 55% acquisition of GG Tronics India (rail signalling) and is targeting higher-margin service revenue and new technologies. Railways faced competitive pricing pressure in FY26 — a reminder that not every vertical is a smooth ride.

06

The order book — the forward-visibility metric

For a project-and-equipment company, this one number tells you more about the future than the P&L does.

The most important forward-looking number for CG isn't its revenue or profit — it's its order book: the value of work already won but not yet delivered. It grew 61% in FY26 to a record ₹17,107 crore (consolidated), which is roughly 1.4× a full year's revenue. That's a large chunk of tomorrow's sales effectively pre-booked — unusually strong visibility for a capital-goods company.

Why the order book is the number that matters

When CG wins a ₹641 crore PowerGrid order, it doesn't book that revenue immediately — it builds the transformers over many months and recognises the sales as it delivers. So the order book is a reservoir of future revenue: a big, growing backlog means the next year or two of sales is already substantially locked in, which de-risks the growth story. Two things make a backlog high-quality: it should be growing faster than revenue (so the reservoir is filling faster than it's draining — CG's book grew 61% vs revenue's 25%, so yes), and it should be profitably priced (won at good margins, not bought with cheap bids). CG's book scores well on both. The catch: a backlog is only as good as execution — orders can be delayed, cancelled or re-priced, and a backlog that grows because the company is bidding cheaply to win share is a warning, not a win. So far, margins rising alongside the book suggests genuine strength.

07

The semiconductor bet — bold, capital-heavy optionality

A transformer-and-motor company betting thousands of crores on chip packaging — the single biggest reason for the sky-high multiple, and the biggest execution risk.

The most audacious — and most debated — part of CG Power is a business that has almost nothing to do with transformers or motors: semiconductors. Through its subsidiary CG Semi, the company is building one of India's first end-to-end OSAT (Outsourced Semiconductor Assembly and Test) plants at Sanand, Gujarat, with a total planned investment of ~₹7,600 crore, under the India Semiconductor Mission (ISM). It's a bold diversification the market is partly pricing as a future gold mine — and one that could go badly if execution stumbles.

What OSAT / ATMP actually means — in plain English

Making a computer chip has two big stages. First, a "fab" (fabrication plant — think TSMC) etches the microscopic circuits onto a silicon wafer; this is the glamorous, ultra-expensive front end. But a raw wafer isn't a usable chip yet. The back end takes that wafer, cuts it into individual chips, assembles them into protective packages, adds the wiring/connectors, marks them, and tests every one to make sure it works — then ships finished chips to customers. That back-end work is called ATMP (Assembly, Testing, Marking & Packaging), and when a company does it for others on contract, it's called OSAT (Outsourced Semiconductor Assembly & Test). Think of it this way: the fab bakes the wafer; the OSAT is the finishing-and-packing factory that turns that wafer into thousands of ready-to-sell chips. It's less capital-intensive than a fab and a sensible first step for a country trying to build a chip industry — which is exactly why India's semiconductor mission is backing OSAT plants like CG's. CG will assemble and test chips (for automotive, consumer electronics, microcontrollers) for global chip firms.

The structure & the economics

  • The JV. CG Semi is majority-owned by CG Power (~92.3%), with Japan's Renesas Electronics (~6.8%) and Thailand's Stars Microelectronics (~0.9%) as technology-and-know-how partners. Renesas brings chip demand and technical credibility; Stars brings OSAT operating experience.
  • Government support de-risks the capital. Under the India Semiconductor Mission, the project draws substantial subsidies — CG's FY26 accounts already show ~₹668 crore of government grant receivables for the OSAT facility. Fiscal support is what makes the economics of a first-mover plant viable.
  • Two facilities, ramping. The G1 pilot line (capacity ~0.5 million units/day) was inaugurated in August 2025 for customer qualification; the far larger G2 plant (~14.5 million units/day) is under construction for completion around end-2026. Commercial production is targeted within CY2026.
  • Plus a design arm. Separately, CG acquired Renesas' RF (radio-frequency) chip-design business for ~$36 million (~₹284 crore) — a fabless design capability that contributed ~$65 million of revenue in FY26. This is the bulk of the ~₹503 crore "semiconductor" segment revenue today; the OSAT plant's revenue is still ahead.
The execution risk is real. The OSAT business is currently loss-making and is a drag on consolidated margins (it's why consolidated margins sit below standalone). Analysts expect losses to narrow from ~FY28 once G2 scales. Semiconductors are a brutally competitive, capital-intensive, cyclical global industry where CG has no track record — landing enough qualified customers at profitable prices is unproven. This is genuine optionality: it could become a major new profit engine, or it could be an expensive distraction. Today it is a bet, not a business.
Why the market loves it anyway

Here's the subtlety: the semiconductor bet is losing money right now, yet it is arguably the biggest single reason CG trades at ~115× earnings. Why? Because the market isn't valuing the semiconductor arm on today's (negative) profit — it's pricing in a probability-weighted future. India desperately wants a domestic chip industry, is subsidising it heavily, and there are very few listed ways to own that theme. If CG's OSAT plant succeeds, it could add a large, high-value profit stream in a scarce, strategically-prized industry — and investors are paying today for that chance. That's what "optionality" means: you're not paying for what it earns now, you're paying for the fat right-tail if the bet pays off. The danger is that optionality is only worth something if it might actually happen — and a heavy chip-plant ramp in a cyclical industry is exactly the kind of promise that can slip. When roughly this much of your valuation rests on a business that doesn't yet make money, the margin for disappointment is thin.

The net-cash balance sheet & Murugappa capital discipline

The fortress that lets CG fund a grid-capacity build-out and a semiconductor plant at the same time — without borrowing.

One quiet reason CG can chase two capital-hungry growth stories at once — a transformer-capacity build-out and a semiconductor plant — is that it does so from a position of financial strength. It is essentially debt-free (borrowings ~₹118 crore against ~₹7,655 crore of reserves), it generates strong cash and returns on capital, and in FY26 it topped up its war chest with a ₹3,000 crore QIP (raising equity from institutional investors). This is capital discipline in the Murugappa mould — the same conservative-finance instinct that rescued the company now funds its growth.

Why a fortress balance sheet is a strategic weapon

Being debt-free isn't just prudent — it's an offensive advantage. It means CG can commit to a ₹748 crore switchgear plant, a big transformer-capacity expansion, and a ₹7,600 crore semiconductor project without the fear of a debt trap if any one bet is slow to pay off. It can invest through a downturn when leveraged rivals are forced to retrench. And it means the earnings that do come through aren't being siphoned to interest payments. The ₹3,000 crore QIP reinforces this: rather than borrow to fund the semiconductor and capacity plans, CG raised equity — diluting shareholders slightly but keeping the balance sheet pristine. For a company that was nearly killed by debt and fraud a few years ago, this fortress-finance posture is both a genuine competitive edge and a deliberate signal: never again. The trade-off is that all this reinvestment (and the equity raise) means dividends are modest — CG is a compounder ploughing cash back into growth, not an income stock.

  • High returns on capital. ROCE around 27% (and a 3-year ROE north of 30%) marks CG as a genuinely high-quality operator — it earns strong returns on the money it invests, which is what justifies reinvesting rather than paying it all out.
  • Reinvestment over payout. The FY26 dividend was a modest ₹1.30/share interim — CG is deliberately retaining cash to fund grid-capacity expansion and the semiconductor plant. This is a growth-and-compounding story, not a yield play.
  • The QIP dilution nuance. Raising ₹3,000 crore of new equity adds shares, so per-share metrics carry more shares to divide across — worth remembering when comparing EPS across years. It's the price of keeping the balance sheet debt-free while investing heavily.

How to think about the ~115× multiple

At roughly ₹1.5 lakh crore market cap on ~₹1,232 crore of FY26 profit, CG trades near 115× trailing earnings — a multiple that would be extreme for almost any company, let alone a capital-goods maker. That price is paying for four things at once: (1) the visible, multi-year grid-and-export order book; (2) genuine quality — net cash, ~27% ROCE, a trusted Murugappa-governed franchise; (3) the semiconductor optionality, where the market pays today for a chance the OSAT bet becomes a major future profit engine; and (4) scarcity and momentum — few clean listed ways to own India's grid + chip themes, plus a stock up ~48% in 2026. Bulls argue that a business compounding earnings ~30%+ (analysts model Power Systems at ~40% revenue CAGR) with a real chip option can grow into the multiple. Bears counter that ~115× bakes in years of flawless execution and a semiconductor success that hasn't happened yet — leaving no cushion. If order intake slows, margins wobble, or the OSAT ramp disappoints, a stock priced this richly can de-rate sharply. Both are right at different points; which is why every quarter's order intake and margin print moves the stock so much.

The industry and the rivals

A market where reliability, track record and capacity — not just price — decide the winner.

CG competes in two very different arenas. In power equipment (transformers, switchgear) it's a domestic heavyweight up against global majors and strong Indian rivals, in a market where a proven track record matters as much as price. In motors & industrial systems it's a leading brand in a more commoditised, competitive field. And in semiconductors it's a brand-new entrant with no incumbency at all.

Where CG genuinely wins — and where it's the underdog

In its core power-and-industrial business, CG's edge is a potent combination: a heritage brand that utilities trust, a net-cash balance sheet that lets it expand capacity aggressively into the grid boom, Murugappa's clean governance (which matters when utilities and Western buyers vet suppliers), and real export momentum at a moment when Western transformer capacity is scarce. That's a strong hand in a market where buyers fear failure more than they chase the lowest price. But be clear-eyed about the semiconductor arena: there, CG is a brand-new entrant against global giants with decades of scale and customer relationships. Its advantages there are Indian government subsidy, the Renesas partnership, and first-mover status at home — not incumbency. So the honest picture is a strong, entrenched competitor in its core making a speculative, subsidised bet in a field it doesn't yet know. The valuation, awkwardly, prices in success at both.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely excellent turnaround — and a price that assumes the next chapter is just as good.

CG Power is one of the most impressive stories in Indian capital goods: a fraud-hit near-corpse rebuilt by a trusted owner into a debt-free, high-return, fast-growing franchise, riding a real grid supercycle with a record order book — and layered with a bold semiconductor option. The business quality and the tailwinds are genuine. The investment debate is almost entirely about the extraordinary price and whether the newest bets deliver.

The bull case

  • Grid supercycle — a multi-year transmission & renewable build-out drives Power Systems; order book +61%, book >1× revenue, ~40% Power Systems growth modelled.
  • Export windfall — Western transformer shortages let CG win landmark orders (the ₹900 Cr US data-centre deal); capacity being expanded to serve it.
  • Trusted franchise — Murugappa governance, net cash, ~27% ROCE; the rare turnaround where trust was fully restored.
  • Semiconductor optionality — a subsidised, Renesas-backed OSAT plant that could become a large new profit engine in a scarce, prized industry.
  • Fortress finance — debt-free, ₹3,000 Cr QIP war chest; can fund grid capacity and the chip bet without leverage.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — ~115× earnings, ~19× book; prices in years of flawless execution and a semiconductor success that hasn't happened.
  • Semiconductor execution risk — the OSAT arm is loss-making, unproven, cyclical and capital-heavy; a slip on the ramp hits a stock priced for its success.
  • Capex-cycle dependence — order intake is lumpy; a slowdown in grid tenders or export approvals (1–3 yr qualification) would dent the growth story.
  • Margin pressure points — competitive railway pricing and commodity costs squeeze Industrial Systems; consolidated margins already carry the OSAT drag.
  • Dilution & modest yield — heavy reinvestment plus the QIP means little dividend; per-share growth must come from execution, not payout.
The bottom line

CG Power is a superb business at a demanding price. The core story — a trusted-governance turnaround compounding earnings on a real grid supercycle, with a net-cash balance sheet and a record order book — is about as good as Indian capital goods gets, and the export windfall adds genuine upside. The complication is that the market has already paid for all of that and for a semiconductor bet that is still losing money and unproven. So the risk here isn't that the business is bad; it's that the price leaves no room for the newest chapter to disappoint. The story over the coming quarters will be written in three numbers: order intake (is the grid-and-export pipeline still filling?), margins (is execution profitable, or is growth being bought cheaply?), and the OSAT ramp (is the semiconductor bet converging toward profit, or slipping?). Get all three right and a ~30%-growth, net-cash, trusted franchise can grow into even this multiple; stumble on any of them — a tender air-pocket, an export shock, a chip-plant delay — and a stock at ~115× has a very long way to fall.
