00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Borosil Renewables makes solar glass — the flat sheet of tough, ultra-clear glass that forms the front cover of a solar panel. It is not ordinary window glass: it is a low-iron, textured, tempered glass engineered to let the maximum amount of sunlight through to the cells underneath while surviving 25 years of hail, heat and weather on a rooftop or in a field. Borosil is India's first and, for years, its only large-scale maker of this product — a genuine domestic near-monopoly in a component the country otherwise imported, overwhelmingly from China.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a pure play on India making its own solar panels. Every gigawatt of solar module capacity India builds at home needs solar glass — and government policy (the ALMM approved-list, production-linked incentives, import curbs) is deliberately pushing module-making onshore. Borosil sells into that build-out. Its India furnaces at Bharuch (Gujarat) run at ~1,000 tonnes per day (TPD), enough glass for roughly 6 GW of modules a year, and it is expanding toward 1,600 TPD.
  • A border duty reset its pricing — and its profits. In December 2024 India imposed a provisional anti-dumping duty (ADD) on solar glass from China and Vietnam (made final for five years in 2025), with a countervailing duty on Malaysia added in 2026. That let Borosil's average ex-factory realisation climb from ₹113.4 to ₹146.7 per mm. On essentially the same furnaces, FY26 EBITDA margin doubled from ~16% to ~32% and standalone EBITDA jumped ~172% to ~₹492 Cr.
  • India boomed while Europe broke. Borosil bought Europe's largest solar-glass maker (the German Interfloat/GMB business) in 2022 — and it was a disaster. Chinese oversupply crushed European solar manufacturing; the German units bled roughly €0.9 million a month and, in 2025, filed for insolvency. The same product, the same company: protected and thriving on one continent, unprotected and failing on the other.
In plain terms

Borosil Renewables is a glass furnace with a national strategic tailwind. It melts sand into a specialised sheet that sits on top of solar panels, and its whole economics reduce to one line: how many tonnes a day it can melt × how full it runs those furnaces × the price it gets per millimetre of glass. For years the price leg was set by Chinese exporters dumping cheap glass into India; the anti-dumping duty handed that price leg back to the domestic maker. The result is a company whose fortunes swung from a heavy loss to a strong profit in a single year — not because it built anything new, but because a policy line moved the price it could charge on the furnaces it already had.

01

Solar glass 101: why the glass on top is a specialised product

No chemistry degree needed — just why this pane is not the glass in your window.

A solar panel is a sandwich. At the very front, facing the sun, sits a sheet of glass; behind it lie the silicon cells that turn light into electricity, sealed in a protective film and backed by a rear sheet, all held in an aluminium frame. That front sheet has two impossible-sounding jobs at once: let as much light through as physically possible, and survive decades outdoors — hail, sand, thermal shock, its own weight over a two-metre span. Ordinary float glass fails both. Solar glass is engineered specifically to pass them.

Why this is not window glass — the four properties that matter
PropertyWhat it means (and why the panel needs it)
Low-ironNormal glass has a faint green tint from iron impurities — fine for a window, wasteful for a solar panel, because that tint absorbs light the cell could have used. Solar glass is made from ultra-pure, low-iron sand so it is water-clear and transmits ~91–94% of sunlight. Every extra percentage point of light is extra electricity for 25 years.
Textured / patternedThe outer surface is rolled with a fine texture so it scatters and traps light instead of reflecting it back off a mirror-flat face. This "anti-reflective" patterning squeezes out more power — which is why the industry calls it patterned or textured glass.
Tempered (toughened)The glass is heat-tempered so it is several times stronger than ordinary glass and, if it ever breaks, crumbles into harmless granules rather than shards. A panel must pass hail-impact and load tests; the front glass is its armour.
CoatedMost solar glass gets an anti-reflective coating baked on to lift transmission another notch. It is the finishing step that separates a commodity pane from a high-performance one.
The surprise most people miss

Ask anyone what a solar panel is "made of" and they will say silicon. True — but the single largest component by weight and area is the glass, and it is a genuinely specialised, hard-to-make product. Getting low-iron transmission, a uniform anti-reflective texture and reliable tempering right, at scale, on a continuous furnace, is not something a general glassmaker just switches on. That is why, for years, a country installing gigawatts of solar had essentially one domestic firm able to supply the glass — and imported the rest. The panel's brain is the cell; its face is a piece of precision glass, and that face is Borosil's entire business.

Why India imported nearly all of it

China built enormous, low-cost solar-glass capacity to feed the world's biggest module industry, and for years it was simply cheaper for Indian module-makers to buy imported glass than to support a domestic supplier. That left Borosil — which had bet early on domestic solar-glass manufacturing — exposed to waves of cheap imports that repeatedly undercut its prices. The company's competitive existence, in other words, has always been entangled with trade policy: when imports flood in freely, the domestic maker's margins collapse; when they are curbed, the maker breathes. Hold that idea — it is the hinge of the whole investment case.

02

What Borosil actually makes: sand in, solar armour out

One product, one furnace-driven value chain — read this once and the company clicks.

Unlike a diversified industrial, Borosil Renewables is close to a single-product company: solar glass. That focus is a feature — it means the business is unusually easy to model, because it is basically raw materials and energy going into a furnace and finished glass coming out. But it also means there is nowhere to hide when the one product's price moves. Here is the chain from sand to a panel's front cover.

The product lines, decoded

  • Front-cover solar glass — the core. The 2mm–3.2mm tempered, low-iron, textured sheet that goes on the sun-facing side of a crystalline-silicon module. This is the overwhelming majority of revenue, sold to India's solar module manufacturers.
  • Bifacial / back glass — newer panels are bifacial, generating power from both faces, so they use glass on the back too instead of a plastic sheet. That quietly doubles the glass content of a panel and is a structural tailwind for a glass-maker as the industry shifts to glass-glass modules.
  • Specialty & allied glass — the same low-iron know-how extends to extra-clear patterned glass for greenhouses, flat-plate solar water heaters and architectural uses. A modest sideline, but it uses the same furnace competence.
Why "one product" cuts both ways

A single-product furnace business is beautifully legible: you can almost value it on a napkin from tonnes per day, utilisation and price per mm. But it also strips out the shock absorbers a multi-segment company enjoys. When solar-glass prices crashed under Chinese dumping, Borosil had no other division to lean on — the FY25 loss was the whole company feeling the one price that matters fall at once. The bifacial shift is the most interesting internal hedge: it is demand growth that comes from the product mix changing, not from India simply installing more panels, and it lifts glass consumed per gigawatt regardless of the price war.

03

The company, the demerger, and who controls it

A Borosil-family business — and why a "renewables" stock carries a 1960s glassware scrip code.

Borosil Renewables belongs to the Borosil group, the old Indian glassware house behind the familiar "Borosil" brand of laboratory and kitchen glass, controlled by the Kheruka family (Pradeep and Shreevar Kheruka). The solar-glass venture began inside the legacy listed company; a 2020 group reorganisation split the two, leaving the consumer/scientific glassware in a separate entity (Borosil Ltd) and the solar-glass business as the standalone listed company, renamed Borosil Renewables.

A genuine quirk worth knowing: Borosil Renewables trades under BSE scrip code 502219 — an unusually low, old number for a renewable-energy company. That is because this listed entity is the legacy Borosil Glass Works vehicle, decades old, which was rechristened when the businesses were demerged. The consumer glassware you might picture was hived off into a newer listing; the solar-glass arm kept the historic code. So the "502219" you will see on BSE is not a data error — it is a fingerprint of the company's origins in India's oldest glassmaking house. 04

The duty that reset the price — and the profits

The single most important event in the story, and the reason FY26 looks nothing like FY25.

For most companies you cannot point to one government notification and say "that is why the profit moved." For Borosil Renewables in FY26, you almost can. The anti-dumping duty on imported solar glass is the hinge on which the entire recent re-rating turns — because Borosil's problem was never demand or capacity. It was that Chinese and Vietnamese exporters were selling glass into India below fair value, dragging the domestic price down to where Borosil lost money. Curb those imports, and the price the domestic maker can charge resets upward.

A ~29% rise in realisation, from ₹113.4 to ₹146.7 per mm, is the single number behind the FY26 turnaround. Solar glass is priced per millimetre of thickness (a 3.2mm sheet is quoted at 3.2 × the per-mm price), a convention worth knowing because it is the metric management itself reports.
The device that only fits this company: same furnaces, price did the work

Here is the sentence that defines Borosil Renewables and could belong to no other primer in the library: the company did not build a single new furnace between the loss and the boom — it ran essentially the same ~1,000 TPD of India capacity in FY25 and FY26 — yet EBITDA margin went from ~16% to ~32% and standalone EBITDA rose ~172% to ~₹492 Cr, almost entirely because a border duty let the ex-factory price move from ₹113 to ₹147 per mm. The volume grew, but the profit explosion was price, and the price was policy. That is the coiled mechanism to hold onto: value here = tonnes melted × utilisation × a realisation that a trade authority, not the company, ultimately controls.

How the duty actually works

The duty is not a flat tariff; it is built around a reference price. In simplified terms, if an imported sheet lands below that reference level (after adding freight, insurance and basic customs duty), a duty is charged to bridge the gap — so cheap dumped glass effectively cannot arrive below the floor the domestic industry needs. Industry estimates put the duty's effect at roughly 48–63 paise per watt of added cost on a module built with imported glass, depending on the type — enough to tilt module-makers toward buying domestic. Alongside the anti-dumping duty on China and Vietnam, India added a countervailing duty on Malaysian solar glass in 2026, closing an obvious re-routing loophole.

The dependency to respect: the same mechanism that made FY26 wonderful is a standing risk. Borosil's margin now leans on a duty that runs for a fixed five-year term and can be reviewed, diluted or circumvented (via a third country, a product tweak, or a policy change). A business whose profitability is set by a trade notification is, by definition, exposed to that notification changing. The bull case needs the duty to hold and the domestic industry to build enough capacity to justify it; the bear case is any crack in that protection letting cheap imports back in.

05

Furnace economics: why this business is capital-heavy and cyclical

The single fact that explains the volatility — a glass furnace never sleeps.

To understand why Borosil's profits swing so violently, you have to understand the object at the centre of the company: a continuous glass furnace. It is a giant, refractory-lined tank that melts a raw batch at around 1,500°C and runs non-stop for years — you do not turn it off at night or when demand dips, because reheating and relining it is enormously expensive and can take months. That single operational fact drives everything about the economics.

The three forces a furnace business lives with
ForceWhat it isWhy it makes profits swing
High fixed costThe furnace, the plant and the energy to keep it molten are large fixed costs that don't shrink when prices fall.Operating leverage cuts both ways: a small rise in price or volume drops straight to profit; a small fall in price can wipe it out — exactly the FY25→FY26 swing.
Must run near-fullBecause the furnace can't be idled, utilisation has to stay high. Selling below cost still beats banking a cold furnace.In a glut, makers keep producing and dump output to clear it — which is precisely how solar-glass prices crash worldwide when China over-builds.
Energy exposureMelting glass is energy-intensive (gas/power is a major cost line).Fuel-price spikes squeeze margins independent of glass prices; efficiency and captive power matter a lot.

Put the three together and you get a business with the classic commodity-processor personality: heavy capital going in up front, a furnace that must keep melting whatever the market does, and a selling price set by global supply and demand rather than by the maker. When the world is short of solar glass, a maker like Borosil earns superb returns; when China floods the market, the same plant loses money. The anti-dumping duty is powerful precisely because it partially removes the worst leg of that cyclicality — the imported-glut price crash — from the Indian market.

Why utilisation is the quiet co-star of the story

Price gets the headlines, but the second lever is utilisation — the share of that ~1,000 TPD the company can actually sell. A duty-protected price only reaches the bottom line if the furnaces are running full and the glass is moving out the door. In FY26 both aligned: domestic demand was strong enough to absorb the output and the duty held the price up, so volume and realisation rose together. The risk on the horizon is that everyone can see the same opportunity — if the whole domestic industry adds furnaces faster than India adds module capacity, utilisation and price both come under pressure again, duty or no duty. The furnace that must run full is a blessing in a tight market and a trap in a loose one.

06

The India localisation play: a bet on India making its own panels

Why Borosil is really a leveraged wager on domestic solar manufacturing, not just solar demand.

It is worth being precise about what Borosil is a play on. It is not simply a bet that India installs more solar — a solar farm can be built from imported panels and never touch an Indian glass furnace. Borosil is a bet on the narrower, policy-driven idea that India manufactures its own solar panels at home, because only domestically-made modules pull demand through to a domestic glass-maker. The government has spent years engineering exactly that shift, and Borosil sits directly downstream of it.

The distinction that separates a good bet from a lazy one

Plenty of "solar" stories are really bets on global panel prices or on India's installation targets. Borosil is a bet on a more specific and more local thing: that panels get built in India, not just deployed in India. That is a policy choice the government keeps reinforcing, but it is a choice — and a company whose demand depends on domestic module factories succeeding is only as strong as that onshoring push. The comfort is that the same state that protects Borosil's price with duties is also the state funding its customers with incentives; the two policies point the same way, which is why the localisation thesis has more than one leg to stand on.

07

The German misadventure: the same product, the opposite outcome

A cautionary chapter — and the cleanest illustration of why the duty matters.

In 2022 Borosil made an ambitious move: it acquired around 86% of the Interfloat Group, whose German unit GMB Glasmanufaktur Brandenburg ran a roughly 300 TPD solar-glass plant — at the time, Europe's largest domestic solar-glass maker. On paper it turned Borosil into a two-continent producer riding both India's and Europe's energy transitions. In practice it became a painful lesson in what happens to a solar-glass maker with no protection from Chinese oversupply.

The European solar-manufacturing ecosystem was overwhelmed in exactly the way India's would have been without duties: Chinese producers flooded Europe with severely underpriced modules and glass, and European makers could not compete on cost. Borosil's German operations bled roughly €0.9 million every month — on the order of ₹9 Cr monthly — and the group's exposure there ran to about €35 million. In 2025, the units gave way: GMB filed for insolvency under German law, followed by the step-down subsidiary Geosphere Glassworks in December 2025. Borosil took one-time charges (the heavy consolidated losses in FY25 and the swings in FY26 largely trace to Europe) and pivoted back to an India-focused business.

The natural experiment hidden in the accounts

Borosil accidentally ran a controlled experiment on itself. Two solar-glass operations, same parent, same product, same era — one in India, one in Germany. The Indian one got an anti-dumping duty and its margins doubled; the German one got no such shield against Chinese dumping and went insolvent. There is no cleaner demonstration of the thesis at the centre of this company: in commodity solar glass, the difference between a strong business and a failed one can be a border policy rather than anything the operator does differently. It is also a warning against over-crediting management for the India boom — the decisive variable was the duty, and the same team's overseas bet failed for want of one.

What to watch on Europe: the insolvencies should de-risk the story going forward — the cash drain stops and Borosil becomes a cleaner India pure play — but insolvency processes carry tails (residual liabilities, guarantees, legal costs, write-offs) that can dribble through the consolidated numbers for a few quarters. Read Borosil on its standalone (India) figures for the true earning power, and treat the consolidated line as still carrying German noise until the process fully closes.
08

The capacity build-out: from 1,000 to 1,600 TPD

The growth lever — and the reason execution and demand timing now matter.

With pricing repaired and the domestic demand pool expanding, Borosil is doing what a furnace business does when the market turns: adding melting capacity. The plan is to lift India capacity from ~1,000 TPD toward ~1,600 TPD by around the end of 2026, via two new furnaces (SG-4 and SG-5), in a project costed in the region of ₹950 Cr. Capacity is the only way a glass-maker grows volume, so this is the clearest signal of management's confidence that the duty-protected up-cycle has legs.

The build-out is the bull case and the risk in the same breath. If India's module manufacturing keeps scaling and the duty holds, more furnaces mean more tonnes sold at healthy prices — a straightforward volume-plus-price compounding. But furnaces are lumpy, all-or-nothing additions: bring 600 TPD online just as domestic demand pauses or as rival domestic capacity floods in, and utilisation dips and pricing softens exactly when the new depreciation and interest hit. The timing of demand versus supply is the thing to watch.

The number that will tell you the expansion is working

When SG-4 and SG-5 light up, the metric to follow is not headline capacity but realisation holding as volume rises — can Borosil sell ~60% more glass without the extra tonnes pushing its own ₹/mm price down? If realisation stays near FY26 levels while volume climbs, the expansion compounds beautifully. If adding capacity (Borosil's own, plus new domestic entrants chasing the same fat margins) drags the price back toward FY25 levels, the company will have more furnaces earning thinner spreads. Watch price-per-mm and utilisation together, quarter by quarter; capacity alone tells you nothing.

The industry and the rivals: a near-monopoly under pressure

Why Borosil's biggest competitive question is who else builds furnaces from here.

Borosil Renewables is unusual in this library for being close to a domestic monopoly — for years it was the only large-scale solar-glass maker in India. But a monopoly earning ~32% margins behind a protective duty is a magnet. The competitive question for the next few years is less "who is taking Borosil's share today" and more "how much new capacity — domestic and imported — comes hunting for the same fat, duty-protected spread."

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Borosil's moat is real but unusual: it is part capability (a decade of hard-won furnace and low-iron know-how that a newcomer cannot copy overnight) and part policy (a duty that keeps the cheapest competitor out). The capability moat is durable; the policy moat is rented. The most likely way this business gets less attractive is not a foreign giant storming in — the duty blocks that — but a wave of domestic furnaces chasing 32% margins until the domestic market is over-supplied and prices normalise. First-mover scale, cost leadership and the coming 1,600 TPD are Borosil's defence; being demonstrably the low-cost, highest-quality domestic maker is what keeps it winning if the field gets crowded.

If the ~₹147/mm price holds while Borosil sells ~60% more glass, FY26's margins are a floor and the earnings compound; if new domestic furnaces or any duty dilution drag realisation back toward ₹113/mm, the same expanded plant earns a fraction as much. The scoreboard is price-per-mm × tonnes sold × the duty on the statute book — everything else is commentary.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A repaired, focused, duty-protected leader — asked to prove the good times aren't just the duty.

Borosil Renewables enters FY27 in its strongest shape: a duty-protected home market, an expanding furnace base, the German cash drain being cauterised, and a national push to make solar panels in India that pulls demand straight to its door. The real debate is whether FY26's ~32% margin is a durable new level or a peak that fresh capacity and any policy softening will grind down.

The bull case

  • Duty-protected near-monopoly — India's largest solar-glass maker, shielded by anti-dumping and countervailing duties, with the deepest track record and scale lead.
  • Onshoring tailwind — ALMM, PLI and import curbs keep pushing module manufacturing into India, expanding the domestic glass-buyer pool Borosil sells into.
  • Operating leverage — a high-fixed-cost furnace business throws profit off fast when price and utilisation rise together, as FY26 showed (EBITDA +172% on +38% revenue).
  • Capacity growth — the move to ~1,600 TPD adds ~60% more volume into a growing, protected market.
  • Cleaner story — exiting the loss-making German operations turns Borosil back into a focused India pure play.

The bear case

  • Rented moat — the fat margin leans on a five-year duty that can be reviewed, diluted or circumvented; profitability is partly a policy variable.
  • Domestic over-supply — 32% margins invite new Indian furnaces (and customer captives); a home-grown capacity glut could re-soften prices from the inside.
  • Commodity cyclicality — solar-glass prices swing hard on global China supply; a furnace that must run full has little defence in a glut.
  • Execution & timing — lumpy furnace additions can land just as demand pauses, hitting utilisation and margin while new depreciation/interest bite.
  • Theme valuation & Europe tail — priced for structural success; residual German insolvency costs and any onshoring wobble could disappoint.
The bottom line

Borosil Renewables spent years as the right idea at the wrong price — the domestic maker of a genuinely specialised product, undercut into losses every time cheap glass sailed in. FY26 flipped that: a border duty handed the price leg back to the incumbent, and a single-product furnace business turned a ~₹200 Cr quarterly loss into a ~₹169 Cr quarterly profit without laying a new brick. What remains unproven is durability — whether ~₹147/mm glass survives the company's own 1,600 TPD expansion and the domestic rivals that 32% margins will inevitably summon. The next milestone that answers it is concrete and dated: SG-4 and SG-5 lighting up around end-2026, and whether realisation per mm holds as those tonnes hit the market. Watch price-per-mm alongside utilisation each quarter; that pairing, not the capacity headline, will tell you whether Borosil is a structural winner or a duty-cycle trade.
