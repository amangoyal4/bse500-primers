00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Westlife Foodworld Limited (formerly Westlife Development) is the listed holding company that operates McDonald's restaurants across West and South India. It does not own the McDonald's brand; it is the licensed operator. The actual restaurants sit inside its wholly-owned subsidiary Hardcastle Restaurants Pvt Ltd (HRPL), which holds the master-franchise / developmental-licensee rights for McDonald's in the western and southern states. At the end of FY26 that was 478 restaurants across 78 cities — Maharashtra, Gujarat, Karnataka, Telangana, Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Goa and more — serving roughly 200 million guests a year. It is, in short, a pure-play quick-service-restaurant (QSR) operator: no other business, no other brand.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It rents a brand and owns the economics. McDonald's Corporation supplies the name, the menu system, the operating playbook and the supply-chain standards; Westlife supplies the capital, builds and runs the restaurants, hires the crew, signs the leases and keeps the profit or loss. In return it pays McDonald's a royalty of roughly 4–5% of sales. So Westlife is not a franchise seller collecting fees — it is the operator on the hook for every restaurant's P&L.
  • The profit engine is same-store sales, not store count. A QSR restaurant carries heavy fixed costs — rent and a core of staff that must be paid whether the tables are full or empty. Once a store is mature, a rupee of extra sales drops to profit at a very high rate; a rupee of lost sales bleeds the other way. That is why the single number that decides Westlife's year is same-store sales growth (SSSG) — and why FY24–FY25, with SSSG barely positive, saw profit collapse even as revenue crept up.
  • FY26 was a recovery quarter-by-quarter, not a boom. Revenue rose ~5.4% to ~₹2,626 Cr; Q4 SSSG was a modest +1.5% on mid-single-digit guest-count growth; operating EBITDA margin held near 13%. Reported net profit was a slim ₹32 Cr — but that headline is distorted by lease accounting, and the number management runs on, cash PAT of ~₹235 Cr, grew ~23%. The stock had fallen sharply from its highs; the market is waiting to see the demand cycle turn.
In plain terms

Westlife is the company that gets to run McDonald's in half the country. It puts up the money to build each restaurant, pays McDonald's a slice of every sale for the golden arches and the recipe book, and lives on what's left after wages, rent and food cost. Because rent and the base crew are fixed, the business behaves like a lever: when a mature restaurant sells a bit more, most of that extra flows straight to profit; when it sells a bit less, profit falls faster than sales. India's eating-out demand went soft through FY24 and FY25, the lever swung the wrong way, and a company that earned ₹112 Cr in FY23 earned just ₹12 Cr in FY25. The whole investment question is whether that lever is now starting to swing back up.

01

The QSR machine: how a burger chain actually earns

No industry background needed — just the format, the guest, and where the money leaks.

A quick-service restaurant is a deceptively simple-looking machine: a standardised kitchen, a limited menu, fast throughput, and a brand that pulls a predictable stream of guests through the door at a predictable average spend. The economics live at the level of a single restaurant, repeated hundreds of times. To follow Westlife you need six terms — none of them technical.

The one structural fact that shapes the whole model: fixed costs sit under variable sales

A McDonald's restaurant's costs split into two buckets that behave very differently. Food and packaging are variable — they rise and fall roughly with how many burgers you sell. But rent, the core crew roster, utilities and the store's depreciation are largely fixed — they barely move whether the restaurant does ₹4 crore or ₹5 crore of annual sales. That single split is the engine of everything that follows. It means a restaurant has a break-even level of sales it must clear before it earns anything, and above that level, extra sales are extremely profitable because the fixed costs are already paid. Miss the level and the same store loses money. This is why a QSR operator's fortunes swing so violently on demand it only partly controls — and why counting stores tells you far less than watching what each store sells.

Who the guest is, and when they come

  • The everyday value guest — the ₹99 McSaver-meal customer, the after-school group, the family treat. High-frequency, price-sensitive, the volume base of the business. Value affordability is what keeps guest counts up when wallets are tight.
  • The occasion & premium guest — a fuller meal, a McCafé coffee, a premium burger. Lower frequency, higher check, better margin. Growing this cohort is how Westlife lifts average spend without losing the value guest.
  • The convenience guest — orders on the app, collects at the drive-thru, or has McDelivery bring it home. Increasingly digital-first: in FY26 roughly 76% of sales were digitally originated through the app and in-store kiosks.
02

What a franchisee actually owns — and what McDonald's keeps

The defining structural fact: this is a licensed operation, not a brand owner.

The most common misunderstanding about Westlife is to treat it as "the Indian McDonald's company." It is not. McDonald's Corporation owns the brand, the intellectual property and the global system; Westlife, through HRPL, is a developmental licensee — a long-term operator granted the exclusive right to build and run McDonald's restaurants in a defined territory. Understanding exactly where the line falls is the key to the whole business.

The brand and the recipe come from Oak Brook; the money, the risk and the residual profit are all Westlife's. The royalty is the toll it pays to stand under the arches.

Why does this arrangement exist? Because it plays to each side's strength. McDonald's gets a committed, well-capitalised local partner who understands Indian tastes, real estate, supply chains and labour far better than a distant global HQ could — one who will patiently build a market over decades. Westlife gets the single most valuable brand in fast food, a proven operating system, and a protected territory it does not have to win from scratch. The relationship is deliberately long-dated: developmental-licence agreements run for many years and carry store-opening commitments, so both sides are locked into the same multi-decade project.

The double edge every franchisee investor must hold

Renting the brand is a bargain and a cage at once. The bargain: Westlife gets instant, unmatched brand pull and a battle-tested playbook without the cost or risk of building either — a colossal head start over any independent burger chain. The cage: it does not control the thing that matters most. It cannot change the core menu at will, cannot unilaterally reset the royalty, and must invest to McDonald's standards and cadence. If the global system stumbles, or the licence terms tighten at renewal, Westlife feels it directly. The brand is its greatest asset and the one asset it will never own — a dependency that no amount of good operating can remove.

A crucial territorial point often missed: Westlife runs McDonald's only in the West and South. The North and East were operated by a separate licensee, Connaught Plaza Restaurants — a different company entirely — until McDonald's took full control of that territory in 2019. So "McDonald's India" is not one listed entity: Westlife is the pure-play way to own the West-and-South half, and its numbers say nothing about how the arches perform up north.
03

The 478-to-630 runway: growth by opening restaurants

One of the two engines — a long, funded pipeline of new stores in an under-penetrated market.

Westlife has two ways to grow: sell more from the restaurants it already has (SSSG, section 05), and build more restaurants. The second is the visible, controllable one. India is strikingly under-served by organised QSR relative to its population and rising urban middle class, and Westlife's territory — the affluent, urbanising west and south — is exactly where the runway is longest. The company has framed this as an explicit medium-term target.

Where the new stores go

  • Deeper into core cities — Mumbai, Pune, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Ahmedabad and Chennai still absorb more restaurants; density lowers delivery times and marketing cost per store.
  • New corridors and formats — highways (via fuel-retail tie-ups such as Jio-bp forecourts), airports and malls, where a drive-thru or a compact counter reaches guests the high-street store never will.
  • Smaller towns — as brand awareness and incomes spread beyond the metros, the map of viable locations keeps widening across the nine-plus states in the territory.
Why new stores are a slow burn, not an instant win

Store expansion is the growth lever the market can see and count, but it flatters revenue while quietly dragging near-term margin. A newly-opened restaurant carries the full weight of rent, a fresh crew and depreciation from day one, yet takes time — often a couple of years — to build the guest habit that lifts it to mature sales and full profitability. So a burst of openings adds revenue and dilutes margin at the same time: the new-store drag. This is precisely why counting outlets can mislead. A year of aggressive openings can look like great top-line growth while the average restaurant is actually earning less. The runway is real and long, but it pays out on a delay measured in years, not quarters.

04

The company, the family, and who controls it

A Jatia-family enterprise built patiently over three decades around a single brand relationship.

Behind Westlife sits the Jatia family, who have run McDonald's in western India since the brand entered the country and have progressively consolidated and expanded the business into the listed pure-play it is today. Unlike a sprawling conglomerate, this is a focused operator: one brand, one format, one long partnership.

  • 2022

  • FY24–FY25

  • FY26
    Network reaches 478 restaurants; revenue ~₹2,626 Cr (+5.4%); cash PAT ~₹235 Cr (+23%); Q4 SSSG +1.5%. Management reaffirms the 580–630-store Vision-2027 goal and a ₹3,000 Cr sales aspiration.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter: the Jatia family and their holding entities control roughly 56% (approx.) of Westlife Foodworld. The balance is public float, with domestic institutions (mutual funds, insurers) a notably large holder at around a quarter of the company, and foreign investors a smaller slice. Confirm the exact promoter percentage in the latest shareholding filing.
  • The operating subsidiary: the restaurants themselves live in Hardcastle Restaurants Pvt Ltd (HRPL), a wholly-owned subsidiary. Westlife Foodworld is essentially the listed holding company through which investors own HRPL and its 478 McDonald's outlets.
  • The McDonald's relationship: not an equity link but a licence. McDonald's is the franchisor and standard-setter; Westlife is the committed local operator. The health of that relationship — and the terms at each renewal — is a structural dependency no shareholding can capture.
Why a focused operator is both a virtue and a constraint: Westlife's single-minded concentration on one brand in one territory is a strength — decades of accumulated operating skill, real-estate instinct and supply-chain depth in exactly the market it serves. But it is also an undiversified bet: there is no second business to cushion a bad QSR cycle, no other brand if McDonald's stumbles, and no geography outside the west and south. Investors are buying management's operating craft applied to one franchise — nothing more, nothing less.
05

Same-store sales and the leverage that makes or breaks the year

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a mature QSR base.

Here is the mechanism that decides Westlife's profits, and it is worth slowing right down for. A restaurant's costs are mostly fixed once it is open. So the profit it earns is exquisitely sensitive to how much it sells above its break-even point. Multiply that across a base of 478 stores and you get a company whose bottom line swings far harder than its top line — up in good demand years, down in bad ones. The number that captures it is same-store sales growth, and no single figure tells you more about how the year is going.

Now watch what that flat stretch did to profit. Between FY23 and FY26 Westlife's revenue actually rose — from roughly ₹2,278 Cr to ₹2,626 Cr — helped by all those new stores. Yet reported net profit went the other way, from ₹112 Cr to a trough of ₹12 Cr and back to just ₹32 Cr. Same brand, more restaurants, higher revenue — and a fraction of the profit. That is operating leverage in reverse: when same-store sales stall, the fixed cost base is no longer being out-run, new-store drag piles on top, and the margin the whole business earns compresses hard.

The device that only fits a mature QSR: sales past break-even, not sales themselves

The number that runs Westlife is not "how much did we sell" but "how much did each restaurant sell above the fixed costs it must cover anyway." Picture a mature McDonald's doing, say, ₹5 crore a year, of which the first ~₹4.3 crore just pays for rent, the base crew, utilities and depreciation. The profit is almost entirely in the last stretch. Lift that store's sales 6% and a large share of the extra ₹30 lakh falls to profit, because the fixed costs are already paid — the incremental margin is a multiple of the average margin. Drop its sales 4% and profit can vanish, because you are eating into the thin layer that sat above break-even. That is why a 20-point swing in SSSG (FY23 to FY25) produced a near-tenfold swing in profit on almost unchanged revenue. In a full-service restaurant with thinner fixed leverage, or a franchise fee collector with no store P&L at all, this maths simply does not apply. It is the specific, non-transferable engine of a self-operated QSR base.

Hold that lens for the rest of this primer.

06

The two profit numbers: why ₹32 Cr and ~₹235 Cr are both true

The accounting quirk that makes a ₹9,000 Cr company look like it barely earns — and what to read instead.

Here is the fact that startles almost everyone opening Westlife's accounts for the first time. A company worth around ₹9,100 Cr reports a net profit of just ₹32 Cr — which would imply an absurd valuation of nearly 300× earnings. But that reported number is heavily distorted by how modern accounting treats store leases. The figure management actually runs the business on, cash PAT of ~₹235 Cr, tells a very different — and far more sensible — story.

The culprit is a lease-accounting standard (Ind-AS 116). A QSR operator like Westlife rents almost all its restaurants on long leases. The old way simply expensed the rent each year. The new way forces the company to put the whole future lease commitment on its balance sheet as an asset and a liability, and then to split what looks like "rent" into depreciation of that asset plus interest on that liability. The quirk is that this method front-loads the cost — the combined depreciation-plus-interest charge is higher than the plain cash rent in a lease's early years, and a company that is busily opening new stores is perpetually stacking up young leases. The result: reported profit is pushed down well below the actual cash the business generates.

The ~₹200 Cr gap is almost entirely the front-loaded, non-cash portion of lease accounting on a fast-growing store base — not a sign of a weak operation. It is why management, and most analysts, watch pre-Ind-AS operating EBITDA and cash earnings rather than the reported bottom line.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with

The same company can honestly report a tiny profit and a healthy one at once — and a first-time reader who anchors on the ₹32 Cr headline will badly misjudge it. The reported figure is not fake, but it is an accounting artefact of renting hundreds of stores and opening more every year; it says little about how the restaurants are actually doing. The number that reflects the operating reality is pre-Ind-AS operating EBITDA (a ~13% margin in FY26) and the cash PAT beneath it. Management even frames its Vision-2027 target in pre-Ind-AS EBITDA-margin terms (13–15%) precisely to sidestep the lease-accounting fog. When you value Westlife, value the cash the base throws off — not the headline the standard produces.

Why this reframes the whole company: it means the eye-watering "300× P/E" you might read off a screener is not the real multiple. Measured against cash earnings and pre-lease-accounting operating profit, Westlife is an expensive-but-not-insane consumer-growth stock, not a company that has stopped making money. Get the denominator right before you judge the price — and always read Westlife's operating EBITDA and cash PAT alongside, never instead of, the reported PAT.
07

Dine-in versus the channels: where the sales come through

On-premise, drive-thru, McDelivery and McCafé — four ways to reach the same guest, with different economics.

A modern McDonald's is no longer just a place you walk into and eat. Westlife sells through several channels at once, and the mix matters — for margin, for convenience, and for how resilient sales are when footfall softens. Broadly, sales split into on-premise (dine-in) and off-premise (everything taken away), with McCafé sitting across both as a margin-rich add-on.

Roughly 58% of sales are still on-premise dine-in and about 42% off-premise — the convenience channels of drive-thru, McDelivery and takeaway, now the faster-growing half. Cutting across both, some 76% of sales are digitally originated: three-quarters now start on the app or a self-order kiosk rather than at a till.

The four channels, and why each matters

  • Dine-in (on-premise) — the traditional restaurant experience, upgraded through the EOTF format with kiosks and table service. Still the biggest channel and the anchor of the brand experience, but the most exposed to a footfall slowdown.
  • Drive-thru — about a quarter of Westlife's restaurants have a drive-thru lane. High-convenience, high-throughput, and a natural fit for highway and forecourt locations; a structurally growing format as the network spreads beyond dense high streets.
  • McDelivery — food brought to the door, via both McDonald's own app and aggregator platforms. Captures the at-home occasion and adds a resilient, convenience-led sales stream — though delivery carries its own commission and packaging costs.
  • McCafé — the in-store coffee-and-beverage counter, now rolled across effectively the whole eligible base. Beverages carry high gross margins, so McCafé is less a separate channel than a margin lever laid over the others — every coffee added to an order lifts the check and the spread.
Why the channel spread is quietly defensive

The mix does more than chase convenience — it hardens the business against a soft patch. When dine-in footfall dips, a guest who now orders on the app for delivery, or swings through the drive-thru, keeps the sale on Westlife's books instead of losing it. Spreading sales across dine-in, drive-thru, delivery and pickup means no single behavioural shift can hollow out the top line the way it might for a pure sit-down chain. And because roughly three-quarters of sales now originate digitally, Westlife also gets something a walk-in-only restaurant never had: data on who its guests are, what they buy and how to bring them back — the raw material for loyalty, targeted value offers and higher visit frequency. McCafé rides on top of all of it, turning a burger visit into a higher-margin coffee-and-burger visit.

08

Unit economics: the anatomy of a rupee of sales

Gross margin, wages, rent and royalty — where a hundred rupees of McDonald's sales actually goes.

To judge Westlife you have to see the shape of a single sale. Of every ₹100 a restaurant rings up, a predictable set of costs is peeled away in order, and what survives at the bottom is the operating margin the whole company lives on. The striking feature is how strong the gross margin is — and how much of the eventual profit is then decided by two big, largely fixed lines: people and occupancy.

Two forces shape this stack. First, food-cost inflation: the price of items such as cheese, chicken, edible oil, wheat and vegetables moves the gross-margin line directly, and Westlife can only partly offset it through scale sourcing, menu engineering and measured price rises — push prices too hard and the value guest walks. A record gross margin (above 71%) in a recent quarter showed how favourable input costs can help; a spike would do the reverse. Second, fixed-cost inflation: rents and wages ratchet up over time regardless of sales, so the business must grow same-store sales just to stand still on margin. This is the everyday grind beneath the leverage story of section 05.

The lever management genuinely controls: mix and menu

Most of Westlife's costs are set by landlords, labour markets, commodity prices and a royalty rate it does not write. What it does own is what sits on the tray. Steering guests toward a McCafé coffee, a premium burger or a fuller meal lifts the average check and the margin without needing more footfall; anchoring the range with sharp value — the ₹99 McSaver meal, coffee subscriptions — protects guest counts when budgets are tight. The art is running both at once: value to keep the crowd coming, premiumisation and beverages to make each visit worth more. That barbell — cheap enough to visit often, tempting enough to trade up — is the clearest dial Westlife turns to widen the ₹13 at the bottom of the stack, quite apart from waiting on the demand cycle.

09

The industry and the rivals

A branded-QSR land-grab — several listed operators, each renting a different global brand.

India's organised quick-service market is a race between a handful of listed operators, each of which — like Westlife — typically runs a foreign brand under licence rather than owning it. They compete for the same urban stomach, the same high-street and mall real estate, and the same delivery riders. The peer set is best understood by the brand each one carries.

Westlife's network — 478 stores — looks small next to the pizza and fried-chicken operators (Jubilant's Domino's runs well over 2,000 outlets; the KFC/Pizza Hut franchisees several hundred to over a thousand each). But that comparison flatters them and understates Westlife: it runs only half the country (West & South) and a single brand, and its restaurants are large-format, high-average-volume outlets that out-earn a small franchise unit when demand is there. Store count measures reach, not economics.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things distinguish this contest from an ordinary retail price war. First, the brands rarely fight on headline price — a McSaver meal and a value pizza coexist because guests self-sort by craving (burger, chicken or pizza) more than by a few rupees. The real competition is for the best real estate, the fastest delivery, the strongest brand pull and the lowest cost per store. Second, Westlife's edge is the McDonald's brand itself — arguably the most recognised in fast food — paired with large, high-throughput, drive-thru-and-McCafé-equipped stores that can out-earn a small franchise unit when demand is there. Its constraint is the flip side: only half the country, only one brand, and a network still a fraction of the pizza and chicken operators'. It is the quality franchise in its lane, not the biggest footprint on the map.

10

Financials & valuation

A premium growth multiple on a business waiting for its demand cycle to turn.

Westlife is priced as a long-duration consumer-growth compounder, not a cheap cyclical. That is the crux of the valuation debate: the market pays up today for a store-expansion runway and an eventual demand recovery it cannot yet see in the results. FY26's numbers were a modest step forward — revenue up ~5.4%, cash earnings up ~23%, margins stabilising — but same-store sales were still tepid, and the price already assumes they re-accelerate.

The one number that settles the thesis: same-store sales growth

At a premium multiple on cash earnings, Westlife does not need to get cheaper — it needs its same-store sales growth to re-accelerate into the mid-single digits and beyond. That single figure, read each quarter with restaurant-level margin, is what proves or breaks the case. Here is why it dominates everything else: with a largely fixed cost base, every extra point of SSSG drops to operating profit at a high rate, so a move from ~1.5% back toward the high-single-digit SSSG of the good years would lift margins and cash earnings far faster than revenue — vindicating the multiple. If SSSG stays stuck near zero while rents and wages keep ratcheting up, the fixed-cost base keeps grinding margins down, new-store drag compounds it, and the premium price looks unmoored from the earnings. Store openings are the visible growth; the demand recovery in the existing base is the real one. Watch the like-for-like line — it is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A quality franchise with a long runway, asked the only question that matters: will the base sell more?

Westlife enters FY27 with the pieces of a good story in place — the strongest brand in fast food, a long store-opening runway, a fully-upgraded EOTF-and-McCafé base, a growing digital and delivery mix, and early signs the demand cycle is thawing (Q1 FY27 saw double-digit guest-traffic growth lift revenue ~12%). What it cannot manufacture is the one input that decides everything: consumers in the west and south choosing to eat out, and eat McDonald's, a little more often. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Operating leverage, ready to reverse upward — with the base fully built out and costs largely fixed, a return to mid-single-digit-plus SSSG would flow to margins and cash profit at a high rate.
  • Long, funded store runway — 478 heading to 580–630 by CY27, 60+ openings a year, into a still under-penetrated affluent territory.
  • The McDonald's brand & format edge — unmatched pull, large high-AUV drive-thru-and-McCafé stores, and a data-rich digital base (~76% of sales) for loyalty and frequency.
  • Mix levers self-help — McCafé and premiumisation lift the check and margin; sharp value keeps guest counts up — profit growth that need not wait on the cycle alone.
  • Early turn visible — Q1 FY27's double-digit traffic and ~12% revenue growth hint the demand trough may be behind it.

The bear case

  • Demand is not in its gift — SSSG has hovered near zero for two years; if the consumer stays cautious, the fixed-cost base keeps grinding margins lower.
  • A premium price on thin reported profit — the market already pays up for a recovery; disappointment on SSSG has little valuation support beneath it.
  • Cost ratchet — rents, wages and food inflation rise regardless of sales; the business must grow same-store sales just to hold margin.
  • New-store drag & capex — aggressive openings dilute near-term margin and consume cash before they mature.
  • Single brand, half a country, a licence it doesn't own — no diversification, and a structural dependence on the McDonald's relationship and its renewal terms.
The bottom line

Westlife Foodworld is a well-run operator of a superb franchise, caught between a long structural growth story and a stubborn near-term demand lull — and priced as if the lull is nearly over. The self-help is genuine: more stores, a richer channel and beverage mix, sharper value, a fully modern base. But the engine that swings its profit is the like-for-like sales of the restaurants it already has, and that engine has been idling for two years. The next test is concrete and datable: whether the double-digit guest traffic of Q1 FY27 hardens into a sustained mid-single-digit-plus SSSG across the FY27 quarters, pulling operating margin back toward the 15% Vision-2027 ceiling. Deliver that, and the fixed-cost leverage turns a modest sales recovery into a sharp earnings one, and the premium multiple earns its keep. Stay stuck near flat, and no number of new store openings will keep the margin from bleeding — because in this business, it was never the count of restaurants that mattered, but what each one sells this year against last.
