00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

ITC Limited is, on paper, a diversified conglomerate — cigarettes, packaged foods, personal care, paperboards and agri-business — but in profit terms it is still, overwhelmingly, a cigarette company that is patiently building a large FMCG business with the cash. Cigarettes generate roughly ₹21,000 crore of segment profit — the great majority of ITC's earnings — which funds everything else, from Aashirvaad atta to its paperboard mills.

Three facts frame everything:

  • An extraordinary cash engine. FY26 net revenue was ~₹79,000 Cr and continuing-operations net profit ₹21,018 Cr, at a ~35% operating margin and a remarkable ~39% return on capital. (The headline YoY profit "fall" is purely optical — FY25's number was inflated by a one-off gain from demerging the hotels business.)
  • The cigarette business is both the strength and the cage. It is a near-monopoly with huge margins and pricing power — but it is taxed ferociously. A major tax overhaul effective February 2026 (cigarettes moved to a 40% GST slab plus a new per-stick excise) pushes total tax toward 65–75% of the retail price, the structural ceiling on the whole company's growth and valuation.
  • The diversification is real and improving. The non-cigarette FMCG-Others business (foods, snacks, personal care) crossed ~₹37,000 Cr of consumer spend and is expanding margins; ITC has also become an income stock, paying out most of its profit at a ~5% dividend yield, even as an ESG/tobacco "discount" keeps the valuation low.
In plain terms

Think of ITC as a money-printing cigarette business wearing a consumer-goods coat. Selling cigarettes in India is wildly profitable — ITC dominates it, and demand barely flinches at price hikes — but the government takes the lion's share through taxes and raises them most years, so the business can't grow fast. ITC's long game is to channel that reliable cash into everyday products — flour, biscuits, chips, soap, stationery — building a second engine that the market will value more highly and that isn't taxed to death. Meanwhile it hands shareholders a fat dividend. The catch: as long as cigarettes are the profit, ITC carries a tax sword over its head and an ESG stigma that keeps its price low — which is also, for income investors, the appeal.

01

The cigarette engine: how ITC really earns

One product line generates most of the profit — here's why it's so powerful.

To understand ITC you must first understand its cigarette business, because that is where the profit is. It is one of the great consumer franchises in India: a dominant share of the legal market, iconic brands (Gold Flake, Classic, Navy Cut), and pricing power so strong that ITC can pass on tax hikes and still hold volumes.

FeatureWhat it means
Dominant shareITC controls the large majority of India's legal cigarette market — a near-monopoly with decades-old brand loyalty that's almost impossible to dislodge.
Inelastic demandSmokers are loyal and price-insensitive in the short run, so ITC can raise prices to offset tax increases without losing much volume.
Huge segment profitCigarettes earned ~₹21,051 Cr of segment profit in FY26 (+5%) — the overwhelming majority of ITC's total profit, at very high margins.

The pass-through model — and its limit

Here's the core mechanic. When the government raises cigarette tax, ITC doesn't usually absorb it — it raises the retail price to pass the tax to smokers, protecting its own margin. Because demand is inelastic, moderate hikes barely dent volumes, so ITC's profit keeps growing. But there's a ceiling: if taxes (and therefore prices) rise too far, too fast, two things happen — some smokers quit or cut back, and many switch to cheap illegal/illicit cigarettes that pay no tax. So the cigarette engine is powerful within a band, and dangerous beyond it. The entire ITC investment debate hinges on whether the government taxes gradually (good — ITC keeps compounding) or shocks the market (bad — volumes and the illicit problem bite). That tension is the subject of §04.

03

The company and who controls it

A company with no promoter — and a foreign tobacco giant as its biggest shareholder.

ITC is unusual: it is a professionally-managed company with no promoter family. Its single largest shareholder is British American Tobacco (BAT) with ~28–29%, a legacy of ITC's origins as the "Imperial Tobacco Company". The rest is held by Indian institutions (LIC, SUUTI and others), foreign funds and the public — making ITC effectively a widely-held, board-run institution.

Timeline

  • 1910
    Founded as the Imperial Tobacco Company of India — the root of today's cigarette dominance and the BAT relationship.
  • 1970s
    Indianised and renamed I.T.C. Limited; begins diversifying beyond tobacco — first into hotels (1975) and paperboards (1979).
  • 1990s–2000s
    Builds agri-business (the famed e-Choupal) and launches packaged foods — Aashirvaad atta, Sunfeast biscuits — the FMCG journey begins.
  • 2010s
    Scales FMCG hard: Bingo, Yippee, Fiama, Engage, Savlon, Classmate — building a portfolio of household brands with cigarette cash.
  • Jan 2025
    Demerges ITC Hotels into a separately-listed company — a landmark capital-allocation and value-unlock move (see §06).
  • Feb 2026
    Major tobacco tax overhaul — cigarettes shift to a 40% GST slab and a new per-stick excise; compensation cess abolished (see §04).
  • FY26
    Continuing-ops profit ₹21,018 Cr; FMCG consumer spend tops ~₹37,000 Cr; ~₹14.50/share dividend.

Ownership

  • BAT ~28–29%: the largest single shareholder and a strategic partner — but also an overhang, as BAT has trimmed its stake before and could sell more (see §08).
  • Domestic institutions ~49%: LIC, SUUTI and mutual funds hold large blocks — ITC is a core income holding for Indian institutions.
  • No promoter: with no controlling family, the board and professional management run the company — governance is institutional rather than founder-led.
04

The taxation overhang

The single force that governs ITC's profits, volumes and valuation — read this closely.

No company in this primer series is as defined by government taxation as ITC. Cigarettes are a "sin good", and the Indian state taxes them ferociously and unpredictably — total tax can be 65–75% of the retail price. Every Budget, every GST Council meeting is a potential shock. In February 2026 a sweeping overhaul reshaped the whole tax structure, and it is the defining event of ITC's current chapter.

The Feb 2026 tobacco tax overhaul
ChangeWhat happened
GST to the 40% slabCigarettes (and most tobacco) moved to the new top 40% "demerit" GST rate, up from 28%.
Compensation cess abolishedThe old GST compensation cess on tobacco was scrapped (31 Jan 2026)…
…replaced by per-stick excise…and replaced with a revived Additional Excise Duty of ~₹2,050–₹8,500 per 1,000 sticks (by length), plus a shift to MRP-based valuation.
Net effectTax per stick estimated up ~20–40%; companies likely to raise prices ~18–35%; total tax incidence rises further.
Why this is the whole ball game

Taxation governs three things at once for ITC. (1) Volumes: sharp price hikes (to pass on tax) can suppress demand and, worse, push smokers to illicit cigarettes — which already account for a large slice of the market and pay no tax, directly stealing ITC's volume. (2) Profit: ITC's pass-through model works for gradual hikes but breaks if a shock forces it to choose between protecting margin and protecting volume. (3) Valuation: the uncertainty itself — never knowing when the next big hike lands — is why ITC trades at a discount to other consumer giants despite superior returns. The reassuring history is that India has mostly favoured stable, moderate taxation (it doesn't want to kill the golden goose or fuel illicit trade), and ITC has compounded through decades of tax hikes. But every overhaul, like Feb 2026's, reopens the question — and the stock reacts violently to tax news.

ITC's own warning: the company has repeatedly flagged that steep, sudden tax increases backfire — they grow the illegal-cigarette trade (which hurts tax revenue and ITC) rather than reducing smoking. The bull case assumes policymakers keep taxation gradual; the bear case is a punitive shock that breaks the pass-through model and accelerates the shift to illicit and alternatives.
05

The FMCG diversification

The second engine ITC is building — slowly profitable, strategically vital.

ITC's long-term answer to the cigarette cage is its non-cigarette FMCG business — a portfolio of everyday Indian brands built with cigarette cash over two decades. It now represents over ₹37,000 crore of annual consumer spend and is the company's clearest path to a future that doesn't depend on tobacco.

Why the FMCG story matters — and why it's slow

The strategic logic is compelling: ITC takes the reliable cash from cigarettes and builds non-tobacco brands that (a) diversify away from the tax risk, (b) carry no ESG stigma, and (c) the market values at far higher multiples than tobacco. The progress is real — ITC is already a top-tier Indian foods player, and crucially its FMCG margins are now expanding as scale kicks in, moving from "land-grab" mode toward genuine profitability. The frustration for investors is that it's slow: building distribution and brand against entrenched giants (HUL, Nestlé, Britannia) takes decades and heavy investment, and FMCG-Others still contributes only a small slice of profit despite its large revenue. The bull case is that the margin inflection compounds for years; the bear case is that it never grows large or profitable enough to truly free ITC from cigarettes.

06

The hotels demerger

A capital-allocation milestone — and the reason FY26 profit "fell".

For decades, investors complained that ITC's capital-hungry, low-return hotels business was a poor use of cigarette cash. In January 2025, ITC demerged its hotels arm into a separately-listed company — ITC Hotels — handing shareholders shares in the new entity directly. It was a landmark response to that criticism, and it explains a quirk in the recent numbers.

Why the demerger was smart — and the FY26 optical "fall"

Hotels are asset-heavy and low-return — the opposite of ITC's high-margin core — so housing them inside ITC dragged down the group's overall returns and consumed cash that could fund FMCG or dividends. Spinning hotels off does two good things: it lets the market value the hotel assets separately (often at a higher multiple than they got buried inside ITC), and it leaves the remaining ITC more capital-light, higher-return and simpler — exactly what shareholders wanted. The accounting wrinkle: the demerger created a large one-off gain in FY25, inflating that year's reported profit to ~₹35,000 Cr. So FY26's ₹21,018 Cr looks like a 72% crash versus the prior-year quarter — but that's an illusion. On a clean, like-for-like continuing-operations basis, profit actually rose ~5%. Always compare ITC on continuing operations, not the headline.

The pattern to watch: the hotels demerger signalled a more shareholder-friendly capital allocation — focusing on the high-return core, returning more cash, and unlocking value from non-core assets. Whether ITC extends this discipline (e.g. how it treats other non-core pieces, and how generously it keeps returning cash) is a key part of the re-rating thesis.
07

Paperboards & Agri-business

The steady, lower-profile B2B engines that round out the company.

Beyond cigarettes and FMCG, ITC runs two substantial business-to-business operations that provide scale, stability and — in agri's case — a strategic supply backbone for its foods business.

  • Paperboards, Paper & Packaging: ITC is a leading Indian maker of paperboard and specialty packaging (it supplies its own products and external customers). It's a cyclical industrial business — FY26 segment profit actually fell ~13% to ~₹797 Cr on input-cost pressure, cheap Chinese imports and soft demand. Solid through-cycle, but margin-sensitive to pulp prices and global oversupply.
  • Agri-business: sourcing, trading and value-added agri products (wheat, rice, spices, leaf tobacco). FY26 revenue ~₹20,296 Cr (+2.7%), profit ~₹1,496 Cr. Lower-margin and commodity-linked, but strategically vital — it is the supply chain that feeds ITC's packaged-foods business (the famous e-Choupal farmer network), and a growing value-added exports story.
How to weigh these

Neither paperboards nor agri is a glamour business, and together they're a modest share of profit. But they matter for two reasons: stability and integration. They give ITC scale and diversification beyond consumer products, and agri in particular is a genuine competitive asset — direct, large-scale farm sourcing underpins the cost and quality of Aashirvaad, Sunfeast and the rest. Don't expect them to drive the stock; do recognise they make ITC a more resilient, vertically-integrated whole than a pure consumer company.

08

The tobacco discount, the dividend & the BAT overhang

Why a ~30%-return business trades cheap — and why income investors love it anyway.

Here's ITC's central valuation paradox: it earns ~29% return on equity and ~39% return on capital — numbers most companies would kill for — yet trades at a modest ~17× earnings, far below lower-returning consumer peers. Three forces explain the discount, and one of them is exactly why a different set of investors find ITC irresistible.

Why ITC is cheap — and who that suits
ForceEffect on the stock
The ESG / tobacco stigmaMany global and domestic funds cannot or will not own tobacco, shrinking the buyer pool and capping the multiple — regardless of how good the business is.
The tax uncertaintyThe permanent risk of a punitive tax shock (see §04) means the market refuses to pay a full "consumer" multiple for the cigarette profit.
The BAT overhangBAT's ~29% stake is a known seller — it has trimmed before to fund its own needs, and the prospect of further block sales caps the price.
…but a ~5% dividend yieldThe flip side: ITC pays out most of its profit, giving a ~5% dividend yield — a rare, growing income stream that makes it a favourite of yield-seeking and retiree portfolios.
The discount as a feature, not just a bug

For a growth investor, ITC's stigma and tax overhang are reasons to stay away. For an income or value investor, they're the opportunity: you get a dominant, cash-gushing, high-return business at a low multiple, paying a ~5% dividend that grows, with optionality if the FMCG business re-rates or the tobacco stigma ever eases. The BAT overhang cuts both ways — a clean, well-absorbed selldown could actually remove an overhang and broaden the shareholder base, while a messy block sale pressures the price near-term. ITC is, in short, the market's clearest example of a wonderful business with a permanently capped multiple — which is precisely why it's a perennial debate between "cheap for a reason" and "too cheap to ignore".

The industry and the rivals

Near-unchallenged in cigarettes; a determined challenger in FMCG.

ITC competes in two very different arenas. In cigarettes it is dominant, with only small legal rivals and one large illegal one. In FMCG it is the ambitious #3-or-so challenger taking on India's consumer-goods titans.

The structural backdrop: in cigarettes, ITC's enemy isn't a competitor — it's taxation and the illicit trade it fuels. In FMCG, ITC is the well-funded outsider trying to out-distribute and out-innovate India's best consumer companies; its agri backbone and cigarette cash give it staying power, but share gains against a HUL or Nestlé come slowly and expensively. The two arenas demand completely different playbooks — defend a fortress on one side, storm the castle on the other.

How to think about the valuation

ITC is the market's purest "great-business, cheap-multiple" debate. On the numbers — ~39% ROCE, ~35% margins, huge free cash flow, ~5% yield — it screams quality and value. The ~17× multiple exists because of the tobacco stigma, the tax uncertainty and the BAT overhang (§08), not because the business is weak. The bull case is a re-rating: if the FMCG business keeps inflating margins, if capital allocation stays shareholder-friendly (more demergers, more cash returns), or if the tobacco discount narrows, a high-return company on 17× has obvious room. The bear case is that the discount is permanent and deserved — tobacco's structural decline in the West eventually reaches India, taxes keep climbing, and ESG exclusion never lifts — so ITC stays a cheap dividend stock that compounds modestly. Either way, the dividend pays you to wait.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A superb cash machine, caged by tax and stigma — and paying you to hold it.

ITC is one of India's highest-quality, highest-return businesses, with a fortress cigarette franchise funding a credible FMCG build-out and a generous dividend. The investment debate is almost entirely about the tax/regulatory cage and the valuation discount — not the quality of the cash generation.

The bull case

  • Cash-machine core — dominant cigarettes with pricing power, ~39% ROCE, huge free cash flow.
  • FMCG inflection — a large, growing non-tobacco business with expanding margins and re-rating potential.
  • Shareholder-friendly — the hotels demerger plus a ~5% dividend yield signal better capital allocation.
  • Cheap for the quality — ~17× earnings for a 29% ROE business leaves room if the discount narrows.
  • Defensive & resilient — staples demand and integrated agri supply make it a low-volatility compounder.

The bear case

  • Tax shock risk — the Feb 2026 overhaul and future hikes can hit volumes and fuel illicit trade.
  • Structural cigarette decline — long-run tobacco volumes face health/ESG headwinds; the engine can't grow forever.
  • Slow FMCG payoff — non-cigarette profit is still small; the diversification may never fully free ITC from tobacco.
  • Permanent discount — ESG exclusion and the BAT overhang may keep the multiple low indefinitely.
  • Cyclical drags — paperboards and agri margins swing with commodities and global oversupply.
The bottom line

ITC is a wonderful business wearing a regulatory ball-and-chain: a dominant, ultra-cash-generative cigarette franchise, a genuine and improving FMCG second act, and a balance sheet that returns most of its cash to shareholders. The business quality is not in doubt; the questions are how hard the government taxes the goose, and whether the market ever pays up for the rest. The story over the next few years will be written in three things — the trajectory of cigarette taxation and illicit trade (gradual or punitive?), the FMCG margin and profit ramp (does the second engine finally matter?), and capital allocation (more value-unlock and cash returns?). For income investors, the ~5% dividend pays you to wait for the answers; for growth investors, the cage is the catch.
