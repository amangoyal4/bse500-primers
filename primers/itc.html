ITC Limited, the cigarette cash machine going FMCG

A complete walk through India's most profitable consumer company — how a colonial tobacco firm turned its hugely cash-generative cigarette near-monopoly into Aashirvaad atta, Sunfeast biscuits and Bingo chips; why a relentless tax screw both funds the government and caps the stock; why ITC spun off its hotels and lost its old foreign veto-holder; and why a business earning ~39% returns on capital still trades cheaply on a ~5% dividend yield.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

ITC Limited is, on paper, a diversified conglomerate — cigarettes, packaged foods, personal care, paperboards and agri-business — but in profit terms it is still, overwhelmingly, a cigarette company that is patiently building a large FMCG business with the cash it throws off. In FY26 the cigarette segment alone earned ₹21,051 crore of segment profit — roughly 80% of ITC's total segment profit — which funds everything else, from Aashirvaad atta to its paperboard mills to one of the largest dividend cheques on the Indian market.

Five facts frame everything that follows:

  • An extraordinary cash engine. FY26 net revenue was ₹78,868 Cr (gross consolidated revenue ₹89,913 Cr, +10.1%) and continuing-operations net profit ₹21,018 Cr, at a ~35% operating margin and a remarkable ~39% return on capital employed. Few large companies anywhere earn returns this high.
  • Cigarettes are both the strength and the cage. It is a near-monopoly of India's legal market with huge margins and pricing power — but it is taxed ferociously. A major overhaul effective 1 February 2026 moved cigarettes to a 40% GST slab plus a revived per-stick excise; total tax can now reach 65–75% of the retail price. That tax is the master variable for the entire company.
  • The diversification is real and inflecting. The non-cigarette FMCG-Others business (foods, snacks, personal & home care, stationery) crossed ₹37,000 Cr of annual consumer spend, grew revenue ~10–11% and — crucially — expanded its margin ~145 bps in FY26 with segment profit up 14%. The "build" phase is turning into a "profit" phase.
  • The portfolio just got simpler. ITC demerged its hotels business (ITC Hotels listed Jan 2025); ITC now holds only ~40% of the listed hotel company and it is no longer a consolidated segment — a deliberate move to a more capital-light, higher-return core.
  • It's an income stock with no promoter. ITC pays out most of its profit at a ~5% dividend yield; it has no controlling family; and its old foreign anchor, British American Tobacco (BAT), has cut its stake to ~22.9%, slipping below the 25% level that gave it a veto — a known overhang and a quiet shift in ITC's power structure.
In plain terms

Think of ITC as a money-printing cigarette business wearing a consumer-goods coat. Selling cigarettes in India is wildly profitable — ITC dominates it, and demand barely flinches at price hikes — but the government takes the lion's share through taxes and raises them most years, so the cigarette engine can't grow fast. ITC's long game is to channel that reliable cash into everyday products — flour, biscuits, chips, soap, incense sticks, notebooks — building a second engine that the market will value more highly and that isn't taxed to death. Meanwhile it hands shareholders a fat dividend and has trimmed its sprawl (out went the hotels). The catch: as long as cigarettes are the profit, ITC carries a tax sword over its head and an ESG stigma that keeps its price low — which is also, for income and value investors, precisely the appeal.

01

The cigarette engine: how ITC really earns

One product line generates ~80% of segment profit — here's the economics of why it's so powerful.

To understand ITC you must first understand its cigarette business, because that is where the profit lives. It is one of the great consumer franchises in India: a dominant share of the legal cigarette market, iconic brands (Gold Flake, Classic, Navy Cut, Wills), and pricing power so strong that ITC can pass on big tax hikes and still hold its volumes. In FY26 the segment produced ₹37,100 Cr of net revenue (+13.7%) and ₹21,051 Cr of segment profit (+5.1%) at margins around 55% of net sales — the engine for the whole group.

Why the economics are so good

The four pillars of the cigarette profit machine
FeatureWhat it means — and why it matters
A legal near-monopolyITC controls the large majority (commonly estimated ~75–80%) of India's legal cigarette market. Decades-old brand loyalty, an unmatched distribution reach, and the fact that tobacco advertising is banned (so incumbents can't be out-marketed) make this position almost impossible to dislodge. New entrants can't advertise their way in.
Inelastic demandSmoking is a habit. In the short run smokers are loyal and relatively price-insensitive, so ITC can raise prices to offset tax increases without losing much volume. That is the single mechanic that lets profit grow despite a rising tax burden.
PremiumisationBeyond raw volume, ITC steadily shifts smokers up to premium, king-size and capsule variants that carry a higher price per stick. This "mix" effect quietly lifts revenue and margin per cigarette even when stick volumes are flattish — a hidden tailwind.
Low capital intensityCigarettes need little ongoing capital — the factories are built, the brands are made. So almost all the profit converts to free cash flow, which is what funds FMCG investment and the dividend. This is why ITC's return on capital is ~39%.
The pass-through model — and its limit

Here is the core mechanic that powers ITC. When the government raises cigarette tax, ITC usually doesn't absorb it — it raises the retail price to pass the tax on to smokers, protecting its own margin. Because demand is inelastic, moderate hikes barely dent volumes, so profit keeps compounding. But there is a ceiling. If taxes (and therefore prices) rise too far, too fast, two bad things happen at once: some smokers cut back or quit, and — far more damaging — many switch to cheap illegal cigarettes that pay no tax. India's illicit-cigarette trade is already one of the largest in the world (by industry estimates roughly a quarter of consumption), and it is the real competitor for ITC's volume. So the cigarette engine is wonderful within a band of taxation and dangerous beyond it. The entire ITC investment debate reduces to one question: does the government tax gradually (good — ITC keeps compounding) or shock the market (bad — volumes fall and the illicit trade grows)? That tension is the subject of §03.

One nuance worth holding onto: ITC's reported cigarette revenue figure can jump or fall sharply purely because of accounting for excise duty. After the February 2026 tax change, more of the tax is presented "gross" on the revenue line, which is why FY26 gross cigarette revenue (~₹40,600 Cr) looks like it grew far faster than the underlying business. Always look at the net-of-tax revenue (₹37,100 Cr) and the segment profit (₹21,051 Cr) for the true picture — the headline gross numbers are distorted by tax mechanics.

02

The conglomerate map

Four continuing businesses — one provides the cash, three spend or diversify it (hotels having been spun off).

ITC reports across several segments, but the way to read them is by their role, not their revenue. Cigarettes generate the cash. FMCG-Others is the big diversification bet, soaking up that cash to build a tobacco-free future. Paperboards and Agri are solid, lower-margin industrial businesses that add scale and feed the supply chain. Hotels used to be the fifth segment — capital-hungry and low-return — but ITC demerged it in 2025 (see §05), so it is no longer consolidated.

03

The taxation master-variable

The single force that governs ITC's profits, volumes and valuation — read this closely.

No company in this primer series is as defined by government taxation as ITC. Cigarettes are a "sin good", and the Indian state taxes them ferociously and somewhat unpredictably — total tax can run to 65–75% of the retail price. Every Union Budget and every GST Council meeting is a potential shock. In February 2026 a sweeping overhaul reshaped the whole tax structure, and it is the defining event of ITC's current chapter.

The Feb 2026 tobacco tax overhaul
ChangeWhat happened
GST raised to 40%Cigarettes (and most tobacco) moved to the new top 40% "demerit/sin" GST slab, up from the old 28%.
Compensation cess abolishedThe old GST compensation cess on tobacco was scrapped (effective 31 Jan 2026) — that cess was being wound down across the economy.
…replaced by a per-stick exciseIn its place came a revived, fixed Additional Excise Duty of roughly ₹2.05–₹8.50 per stick (by cigarette length/type), plus a switch to valuing GST on the printed retail sale price (MRP/RSP) rather than the lower factory price.
Why the RSP switch stingsCharging 40% GST on the retail price rather than the wholesale price sharply increases the tax base. And critically, the new excise cannot be offset against input GST credit — it's a hard cash cost per stick.
Net effectAn "unprecedented" rise in tax incidence (ITC's own words). Companies have been raising prices to pass it on; total tax incidence climbs toward the top of the 65–75% range.
Why taxation is the whole ball game

Taxation governs three things at once for ITC. (1) Volumes: sharp price hikes (to pass on tax) can suppress demand and, worse, push smokers to illicit cigarettes — which already account for a large slice of the market and pay no tax, directly stealing ITC's volume. (2) Profit: the pass-through model works for gradual hikes but breaks if a shock forces ITC to choose between protecting margin and protecting volume. (3) Valuation: the uncertainty itself — never knowing when the next big hike lands — is why ITC trades at a discount to other consumer giants despite superior returns. The reassuring history is that India has mostly favoured stable, moderate taxation (it doesn't want to kill the golden goose, lose revenue, or fuel a smuggling economy), and ITC has compounded through decades of tax hikes. But every overhaul, like Feb 2026's, reopens the question — and the stock reacts violently to tax news.

ITC's own warning: the company repeatedly flags that steep, sudden tax increases backfire — they grow the illegal-cigarette trade (which hurts government revenue and ITC) rather than reducing smoking. ITC has argued for a long period of tax stability so legal volumes can recover share from the illicit market. The bull case assumes policymakers keep taxation gradual from here; the bear case is a punitive shock that breaks the pass-through model and accelerates the shift to illicit cigarettes and other nicotine products.
04

FMCG-Others: the second engine ITC is building

"Cigarettes fund FMCG" — slowly profitable, strategically vital, and finally inflecting on margin.

ITC's long-term answer to the cigarette cage is its non-cigarette FMCG business — a portfolio of everyday Indian brands built with cigarette cash over more than two decades. In FY26 it crossed ₹37,000 crore of annual consumer spend, the FMCG-Others segment posted revenue of ~₹24,210 Cr (+10.1%), and — the part that matters most — segment profit rose 14.1% to ₹1,803 Cr on a ~145 bps EBITDA-margin expansion. This is the clearest path to a future that doesn't depend on tobacco.

Why the FMCG story matters — and why it's slow

The strategic logic is compelling: ITC takes the reliable cash from cigarettes and builds non-tobacco brands that (a) diversify away from tax risk, (b) carry no ESG stigma, and (c) the market values at far higher multiples than tobacco profit. The progress is real — ITC is already a top-tier Indian foods player, and crucially its FMCG margins are now expanding as scale, premiumisation and cost programmes kick in, moving the business from "land-grab" mode toward genuine profitability (the FMCG EBITDA margin crossed ~8% in FY26, with analysts modelling low-teens by FY28). The frustration is that it's slow and capital-intensive: building distribution and brand against entrenched giants (HUL, Nestlé, Britannia) takes decades and heavy ad-spend, and FMCG-Others still contributes only ~9% of profit despite ~30% of revenue. The bull case is that this margin inflection compounds for years and the segment eventually rivals cigarettes in profit; the bear case is that it never grows large or profitable enough to truly free ITC from tobacco.

The "why now" on margins is worth spelling out. A new consumer brand starts unprofitable — you spend on advertising, trade schemes and distribution long before you earn the volumes back. ITC spent the 2000s and 2010s in that loss-leading "build" phase across category after category. The inflection happens when brands reach the scale where fixed costs are spread thin, the supply chain is integrated (cheaper inputs via agri), and the portfolio premiumises — at which point each extra rupee of sales drops far more to the bottom line. FY26's +145 bps is the visible signal that several of ITC's food brands have crossed that scale threshold at once. If it persists, the FMCG segment's profit could begin to grow much faster than its revenue.

05

The hotels demerger

A capital-allocation milestone — and the reason the headline FY25→FY26 profit comparison looks bizarre.

For decades investors complained that ITC's capital-hungry, low-return hotels business was a poor use of cigarette cash. ITC answered them: it demerged its hotels arm into a separately-listed company, ITC Hotels Limited (demerger effective 1 January 2025; the shares listed on the NSE and BSE on 29 January 2025). Existing ITC shareholders received 1 ITC Hotels share for every 10 ITC shares they held, and ITC itself retained ~40% (≈39.88%) of the new company. Hotels is therefore no longer a consolidated ITC segment — ITC now carries it as an associate stake, not as an operating business.

Why the demerger was smart — and the FY26 optical "crash"

Hotels are asset-heavy and low-return — the opposite of ITC's high-margin core — so housing them inside ITC dragged down the group's overall return on capital and consumed cash that could have funded FMCG or dividends. Spinning hotels off does two good things: it lets the market value the hotel assets separately (often at a richer multiple than they earned buried inside ITC), and it leaves the remaining ITC more capital-light, higher-return and simpler to analyse — exactly what shareholders had asked for. The accounting wrinkle to know: the demerger created a large one-off accounting gain in FY25, inflating that year's reported profit to ~₹35,052 Cr. So when Q4 FY26 profit came in at ₹5,470 Cr it looked like a ~72% YoY crash — but that is an illusion created by comparing against the demerger-inflated base. On a clean, like-for-like continuing-operations basis, FY26 PAT (₹21,018 Cr) actually rose ~5% over FY25's continuing PAT (~₹20,036 Cr). Always compare ITC on continuing operations, never the headline.

What it means for capital allocation and the SOTP

The demerger reframed ITC as a "sum of the parts" story. Holding ~40% of separately-listed ITC Hotels gives ITC a marketable financial asset whose value is now visible to the market rather than hidden. More importantly, it signalled a more shareholder-friendly capital-allocation philosophy: focus capital on the high-return cigarette and FMCG core, return more cash as dividends, and unlock value from non-core assets by listing them. The open questions in the re-rating thesis are how ITC treats its other non-core pieces over time (its sizeable IT-services arm, ITC Infotech, is sometimes floated as a future unlock), and what it eventually does with its retained 40% hotels stake — hold it as a financial investment, or distribute/monetise it. Note too that BAT, which received ITC Hotels shares in the demerger, has already begun selling down that hotels stake (a ~9% block in December 2025) — a reminder that "unlocked" value can also be a source of share supply.

The pattern to watch: the hotels demerger is the clearest evidence ITC's capital allocation has turned shareholder-friendly — high-return core, generous cash returns, value-unlock from non-core. Whether management extends that discipline (and how generously it keeps returning cash) is central to whether ITC's perennial valuation discount ever narrows.
06

Agri-business & Paperboards

The steady, lower-profile B2B engines — one is the supply backbone for the food brands, the other a cyclical industrial.

Beyond cigarettes and FMCG, ITC runs two substantial business-to-business operations that provide scale, stability and — in agri's case — a strategic supply backbone for the foods business. Neither will drive the stock, but both make ITC a more resilient, vertically-integrated whole than a pure consumer company.

Agri-business — the rural sourcing backbone

ITC's Agri-business sources, trades and adds value to farm commodities — wheat, rice, spices, leaf tobacco, coffee, and a growing "value-added" export portfolio. In FY26 it generated revenue of ~₹20,296 Cr (+2.7%) and segment profit of ~₹1,496 Cr (+1.2%). It is lower-margin and commodity-linked, and FY26 was a choppy year — Q4 revenue actually fell ~16% on US tariff measures, domestic stock limits and export restrictions on agro-commodities, plus a high prior-year base. But agri's real importance is strategic, not financial: it is the supply chain that feeds ITC's packaged-foods business. ITC's famous e-Choupal network — internet kiosks and aggregation points connecting directly to millions of farmers — gives ITC direct, large-scale, traceable farm sourcing. That underpins the cost, quality and consistency of Aashirvaad wheat, the spices in its blends, and much else. In effect, agri is the wholesale layer that makes ITC's foods business cheaper and more reliable than rivals who buy everything on the open market.

Paperboards, Paper & Packaging — the cyclical, capital-heavy arm

ITC is a leading Indian maker of paperboard, specialty paper and packaging, supplying both its own products (cigarette packs, Classmate notebooks, FMCG cartons) and external customers. In FY26 the segment did revenue of ~₹8,766 Cr (+4.1%), but it is a genuinely cyclical industrial business whose profit swings with input costs and global supply. FY26 was a tale of two halves: the first half was squeezed by cheap Chinese and Indonesian board imports and elevated domestic wood (pulp) costs, pushing segment profit down; the second half recovered — Q4 segment results rose ~21% YoY — helped by a government Minimum Import Price (MIP) on virgin multi-layer paperboard (effective 22 August 2025) that curbed dumping, plus moderating wood prices and strong specialty-paper demand (décor paper). It is a solid through-cycle business, but it is the most margin-sensitive and capital-intensive part of ITC, and a hostage to commodity prices and trade policy.

How to weigh these two

Neither paperboards nor agri is a glamour business, and together they're a modest share of profit. But they matter for two reasons: stability and integration. They give ITC scale and diversification beyond branded consumer products, and agri in particular is a real competitive asset — direct, large-scale farm sourcing is hard to replicate and underpins the economics of the food brands. The watch-items are different for each: for paperboards, watch global board prices, Chinese/Indonesian import volumes and trade barriers (the MIP); for agri, watch export policy, monsoon and commodity cycles. Don't expect either to move the share price; do recognise they make the whole more durable.

07

The company, its owners, and the BAT overhang

A professionally-run company with no promoter — and a foreign tobacco giant quietly heading for the exit.

ITC is unusual among India's giants: it is a professionally-managed company with no promoter family. Its single largest shareholder is British American Tobacco (BAT) — a legacy of ITC's 1910 origins as the "Imperial Tobacco Company of India" — but BAT has been cutting its stake, and in 2025 it fell to ~22.9%, slipping below the 25% threshold that gave it a veto over special resolutions. The rest is held by Indian institutions (LIC and others), foreign funds and the public. ITC is, in effect, a widely-held, board-run institution.

Timeline

  • 1970s

  • 1990s–2000s
    Builds agri-business (the famed e-Choupal farmer network) and launches packaged foods — Aashirvaad atta, Sunfeast biscuits — the FMCG journey begins.
  • 2010s
    Scales FMCG hard: Bingo, Yippee, Fiama, Engage, Savlon, Classmate — building a portfolio of household brands with cigarette cash.
  • Mar 2024
    BAT begins selling down — trims ~3.5% to fund its own share buyback, the start of a multi-year reduction.
  • Jan 2025
    ITC Hotels demerged and listed (effective 1 Jan; listed 29 Jan) — a landmark capital-allocation and value-unlock move (see §05).
  • May 2025
    A further BAT sale takes its ITC holding to ~22.9%, below 25% — BAT loses its veto rights over special resolutions.
  • Feb 2026
    Major tobacco tax overhaul — cigarettes shift to a 40% GST slab and a new per-stick excise; compensation cess abolished (see §03).
  • FY26
    Continuing-ops profit ₹21,018 Cr; FMCG consumer spend tops ₹37,000 Cr; total dividend ₹14.50/share.

Ownership (Mar 2026)

  • Domestic institutions ~49%: LIC (the largest single holder after BAT, ~15–16%), SUUTI, mutual funds and insurers — ITC is a core income holding for Indian institutions, which gives the register a stable, yield-oriented base.
  • Foreign portfolio investors ~35%: this figure includes BAT's ~22.9% strategic stake; the rest is global funds. Excluding BAT, "free-float" foreign ownership is much smaller.
  • Public ~16%: retail and other holders — ITC has one of the largest retail shareholder bases in India (millions of holders), reflecting its dividend appeal.
  • No promoter: with no controlling family, the board and professional management run the company. Governance is institutional rather than founder-led — a double-edged trait (no entrenchment, but also no single owner-operator with skin in the game).
The BAT overhang explained: BAT is a known seller. It has trimmed its ITC stake repeatedly — partly to fund its own buyback and debt reduction — and having dropped below 25% it has signalled it no longer needs "strategic control". That creates a persistent overhang: the market knows further block sales are possible, which caps the share price near-term. The flip side (see §08): a clean, well-absorbed selldown could eventually remove the overhang and broaden the shareholder base — turning a negative into a positive once it's behind the stock.
08

The dividend machine & the tobacco discount

Why a ~39%-return business trades cheap — and why income investors love it precisely because of that.

Here is ITC's central valuation paradox. It earns a ~29% return on equity and ~39% return on capital employed — numbers most companies would kill for — and converts almost all its profit into cash. Yet it trades at a modest ~17× earnings, far below lower-returning consumer peers (HUL, Nestlé and Britannia routinely trade at 40–60×). Three forces explain the discount, and the third is exactly why a different set of investors find ITC irresistible.

Why ITC is cheap — and who that suits
ForceEffect on the stock
The ESG / tobacco stigmaA large and growing pool of global and domestic funds cannot or will not own tobacco on ESG mandates. That permanently shrinks the buyer pool and caps the multiple — regardless of how good the underlying business is.
The tax uncertaintyThe standing risk of a punitive tax shock (see §03) means the market refuses to pay a full "consumer-staples" multiple for the cigarette profit — it discounts that earnings stream for regulatory risk.
The BAT overhangBAT's ~22.9% stake is a known potential seller (see §07). The prospect of further block sales keeps a lid on the price and adds an "exit-supply" risk that growth stocks don't carry.
…but a ~5% dividend yieldThe flip side and the attraction: ITC pays out the great majority of its profit (FY26 dividend ₹14.50/share — ₹6.50 interim + ₹8.00 final), giving a ~5% yield that grows. That's a rare, dependable income stream that makes ITC a staple of yield-seeking and retiree portfolios.
The discount as a feature, not just a bug

For a growth investor, ITC's stigma and tax overhang are reasons to stay away. For an income or value investor, they're the opportunity: you get a dominant, cash-gushing, ~39%-return business at a low multiple, paying a ~5% dividend that grows, with free optionality if the FMCG business re-rates, if the hotels/IT stakes get monetised, or if the tobacco stigma ever eases. The dividend matters mechanically here — because cigarettes need little reinvestment, ITC can return most of its cash without starving growth, so the yield is sustainable, not a sign of distress. ITC is, in short, the market's clearest example of a wonderful business with a permanently capped multiple — which is precisely why it's a perennial debate between "cheap for a reason" and "too cheap to ignore". And as the saying goes for this stock: the dividend pays you to wait for the argument to resolve.

09

The industry and the rivals

Near-unchallenged in cigarettes; a determined, well-funded challenger in FMCG.

ITC competes in two very different arenas with two completely different playbooks. In cigarettes it is dominant, with only small legal rivals and one large illegal one. In FMCG it is the ambitious challenger taking on India's consumer-goods titans.

The structural backdrop: in cigarettes, ITC's enemy isn't a competitor — it's taxation and the illicit trade it fuels. The legal market is a fortress ITC defends. In FMCG, ITC is the well-funded outsider trying to out-distribute and out-innovate India's best consumer companies; its agri backbone, paperboard integration and cigarette cash give it staying power, but share gains against a HUL or Nestlé come slowly and expensively. The two arenas demand opposite strategies — defend a fortress on one side, storm the castle on the other — and ITC has to do both at once.
10

Financials & valuation

Exceptional returns and cash generation — at a permanently modest multiple. Read profit on a continuing-ops basis.

ITC's financials are the picture of a cash machine: high margins, very high returns on capital, low capital intensity and enormous free cash flow paid out as dividends. The numbers are pristine; the valuation is deliberately cautious. The one thing you must do is read profit on a continuing-operations basis, because the FY25 hotels-demerger one-off badly distorts the headline year-on-year comparison.

How to think about the valuation

ITC is the market's purest "great-business, cheap-multiple" debate. On the numbers — ~39% ROCE, ~35% operating margin, huge free cash flow, ~5% yield — it screams quality and value. The ~17× multiple exists because of the tobacco stigma, the tax uncertainty and the BAT overhang (§08), not because the business is weak. The bull case is a re-rating: if the FMCG business keeps inflating margins and growing into a real second profit engine, if capital allocation stays shareholder-friendly (more value-unlock, more cash returns), or if the BAT overhang clears, a high-return company on 17× has obvious upside. The bear case is that the discount is permanent and deserved — Western-style structural decline in tobacco eventually reaches India, taxes keep ratcheting up, and ESG exclusion never lifts — so ITC stays a cheap dividend stock that compounds only modestly. Either way, the dividend pays you to wait for the answer.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A superb cash machine, caged by tax and stigma — and paying you to hold it while the second engine builds.

ITC is one of India's highest-quality, highest-return businesses: a fortress cigarette franchise funding a credible, now margin-inflecting FMCG build-out, plus a generous dividend and a newly simplified portfolio. The investment debate is almost entirely about the tax/regulatory cage and the valuation discount — not the quality of the cash generation, which is not in doubt.

The bull case

  • Cash-machine core — dominant legal cigarettes with proven pricing power, ~39% ROCE, huge free cash flow.
  • FMCG inflection — a ₹37,000 Cr+ non-tobacco business with margins now expanding (+145 bps) and re-rating optionality.
  • Shareholder-friendly turn — the hotels demerger plus a ~5% dividend yield signal better, cleaner capital allocation.
  • Cheap for the quality — ~17× earnings for a ~29% ROE business leaves clear room if any part of the discount narrows.
  • BAT overhang can clear — once BAT's selldown is absorbed, a known negative could flip to a positive (broader float).
  • Defensive & integrated — staples demand and an agri/paperboard supply backbone make it a low-volatility compounder.

The bear case

  • Tax-shock risk — the Feb 2026 overhaul and future hikes can suppress volumes and feed the illicit trade.
  • Structural cigarette decline — long-run tobacco volumes face health/ESG headwinds; the engine can't grow forever.
  • Slow FMCG payoff — non-cigarette profit is still only ~9% of the total; the second engine may never fully free ITC from tobacco.
  • Permanent discount — ESG exclusion plus the BAT overhang may keep the multiple low indefinitely.
  • BAT supply — further block sales (in ITC and in ITC Hotels) can pressure the price near-term.
  • Cyclical drags — paperboards and agri margins swing with commodities, imports and trade/export policy.
The bottom line

ITC is a wonderful business wearing a regulatory ball-and-chain: a dominant, ultra-cash-generative cigarette franchise, a genuine and finally-profitable FMCG second act, two steady B2B arms, a simpler post-demerger structure, and a balance sheet that returns most of its cash to shareholders. The business quality is not the question; the questions are how hard the government taxes the goose, and whether the market ever pays up for the rest. The story over the next few years will be written in four things — the trajectory of cigarette taxation and the illicit trade (gradual or punitive?), the FMCG margin and profit ramp (does the second engine finally matter to the bottom line?), capital allocation (more value-unlock and cash returns?), and the BAT selldown (overhang or, once cleared, relief). For income and value investors, the ~5% dividend pays you to wait for those answers; for growth investors, the cage is the catch.
