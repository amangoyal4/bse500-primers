00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

IndiGo — run by the listed company InterGlobe Aviation Limited — is India's dominant airline, carrying roughly three of every five domestic passengers (~63% market share). It is a textbook low-cost carrier (LCC): a young, near-uniform fleet of Airbus A320-family jets flown hard, at low cost, at high fill rates, to as many places as possible. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) it flew a record 123.4 million passengers and booked ~₹89,513 crore of total income — the first Indian airline past the US$10 billion revenue mark.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Dominant, but in a brutal industry. IndiGo owns the Indian sky — the largest Airbus A320neo-family operator in the world, ~63% of domestic seats, 400-plus aircraft. Yet airlines are notoriously low-margin and cyclical, and FY26 proved it: despite record scale, IndiGo reported a net loss of ~₹2,394 crore (versus a ₹7,258 crore profit the year before) — a swing driven not by the flying business but by the rupee and one-off charges.
  • The biggest order book in aviation history. IndiGo has ~900+ aircraft on firm order — including a record 500-jet A320neo-family order (June 2023), 69 long-range A321XLRs, and 60 A350-900 widebodies — enough to keep growing for a decade-plus and to lock in its domestic lead while it pushes abroad.
  • Fuel and forex are the profit dial. Jet fuel is IndiGo's single biggest cost (~30% of the total), and it is priced in dollars; so are aircraft leases and much of maintenance. Revenue is mostly in rupees. When crude spikes or the rupee weakens, profits can vanish — which is exactly what turned a record operating year into a reported loss in FY26.
In plain terms

Think of IndiGo as the Maruti of Indian skies — it wins the same way a mass-market champion does: relentless low cost, huge scale, wide reach and disciplined execution, all aimed at the value-conscious middle. It flies one basic type of plane, keeps it in the air more hours than almost anyone, fills more of the seats, and charges fares low enough to keep rivals out and Indians choosing planes over trains. The catch is the industry itself: airlines earn a few rupees of profit on every hundred of revenue, and a bad fuel or currency year wipes that out. So the whole investment story is one tension — a genuinely great operator inside a genuinely terrible industry, now trying to turn its domestic monopoly-like grip into a global network before the cheap-money era of endless growth is tested by cost, currency and competition.

01

Airline / LCC economics 101: how an airline earns

No finance degree required — just the handful of numbers that decide whether an airline lives or dies.

An airline sells one thing: a seat on a flight, which vanishes the instant the door closes. An empty seat can never be sold again — the "inventory" is perishable to the second. That single fact, layered on enormous fixed costs (planes, crew, fuel commitments, airport slots), makes airlines one of the hardest businesses on earth to run at a profit. The whole discipline reduces to a few metrics built around one unit: the Available Seat Kilometre (ASK) — one seat flown one kilometre.

The airline metrics that matter (and IndiGo's FY26 numbers)
MetricWhat it means — and IndiGo FY26
ASKAvailable Seat Kilometres — total capacity (seats × distance flown). The denominator for almost everything. IndiGo FY26: 172.4 billion, up 9.5%.
RPKRevenue Passenger Kilometres — capacity actually sold (paying passengers × distance). IndiGo FY26: 145.5 billion.
Load factorRPK ÷ ASK — the fill rate, how full the planes fly. An LCC lives or dies here. IndiGo FY26: 84.4% (down 1.6 pts) — very high by global standards.
YieldAverage fare revenue per passenger-kilometre — how much each sold seat-km earns. IndiGo FY26: ₹5.06 per km.
RASKRevenue per Available Seat Kilometre — total revenue ÷ ALL capacity (sold or not). The true "revenue engine" number. IndiGo FY26: ₹4.99.
CASKCost per Available Seat Kilometre — total cost ÷ ALL capacity. IndiGo FY26: ₹5.00.
The spreadRASK − CASK is the airline's profit per unit of capacity. In FY26 IndiGo's was roughly flat-to-slightly-negative (₹4.99 vs ₹5.00) — which is precisely why a record year still ended in a loss.
The whole business in one sentence

An airline's profit is (RASK − CASK) × ASK — the spread between what each seat-kilometre earns and what it costs, multiplied by how much flying you do. That spread is usually measured in paise, not rupees: IndiGo earned about ₹4.99 per available seat-km and spent about ₹5.00 — meaning the entire fate of a ₹90,000-crore company hinges on a gap of a fraction of a rupee per seat-km. Move fuel a bit, move the currency a bit, discount fares a bit, and that wafer-thin spread flips from positive to negative. This is why airlines are famous for huge revenues and tiny (or vanishing) margins — and why cost discipline, not glamour, is the only durable edge.

Why airlines are notoriously low-margin and cyclical

  • Perishable inventory + high fixed costs. Most of a flight's cost is committed before a single ticket sells (fuel, crew, lease, landing fees). So the airline is desperate to fill the last seats — and will discount to do it, which crushes yield.
  • Fuel is a third of the cost and out of your control. Jet fuel (ATF) tracks global crude and is dollar-priced. A carrier can hedge partially, but a sustained oil spike hits every airline at once.
  • Demand is cyclical and shock-prone. Air travel booms in good times and collapses in recessions, pandemics and geopolitical shocks — while the planes (and their lease payments) stay on the books regardless.
  • Capacity discipline is everything. When airlines over-order aircraft and flood the market with seats, fares collapse and everyone loses money. When they hold back, fares firm and margins recover. India's history is littered with airlines (Kingfisher, Jet Airways, GoFirst) that grew too fast and died.
"How do you make a small fortune in the airline business? Start with a large one." — an old aviation joke that is also the industry's actual track record.
02

IndiGo's low-cost moat: one plane, flown hard, at low cost

The five deliberate design choices that make IndiGo structurally cheaper to run than any rival.

If airlines mostly lose money, why has IndiGo made so much of it for so long? Because it engineered its cost base to be the lowest in India — and in an industry where the profit is the thin spread between RASK and CASK (Section 01), the low-cost operator wins by default. IndiGo's CASK-ex-fuel-ex-forex was just ₹3.00 in FY26 — the number management obsesses over, because it strips out the two things it can't control (fuel and currency) to reveal the cost it can. Five design choices drive it.

The five levers, explained

  • One aircraft type (the A320 family). IndiGo flies overwhelmingly Airbus A320neo, A321neo and A321XLR jets (plus small ATR turboprops for regional routes). A single family means one pool of spare parts, one set of pilot and engineer training, one maintenance procedure, one simulator, interchangeable crews and planes. This "fleet commonality" is the biggest single cost lever in low-cost aviation — it slashes training, inventory and downtime costs that airlines with mixed fleets carry.
  • A young fleet. New-generation A320neo ("new engine option") jets burn ~15–20% less fuel than older planes and need far less maintenance. A young average fleet age means lower fuel bills and fewer heavy checks — directly lowering CASK.
  • High aircraft utilisation. A parked plane earns nothing but still costs money. IndiGo keeps its jets flying long days (historically ~13 hours a day), spreading those fixed costs over more flights and passengers. More flying per plane = lower cost per seat-km.
  • Sale-and-leaseback (SLB) financing. IndiGo often buys aircraft at a deep bulk-order discount, then immediately sells them to a leasing company and leases them straight back. This keeps the airline "asset-light" (less debt on the balance sheet), converts the order discount into upfront cash/gains, and shifts residual-value risk to lessors. It has been a genuine profit and cash contributor — though it means most planes are rented, not owned, so lease costs sit in the P&L forever.
  • A lean, no-frills product. Single-class dense cabins, no free meals, quick turnarounds, tight on-time performance, and a growing slice of ancillary revenue (bags, seats, food, cargo — Section 09). This is the classic LCC playbook: strip the base fare to the bone, then charge for extras.
Why the moat is real — and where it's fraying

IndiGo's edge isn't a secret formula; it's relentless execution of the LCC model at Indian scale. Being the lowest-cost operator in a commodity business means it can set the fare floor: it can price low enough to fill planes and still cover its costs, at levels where higher-cost rivals bleed. That's how it drove weaker competitors out and built a ~63% share. But the moat is being tested on two fronts. First, the huge P&W engine grounding (Section 08) forced IndiGo to lease in older, less-efficient jets ("damp leases"), temporarily raising its costs. Second, the push into widebody long-haul (Section 06) deliberately breaks the single-fleet purity — an A350 is a completely different plane needing its own training, parts and crews. Both are managed bets, but each nibbles at the very cost discipline that made IndiGo great.

03

The company and its owners

Two founders, a famous falling-out, and a promoter group in transition.

IndiGo launched in 2006, founded by two men from very different worlds: Rahul Bhatia, whose family runs the InterGlobe travel-and-hospitality group (the "IGE Group"), and Rakesh Gangwal, a veteran US airline executive (ex-US Airways CEO). Bhatia brought the Indian on-the-ground network; Gangwal brought deep airline operating know-how. Together they built the machine — and later fought bitterly over how to run it.

Ownership & the promoter transition

  • Promoter (Bhatia / IGE Group): Rahul Bhatia is the Promoter and Managing Director, holding his stake principally through InterGlobe Enterprises. The Bhatia-aligned promoter block sits in the low-30s percent range (InterGlobe Enterprises trimmed toward ~32% via a planned ~4% sale) — the anchoring long-term owner.
  • Gangwal's phased exit: since 2022, Rakesh Gangwal and his family trust (The Chinkerpoo Family Trust) have steadily sold down a large stake — cumulatively de-classified as a controlling promoter and offloading tens of thousands of crores of shares. This is an ongoing supply overhang on the stock, but each sale also widens the free float and institutional ownership.
  • Institutions & public: the balance is held by domestic and foreign institutions (mutual funds, FPIs, insurers) and retail — a large and growing free float as Gangwal exits.
The governance lens: the Bhatia–Gangwal feud was a genuine risk chapter — two co-founders openly at war rarely helps a company. Its resolution (Gangwal stepping back, Bhatia consolidating control) removed that uncertainty, but Gangwal's continued share sales are a periodic technical drag on the stock. On balance the market has treated the transition as a clearing event: a single, aligned promoter is simpler than a divided one.
04

The mega order book & domestic dominance

~900+ aircraft on order — the biggest bet in aviation, and the guarantee of IndiGo's future scale.

IndiGo's single most defining strategic fact is its order book: the largest in commercial-aviation history. It has roughly 900+ aircraft on firm order — headlined by a record 500-jet A320neo-family order placed in June 2023 (worth ~US$55 billion at list price, deliveries running into the mid-2030s), on top of earlier orders. This is not vanity; it is the mechanism by which IndiGo guarantees itself capacity, keeps its fleet young, and pre-empts rivals in a country where aircraft are scarce and delivery slots are booked years out.

Why a giant order book is a competitive weapon, not just a shopping list

In aviation, you can't grow faster than you can get aircraft — and the world's plane-makers (Airbus, Boeing) are sold out years in advance. By locking in 900+ jets, IndiGo has effectively reserved most of India's near-term aircraft supply for itself, starving rivals of the metal they'd need to challenge it and guaranteeing its own capacity to ride India's booming travel demand. The bulk order also earns enormous discounts off list price (which feed the sale-and-leaseback gains, Section 02), and steady deliveries keep the fleet young and cheap to run. The flip side: it is a colossal financial commitment — hundreds of jets to be paid for and profitably deployed over a decade, in an industry that can turn on a dime. If Indian demand ever softens or fuel spikes for years, that order book converts from a weapon into an anchor.

05

Fuel & forex: the two swings that whipsaw profits

The reason a record operating year (FY26) still ended in a reported loss.

Here is the FY26 puzzle in one line: IndiGo grew capacity 9.5%, carried a record 123.4 million passengers, and generated ₹15,089 crore of EBITDAR (its core operating cash profit) — yet reported a net loss of ~₹2,394 crore. What went wrong wasn't the flying; it was the two variables an airline cannot control — jet fuel and the currency. Strip out forex and one-off charges, and IndiGo's underlying profit was actually positive at ~₹7,503 crore. The gap between "reported loss" and "underlying profit" is the fuel-and-forex story.

Why fuel (ATF) whipsaws profit

  • It's the biggest single cost. Aviation turbine fuel (ATF) was ~₹25,389 crore in FY26 — roughly 30% of total operating cost. A 10% move in fuel is worth thousands of crores.
  • It tracks global crude and is dollar-priced. IndiGo can't set the oil price; a war, an OPEC decision or a crude spike hits it directly. In India, ATF also carries heavy state taxes, making it dearer than in many countries.
  • Limited ability to pass it on. In a competitive market, raising fares to cover fuel risks losing passengers. So fuel spikes often come straight out of that thin RASK−CASK spread.

Why forex whipsaws profit even harder

  • Costs in dollars, revenue in rupees. Fuel, aircraft leases, spare parts and much maintenance are priced in USD; most tickets are sold in INR. A weaker rupee makes every dollar cost more rupees — squeezing margins on flights already sold.
  • Lease liabilities get "marked to market". IndiGo's aircraft leases sit on the balance sheet as large dollar-denominated liabilities. When the rupee falls, accounting rules force a revaluation loss straight through the P&L — a non-cash hit, but a real reported one. In FY26 this forex effect was on the order of ~₹8,100 crore, the single biggest reason EBITDAR (17.8% reported) was far below the ex-forex figure (27.3%), and the reason the year printed a loss.
How to read past the noise

The crucial skill in reading an airline is separating the operating business from the fuel-and-forex weather. IndiGo's operating performance in FY26 was strong — record traffic, 84.4% load factors, ₹15,089 crore of EBITDAR. But a large, mostly non-cash forex revaluation (the rupee weakening against its dollar lease book) plus new labour-code and disruption charges dragged the reported number into a loss. That's why analysts watch the ex-forex, ex-fuel metrics (like CASK-ex-fuel-ex-fx of ₹3.00) to judge whether the airline itself is getting better or worse — and treat the headline profit/loss as heavily coloured by things outside management's control. The honest takeaway cuts both ways: the FY26 loss overstates how badly the business did, but it's also a real reminder that a rupee slide or oil spike can erase an airline's profit at any time.

The caveat that never goes away: forex revaluation losses can reverse (a stronger rupee would swing them back to gains), and fuel is cyclical. But the structural mismatch — dollar costs vs rupee revenue — is permanent for an Indian airline. It is the single biggest reason airline earnings are so volatile, and why one year's number rarely predicts the next.
06

International & long-haul: the A350 ambition

From a domestic LCC to a global network carrier — the boldest, riskiest leg of the story.

IndiGo conquered India; now it wants the world. Its most ambitious strategic move is a deliberate push up the range curve into international and long-haul flying — a market historically dominated by Gulf carriers (Emirates, Qatar, Etihad) that funnel Indian travellers through Dubai, Doha and Abu Dhabi to Europe and beyond. IndiGo's goal: capture that connecting traffic itself, flying Indians directly to Europe and long-haul destinations, and lift international capacity to ~40% of the total by FY30 (from a smaller share today).

How IndiGo is building the network — in three layers

  • Narrowbody international (now). A320/A321 jets already fly IndiGo across the Gulf, Middle East, Central and Southeast Asia — short- and medium-haul routes an LCC does well. This is the established base of its 45 international destinations.
  • The A321XLR bridge (2026 onward). The extra-long-range A321XLR (first delivered Jan 2026, 69 on order) flies ~8,700 km on single-aisle economics — reaching the fringes of Europe and deeper Asia without the cost of a widebody. It's the pragmatic middle step: LCC costs, long-haul reach.
  • Widebody long-haul (2027 onward) — the A350. The 60 firm A350-900s (deliveries from 2027, with rights on ~70 more) are true long-haul jets aimed at Europe — Delhi/Mumbai/Bengaluru/Hyderabad to London, Paris, Frankfurt, Amsterdam and beyond — even with a premium business-class cabin, a first for IndiGo.

The interim playbook: damp-lease + codeshare

IndiGo can't wait for A350s to arrive, so it is bridging with two clever tactics. First, damp-leasing widebodies — leasing in Boeing 787s (and earlier 777s) with crew from other operators — to launch European routes (Manchester, Amsterdam, with more to come) right now, before it owns a single widebody. Second, codeshares and partnerships with foreign carriers, so an IndiGo passenger can be sold a seamless ticket onward to a global network the airline doesn't yet fly itself. Together these let IndiGo build the brand and the routes on the long-haul map while its own metal is still on order.

Why this is both the biggest opportunity and the biggest risk

The opportunity: India generates enormous international demand that today largely enriches foreign airlines. If IndiGo can fly that traffic itself — non-stop, at lower cost — it taps a market many times larger than domestic India, with higher fares and a structural tailwind (a rising, travelling Indian middle class). The risk: long-haul widebody flying is a different, harder business than domestic LCC — it breaks the single-fleet cost discipline (Section 02), pits IndiGo against battle-hardened global carriers with premium products and loyalty programmes, exposes it to more currency and fuel on ultra-long routes, and has a graveyard of failed "low-cost long-haul" attempts worldwide. IndiGo is doing it carefully (bridge tactics, phased delivery, a modest premium cabin), but this is the leg of the strategy with the widest range of outcomes — genuinely transformational if it works, expensively humbling if it doesn't.

07

The Air India–Vistara duopoly & capacity discipline

India's sky is now essentially a two-horse race — and that's largely good news for both.

India's aviation market has consolidated into a duopoly. On one side, IndiGo with ~63% of domestic seats. On the other, the Tata-owned Air India group — which absorbed Vistara (the Tata–Singapore Airlines full-service carrier) in a November 2024 merger, and also folded in AIX Connect (AirAsia India) — sitting at ~26–27%. Together these two carry ~90% of all domestic passengers. Everyone else (Akasa Air ~5%, SpiceJet ~4%) is a rounding error by comparison.

Why a duopoly is (counter-intuitively) good for IndiGo

India's aviation history is a graveyard of over-competition: too many airlines chasing share with irrational fares, all losing money, until the weak ones (Kingfisher, Jet Airways, GoFirst) collapsed. A consolidated market of two disciplined big players is far healthier. With only one serious rival, both IndiGo and Air India have less incentive to start suicidal fare wars and more ability to match capacity to demand and hold fares at levels that actually cover costs. This "capacity discipline" is the single biggest structural improvement in Indian aviation — and it favours the lowest-cost operator (IndiGo) most of all, because in any pricing standoff IndiGo can undercut and still profit. The one to watch is Air India's turnaround: under Tata, it is spending heavily to modernise its fleet and product. A resurgent, well-run Air India is IndiGo's first genuine full-service rival in years — a real competitive threat, especially on premium and international routes, but still one operating from a higher cost base.

08

The P&W engine-grounding saga

A supplier's manufacturing defect that grounded dozens of IndiGo's jets — history and resolution.

Many of IndiGo's A320neo jets are powered by Pratt & Whitney (P&W) GTF engines (the "geared turbofan"). Beginning in 2023, P&W disclosed a serious defect: contaminated powdered metal used in certain engine parts, requiring hundreds of engines worldwide to be pulled off the wing early for inspection and repair. Because IndiGo operates so many of these engines, it became one of the most affected airlines on earth — at the peak, dozens of its aircraft ("AOG" — aircraft on ground) were parked, waiting for scarce spare engines and slow shop turnaround.

Why it hurt IndiGo specifically

  • Grounded planes still cost money. A parked jet earns nothing but still carries lease, insurance and financing costs — the opposite of IndiGo's high-utilisation cost model (Section 02).
  • It capped growth in a booming market. With aircraft grounded just as Indian demand surged, IndiGo couldn't add the capacity it wanted — leaving fares high but volume constrained.
  • The fix raised costs. To keep flying, IndiGo damp-leased older aircraft (leased-in jets with crew, often older and less fuel-efficient) — a stopgap that pushed up its cost per seat-km precisely when its own young fleet was idled. Partial compensation from P&W softened, but didn't erase, the blow.
  • 2023
    P&W discloses the powder-metal contamination in GTF engines; a global inspection-and-repair programme begins.
  • 2023–24
    IndiGo's grounded-aircraft count climbs; it damp-leases older jets and extends leases to protect capacity, and negotiates compensation from P&W.
  • 2025
    The worst passes: with more repair capacity and spare engines, groundings begin declining; IndiGo starts returning damp-leased jets and normalising the fleet.
  • 2026
    Industry-wide, GTF groundings fall further (RTX/P&W guide to 150+ jets returning to service in 2026); IndiGo's fleet availability improves, though it remains a live watch-item.
The lesson the saga teaches about airlines

The engine crisis is a perfect illustration of a hidden airline risk: concentration on a single supplier or technology. IndiGo's greatest strength — one aircraft type, one dominant engine choice — is also a vulnerability, because when that one thing has a problem, the whole fleet is exposed at once. The good news is that this was a supplier defect, not an IndiGo failure, it came with compensation, and it is now resolving — groundings are falling and the fleet is normalising. The lasting takeaway for a reader: an airline's fate rests partly on things it doesn't build (engines, aircraft, fuel), and IndiGo's scale means any single-point supplier issue lands on it harder than on anyone else. Watch fleet-availability and damp-lease levels as the clean read on how fully this has healed.

09

Ancillary revenue & the value chain

The extras that turn a wafer-thin ticket into a real profit — and the ecosystem around the seat.

A modern LCC doesn't just sell a seat; it sells a base fare plus a menu of paid extras — and those extras are disproportionately profitable. This is ancillary revenue: baggage fees, seat selection, priority boarding, onboard food, and — increasingly important for IndiGo — cargo. Because the plane is flying anyway, most ancillary revenue drops almost straight to the bottom line, cushioning the razor-thin margins on the ticket itself.

The revenue stack — beyond the base fare

  • Passenger fares: the core — the ticket price, driven by yield and load factor.
  • Ancillary (unbundled) revenue: bags, seats, meals, change fees, priority. High-margin, growing faster than fares, and a lever IndiGo pulls to keep headline fares low while protecting profit.
  • Cargo & belly-hold: IndiGo carries freight in the hold (and runs dedicated A321 freighters). A fast-growing, high-margin adjacency as e-commerce and trade expand.
  • Sale-and-leaseback gains & other income: as covered in Section 02, the SLB machine and treasury income on its large cash pile add a further, if lumpy, layer of profit.

Why ancillaries are the quiet margin hero

Imagine two identical flights. On the first, the airline sells only tickets at a fare that just covers cost — it makes nothing. On the second, it sells the same tickets a little cheaper (to fill more seats), then charges for a checked bag, an aisle seat, a sandwich, and packs the hold with cargo. The second flight is more profitable despite lower fares, because those extras cost the airline almost nothing to provide once the plane is flying. That is the entire logic of unbundling: keep the visible fare low to win the price-sensitive Indian traveller, then make the money on the margins. As IndiGo grows ancillary and cargo, it makes its profit less dependent on the volatile fare — a genuine quality-of-earnings improvement, even if fuel and forex still dominate the headline.

How to think about valuing a loss-making airline at ₹2 lakh crore

On paper it looks absurd — a ₹2.1-lakh-crore market value on a company that just lost money, giving a meaningless negative P/E. But the market is looking straight through the FY26 loss for three reasons. (1) The loss was forex-driven and largely non-cash — the underlying operating business was profitable (~₹7,500 Cr ex-forex), so investors value the airline on normalised earnings, not the reported figure. (2) IndiGo is a structural growth story — India's air travel is under-penetrated, IndiGo dominates it, and the order book guarantees a decade of expansion plus the international optionality. (3) The balance sheet is a fortress — a huge cash pile funds growth without dilution. The bear counter: airlines are perennially cyclical and forex-exposed, so "look through the loss" cuts both ways — you can just as easily look through a good year to a bad one. Valuing IndiGo is really a bet on whether its dominance and growth are durable enough to earn a premium the airline industry almost never deserves.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A great operator, a dominant franchise, a brutal industry — priced for the dominance to endure.

IndiGo is one of the world's best-run low-cost airlines, dominant in one of the world's fastest-growing travel markets, with the biggest order book in aviation and a fortress balance sheet. It is also, unavoidably, an airline — cyclical, fuel-and-forex-whipsawed, capital-hungry, and now placing a large, uncertain bet on long-haul flying. The investment debate is whether the quality of the franchise can overcome the cruelty of the industry.

The bull case

  • Structural dominance — ~63% of a booming, under-penetrated market; the lowest cost base in India lets it set the fare floor and out-survive rivals.
  • The mega order book — 900+ aircraft locks in a decade of growth, keeps the fleet young, and starves rivals of scarce aircraft supply.
  • Fortress balance sheet — a ~₹51,650 Cr cash pile funds the entire growth plan without diluting shareholders — rare for any airline.
  • International optionality — the A321XLR + A350 push opens a market many times larger than domestic India, intercepting Gulf-carrier traffic.
  • Duopoly discipline — a consolidated two-player market favours rational fares and the lowest-cost operator most of all.

The bear case

  • Fuel & forex — dollar costs vs rupee revenue can erase profit any year; FY26's loss is the standing proof.
  • Industry cyclicality — air travel is shock-prone (recessions, pandemics, geopolitics); the fixed lease bill never pauses.
  • Long-haul execution risk — widebody flying breaks the single-fleet moat and enters a market littered with failed low-cost long-haul bets.
  • Supplier concentration — the P&W engine saga showed how a single supplier defect can ground a big slice of the fleet at once.
  • Valuation & overhang — a ~₹2 lakh-crore value on a loss-making airline leaves little room for error; Gangwal's continued selling is a technical drag.
The bottom line

IndiGo is the rare airline that has turned an unforgiving industry into a durable, dominant franchise — through obsessive cost discipline, a single-fleet machine, huge scale, and a balance sheet most carriers can only dream of. That is why the market values it as a growth company, not a typical airline, and looks through a forex-driven loss year. The genuine open questions are three: can it hold its cost edge as it adds widebodies and absorbs engine disruptions; can the international/long-haul bet succeed where so many low-cost long-haul attempts have failed; and can fuel and forex stay tame enough for the wafer-thin airline spread to keep printing profits. Get those right and IndiGo compounds India's travel boom for a decade. Get them wrong and it's a reminder that even the best-run airline is still an airline — where a large fortune has a habit of becoming a small one.
