00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Sarda Energy & Minerals Limited (SEML) is a Raipur, Chhattisgarh-based integrated metals-and-power group, controlled by the Sarda family. It makes two families of commodities — ferro alloys (the manganese-based additives that every steelmaker needs) and steel intermediates (iron-ore pellets, sponge iron, billets, wire rod) — and it feeds those plants with things it owns rather than buys: captive coal mines, captive iron ore, and its own electricity from thermal and hydro plants.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is one of India's lowest-cost integrated producers — by design. Ferro alloys and steel are commodities: you are a price-taker, so the only lever you fully control is cost. SEML pulls that lever by owning the inputs — coal from its Gare Palma blocks, captive iron ore, and self-generated power — so its cost sheet sits below rivals who buy grid electricity and open-market ore.
  • The "energy & minerals" in the name is now the bigger earnings engine. This is the surprise: a company most people file under "ferro-alloy/steel" today earns roughly two-thirds of its EBITDA from power and mining, not from selling metal. Record thermal and hydro generation — plus the turnaround of the acquired 600 MW SKS Power plant — is what drove FY26's jump.
  • FY26 was a record year. Revenue rose ~23% to ~₹5,690 Cr; net profit jumped 58% to ~₹1,109 Cr; operating margin was ~31%. The board declared its highest-ever dividend (₹2/share) and greenlit a ₹500 Cr pellet expansion — while laying out a plan to roughly double power capacity toward ~1,720 MW by FY30.
In plain terms

SEML sells two boring, cyclical commodities — alloying additives and semi-finished steel — into a market where it cannot set the price. Its edge is that it has spent two decades buying up the things those factories consume: a coal mine here, an iron-ore lease there, a string of hydro dams, and a rescued thermal power station. So while a rival ferro-alloy maker watches its margin get squeezed every time electricity or ore prices spike, SEML largely pays itself for those inputs. The result is a metals business wrapped around a power-and-mining business — and lately the wrapper has become the more valuable part.

01

Ferro alloys & steel 101: how an integrated metals maker earns

No metallurgy background needed — just the spread and the cost stack.

Both of SEML's core products are commodities sold by the tonne at a price the world sets. You don't win by charging more; you win by making each tonne for less than the next producer. So the whole economics collapse into one idea: the spread — realisation (selling price per tonne) minus conversion cost per tonne — and how much of your cost you can control.

What the two products actually are

  • Ferro alloys — metals like silico-manganese and ferro-manganese, made by smelting manganese ore with a reductant (coke/coal) in an electric-arc furnace. Steelmakers add them in small doses to give steel strength and remove impurities. There is no steel without ferro alloys — but they are a tiny, price-taking slice of the steel supply chain, and the single biggest cost of making them is electricity (smelting is brutally power-hungry).
  • Steel intermediates — SEML doesn't make cars or bridges; it makes the upstream building blocks: iron-ore pellets (compressed ore, the furnace feed), sponge iron / DRI (reduced iron), billets (cast semi-finished bars) and wire rod (long product). Here the biggest costs are iron ore and coal.
The one equation that governs the P&L

Profit per tonne ≈ (selling price) − (ore + coal + power + conversion). Notice what SEML can and can't touch. It can't set the selling price — that's the global alloy/steel market. It can attack almost every cost line: mine its own coal, dig its own ore, and generate its own electricity. In a business where everyone sells at roughly the same price, the low-cost producer keeps making money in the downturns that bankrupt the high-cost ones. That is the entire strategic logic of SEML — and the reason "energy & minerals" is in its name.

Why this is a cyclical, volatile business

Because prices are set globally and inputs move independently, the spread can swing hard from year to year. Manganese-ore prices, coking-coal prices, Chinese steel demand and the rupee all move the numbers without SEML doing anything differently. That is why a metals company can post ₹524 Cr of profit one year (FY24) and ₹1,109 Cr two years later (FY26) with broadly the same plants — the difference is the spread and, increasingly, the power leg.

02

The captive stack: the whole company on one map

Read this once and SEML's structure clicks — it's built as a vertical chain.

Most metals companies buy their raw materials and their electricity from the market. SEML's defining choice is to own each layer beneath the furnace. Trace a tonne of SEML metal backwards and you rarely leave the group: the ore came from its lease, the coal from its mine, and the electricity from its own turbine.

The lower you go in the stack, the more of the cost is captive. Layers 1–2 also earn money on their own account — coal is sold commercially, and power is sold to the grid — which is why the "inputs" have become a profit centre, not just a cost saving.
Why owning the bottom two layers is the whole point

For a rival ferro-alloy maker, electricity and ore are somebody else's profit margin stacked on top of SEML's cost. Every rupee a competitor pays a power utility or an ore trader is a rupee SEML keeps in the family. And because SEML built more coal and power capacity than its own furnaces strictly need, the surplus gets sold — coal commercially, electricity to the grid as merchant power. So the same assets that de-risk the metals business have quietly turned into standalone earners. That dual role — cost shield and profit centre — is what separates SEML from a plain integrated steelmaker.

03

The company and who controls it

A five-decade Raipur industrial house, family-run and closely held.

SEML is a promoter-driven Sarda family business headquartered in Raipur, Chhattisgarh — the heart of India's mineral belt. What began in the early 1970s as a ferro-alloy venture grew, deal by deal, into an integrated steel-and-power group by acquiring coal, ore and electricity assets around its plants. The family still holds roughly 73%, so incentives are tightly aligned — with the usual flip side of a closely held promoter company.

Timeline

  • 1973
    The Sarda family sets up the ferro-alloy business in Raipur — the seed of today's group (SEML is now in its 50-plus-year lineage).
  • 1990s–2000s
    Adds sponge iron, billets and wire rod, plus captive thermal power at Siltara — becoming an integrated steel producer, not just an alloy maker.
  • 2007
    Merges Chhattisgarh Electricity Company (CECL) into itself and re-brands to Sarda Energy & Minerals — codifying the "energy first" strategy in the name.
  • 2010s
    Builds out hydro power (Sikkim, Chhattisgarh, Uttarakhand) and grows the Vizag ferro-alloy plant via subsidiary Sarda Metals & Alloys; pursues captive iron-ore and coal.
  • 2020–21
    Wins the Gare Palma IV/7 coal block in the first commercial-coal auction (66.75% revenue share) and starts production; commissions the 113 MW Sikkim hydro project.
  • Aug 2024
    Acquires bankrupt SKS Power (600 MW thermal) via NCLT for ~₹2,657 Cr — a coal-fired plant sitting right next to its own Chhattisgarh coal.
  • FY26
    Record year: revenue ~₹5,690 Cr, PAT ~₹1,109 Cr (+58%). Commissions the 24.9 MW Rehar hydro (Jul 2025); Supreme Court upholds the SKS bid; announces ₹500 Cr pellet expansion and the road to ~1,720 MW power by FY30.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters (Sarda family): ~73.2% as of mid-2026 — a high, stable holding led by veteran founder-promoter Kamal Kishore Sarda. FIIs and DIIs hold ~3–4% each; the public float is modest.
  • Key subsidiaries: Sarda Metals & Alloys Ltd (the large Visakhapatnam ferro-alloy plant with its own captive power); Chhattisgarh Hydro Power LLP and Sarda Hydro Power (the hydel assets); SKS Power Generation (the 600 MW thermal plant); plus coal and iron-ore mining entities.
  • Balance sheet: gross debt rose to ~₹2,632 Cr after the SKS acquisition, but strong cash generation keeps net leverage low — management describes the group as broadly net-debt-light and funds expansion largely from internal accruals. ROCE ran ~17% and ROE ~16% in FY26.
The promoter question: a ~73% family holding aligns owner and shareholder tightly and has clearly built value — but it also concentrates control, thins the float, and makes minority investors reliant on the family's capital-allocation discipline. So far that discipline (winning coal auctions, buying a distressed 600 MW plant cheaply) has generally added value; it remains the group to watch on related-party dealings and large capex calls.
04

Ferro alloys — the cash core

The original business, and still one of India's larger alloy exporters.

Ferro alloys are where SEML started and where its export franchise lives. It is one of India's larger manufacturers and exporters of manganese-based alloys, run across two plants: the original Siltara (Raipur) furnaces and the bigger Vizag plant under subsidiary Sarda Metals & Alloys. Combined installed furnace capacity is on the order of ~147 MVA (≈45 MVA Siltara + ≈102 MVA Vizag).

Why power is destiny for a ferro-alloy maker

Smelting manganese ore into alloy is one of the most electricity-intensive processes in metals — power can be 40–50% of the cost of a tonne of silico-manganese. So a ferro-alloy plant's competitiveness is set less by clever chemistry than by its electricity bill. This is precisely why SEML bolted captive power onto both alloy plants: when a merchant smelter is paying ₹6–7 a unit for grid power, and you are running on captive generation at a fraction of that, you can keep smelting profitably through price troughs that force higher-cost rivals to idle their furnaces. The alloy business, in other words, is the clearest single demonstration of why the captive stack exists.

Ferro alloys are also a meaningful export line, which brings a currency dimension: a weaker rupee helps realisations, while global manganese-ore prices (largely imported) drive the input side. The segment is cyclical, but as the group's oldest cash generator it anchors the metals half of the business.

05

Steel — from pellet to wire rod

The integrated steel line: semi-finished products, not consumer steel.

SEML's steel business is upstream and intermediate — it converts iron ore into pellets and sponge iron, then into billets and wire rod. It does not chase the crowded, low-margin end of TMT rebar and finished long products at scale; the volumes here are moderate and the point is to run an integrated, low-cost line that captures margin at each conversion step.

The strategic direction is to add pellet capacity (the ₹500 Cr, +1.1 MT expansion approved with the FY26 results, targeted within ~30 months) and to keep moving a little further down the value chain into value-added wire. Pellets matter because they let SEML sell processed ore rather than raw ore, and they improve furnace efficiency across the integrated site.

Read the steel line as the integration showcase, not the headline earner. On its own, semi-finished steel is a thin-margin commodity. What makes SEML's steel profitable is that the ore, the coal and the power under it are captive — so the modest steel volumes convert at a cost structure most standalone re-rollers cannot match. It is the metals business that best proves the moat, even if it is no longer the biggest profit pool.
06

The captive-everything moat: coal, ore & self-made power

The structural low-cost advantage — and the first of the two bespoke cruxes.

Strip SEML down and the durable competitive edge is one thing: it self-supplies the three inputs that decide a metals producer's cost — coal, iron ore and electricity. In a price-taking commodity, that is as close to a moat as the sector offers, because it lets you stay profitable at prices that push open-market producers into the red.

The three captive legs

  • Captive & commercial coal. SEML won Gare Palma IV/7 in the first commercial-coal auction (66.75% revenue share; ~239 MT geological reserve) and has been producing since 2021 — coal used both captively (to fuel its own power and DRI) and sold commercially. It was later declared preferred bidder for Gare Palma IV/5 (25.75% share), and plans to roughly quadruple coal-mining capacity by FY30. Coal is now a genuine earnings driver, not just a cost saving.
  • Captive iron ore. The group holds iron-ore mining interests that feed the pellet/sponge-iron line, insulating the steel business from open-market ore prices.
  • Self-generated power. Captive thermal at Siltara and Vizag, plus ~167 MW of hydro, plus the acquired 600 MW SKS thermal plant. Electricity — the single largest ferro-alloy cost — is largely made in-house.
The device: two power bills for the same furnace

Picture two identical silico-manganese furnaces, one run by SEML and one by a merchant smelter, both selling alloy at the same market price on the same day. The merchant pays perhaps ₹6–7 per unit for grid electricity; SEML draws largely on captive and hydro power at a fraction of that. On a product where power is ~40–50% of cost, that single difference can be the whole margin. When alloy prices fall far enough, the merchant's furnace goes cold and idle — while SEML's keeps running at a profit, and often gains the share the merchant just gave up. The captive stack isn't only a good-times margin booster; it's a bad-times survival mechanism, and that asymmetry is the real moat.

The catch is that captive integration is capital-hungry and regulatory-heavy. Coal blocks come with vesting conditions, revenue-share obligations and environmental clearances; iron-ore leases carry lease-renewal and royalty risk; hydro projects face hydrology and displacement hurdles. The moat is real, but it is earned mine by mine and clearance by clearance — not granted once.

07

The power pivot: dams, a rescued 600 MW plant, and coal across the fence

The second bespoke crux — and where the surprise lives.

Here is the fact that reframes SEML. A company you'd shelve under "ferro alloys and steel" now derives close to two-thirds of its EBITDA from power and mining. The metals plants are still the identity; the electrons and the coal are increasingly the earnings. FY26's record was driven above all by record thermal and hydro generation and the ramp of the acquired SKS plant.

The surprise, stated plainly: coal that never rides a train

The reason the SKS acquisition is more than "a distressed asset bought cheap" is geography. A coal-fired power station's costliest headache is usually getting the coal to the boiler — rail freight, handling, evacuation logistics that can rival the fuel's own price. SKS Power sits next to SEML's Chhattisgarh coal. So the fuel effectively crosses a fence rather than the country. Buy a stranded 600 MW plant for a fraction of build cost, feed it captive coal from across the road, and run it partly for your own factories and partly as merchant power sold to the grid — and a bankrupt liability becomes one of the group's best returns on capital. That "coal across the fence" logic, not the metals cycle, is what re-rated the earnings.

Why hydro is the quiet stabiliser

A ~167 MW fleet of hydroelectric plants is an unusual thing to find inside a ferro-alloy company, and it does something the metals business never can: it throws off stable, low-cost, long-life, near-zero-fuel-cost generation year after year, largely decoupled from the alloy and steel cycle. Hydro is the annuity leg — a counterweight to the swing of commodity spreads — and it is clean power, which helps the group's carbon profile as smelting comes under more scrutiny. Combined with commercial coal and merchant thermal, it turns "inputs" into a diversified profit stream that a pure metals peer simply doesn't have.

The name promised "energy" for twenty years; the FY26 P&L is where it finally became the larger truth.
08

The commodity cycle: prices, China and cyclicality

The force the whole metals half of the business rides.

Ferro alloys and steel are globally cyclical, and the biggest single swing factor is China — the source of roughly half the world's steel and a huge share of manganese-alloy demand and supply. When Chinese construction and steel output are strong, alloy and steel prices firm and SEML's spread widens; when China exports a glut or its demand softens, prices sag and margins compress.

Profit more than doubled from the FY24 trough to FY26 on broadly the same metals capacity — driven by the spread recovering and the power/coal leg scaling up. That is the case for owning the whole stack: it flattens the cycle's floor.

The input side has its own cycle: manganese-ore prices (mostly imported), coking/thermal coal, and the rupee all move independently of selling prices, so the spread can compress from either end. This is what makes headline "record profit" years and "margin squeeze" quarters coexist — FY26 was a record at the full-year level, yet individual quarters still showed steel-side margin pressure from soft realisations.

How the captive stack changes the cycle

Integration doesn't abolish cyclicality — nothing does, in commodities — but it raises the trough. A merchant producer's profit can go to zero (or negative) at the bottom of the cycle; SEML's captive coal, ore and power mean its cost base falls with it, and the power/coal earnings keep contributing even when alloy and steel spreads are thin. So the peaks are still cyclical, but the floor is higher and steadier than a pure-play alloy maker's. Watching the trough years — like FY24 — tells you more about the moat than the boom years do.

09

The industry and the rivals

Ferro alloys, integrated steel, and the power/coal names it now brushes against.

SEML sits at an unusual intersection, so it has no single perfect peer. On alloys it competes with dedicated ferro-alloy houses; on steel with regional integrated players; and, increasingly, its power-and-coal earnings put it in the orbit of utilities and miners. That breadth is exactly what makes it hard to value against any one comparable.

The comparison that actually matters

Against a clean alloy peer like Maithan, the trade-off is stark: Maithan offers a simpler, cash-rich, lower-risk single business; SEML offers a more complex, more capital-intensive machine whose captive coal and power give it a structurally lower cost floor and an extra, less-cyclical earnings stream. If you believe integration and the power pivot are durable advantages, SEML's complexity is the point. If you prefer a pure, uncomplicated alloy exposure with a fortress balance sheet, the simpler peer is the cleaner expression. The two are not really the same bet dressed differently.

Financials & valuation

A record year — and a valuation caught between a metals discount and a re-rating.

FY26 was SEML's best year on record. Revenue rose ~23% to ~₹5,690 Cr, operating profit to ~₹1,787 Cr (~31% margin), and net profit 58% to ~₹1,109 Cr (EPS ~₹31). The debate isn't whether the year was good — it's how much of it is durable, and what a metals-plus-power hybrid should be worth.

How to think about the valuation

At a ~₹17,400 Cr market cap on ~₹1,109 Cr of FY26 profit, SEML trades around 15–18× trailing earnings — a modest, commodity-style multiple, not a growth premium (the stock swung widely through 2026 as steel-cycle worries and the SKS/expansion optionality pulled in opposite directions). Two ways to see it collide here. The cyclical-discount view says these are peak-ish commodity earnings that will mean-revert, so a low multiple is correct. The sum-of-the-parts / re-rating view says a metals company that now earns most of its profit from long-life hydro, commercial coal and merchant thermal deserves better than a pure-metals multiple — because a chunk of the earnings is annuity-like, not cyclical. Which view wins is not abstract; it turns on one measurable thing.

The metric that settles it: the share of group EBITDA coming from power & mining, and the utilisation (PLF) and realisation of the SKS 600 MW plant and the coal blocks. If that energy share stays near two-thirds and rises as SKS ramps and coal capacity grows — while the alloy/steel spread merely holds — SEML is a re-rating candidate hiding under a commodity label. If the energy contribution proves lumpy and the metals spread rolls over together, the low multiple is the market being right. Track the power-and-coal EBITDA line quarter by quarter; that number, not the steel headline, is the thesis.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A low-cost integrator with a genuine second act — and real commodity and execution risk.

SEML's next few years are unusually legible: ramp SKS toward 1,200 MW, quadruple coal mining, add 1.1 MT of pellet capacity, and roughly double power capacity to ~1,720 MW by FY30, with a stated ambition to double EBITDA over that horizon. The metals base funds it; the energy build-out is where the growth and the re-rating case sit. What could go wrong divides cleanly into cycle and execution.

The bull case

  • Structural low-cost moat — captive coal, iron ore and self-made power keep SEML profitable through troughs that idle merchant rivals.
  • The power pivot is real — ~two-thirds of EBITDA from energy & mining, with SKS 600→1,200 MW and rising commercial coal adding largely non-metals earnings.
  • Hydro annuity — ~167 MW of long-life, low-cost clean power stabilises the floor and improves the carbon profile.
  • Cheap acquisition, cheap fuel — a 600 MW plant bought out of bankruptcy, fed by coal across the fence — a high return on capital.
  • Aligned owner, modest multiple — ~73% promoter skin-in-the-game and a ~15–18× P/E that doesn't yet price the energy mix.

The bear case

  • Commodity cyclicality — alloy and steel spreads swing hard with China; FY26's record could prove near a peak.
  • Regulatory & auction risk — coal blocks, ore leases and environmental clearances carry revenue-share, renewal and compliance risk that can bite earnings.
  • Execution & capital intensity — the SKS 1,200 MW build, coal ramp and pellet line are large, multi-year projects with hydrology, land and cost-overrun risk.
  • Leverage crept up — ~₹2,632 Cr gross debt post-SKS; working-capital days rose sharply in FY26 — worth watching.
  • Closely held, thin float — ~73% promoter ownership concentrates control and leaves minorities reliant on family capital allocation.
The bottom line

SEML is a rare thing in Indian metals: a price-taking commodity producer that has spent decades engineering away its cost disadvantage, and has now grown a power-and-mining business large enough to change what the company is. The business risk — cyclicality, regulation, execution on a big capex program — is genuine and shouldn't be waved away. But the moat is not rhetorical; it shows up most clearly in the trough years, when the captive stack keeps the furnaces lit. The next chapter has concrete, datable milestones to judge it by: the SKS plant climbing from 600 toward 1,200 MW, the +1.1 MT pellet line firing up within ~30 months, and the quarter when power-and-coal EBITDA is unmistakably the larger half. Watch coal cross the fence at Gare Palma into the SKS boilers — that single flow is the company its name has been promising for twenty years.
