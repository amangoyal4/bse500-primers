00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Karnataka Bank is a century-old private-sector bank from Mangaluru, on India's south-west coast. Founded in 1924, it takes deposits from savers — mostly in Karnataka and the neighbouring south — and lends to households, small businesses, farmers and mid-sized companies. It runs roughly 960 branches and a ₹1.9-lakh-crore book of business, and in FY26 it earned a record ₹1,310.50 crore profit. Yet the market values the whole bank at only ~0.8 times its stated net worth — you can, on paper, buy a 100-year-old bank for less than the book value of what it owns.

Three facts frame everything:

  • An "old private-sector bank" mid-turnaround. Karnataka Bank belongs to a small, ageing club of pre-Independence private banks that were never nationalised. For decades it was a sleepy, deposit-rich, slow-growing regional lender earning weak returns. The current story is a deliberate digital-and-SME transformation — a new technology stack, a retail/MSME tilt, and a push to lift return on assets (RoA) from a low base toward the ~1.2%+ that would let it earn its cost of capital.
  • The numbers are genuinely improving. FY26 profit was a record; total business hit an all-time high of ₹1,92,118 crore (deposits ₹1,08,779 Cr + gross advances ₹83,340 Cr); gross bad loans fell to 2.78% and net bad loans to ~0.98%; and Q4 RoA touched 1.27%. The trajectory is up and to the right.
  • Governance is the overhang. In mid-2025 both the MD & CEO and the Executive Director resigned within weeks of each other — after the bank's own auditors flagged certain expenditure. A 38-year insider, Raghavendra S. Bhat, now runs the bank on an RBI-approved one-year term. Cheap-on-book is partly a verdict on that instability.
In plain terms

Picture a well-built, hundred-year-old branch bank sitting on a large, loyal pile of deposits — but running its money lazily, so that after costs and bad loans it barely earns back what its shareholders' capital is worth. For years the market shrugged and priced it below the value of its own net assets. The bet now is that new technology, a shift toward higher-yielding small-business and retail loans, and tighter cost control can lift the bank's returns enough that it finally earns its keep — at which point a stock trading at 0.8× book has room to climb toward, and past, book value. The catch: it has to do this while its top job keeps changing hands.

01

The "old private-sector bank" species — and how a bank earns

No finance degree required — the taxonomy, then the five numbers that drive everything.

Indian banks come in three broad families, and knowing which one Karnataka Bank belongs to explains most of its character. A bank is fundamentally a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors) and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap — scaled to its assets — is the engine. But which kind of bank is running that engine matters enormously.

The entire investment question is whether it can shed those weaknesses without losing the deposit franchise that is its birthright.

The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. A bank gets richer four ways — lend more, fund cheaper (more CASA), lose less (low bad loans), and earn fees. Karnataka Bank's problem for years was that it did each of these only adequately: a thin margin, a middling cost-to-income ratio, periodic lumps of bad SME loans, and modest fee income added up to weak returns. The turnaround is not one heroic move — it is grinding all four levers a little better at once, so RoA creeps from ~0.8% toward ~1.2%+. On a ₹1-lakh-crore-plus balance sheet, even a 0.3% RoA gain is several hundred crore of extra profit a year.

02

How the money flows: deposits → loans → margin

Read this once and the whole bank — and the turnaround lever — clicks into place.

A bank is a conveyor belt for money. Savers' deposits come in one end; loans go out the other; the spread between the two is harvested as net interest income. Two things govern how much profit drips out: how cheap the deposits are (the CASA mix) and what the money is lent out at (the loan yield). Karnataka Bank's whole improvement plan is about shifting the mix at both ends of this belt.

Old private banks lean heavily on Stage-2 term deposits, which keeps funding cost high and the margin thin. The turnaround pushes on both ends — grow cheaper CASA on the left, tilt toward higher-yielding retail/MSME loans on the right — to widen the ~2.88% margin.

Why the margin is thin — and where the lever is

  • Funding is pricier here. A large, loyal but rate-sensitive base of fixed-deposit savers (retirees, conservative south-Indian households) means Karnataka Bank pays more for its money than a CASA-rich new-gen bank. Lifting the CASA share is the single biggest funding-cost lever.
  • The loan book skewed low-yield. A legacy tilt toward safer, lower-margin lending (large corporates, gold, secured mortgages) kept yields modest. The tilt toward retail and MSME — credit cards, personal, business and co-lending — lifts the average yield, at the cost of more careful underwriting.
  • Scale dilutes cost. Spreading a century-old branch and staff base over a bigger book — and digitising routine work — brings the cost-to-income ratio down, so more of the margin survives to the bottom line.
Why a 0.3% margin move is a big deal

Margins in banking look tiny — 2.88% versus a target of, say, 3.2% sounds trivial. But it is applied to a ₹1-lakh-crore-plus balance sheet. A third-of-a-percent improvement in net interest margin is worth roughly ₹350–400 crore of extra pre-tax income a year — on a bank that made ₹1,310 crore of net profit. That is why every basis point of funding cost and loan yield is fought over, and why the CASA share and loan-mix numbers each quarter are the ones to watch. The turnaround is a game of inches on a very large field.

03

The company and who controls it

A hundred years old, headquartered in Mangaluru — and owned by no one in particular.

Karnataka Bank was founded on 18 February 1924 in Mangaluru (then Mangalore), Dakshina Kannada, by a group of local businessmen. It has been a listed, professionally-managed private bank for its entire century of life and — a defining quirk — it has no promoter at all. No family, no group, no state owns a controlling block; the bank is entirely widely held by the public and institutions.

Timeline

  • 1960s–2000s

  • 2017

  • 2020–2023
    Mahabaleshwara M. S. (a long-serving insider) as MD & CEO steers the bank through COVID; profitability and asset quality begin to recover from a weak patch.
  • 2023–24
    Srikrishnan H. Sarma, an external hire with a payments/fintech background, takes over as MD & CEO and accelerates a digital transformation; the bank marks its centenary in Feb 2024. FY24 profit hits ₹1,306 Cr.
  • Jun–Jul 2025
    Governance shock: MD & CEO Sarma and ED Sekhar Rao both resign within weeks, after statutory auditors flag certain expenditure by whole-time directors. The stock drops ~8%. Veteran Raghavendra S. Bhat steps in as interim chief.

    Record annual profit ₹1,310.50 Cr; total business an all-time-high ₹1.92 lakh Cr; GNPA down to 2.78%; Q4 RoA 1.27%; 50% (₹5/share) dividend proposed. The turnaround shows up in the numbers even amid the churn.

Ownership & management

  • No promoter — a genuinely public company. Roughly 71% is held by the public (retail and body-corporate shareholders), with domestic institutions ~16% and foreign institutions ~13%. There is no controlling shareholder to install or protect management — the board and the RBI effectively govern succession.
  • A double-edged structure. No promoter means no entrenched family and a fully professional bank — but it also means no anchor when leadership wobbles, which is part of why the 2025 CEO exit hit so hard. Widely-held old private banks are also perennial consolidation candidates — a larger bank could, in theory, acquire one without a promoter's blessing.
  • Management: Raghavendra S. Bhat (MD & CEO), a career Karnataka Bank man, leads on a one-year RBI-approved mandate — reassuring on continuity of culture, but with the open question of whether a short-tenure, insider chief can drive an ambitious transformation, and who leads beyond the term.
04

The cradle of Indian banking — and its last independent child

Why a few square miles of coastal Karnataka gave India a fifth of its banks — and why only Karnataka Bank stayed private.

To understand Karnataka Bank you have to understand where it comes from. The twin coastal districts of Dakshina Kannada and Udupi, around Mangaluru, are known as the "cradle of Indian banking." Between roughly 1880 and 1935, more than twenty banks were founded in this small stretch of coast — nine of them in Mangaluru city alone. It is one of the most remarkable clusters of financial entrepreneurship in Indian history.

The banks born within a few miles of each other

  • Canara Bank — Mangaluru, 1906. Now a giant public-sector bank.
  • Corporation Bank — Udupi, 1906. Nationalised; later merged into Union Bank of India.
  • Karnataka Bank — Mangaluru, 1924. Still private, still independent, still headquartered where it was born.
  • Syndicate Bank — Manipal, 1925. Nationalised; later merged into Canara Bank.
  • Vijaya Bank — Mangaluru, 1931. Nationalised; later merged into Bank of Baroda.
The non-transferable fact

Here is the detail no other bank primer can borrow: Karnataka Bank was born into a family of five great coastal-Karnataka banks — and it is the only one that was never nationalised and never merged away. Canara swallowed Syndicate; Bank of Baroda swallowed Vijaya; Union Bank swallowed Corporation; all four are now arms of the state. Karnataka Bank alone kept its name, its independence and its Mangaluru head office for a hundred years. That is its identity and its burden at once: it is a proud, self-standing survivor of a lost banking ecosystem — and also a sub-scale, single-region private bank in a country whose banking has consolidated relentlessly around a handful of national giants.

What the heritage means for the business today

  • A deep, sticky deposit franchise in the south. A century of relationships gives Karnataka Bank a loyal, low-attrition deposit base — especially among coastal-Karnataka and NRI-linked households. Deposits are the scarcest resource in banking; a genuinely sticky base is a real, if quiet, asset.
  • Geographic concentration. The flip side: the book is heavily tilted to Karnataka and the south. That concentrates the bank's fortunes in one region's economy, monsoon and credit cycle — a diversification weakness the branch expansion into other states only partly fixes.
  • Brand trust as funding advantage. In a deposit market built on safety, a hundred-year-old, never-failed local name is a genuine edge with conservative savers — the same trust that let it stay independent while its neighbours were absorbed.
The surprise most outsiders miss: this is not a bank with a founding family quietly in control. A century-old institution with no promoter and ~71% public float is unusual anywhere — and it means the story is 100% about management, culture and the RBI, not about a controlling owner's whims. The heritage is a brand and a deposit base; it is not a shield.
05

The digital-and-SME turnaround — the crux of the case

The whole thesis in one sentence: can a hundred-year-old branch bank digitise its way to earning its cost of capital?

Karnataka Bank's investment case is not "cheap old bank stays cheap." It is that a deliberate transformation — a new technology stack, a shift toward higher-yielding retail and small-business lending, co-lending partnerships, and cost discipline — lifts the bank's returns enough to justify a valuation at or above book. Under the banner of "Startup@100" (a nod to a century-old bank trying to behave like a start-up), the bank has been rebuilding the plumbing that old private banks were notoriously slow to modernise.

How to tell if it's actually working

Transformation slideware is cheap; the proof is in a handful of trends moving together, quarter after quarter:

  • RoA rising and holding — from ~0.8–1.0% toward a durable ~1.2%+. Q4 FY26's 1.27% is encouraging, but a single strong quarter is not a trend; the full-year number is the read that counts.
  • NIM widening — the ~2.88% margin creeping up as the loan mix tilts and CASA holds, not falling as the bank chases growth with expensive deposits.
  • Cost-to-income falling — digitisation should let the bank grow the book faster than its costs.
  • Asset quality staying clean — the retail/MSME tilt must not come with a fresh wave of bad loans. A turnaround that lifts yield by lending badly is no turnaround at all.
The one metric that settles it

Strip everything else away and the thesis lives or dies on return on assets, read against the loan mix and the bad-loan trend. If Karnataka Bank can hold RoA durably above ~1.2% while tilting toward retail/MSME and keeping GNPA falling, it will be earning close to its cost of capital — and a bank that earns its cost of capital does not stay at 0.8× book. If RoA slips back toward ~0.8% because the margin doesn't widen or bad loans reappear, the cheapness is deserved and the stock stays a value trap. Everything in the transformation — the tech stack, the co-lending, the SME tilt — is ultimately a means to that single end.

06

The leadership overhang

A turnaround needs a steady hand at the top — and that is exactly what has wobbled.

Every ambitious transformation is, in the end, a bet on the people running it. Karnataka Bank's single sharpest risk is not credit or margin — it is management continuity. In mid-2025, within a few weeks, both the MD & CEO (Srikrishnan H. Sarma) and the Executive Director (Sekhar Rao) resigned. The trigger, per reporting, was friction with the board after the bank's statutory auditors flagged certain expenditure incurred by whole-time directors without full board sign-off. The stock fell ~8% on the news.

Why this is more than a personnel story

  • It hit at the top of the transformation. Sarma was the external, fintech-minded chief hired to drive exactly the digital push that underpins the bull case. Losing the architect mid-build raises the obvious question of whether the strategy survives him intact.
  • It was a governance flag, not just a departure. An auditor-flagged expenditure and a board rift are the kind of governance wrinkle that makes cautious investors demand a bigger discount — and it lands on a bank with no promoter to reassure the market that a steady owner is in charge.
  • The fix is credible but time-boxed. The board turned to Raghavendra S. Bhat — a 38-year insider (clerk in 1981, former COO) — first as interim chief, then, with RBI approval, as regular MD & CEO on a one-year term from November 2025. That restores cultural continuity and calm, but a one-year mandate for an insider is a stabiliser, not obviously the long-term transformational leader — and it re-opens the succession question within a year.
How to weigh the governance discount

The market is pricing two things at once here: the operating turnaround (improving) and the governance record (unsettled). A useful way to hold both: the business is demonstrably getting better — record profit, falling bad loans, rising RoA — while the stewardship has been jumpy, with a mid-transformation CEO exit and a stop-gap leadership arrangement. A durable re-rating toward book value almost certainly requires the governance story to quieten: a full-term, credible MD & CEO in place, a clean audit, and a clear signal that the strategy outlives any one leader. Until then, part of the 0.8×-book discount is simply a "prove the top is stable" tax.

The honest caveat: insider continuity has an upside too — Bhat knows the franchise intimately and a hundred-year-old bank's culture is not easily broken by one exit. But for an outside shareholder, "who runs this bank in eighteen months, and do they keep driving the transformation?" is a live, unanswered question — and it is the reason the improving numbers have not yet earned an improving multiple.
07

Asset quality and the SME credit cycle

The turnaround's tightrope: lift yield by lending to smaller borrowers — without importing their bad-loan risk.

An old private bank can flatter its margins for a while by tilting toward higher-yielding small-business and unsecured retail loans — and then give it all back when that book sours in a downturn. Karnataka Bank's credit history has had exactly these lumps. So the asset-quality trend is not a side-issue; it is the quality control on the entire turnaround. The good news in FY26 is that quality improved while the bank grew: gross NPAs fell to 2.78% and net NPAs to about 0.98%.

What "provisioning" means and why it matters

When a loan looks shaky, a bank sets aside money to cover the expected loss — a provision, charged against profit. The gap between gross NPA (2.78%) and net NPA (~0.98%) shows how much of the recognised bad loans are already covered; a provision-coverage ratio in the region of ~80% means most of the pain is already taken. A conservatively-run bank provisions early, so a bad loan rarely springs a nasty surprise on a future quarter. For a turnaround, healthy coverage is doubly important: it means the improving profit is real, not borrowed from an under-provisioned book that will bite later.

The risk that never fully sleeps

  • SME/MSME cyclicality. Small-business borrowers are more fragile than large corporates in a slowdown; a regional shock to Karnataka's economy or a broad SME stress cycle would test the new, higher-yield book first.
  • Retail/unsecured seasoning. Newer retail and co-lending exposures have not yet been through a full downturn; yields look great until a cycle turns and losses arrive.
  • Concentration. Because the book leans to one region, a single geography's distress (agriculture, a local industry) matters more here than at a nationally spread bank.
08

The industry and the rivals

A favourable market for banks overall — but a tough neighbourhood for a sub-scale regional one.

Indian banking is large, under-penetrated and growing: credit-to-GDP is still low versus developed economies, nominal GDP compounds at ~10%+, and formal credit keeps deepening. That macro tailwind lifts every reasonably-run bank. But the competitive reality for an old private bank like Karnataka Bank is harder than the macro suggests — it competes for the same deposits and borrowers as far larger, better-capitalised, better-branded rivals.

The comparison that frames the stock

The most useful mirror for Karnataka Bank is not HDFC Bank — it is its own old-private-bank cohort. Federal Bank, City Union and Karur Vysya were once sleepy, cheap, sub-1% RoA regional banks too; each ground its way to a ~1.3–1.5% RoA and a re-rating to (or above) book value. That is the template the market is asking Karnataka Bank to follow — and the reason a below-book valuation is a genuine opportunity if it delivers, and a fair price if it doesn't. The bull isn't imagining a new HDFC; the bull is betting Karnataka Bank walks the same path its more successful cousins already walked.

The regulatory backdrop: banks operate under the RBI's tight prudential regime — capital rules (Basel III), cash-reserve (CRR) and statutory-liquidity (SLR) requirements, priority-sector lending quotas, and rules on provisioning and unsecured credit. For a small bank, RBI comfort matters intensely — it governs branch expansion, approves the MD & CEO (as it did Bhat's appointment), and its stance on deposit competition and unsecured lending directly shapes the room to grow. Old private banks are also perennial subjects of consolidation speculation whenever the RBI signals openness to bank M&A.

09

Financials & valuation

Record profit, improving quality — priced below the value of its own net worth.

Karnataka Bank's financials read like a genuine, if unfinished, turnaround: record profit, an all-time-high book of business, falling bad loans and rising quarterly returns — all trading at a valuation that assumes very little of it sticks. The debate is not whether the bank is improving (it plainly is); it is whether the improvement is durable enough to earn the cost of capital.

How to think about the valuation

For a bank, the multiple that matters is price-to-book measured against RoE. The rough rule: a bank should trade near book value when its RoE roughly equals its cost of equity, above book when it earns more, and below book when it earns less. Karnataka Bank earns ~10.4% on equity against a cost of maybe ~13–14% — so a ~0.8× book valuation is the market being consistent, not irrationally harsh. That reframes the whole opportunity: you are not paid to hope the market "realises" the bank is cheap; you are paid only if RoE actually climbs toward and past its cost of capital. Get RoA durably to ~1.2%+ (RoE into the low-to-mid teens) and the stock can re-rate from 0.8× toward 1.0–1.2× book — a double engine of higher earnings and a higher multiple. Fail to, and a cheap bank stays cheap for a reason.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely improving bank, cheaply priced — with a governance question mark over the wheel.

Karnataka Bank is a real turnaround in progress: record profit, an all-time-high book of business, falling bad loans and rising quarterly returns, all trading below the value of its own net worth. The investment debate is a clean two-hander — does the operating improvement prove durable, and does the leadership settle down — because if both go right, a bank earning its cost of capital does not stay at 0.8× book for long.

The bull case

  • Cheap on a real turnaround — ~0.8× book and ~7.8× earnings on record profit, with RoA and RoE visibly climbing.
  • A proven template exists — Federal, City Union and Karur Vysya walked the same old-private-bank path to a ~1.3%+ RoA and a re-rating above book.
  • Deposit-rich, well-capitalised — a century-old, sticky southern deposit base and a loan-to-deposit ratio of only ~77% leave ample room to grow.
  • Improving quality — GNPA down to 2.78% while the book grows, suggesting disciplined underwriting of the higher-yield tilt.
  • Optionality — a promoter-less, deposit-rich bank is a natural consolidation candidate; a bid at/above book would crystallise value.

The bear case

  • Governance overhang — a mid-transformation CEO & ED exit, an auditor-flagged expenditure, and a stop-gap one-year insider chief; the top is not yet settled.
  • Structurally thin margin — a ~2.88% NIM and sub-1.5% RoA history; the turnaround has to fight decades of the breed's weak economics.
  • Sub-scale & concentrated — a small, Karnataka-heavy bank competing with far larger, cheaper-funded rivals and small-finance banks in its own target segments.
  • Value-trap risk — old private banks can stay below book for years; cheapness alone is not a catalyst without RoE actually rising.
  • Cycle exposure — the higher-yield retail/MSME tilt is untested through a full downturn; a regional or SME stress would hit it first.
The bottom line

Karnataka Bank is the last independent child of the cradle of Indian banking, trying to prove that a hundred-year-old branch bank can digitise and re-mix its way to earning its cost of capital — and being priced, at ~0.8× book, as though it won't. The business case is improving in plain sight: record profit, falling bad loans, a rising return on assets. The unresolved half is the wheel — a leadership team that changed hands mid-transformation and a one-year mandate that re-opens the succession question within a year. The story will be settled by two numbers watched together each year: return on assets and the bad-loan ratio, under a management that stays put. Hold RoA above ~1.2% with GNPA still falling and a full-term CEO in the chair, and a below-book survivor finally earns its re-rating; let either the returns or the leadership slip, and the century-old discount endures.
