00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're actually looking at, before we go deep.

Aditya Birla Real Estate Limited (ABREL) is the company formerly known as Century Textiles and Industries — a Mumbai industrial house founded in 1897 that spent a century making cloth and paper. In late 2024 it renamed itself, changed its NSE ticker from CENTURYTEX to ABREL, and declared a new identity: a real-estate developer, operating under the brand Birla Estates. The rename was not cosmetic. It is the public flag on a deliberate transformation, controlled by the Aditya Birla Group, of an old cyclical manufacturer into a residential property company that sits on some of the most valuable land in urban India — the plots beneath its decommissioned textile mills.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • The identity is now real estate — and the company backed that with a sale, not a slogan. In 2025 ABREL agreed to sell its entire pulp-and-paper business, Century Pulp & Paper, to ITC for ₹3,498 Cr in cash. That business was historically the group's steady profit anchor; parting with it is the clearest possible signal that real estate is now the whole plan, and it hands ABREL a large slug of cash to fund the property ramp.
  • Birla Estates is scaling fast — read the pre-sales, not the P&L. In FY26 the developer booked roughly ₹8,136 Cr of apartment sales (pre-sales / bookings), with a blockbuster Q4 of ~₹4,288 Cr, up ~69% on the prior quarter, and collections up ~23% year-on-year. Yet its reported real-estate revenue was only ~₹345 Cr and the continuing business posted a loss. That is not a contradiction — it is how developer accounting works, and understanding the gap is the key to the whole story.
  • The land bank is the crown jewel. The re-rating rests on a large, cheaply-held bank of land — above all the prime former mill land in Worli, South Mumbai, now being developed as the ultra-luxury Birla Niyaara — plus growing portfolios in Bengaluru, the NCR and Pune. The bet is that decades-old industrial land, carried on the books at a fraction of its worth, gets converted into booked apartment sales at today's prices.
In plain terms

Picture a family business that owned mills making cloth and paper — dependable, unglamorous, cyclical. Sitting under those mills, in the middle of a city that grew up around them, was land nobody had ever valued at what it was truly worth. The family has now decided the land is the real asset: it is selling the paper operation to a competitor, letting the textile side fade, and pouring everything into building and selling premium apartments on its own plots through Birla Estates. ABREL, then, is less a normal company you value on this year's profit and more a land-and-brand engine — you value it on how fast it can turn a hoard of well-located land into signed apartment bookings. The reported earnings will look strange for a while precisely because that machine is still winding up.

01

The rename, and the logic of the pivot

Why a 128-year-old textile-and-paper company decided to become a property developer.

To understand ABREL you have to understand what it was. Century Textiles and Industries, incorporated in 1897, was one of Mumbai's foundational industrial companies — a maker of textiles that, over the decades, added a large pulp-and-paper operation and other lines. For most of its life it was a solid, sleepy manufacturer. What made it quietly extraordinary was not its factories but their addresses: several of its plants sat on land that, as Mumbai and other Indian cities expanded, became worth many times the value of the businesses running on them.

In 2016 the company took its first step toward monetising that value directly — rather than just selling land to other developers, it would build on it itself. It launched Birla Estates as a wholly-owned real-estate development arm. For several years Birla Estates was a modest side-story inside a manufacturing company. By the mid-2020s it had become the growth engine, the launches were multiplying, and the mismatch between the company's name and its trajectory was glaring. So in October 2024 the board renamed the company Aditya Birla Real Estate Limited, and the NSE symbol changed from CENTURYTEX to ABREL. The BSE scrip code — 500040 — carried over unchanged from the Century Textiles days.

The pivot logic, spelled out

The move rests on a simple arbitrage. The company holds large parcels of urban land at historic book values — costs from decades ago, or even land it has effectively always owned. The market value of that same land today, especially in Mumbai, is enormous. A company can "unlock" that gap in three ways: sell the land to a developer (a one-time gain), enter a joint venture (share the upside), or develop it itself and capture the full builder's margin on top of the land appreciation. ABREL chose the third, most valuable route — and wrapped it in the Aditya Birla brand and balance sheet, which give a homebuyer confidence and give the developer cheap access to capital. The rename tells the market: stop valuing us as a cyclical manufacturer; value us as the developer of that land.

The three legs, and where each is headed

At the moment of transition, ABREL was really three businesses stitched together. The direction of travel for each is what the rest of this primer traces:

  • Real estate (Birla Estates) — the future. Residential and some commercial development across the Mumbai Metropolitan Region (MMR), Bengaluru, the National Capital Region (NCR) and Pune. Scaling hard; this is the identity now.
  • Pulp & paper (Century Pulp & Paper) — the past profit anchor, now sold. A cash-generating manufacturing business at Lalkuan, Uttarakhand, agreed for sale to ITC for ₹3,498 Cr and reclassified as a discontinued operation. It funded the transition and is now on its way out of the company.
  • Textiles — the fading legacy. The original business the company was named for; a smaller, lower-priority leg that no longer drives the story or the valuation.
A naming trap to avoid: "Aditya Birla Real Estate" is easy to confuse with other Aditya Birla names — Grasim, UltraTech (cement), Aditya Birla Capital, Aditya Birla Fashion. ABREL is a distinct listed company, the former Century Textiles, and its development brand is Birla Estates. Don't conflate it with UltraTech or with unrelated "Birla" builders; the promoter is the Aditya Birla Group under Kumar Mangalam Birla.
02

Developer economics 101: pre-sales versus revenue

The single most important idea for reading any Indian property company — and the reason ABREL's P&L looks odd.

A residential developer does not earn money the way a shop or a factory does. When it sells apartments in an under-construction project, the buyer starts paying — but the developer usually cannot record that as revenue yet. Under Indian accounting, revenue on a real-estate project is recognised largely on completion and handover, not when the flat is booked. So a developer runs on two completely different clocks, and confusing them is the classic beginner's error.

Pre-sales (Step 3) and collections (Step 4) tell you how the business is doing now. Reported revenue (Step 5) reflects projects sold years ago. The two can move in opposite directions at any given moment.

This lag is why the numbers can seem contradictory. In FY26, Birla Estates pre-sold roughly ₹8,136 Cr of homes — a record — while the company's recognised real-estate revenue was only about ₹345 Cr, because the projects generating those bookings are still under construction and haven't been handed over. A fast-growing developer will almost always show bookings running far ahead of reported revenue. The reported P&L is a rear-view mirror; pre-sales are the windshield.

The three numbers that actually matter for a developer

Pre-sales (bookings): the total value of apartments sold in a period. The single best gauge of current demand and momentum — ABREL's ~₹8,136 Cr in FY26. Collections: the cash physically received from buyers as construction progresses (up ~23% for ABREL) — this funds the build and the balance sheet. Recognised revenue: the accounting figure, booked on handover, always lagging. A healthy developer wants pre-sales and collections climbing steadily; recognised revenue will catch up later, in lumps, as towers complete. Judge ABREL on the first two; expect the third to stay noisy while the company is in build-out.

There is a second reason ABREL's income statement looks strange right now: the profitable paper business has been moved to "discontinued operations," so it no longer sits in the headline revenue line. Strip both effects together — paper reclassified out, real-estate revenue still lagging bookings — and you get the odd optics of a "real-estate company" whose reported top line fell in the very year its property arm had its best-ever sales. Section 10 pulls those threads apart.

03

Birla Estates: the residential engine

The developer arm — what it builds, where, and how it grew so fast.

Birla Estates is where ABREL's future is being built, one project at a time. Launched in 2016, it develops primarily residential real estate — apartments and townships — with some commercial exposure, and it has deliberately concentrated on the four markets where Indian housing demand and pricing are strongest: the Mumbai Metropolitan Region, Bengaluru, the NCR (Gurugram), and Pune. It operates through a mix of self-owned land (chiefly the group's mill parcels) and asset-light joint developments with landowners, which lets it grow its project pipeline without buying every plot outright.

The strategy is recognisably that of a premium-branded developer: pick high-demand micro-markets, build to a quality and design standard that supports a price premium, and lean on the Aditya Birla name to reassure buyers who are, in effect, paying years in advance for a home that doesn't exist yet. In Indian real estate, brand trust is worth real money — buyers have been burned by delayed and abandoned projects from weaker developers, so a group with the Birlas' balance sheet behind it can command both faster sales and firmer prices.

Why "branded developer" is a genuine edge

A homebuyer handing over lakhs of rupees for an under-construction flat is taking a leap of faith: will the tower actually get built, on time, to spec? For two decades, Indian buyers watched smaller builders stall, divert cash, and disappear. That fear is exactly why a trusted name sells faster and at a premium. Birla Estates converts the Aditya Birla Group's century of reputation into a sales advantage — it lowers the buyer's perceived risk, which shows up as quicker pre-sales and stronger pricing power. The same logic explains why the biggest listed developers (Lodha, Godrej Properties, DLF) have pulled away from the pack: in a trust-scarce market, brand consolidates share.

How the pipeline expands

  • Own land first: the mill parcels — above all Worli — anchor the portfolio with land the company already controls at low historic cost.
  • Joint development & new acquisitions: asset-light tie-ups with landowners and fresh land buys (funded increasingly by the ITC cash and group support) extend the pipeline into Bengaluru, NCR and Pune without tying up all the capital in land.
  • Phased selling: large townships like Trimaya are sold in phases, so a single land parcel yields bookings across many quarters — smoothing the pre-sales line and letting prices rise between phases.
04

The mill-land bank: the value the whole story turns on

The device that fits no other primer — decades-old, cheaply-held industrial land being converted into booked apartments.

Strip ABREL down to one idea and it is this: the company owns land it acquired long ago, carried on its books at a fraction of what it is worth today, in locations that have since become the heart of India's most expensive property markets. The re-rating from "paper company" to "real-estate developer" is the market waking up to that hidden asset — and to the fact that ABREL has decided to capture its full value by building on it, rather than selling it cheap.

The purest example is Worli. The company's erstwhile Century Mills occupied a large tract in the middle of South Mumbai — an area where, over the decades, the textile industry died and a financial-and-luxury-residential district grew up around the silent mills. That land, on the books for next to nothing, is worth a fortune. ABREL is now developing part of it as Birla Niyaara: roughly 14 acres, ultra-luxury towers (including a new signature tower), with apartment prices starting around ₹5.5 Cr and running far higher. One old mill plot, converted at Mumbai luxury prices, can generate booking value that dwarfs anything the paper business earned in a year.

Each move adds value: change-of-use unlocks it, development-plus-brand multiplies it, and the sale realises it as pre-sales. This is the non-transferable core of the ABREL case — it works only because the land was already owned, cheaply, in the right place.
The value unlock, made concrete

Imagine ABREL's Worli land recorded on the balance sheet at, say, a token historic figure. Develop it, and a single luxury tower can pre-sell thousands of crores of apartments over its life. The difference between that historic land cost and the eventual apartment sales — minus construction and the builder's costs — is the value being unlocked, and it accrues to ABREL rather than to a developer it might otherwise have sold the plot to. Multiply that logic across the group's other well-located parcels and you have the embedded-value case: you are not paying for this year's ₹345 Cr of recognised revenue, you are paying for a land bank that converts, parcel by parcel, into years of premium bookings. That is the entire re-rating in one sentence — and it is why ABREL is valued on embedded land value and pre-sales, not on a normal earnings multiple.

The catch, stated plainly: a land bank is potential, not performance. Converting mill land into delivered, profitable apartments takes approvals, redevelopment consents, years of construction and a market that stays strong through the whole cycle. The value is real; the timing and execution are not guaranteed. The market may also already be paying richly for the land — so the unlock has to keep delivering just to justify today's price, let alone to surprise on the upside.
05

Pre-sales: the forward number, and the gap that defines ABREL

The one operating figure that tells you how the business is really doing — and why the reported revenue is a red herring for now.

If you track only one number for ABREL, track pre-sales (also called bookings or booking value): the total rupee value of apartments Birla Estates sells in a period. It is the developer's true sales figure, the forward engine, the thing that becomes recognised revenue years later. In FY26, ABREL's pre-sales came in around ₹8,136 Cr, with a standout fourth quarter of roughly ₹4,288 Cr — up about 69% on the previous quarter — alongside ~3 million square feet of area sold in Q4 and collections up ~23% year-on-year. Those are the numbers that say the engine is accelerating.

Birla Estates sold roughly ₹8,136 Cr of homes in FY26 but recognised only ~₹345 Cr of real-estate revenue — a ~24× gap. Nothing is wrong; the bookings simply haven't reached handover yet. This gap is the developer, and it is why the reported loss is not the scandal a first glance suggests.

Here is the device that fits ABREL and almost nothing else: the company's health lives in the distance between what it sells and what it reports. A conventional company's sales and revenue are roughly the same number. ABREL's differ by a factor of more than twenty — and that yawning gap is a feature, not a warning. Every crore of pre-sales today is a crore of revenue queued up for two, three, four years out, once the tower it belongs to is handed over. The bigger and faster the pre-sales, the larger the backlog of future recognised revenue silently building up off the income statement.

Why the reported loss is not the story it looks like

In FY26 ABREL's continuing (real-estate) operations reported a loss, and its total income appeared to fall sharply. A newcomer sees "loss-making, shrinking real-estate company" and recoils. But two things drive that optic, and neither reflects the underlying business weakening. First, the profitable paper unit was moved to discontinued operations, so it vanished from the headline top line. Second — the bigger point — the property arm is in heavy build-out: it is spending on land and construction now, booking huge pre-sales now, but not permitted to recognise most of that as revenue until handover later. A developer scaling this fast will always look worse on the P&L than on the sales sheet. The bookings line, not the profit line, is where FY26's success shows up.

06

Pulp & paper: the profit anchor they just sold to ITC

The genuine surprise — a century-old company amputating the business that made the money.

For most of ABREL's life, the quiet hero of the accounts was not real estate or even textiles — it was paper. Century Pulp & Paper (CPP), commissioned in 1984 at Lalkuan (Nainital district, Uttarakhand), was a well-run, integrated manufacturer of writing-and-printing paper, tissue and paperboard, with an installed capacity of around 4.8 lakh tonnes a year. It was a real business with real customers and, crucially, steady cash flow — the profit anchor that, for years, cushioned the group while the real-estate arm was still tiny and investment-hungry.

And in 2025, ABREL sold it. It agreed to transfer the entire CPP undertaking to ITC Limited — the cigarettes-to-FMCG-to-paperboards giant — for a lump-sum cash consideration of ₹3,498 Cr. The Competition Commission of India cleared the deal, and in the FY26 accounts the paper business was reclassified as a discontinued operation (which is exactly why it dropped out of the headline revenue line and distorted the year-on-year comparison). For ITC, the acquisition bolts scale onto its existing paperboards business. For ABREL, it is the decisive act of the pivot.

A 128-year-old textiles-and-paper house selling its steady, profitable paper mill to a rival — in order to become a property developer valued on the land under its old factories.
The surprise, and why it is rational

It sounds backwards: why sell the business that reliably makes money and keep pouring capital into one that currently reports losses? Because the two are being valued on completely different bases. Paper is a mature, cyclical manufacturer — the market pays a modest multiple of its earnings for it. Prime urban land, developed under a trusted brand, is valued on its embedded worth and its pre-sales potential — a far richer basis. By converting the paper business into ₹3,498 Cr of cash and redeploying it into land and construction, ABREL is moving capital from a lowly-valued pot to a highly-valued one. The paper sale is not a retreat; it is the funding event for the real-estate ramp, and the clearest evidence that management means the pivot literally.

Watch what the cash does: ₹3,498 Cr is a large inflow relative to ABREL's size. Where it lands — paying down net debt, buying new land, or funding construction — will shape the next two years. The bullish reading is that it de-risks the balance sheet and refills the land pipeline; the cautious reading is that a capital-hungry development ramp can absorb even a windfall like this quickly, so the deleveraging may be less than the headline suggests.
07

Textiles: the leg the company was named for

The original business — now the smallest and least important part of the story.

There is a certain irony in a company called Century Textiles whose textile business is now its least consequential leg. The manufacturing of yarn and fabric was the company's founding activity in 1897, but decades of competition, the structural decline of India's old organised textile mills, and the far greater pull of real estate and paper have left textiles as a small, low-growth remnant of the group's revenue and profit.

For an investor, the textile business matters in two narrow ways, and neither is about its current earnings. First, it is a modest, lower-margin manufacturing operation that adds cyclicality and complexity without moving the valuation needle much. Second — and far more importantly — it is historically the source of the land. The mills that made the cloth are the very plots that are now the crown jewels of the real-estate story. In that sense the textile legacy has already delivered its most valuable contribution: the addresses.

How to weigh the textile leg

Treat textiles as a small, fading appendix rather than a driver. It contributes some revenue and a little profit, carries the usual input-cost and demand risks of a commodity manufacturer, and adds to the "messiness" of ABREL's sum-of-the-parts. But nobody is buying ABREL for its fabric. The question that matters is whether, over time, the company continues to simplify — as it did dramatically with the paper sale — so that what remains is a cleaner real-estate developer. A further tidy-up of the non-core legs would sharpen the story; keeping them indefinitely leaves ABREL a slightly awkward hybrid.

08

The group behind it: brand, capital, and control

Why the Aditya Birla backing is not a footnote but part of the investment case.

ABREL is a promoter-controlled company, majority-owned by the Aditya Birla Group, one of India's largest and best-regarded industrial conglomerates, chaired by Kumar Mangalam Birla. Promoter (group) holding sits around 50.2%, with no meaningful pledge on those shares. That control and that name are not incidental to the story — for a developer, they are strategic assets in their own right.

What the group backing actually buys

Two concrete advantages, beyond prestige. Buyer trust: in a market scarred by stalled projects, "an Aditya Birla home" sells faster and holds price better than an unknown builder's — trust converts directly into pre-sales momentum. Cheap, patient capital: real estate is capital-hungry and cyclical; a developer that can lean on a AAA-calibre group balance sheet can buy land, ride downturns, and fund construction on better terms than a standalone builder. Those two edges are precisely what let a late entrant like Birla Estates scale to ₹8,000-crore-plus annual bookings in under a decade. The flip side of promoter control is the usual one: minority holders are along for the ride on capital-allocation and related-party decisions made by the group.

09

The industry and the rivals

A consolidating housing market where branded developers are winning share.

ABREL competes in the premium end of Indian residential development — a market that has, over the past decade, consolidated toward large, trusted, listed developers. The regulatory clean-up (RERA), the shake-out of weak builders after the 2018 credit crunch, and buyers' flight to safety have all pushed share toward a handful of well-capitalised names. Birla Estates is a relatively new entrant trying to muscle into that top tier on the strength of its land and its brand.

ABREL's decision to exit that arena and compete instead against Lodha, Godrej and Oberoi is, in one move, a statement about which game it thinks is worth playing.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Indian housing is in a multi-year up-cycle, and the branded, listed developers are taking share from the long tail of weak local builders — a rising tide that lifts the whole top tier, ABREL included. But within that tier the competition is intense and location-specific: ABREL is a challenger in each of its markets, not the incumbent. Its edge is not scale (the leaders dwarf it) but a specific, hard-to-replicate asset — prime Mumbai mill land — married to a top-three-calibre brand. The bet is that this lets a newcomer punch above its weight; the risk is that scaling a development business against entrenched, land-rich rivals is exactly where ambitious builders have historically over-extended.

The real gauges are pre-sales, collections, the land bank, and net debt.

On valuation, ABREL cannot be read off a simple price-to-earnings multiple — with recognised earnings depressed by the accounting lag, a P/E is almost meaningless right now. The market values it instead as a sum-of-the-parts / embedded-value story: the worth of the land bank (above all Worli), plus the capitalised value of the pre-sales pipeline, plus the ITC cash, net of debt. At a market capitalisation of roughly ₹16,000 Cr (approx), the debate is whether that embedded value is being sensibly priced or already generously assumed.

The one metric that settles the thesis

For ABREL, the number that proves or breaks the case is the trajectory of Birla Estates' pre-sales — the annual booking value, and its growth — read together with the new-launch and land-monetisation pipeline that feeds it and the net-debt line that shows whether the ramp is being funded prudently. If pre-sales keep compounding from the FY26 ~₹8,136 Cr base, with fresh Worli and out-of-Mumbai launches replenishing the pipeline and net debt falling as the ITC cash lands, the embedded-value case is being validated in real time and the recognised revenue will follow in later years. If pre-sales stall, launches get stuck in approvals, or the land value proves already fully priced, the story loses its engine — and a company valued on a promise of conversion, rather than on current profit, has little to fall back on. Bookings are the scoreboard; recognised revenue is the delayed replay.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A land-rich, brand-backed developer being asked to prove it can build at scale.

ABREL enters its next phase with a clear identity for the first time: a real-estate developer, backed by a top-tier group, sitting on a genuinely valuable land bank, with a war chest of paper-sale cash and record bookings behind it. The honest question — the one time this primer will use that word — is whether a company that only recently became a full-scale developer can execute like the incumbents it now competes with, and whether the land value that drives the re-rating is still available to be captured or already sits in the price.

The bull case

  • A rare land bank — prime Mumbai mill land (Worli / Birla Niyaara) plus growing Bengaluru, NCR and Pune parcels, held cheaply and being converted at today's premium prices.
  • Record, accelerating pre-sales — ~₹8,136 Cr in FY26 with a +69% QoQ Q4; the forward engine is clearly gaining speed.
  • A clean funding event — ₹3,498 Cr of ITC cash to cut debt and refill the land pipeline as the ramp scales.
  • Brand + group capital — the Aditya Birla name sells faster and at a premium, and the group balance sheet funds land and construction cheaply.
  • Simplification — exiting paper turns a messy conglomerate into a focused developer the market can value on one basis.

The bear case

  • Execution unproven at scale — turning a land bank and a brand into delivered, profitable projects is hard; Birla Estates is younger and smaller than Lodha, DLF or Oberoi.
  • Land value may be in the price — the re-rating already assumes a rich value for the mill land; the unlock must keep delivering just to stand still.
  • Messy sum-of-the-parts — a lagging revenue line, a residual textile leg, discontinued paper and heavy investment make the accounts genuinely hard to read.
  • Capital intensity & cycle — development is cash-hungry and property-cyclical; approvals delays, a housing slowdown or rate shifts can stall bookings and strain the balance sheet.
  • Promoter-driven — capital allocation and related-party choices rest with the group; minorities ride along.
The bottom line

ABREL is one of the more literal re-inventions on the Indian market: a 128-year-old textiles-and-paper company that sold its steady paper mill to a rival and re-pointed its entire identity at the land beneath its old factories. The asset at the centre — cheaply-held, superbly-located urban land, chief among it the Worli parcel becoming Birla Niyaara — is real and rare, and the Aditya Birla brand turns it into fast, premium bookings. What is not yet proven is scale execution: that a young developer can keep converting that land into delivered towers, quarter after quarter, against seasoned rivals, without the land value having already been fully claimed by the share price. The next few years answer it in one checkable figure watched every quarter — the pace at which Birla Estates' pre-sales compound off the FY26 ~₹8,136 Cr base, and how quickly the Worli and other mill-land launches convert into booked, collected rupees. Track the bookings line; it is where the land, the brand and the pivot all finally cash out.
