00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Affle 3i sells outcomes, not advertising space. When an app maker or a brand wants more people to install its app, sign up, or buy something, Affle uses its data and AI to find the right mobile users, show them the right ad, and drive them to actually do the thing — and the advertiser pays only when a real, verified conversion happens. That pay-for-outcome pricing is called CPCU (Cost Per Converted User), and it is the spine of the whole company. In FY26 (year ended March 2026), Affle delivered 45.6 crore converted users and about 99.5% of its revenue came through CPCU.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A fast-growing, cash-rich digital business. Revenue climbed from ~₹1,082 Cr (FY22) to ₹2,709 Cr (FY26), up ~19.5% year on year; EBITDA margin is ~22.5% and rising; profit after tax was ₹455 Cr. It carries almost no debt (~₹15 Cr) against ~₹3,600 Cr of reserves — a net-cash balance sheet funding its own growth.
  • Emerging markets are the home turf, not an afterthought. Unlike a typical Indian IT exporter, Affle earns ~71.6% of revenue from India and other emerging markets and only ~28.4% from developed markets. Its edge is data depth on the "next billion" mobile users — vernacular, low-cost-device, first-time-online consumers whom global platforms model less precisely.
  • The growth is a volume story, not a price story. Converted-user volume has compounded ~24% a year since FY22 while revenue per converted user has barely moved (~₹55 to ~₹59). Affle grows by turning more scrolls into conversions, not by charging more per conversion — a distinction that matters enormously for how you read the ~47× multiple.
In plain terms

Picture a toll booth that collects a coin only when a traveller actually arrives at a destination — never for the ones who merely drive past. Affle is that booth for mobile marketing: it doesn't get paid for showing an ad, only for the person who installs, signs up, or buys at the other end. In FY26 that booth clicked over ~1.25 million times a day — 45.6 crore times across the year — at roughly ₹59 a click. The business rises or falls on two things: keeping that meter turning faster, and keeping Apple's and Google's privacy changes from jamming the machine that decides which traveller to send down the road.

01

Mobile ad-tech 101: how performance advertising works

No marketing background needed — just the four ideas that make the money move.

Digital advertising splits into two philosophies. Brand advertising pays to be seen — you buy impressions and hope awareness follows. Performance advertising pays for an action — an install, a purchase, a sign-up — and treats an ad as a machine for producing measurable results. Affle lives entirely in the second world. To follow the business you need four concepts.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for Affle)
ImpressionOne showing of an ad to one user. The old ad economy is priced per thousand impressions (CPM). Affle deliberately does not sell this way — an impression that leads nowhere earns it nothing.
ConversionThe action the advertiser actually wants: an app install, a first purchase, a registration, a completed transaction. This is Affle's unit of value — the "converted user".
TargetingDeciding which user, on which device, at which moment, is likely to convert. This is where data and AI do the work — and where privacy rules bite.
CPCU"Cost Per Converted User" — Affle's signature pricing. The advertiser pays a fixed price for each verified conversion delivered. No conversion, no charge.
Why "pay only for conversions" is such a big deal

Most of the ad industry sells probability: you buy a million impressions and hope some fraction convert, carrying all the risk that they don't. CPCU flips that. Affle absorbs the risk of wasted ad spend — it buys the impressions, runs the targeting, eats the misses — and only bills the advertiser for the hits. That alignment is why CPCU is a genuinely different product from a Google or Meta ad buy, and why brands that care about return-on-ad-spend gravitate to it. The flip side: Affle must be better than the advertiser at predicting conversions, or it loses money on every campaign. The entire company is a bet that its data and models beat the buyer's own.

Why mobile, and why emerging markets

  • Mobile is where the next billion users live. In India, Indonesia, the Middle East, Africa and Latin America, the smartphone is the first and only computer most people own. App installs and in-app purchases are the digital economy — exactly the actions CPCU is built to drive.
  • Data depth is the moat. Affle's platform touches over 3 billion connected devices and has spent a decade accumulating signals on how emerging-market consumers behave — including vernacular, low-bandwidth and low-cost-device patterns that global models capture less well. More conversions feed more data, which sharpens targeting, which wins more campaigns: a compounding loop.
  • Formalisation tailwind. As small and mid-size app businesses across these markets shift ad budgets from guesswork to measurable performance channels, the addressable pool of CPCU spend keeps widening.

The business model: the CPCU meter and the data flywheel

One diagram, read once, and the whole engine clicks into place.

Affle sits in the middle of a chain that runs from an advertiser who wants results to a real user who takes an action. Its job at every step is prediction: guess who will convert, reach them cost-effectively, and prove the conversion happened so it can bill for it.

Everything left of the last node is cost and risk that Affle carries. Revenue exists only when the green node fires. That is the whole reason the model is unusual.

The two-sided platform stack

Affle groups its technology into two platforms, though the split is lopsided:

  • Consumer platform (~98–99% of revenue). The CPCU business — acquiring new users for advertisers and re-engaging existing ones. This is built from a family of acquired and in-house engines (Appnext, Jampp, mediasmart, RevX, Vizury, YouAppi, MAAS) stitched into one "Affle2.0" stack.
  • Enterprise platform (small). Tools for enterprises to run their own mobile campaigns and analytics. A minor line today, but a foothold for higher-margin software relationships.
The flywheel that makes prediction cheaper over time

Each conversion Affle delivers teaches its models something: which signals precede an install, which users churn, which creative works for which audience in which language. Because it runs campaigns across many verticals and markets, those lessons cross-pollinate — a pattern learned in gaming can improve fintech targeting. The result is a self-reinforcing loop: more converted users produce more data, better data lifts the conversion rate, a higher conversion rate wins more advertiser budget, which produces more converted users. Affle calls the strategy building "self-reinforcing data flywheels" across its verticals; the practical payoff is that its cost to produce a conversion should fall as scale grows, which is exactly what the rising EBITDA margin (22.5% in FY26, up ~120 bps) has been showing.

03

The company and who controls it

A founder-led platform, a Singapore hub, and a 2025 rebrand into its "third decade".

Affle is a founder-run technology business. Anuj Khanna Sohum — Chairman, Managing Director and CEO, based in Singapore — is the through-line: he and co-founders acquired the corporate shell in 2006, built a mobile-marketing platform through the smartphone boom, and listed it in 2019 in what became one of that year's most sought-after Indian IPOs. In April 2025 the company changed its legal name from Affle (India) Limited to Affle 3i Limited, and the ticker on the NSE reflects that from May 2025.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters hold ~54.9% (via Affle Holdings and the founders), down from ~59.9% in 2023 as capital was raised for growth. On conversion of the newly issued warrants, promoter holding is set to rise toward ~57%.
  • Founder concentration is real. Anuj Khanna Sohum is founder, largest shareholder and chief executive at once — decisive for strategy and M&A, but a key-person dependency worth naming.
  • Net-cash balance sheet: ~₹15 Cr of debt against ~₹3,600 Cr of reserves. Acquisitions have been funded largely from cash and equity, and the ₹1,100 Cr warrant raise is explicitly earmarked to keep the roll-up going.
04

The acquisition roll-up

Affle grows by buying capability — a strategy that is both a strength and a liability.

A striking amount of Affle's platform was bought, not built from scratch. Over eight years it has folded in commerce, programmatic, app-recommendation and gaming-ad engines from around the world, then wired them into one CPCU stack under the "Affle2.0" umbrella. Understanding the roll-up explains both the breadth of the platform and the goodwill sitting on the balance sheet.

Why the roll-up cuts both ways

Buying rather than building let Affle assemble a global, multi-format platform in years instead of a decade, and each purchase brought advertisers, engineers and data at once. But a serial acquirer accumulates two quieter liabilities. First, goodwill and intangibles pile up on the balance sheet; if an acquired platform underperforms, those values can be written down, denting reported profit. Second, integration is never free — merging distinct codebases, teams and data pipelines into one CPCU engine is ongoing work, and the returns depend on it actually happening rather than the acquisitions running as loosely-connected islands. The ₹1,100 Cr warrant raise signals the roll-up is not finished, which keeps both the upside (more capability, more reach) and the risk (more goodwill, more integration) live.

The EFGH verticals — everyday app economies

Affle organises its advertiser demand around consumer categories it labels "EFGH" and adjacencies. In plain terms, these are the app businesses that most need new users and repeat buyers:

  • E — E-commerce, EdTech & entertainment: shopping apps, learning apps, streaming/OTT — high install and repeat-purchase demand.
  • F — Fintech, FMCG & foodtech: payments, lending, consumer goods and food-delivery apps chasing sign-ups and first transactions.
  • G — Gaming & government/groceries: mobile gaming (a huge install-and-monetise category, reinforced by the YouAppi deal) plus public-service and grocery apps.
  • H — Healthtech, hospitality & home utilities: health, travel and services apps rounding out the demand base.
The concentration to watch: ~99.5% of revenue rides on a single pricing model (CPCU) and ~98–99% on a single platform (consumer). That focus is a strength — it is what Affle is world-class at — but it also means there is little diversification if the performance-advertising economics shift. The enterprise platform and new verticals are, in part, an attempt to widen that base over time.

The one thing that could appear in no other primer

Split Affle's four-year growth into its two drivers and something clarifying falls out. The competitive question that actually matters

Affle will never out-scale Google or AppLovin, and it does not try to. The strategic test for the next few years is whether that specialist edge compounds fast enough (via more verticals, more geographies, the AI flywheel) to stay ahead of both the giants moving down-market and the walled gardens changing the data rules — which brings us to the one force that hangs over the entire model.

08

The privacy question

The structural force unique to ad-tech — and Affle depends on the very signals being taken away.

Every targeting-based advertising business runs on one raw material: data about who a user is and what they do. For a decade that data flowed freely via device identifiers — Apple's IDFA and Google's advertising ID — and web cookies. The defining shift of the era is that the platforms controlling those identifiers are switching them off in the name of privacy. This is the force that could jam Affle's machine, and it deserves a clear-eyed look rather than a footnote.

What is actually changing

  • Apple's App Tracking Transparency (ATT), from 2021: apps must ask permission before tracking a user across other apps and sites. Most users decline, which sharply reduced the availability of the IDFA identifier — the signal mobile marketers had relied on to target and measure. When ATT landed, even Meta's ad network took a multi-billion-dollar hit.
  • Google's privacy moves on Android and Chrome: the long-running effort to deprecate third-party cookies and restrict the Android advertising ID (the "Privacy Sandbox" direction) points the same way — less individual-level tracking, more aggregated and consent-based signal.
  • The industry's answer — probabilistic and aggregated measurement: tools like Apple's SKAdNetwork give advertisers privacy-preserving, aggregated conversion data instead of user-level tracking. Players have shifted toward context, historical performance and machine-learning models that predict behaviour without identifying the individual.
Why this is genuinely a two-edged force for Affle

The bear reading is direct: Affle's edge is precise targeting, and precise targeting got structurally harder. If the signal keeps degrading, either its conversion rates fall (fewer converted users per rupee of ad spend) or it must work harder and spend more to hit the same outcome — squeezing the very margins that justify the multiple. That risk is real and permanent, and it sits largely outside Affle's control, in the hands of Apple and Google. The more constructive reading is that a pay-for-outcome, model-heavy platform is relatively well-suited to a post-identifier world: Affle already gets paid only on verified conversions (so it is measured on results, not on tracking), and its scale of first-party conversion data and AI modelling is exactly the substitute the industry is reaching for as identifiers vanish. The fair position is that this is the single biggest unknowable in the whole case — and the reason margins and conversion volumes must be watched together, quarter by quarter, as the rules keep moving.

The tell to watch: if a wave of privacy tightening arrives and Affle's converted-user growth and EBITDA margin both hold, its models are absorbing the shock and the flywheel is working. If volume growth slows while margin slips at the same time, the privacy drag is winning — that combination, not any single quarter's revenue, is the real signal on this thesis.

How to read the ~47× multiple

At roughly ₹21,200 Cr of market value on ₹455 Cr of FY26 profit, Affle trades near 47× trailing earnings — a rich multiple by any measure, and it reflects the market pricing in years of continued ~20% compounding. Return on capital (ROCE ~17%, ROE ~14%) is solid but not spectacular, partly because the acquisition roll-up ties up capital in goodwill; the company pays no dividend, reinvesting everything into growth and M&A. The multiple is only defensible if converted-user volumes keep compounding at scale and margins keep widening through the privacy transition. What settles it is not a re-rating narrative but that specific trio from Section 06 — conversion volume, price per conversion, and margin — holding together. If they do, the earnings grow into today's number; if privacy or competition breaks the volume-and-margin combination, a 47× stock reprices quickly.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality performance platform, priced for the flywheel to keep spinning.

Affle is one of the cleaner growth-technology stories on the Indian market: a founder-led, net-cash, high-margin platform that has compounded conversions and profit for years, with a specific and defensible niche in emerging-market performance advertising. The debate is about durability and price — whether the model survives the privacy shift, and whether the multiple already spends the upside.

The bull case

  • Aligned pricing — CPCU pays only on outcomes; advertisers keep the risk-sharing, Affle keeps the loyalty.
  • Data flywheel — a decade of emerging-market conversion data feeding AI models that get cheaper and sharper with scale.
  • Volume runway — conversions compounding ~20%+, with "next billion" users and rising performance-ad budgets ahead.
  • Net-cash, self-funding — no debt, ~₹3,600 Cr reserves, and M&A firepower via the warrant raise.
  • Well-positioned for post-identifier ads — a model-heavy, outcome-billed platform is what the industry is reaching for as tracking fades.

The bear case

  • Privacy dependence — Apple ATT and Google's cookie/ID changes can degrade the targeting signal Affle relies on, outside its control.
  • Very rich valuation — ~47× earnings assumes years of uninterrupted compounding.
  • Ad-budget cyclicality — performance spend is discretionary and falls fast in downturns.
  • Acquisition & goodwill risk — a serial roll-up carries integration burden and write-down exposure.
  • Giants and concentration — Google, Meta, Apple and AppLovin loom large; ~99.5% of revenue rides one pricing model.
The bottom line

Affle is a genuinely differentiated business — a pay-per-conversion platform that has turned emerging-market mobile data into a compounding stream of verified outcomes, all on a debt-free balance sheet. The business risk is not mediocrity; it is that the ground rules of targeting are being rewritten by the very platforms Affle depends on. Everything reduces to whether the CPCU meter keeps clicking faster while the margin holds: from ~1.25 million conversions a day in FY26, the case wants the next milestone — crossing 60 crore converted users in a year with the EBITDA margin still climbing through the privacy transition. Reach that with margins intact and the ~47× multiple looks like patience rewarded; miss it because the signal degraded faster than the models adapted, and the same multiple becomes the problem.
