Page Industries, the company that is Jockey in India

A full walk-through of India's dominant premium innerwear business — how a family from the Philippines turned a licence for an American brand into a ₹5,200-crore, 64%-return-on-capital compounder, why "Textiles" is the wrong label for what is really an FMCG-style franchise, the distribution machine that keeps it ahead, and the honest tension of a legendary business whose growth slowed and whose once-stratospheric valuation de-rated.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Page Industries is the exclusive Indian licensee of Jockey — the American innerwear brand — and also of Speedo (swimwear). It does not own Jockey; it holds a long-term licence to manufacture, market and distribute Jockey products across India and a cluster of neighbouring countries, and pays a royalty back to the brand owner in the United States. Over three decades it turned that licence into the dominant premium innerwear brand in India — the single largest branded apparel name in the country by value — and, in the process, into one of the highest-quality consumer compounders on the Indian market.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It's a branded-consumer business wearing a "Textiles" label. Page sits in the NSE "Textiles" bucket, but it behaves like an FMCG company: a beloved brand, high-frequency-replacement products, deep distribution, pricing power, and financials most FMCG names would envy — ~22.5% EBITDA margin, ~64% return on capital employed (RoCE), ~54% return on equity, and effectively no net debt. FY26 revenue was ₹5,247 Cr and net profit ₹764 Cr.
  • Its flagship brand is borrowed, not owned. Roughly 99% of revenue is Jockey. Page holds the licence — renewed and now running to 31 December 2040 — and pays a royalty (~5% of relevant sales) to Jockey International. That is both the making of the company (it got a world-class brand without building one) and its single most existential dependency.
  • A legendary compounder that hit a growth wall — and de-rated. For years Page grew ~20%+ and traded at a breathtaking 60–90× earnings. Then came a multi-year slowdown: post-COVID over-stocking, a wrenching distribution/ERP transition, weak urban discretionary demand and rising competition from Van Heusen, D2C brands like XYXX, and others. Growth decelerated to single digits, and the stock de-rated. FY26 shows the tension in one line: revenue +6.3%, volume +3.9% — respectable, not the old magic — yet the stock still trades at ~60× earnings.
In plain terms

Think of Page as the franchisee that became bigger and better-run than most brand owners. Jockey International owns the name; Page owns everything that makes the name work in India — the factories, the design for the Indian body and climate, the army of shops, the trucks, the ad budget, the relationship with every retailer. It pays a modest cut to America for the right to use the name, and keeps the rest. For 30 years that was an astonishingly good deal. The question now is not whether Page is a great business — it plainly is — but whether a slower-growing great business still deserves a price that once assumed it would grow forever.

01

The licensing model: renting a world-class brand

The one idea that explains both Page's success and its central risk — read this first.

Most consumer companies you'll study own their brand. Page's defining feature is that it does not. Its crown jewel, Jockey, belongs to Jockey International, Inc. of Kenosha, Wisconsin. Page is Jockey's exclusive Indian licensee — and, in fact, Jockey's largest licensee anywhere in the world. Understanding how that licence works is the key to the whole company.

How the licence actually operates

  • The grant. Page holds the exclusive right to manufacture, market and distribute Jockey innerwear, leisurewear and related products in a defined territory: India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh, Nepal, the UAE, Oman, Qatar, the Maldives, Bhutan and Saudi Arabia. Within that territory, no one else can sell Jockey — and Page cannot take Jockey outside it.
  • The royalty. In return, Page pays Jockey International a royalty of roughly 5% on relevant sales. That is the "rent" on the brand: it flows out of India every year, is a real cost, and rises automatically with Page's own success.
  • The term. The relationship began with a licence signed on 1 April 1994. It has been renewed and now runs to 31 December 2040 — a very long runway that removes near-term renewal anxiety, but a fixed horizon nonetheless.
  • The obligations. A licence is not a free hand. Page must protect and grow the brand to the standards Jockey sets, share design and quality DNA, and operate as a faithful custodian. The brand owner retains ultimate ownership and, over time, leverage.
Why a licence was genius — and why it's a risk

The genius: building a trusted innerwear brand from scratch takes decades and enormous marketing money, and most attempts fail. Page skipped all of that. It inherited an already-iconic name (Jockey invented the modern men's brief in 1934) and spent its energy on the hard, unglamorous work that actually wins in India — factories, fit, distribution and service. It got the best of both worlds: a premium brand and operational control.

The risk: you can pour 30 years of blood, sweat and capital into making a brand dominant in your market — and still not own it. If the licence were ever not renewed, or renewed on worse terms, Page would face an existential problem: it would have built a distribution empire around a name it no longer controlled. The 2040 term makes this remote for now, but it is the reason a superb business does not, and arguably should not, trade like an outright brand owner such as Nestlé or Hindustan Unilever.

The Speedo footnote: Page also holds the Indian licence for Speedo, the premium swimwear brand. It is a genuine business but a tiny one — around 1% of revenue — and does not change the shape of the company. Page is, for practical investment purposes, Jockey India. Everything else is a rounding error.
02

What Page actually sells

Four product engines around one body — innerwear is the core, but the mix is shifting.

"Innerwear company" undersells what Page has become. Jockey-India today spans four broad product families, and the shift in that mix — from pure men's innerwear toward athleisure, women's and premium — is one of the most important growth stories in the business.

The four engines, decoded

  • Men's innerwear — the foundation. Vests, briefs, trunks and boxers. This is where Page is dominant: it holds an estimated ~19–20% share of the branded men's innerwear market, far ahead of any single rival. High-frequency replacement, strong brand recall, and the base on which everything else was built.
  • Athleisure / activewear — the growth star. Track pants, T-shirts, joggers, loungewear. Around a quarter of revenue and structurally the fastest-growing line, riding the global shift to casual, comfortable, "work-from-anywhere" dressing. It lets Page sell a customer far more than just underwear, at higher price points.
  • Women's innerwear — the big underpenetrated prize. Bras, panties, loungewear. Page's share of the women's innerwear market is only ~5–6%, versus ~19–20% in men's — a gap that is, in effect, the size of the opportunity. Women's innerwear is a larger, more design-intensive, higher-margin category, and Page's under-representation is the clearest single growth lever it has.
  • Kids' wear — the adjacency. Innerwear and casuals for children — a natural extension of the trusted family brand, smaller today but a sensible way to deepen household share.
Volume vs value — why "pieces" and "rupees" diverge

Page reports both how many pieces it sells and how much revenue it earns — and the gap between them tells you a lot. In FY26 it sold 228.4 million pieces (+3.9%) but revenue rose +6.3%. That "delta" is premiumisation: Page is selling a richer mix — more athleisure, more women's, more high-price ranges like its "Bonded" collection — so each piece earns more, even without across-the-board price hikes. When value growth runs ahead of volume growth, mix and pricing are doing the work; when they run together, it's raw demand. Watching that spread is one of the sharpest ways to read the business quarter to quarter.

03

The company and who controls it

A three-generation Jockey family that brought the brand to India — and still runs it.

Page is a promoter-family business, and the family's history is the licence. The Genomal family — brothers Sunder, Nari and Ramesh Genomal — had been Jockey's exclusive licensees in the Philippines for three generations before they were invited to bring the brand to India in the 1990s. Page exists because Jockey trusted a family it had known for decades.

Timeline

  • 1959
    The Genomal family's innerwear journey begins — the elder Genomal sets up the family's first Jockey-licensee factory in the Philippines; three generations of brand stewardship follow.
  • 1994
    Jockey International invites Sunder Genomal to become its exclusive Indian licensee. Page Industries is incorporated; the foundational Jockey licence is signed on 1 April 1994.
  • 1995
    Jockey launches in India — building fit, fabric and product tuned to the Indian consumer and climate, not just importing the US catalogue.
  • 2007
    IPO on NSE & BSE at ₹360 per share — the beginning of one of the great long-run compounding stories on the Indian market.
  • 2011
    Adds the Speedo swimwear licence for India — a premium adjacency, though it stays a small part of the business.
  • June 2018
    Jockey licence extended to 31 December 2040 — removing renewal uncertainty for a generation; the stock hits a record high on the news.
  • FY21–FY23
    Post-COVID boom then bust: over-stocking, an ARS distribution transition, weak urban demand and a growth slowdown; margins hit a multi-year low and the stock de-rates sharply.
  • FY26
    Revenue ₹5,247 Cr (+6.3%), PAT ₹764 Cr (+4.8%), volume 228.4 m pieces (+3.9%); Q4 pieces up +10.8% — the first clear signs of a volume recovery.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Genomal family holds roughly 42.9% of the company (down modestly over recent years as the family has trimmed stake). The promoter group has long been the strategic and cultural anchor, though day-to-day management has professionalised, with a mix of family and career executives running operations.
  • Institutional base: the balance is held by domestic and foreign institutions and public shareholders — Page has always been a favourite "quality compounder" holding for mutual funds and FIIs, which is part of why its valuation ran so high.
  • Balance sheet: Page runs essentially debt-free with strong cash generation, and returns most of its profit to shareholders as dividends (a ~2.1% dividend yield even at a rich valuation — see §09). It is a business that funds its own growth and still hands cash back.
Why the family history matters: Jockey didn't license India to a stranger — it licensed to a family that had been Jockey across the water for three generations. That deep, trust-based relationship is a subtle but real reason the licence has been renewed on long terms and to 2040. It is also, ultimately, a relationship — and relationships, however deep, are not the same as ownership.
04

The distribution moat

The real reason Jockey stays on top isn't the brand — it's the machine behind the shelf.

A brand gets a customer to ask for Jockey. Distribution makes sure that when they walk into any shop, in any town, the right size and style is actually there. Page's decades-built distribution network is arguably a deeper moat than the brand itself — because a rival could, in theory, build a good brand, but replicating this physical footprint would take many years and enormous capital.

The three-layer channel — and the ARS transition

  • MBOs (multi-brand outlets) — the ~1,13,600-strong backbone: independent innerwear and garment shops that stock many brands. This is the deep-reach layer that puts Jockey within arm's length of the mass premium consumer, including a big push into smaller tier-3 and tier-4 towns.
  • EBOs (exclusive brand outlets) — ~1,556 Jockey-only stores, growing at a healthy clip (roughly high-teens CAGR over five years). EBOs give Page control of the shelf, the full range on display, richer data, and brand experience — the premiumisation storefront.
  • LFS & e-commerce — department and large-format chains (Reliance Retail, Shoppers Stop-type formats) plus online marketplaces and Page's own site — the fast-growing modern-retail and digital layers.
What "ARS / auto-replenishment" means — and why it caused pain

Historically, apparel distribution was a "push" system: the company sold (primary billing) to distributors, who then pushed stock to shops — and everyone guessed how much to stock. Page moved toward an Auto-Replenishment System (ARS): instead of guessing, the system watches what actually sells through at the shop (secondary sales) and automatically restocks just what's needed. Done right, this is powerful — leaner inventory, fewer stock-outs, better cash conversion, and a cleaner read on real demand. But the transition was painful: shifting a giant network from push to pull meant distributors and shops had to run down bloated pandemic-era inventory first, which suppressed Page's own billing for several quarters and was a big part of the growth slowdown. It's a classic case of a good long-term change causing short-term reported-growth pain.

Manufacturing & supply

Page manufactures the bulk of what it sells in its own network of factories in and around Karnataka and elsewhere, giving it control over quality, fit and lead-times — critical for a fashion-adjacent, fast-replenishment product. It has periodically announced capacity expansions to support the growth runway. In-house manufacturing plus ARS-driven distribution is what lets Page keep shelves full of the right sizes at high speed — the unglamorous engine beneath a glamorous brand.

05

The economics of innerwear

Why underwear is a structurally attractive, FMCG-like business — not a fashion gamble.

Innerwear looks boring. That's exactly why it's a wonderful business. Compared with outer fashion — fickle, seasonal, discount-driven, prone to unsold stock — innerwear is closer to a consumer staple: bought regularly, replaced routinely, chosen on comfort and trust rather than fashion whim, and remarkably resistant to markdown. Understanding this is understanding why Page earns FMCG-grade returns.

Why the category is attractive

  • High-frequency replacement. Underwear and vests wear out and are re-bought routinely — a repeatable, non-discretionary demand stream, unlike a suit or a dress that lasts years. That gives revenue a staple-like base.
  • Low fashion risk, low markdown risk. A brief is a brief across seasons. Page isn't sitting on racks of last year's unsellable fashion; inventory obsolescence is far lower than in outerwear, protecting margins.
  • Genuine pricing power & premiumisation. Consumers pay up for comfort and trust next to the skin. As Indian incomes rise, buyers trade up from unbranded/economy innerwear to premium brands — and Jockey is the aspirational default. That premiumisation lifts revenue faster than volumes (the value-vs-volume gap from §02).
  • Capital-light, brand-heavy. The value is in the brand and distribution, not in heavy fixed assets. That's why RoCE sits near 60%+: Page earns a lot of profit on a relatively modest capital base, and can return cash while still growing.
The structural tailwind: formalisation + premiumisation

Two forces power the category for years. Formalisation: a large chunk of Indian innerwear is still unbranded or sold by tiny local players; as the market organises, branded players take share — a penetration tailwind independent of the economy. Premiumisation: as incomes rise, consumers move up the price ladder — from economy brands (Rupa, Dollar, Lux) toward premium (Jockey, Van Heusen). Page sits at the top of that ladder, so it benefits twice: from people entering branded innerwear and from people trading up to premium. This is why the innerwear opportunity is measured in a decade-plus of runway, not a cyclical bounce.

The growth vectors

Where the next decade of growth comes from — and which levers matter most.

Page's growth is no longer just "sell more men's briefs." The compelling part of the story is a set of stacked, mutually reinforcing vectors — each of which can grow faster than the mature core, pulling up the blended rate and lifting the mix toward higher value.

The vectors, ranked by importance

  • Women's innerwear — the single biggest lever. At ~5–6% share versus ~19–20% in men's, Page is barely scratching a category that is larger and higher-margin than men's. Closing even part of that gap is a multi-year growth driver on its own. It's design- and fit-intensive (bras especially), so execution matters — but the headroom is enormous.
  • Athleisure / activewear — riding a structural shift. The global move to casual, comfortable dressing plays directly to Jockey's comfort-first DNA. Athleisure lets Page expand the wallet — from ₹200 briefs to ₹1,000+ track sets — and reach consumers in a fast-growing, higher-ticket category.
  • Premiumisation of the range. Newer, higher-priced lines (e.g. the "Bonded" collection launched in FY26 across men's innerwear and bras) lift the average selling price and margin without needing more units — the cleanest form of growth.
  • Distribution deepening. More EBOs, deeper MBO penetration into tier-3/4 towns, and e-commerce all add reach on top of the existing lakh-plus outlet base — the same product, more places to buy it.
  • Rising incomes & the long premiumisation runway. As India's middle class expands and trades up, the pool of consumers who can afford premium innerwear grows every year — the demographic wind at Page's back.
Why this matters: the bull case for Page rests on these vectors re-accelerating growth from the recent single digits back toward the low-to-mid teens. If women's, athleisure and premiumisation fire together on top of a recovering core, Page can grow value faster than volume for years — the classic recipe for a consumer compounder. If they underwhelm, Page becomes a slow-growing mature brand at a still-premium price (see §08).
07

The industry and the rivals

A three-tier market where Page sits at the premium top — but the walls are being tested.

India's innerwear market splits neatly by price into three tiers, and Page's competitive position is very different in each. It dominates the premium tier, is largely insulated from the economy tier, and faces its sharpest new threats from a mix of established mid-premium rivals and nimble D2C upstarts.

The competitive dynamic to watch

Two shifts matter. First, the premium band is getting crowded. For years Jockey more or less was premium innerwear in India. Now Van Heusen has pushed up-market with a big group behind it, and global brands are more present — so Page can no longer assume it's the only credible premium choice. Second, D2C brands are attacking from below and online. XYXX, DaMENSCH and others are digitally-native, design-forward, and grabbing the young urban consumer who shops online — precisely the customer Page needs for its next decade. None is close to Page's scale, but collectively they raise the marketing and innovation bar and chip at the premium mystique. The competitive question isn't survival — Jockey's brand and distribution are formidable — it's whether Page can keep growing share against sharper, better-funded competition than it faced in its easy compounding years.

08

The growth slowdown and the great de-rating

The defining tension of the stock: a magnificent business that stopped compounding at 20% — and got re-priced for it.

For over a decade, Page was the textbook Indian "quality compounder": ~20%+ revenue growth, a fortress balance sheet, ~60%+ RoCE, and a share price that seemed to only go up. The market rewarded it with a stratospheric valuation — a price-to-earnings multiple that spent years in the 60–90×+ range, among the highest for any large Indian company. Then the compounding machine hit a wall. This section is the honest reckoning with that.

What went wrong (roughly FY22–FY24)

  • Post-COVID over-stocking. Anticipating a strong demand rebound, the channel loaded up on inventory — inventory days ballooned (reportedly from ~90 to ~120 days). When demand came in softer than hoped, the whole chain had to de-stock, which crushed Page's own billing for several quarters. Growth optically collapsed even where end-demand held up.
  • The ARS / distribution transition. The shift from a push (primary-billing) model to auto-replenishment (§04) was the right long-term move but disruptive in the short term — distributors ran down old stock before the new system normalised, adding to the growth air-pocket.
  • Weak urban discretionary demand. A stretch of soft urban consumption and inflation-squeezed household budgets hit exactly Page's premium, urban-skewed customer. Even a staple-like product feels a slowdown when wallets tighten.
  • Rising competition. Van Heusen up-market, D2C brands online, more choice everywhere — the easy share gains of the past got harder.
  • Margin pain. The combination hit profitability: with volumes soft, fixed costs weren't absorbed, high-cost inventory was consumed, and ad spend rose — pushing EBITDA margin to a multi-year low at the trough. In one weak quarter the stock fell ~15% to a 19-month low.

Why the multiple mattered so much — the de-rating mechanism

A very high P/E is, mechanically, a bet on future growth. When a stock trades at 80× earnings, the price already assumes many years of rapid compounding; there's no margin for disappointment. So when Page's growth fell from ~20% to low-single-digits (and even negative in FY24), two things happened at once: earnings grew slower and the multiple the market was willing to pay compressed — because the growth that justified 80× no longer existed. That double hit — slower earnings times a lower multiple — is a "de-rating", and it's why the stock could fall hard even though the business remained excellent. The multiple has come down from its 70–90× peak toward the ~60× area today — still a premium, but a meaningfully humbler one.

The honest tension: Page is unambiguously a great business — the debate was never about quality. It's that a great business bought at a great-times price can still be a poor investment if growth normalises. "Great business, expensive stock, decelerating growth" is the three-word summary of the Page dilemma. The bull says growth is recovering and 60× is finally defensible; the bear says a mature brand growing ~6–10% shouldn't trade anywhere near 60×, and there's further to fall if growth stays pedestrian.

How to think about the ~60× multiple

At ~₹47,700 Cr market cap on ~₹764 Cr of FY26 profit, Page trades near 60× trailing earnings — expensive by any normal yardstick, though notably cheaper than its own history. The bull frame: this is a debt-free, ~64%-RoCE, ~54%-RoE consumer franchise with a 2040 licence, a decade-plus premiumisation runway, big under-penetrated categories (women's), and early recovery signs — scarcity of that quality warrants a premium. The bear frame: a brand growing ~6% in volume shouldn't command a 60× multiple; the number implies a return to mid-teens growth that may not come, and if it doesn't, the multiple has room to compress further. Both can be right at different points — which is why volume growth and value/volume mix are the two numbers to watch every quarter.

The payout signal: Page returns most of its earnings as dividends (FY26 total ~₹550/share across four interim payouts). That's a feature and a tell: a business that doesn't need much capital to grow can afford to hand cash back — evidence of the capital-light economics. But heavy payout also quietly signals that management doesn't see enough high-return reinvestment to consume all the profit — a subtle counterpoint to a 60× "high-growth" multiple.

The recovery signs

After the air-pocket — is the compounder waking up? The evidence, honestly weighed.

The most important question for the next few years is simple: is growth re-accelerating, or is single-digit the new normal? FY26 offers genuine, if not yet conclusive, evidence that the worst is past and momentum is building.

The green shoots

  • Q4 acceleration. Pieces grew +10.8% and revenue +14.1% in Q4 FY26 — a marked pick-up from the low-single-digit full-year pace, hinting the destocking drag is finally behind and demand is normalising.
  • Mix improving. Women's innerwear and outerwear performed better; premium/higher-priced athleisure and winter products saw strong traction; the higher-priced "Bonded" range gained acceptance — all lifting value per piece.
  • Distribution normalising. With the ARS transition largely digested, the channel is healthier, inventory leaner, and the primary-billing headwind that suppressed reported growth has eased.
  • Margins holding. FY26 EBITDA margin was ~22.5% (vs a mid-decade trough well below that), and management guided to a ~19–21% band for FY27 — recovered and stable, not fragile.
Why it's "signs", not "confirmation"

Be honest about the caveats. Full-year FY26 volume was still only +3.9% — a long way from the old ~20%. A single strong quarter can flatter a weak base, and urban discretionary demand remains uneven. The value-over-volume gap is encouraging (premiumisation working) but also means part of the "growth" is price/mix rather than more people buying more Jockey. The recovery is real enough to matter but not yet proven enough to declare the compounding era back. The next few quarters of volume growth are the acid test: sustained high-single-digit-plus pieces growth would validate the recovery; a relapse to ~3–4% would tell you single-digit is the new normal — with big implications for a 60× stock.

The FY26 exit rate is the most encouraging data in years — but a compounder isn't back until the volumes are.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely elite business — and an unusually clean debate about price and growth.

Page is one of the highest-quality consumer franchises on the Indian market: a dominant premium brand, elite returns on capital, a debt-free balance sheet, deep distribution, and a long premiumisation runway. The investment debate is refreshingly clear — it's almost entirely about growth and valuation, layered over one structural question about the borrowed brand.

The bull case

  • Best-in-class economics — ~64% RoCE, ~54% RoE, ~22.5% EBITDA margin, debt-free, heavy cash return. Textbook capital-light compounder.
  • Dominant, trusted brand — Jockey is the default premium innerwear name; ~19–20% men's share and unmatched distribution.
  • Big underpenetrated levers — women's innerwear at only ~5–6% share; athleisure; premiumisation; tier-3/4 reach — years of runway.
  • Recovery under way — Q4 FY26 pieces +10.8%, revenue +14.1%; the destocking / ARS drag is largely behind.
  • Licence secured to 2040 — no renewal cliff for a generation; deep three-generation Jockey relationship.

The bear case

  • Still-rich valuation — ~60× earnings for a business growing ~6% leaves little margin for error; further de-rating possible if growth stays pedestrian.
  • Structural growth question — is single-digit the new normal for a now-mature premium brand? The ~20% era may not return.
  • The borrowed brand — Page doesn't own Jockey; a licensee, however dominant, carries a permanent (if distant) dependency and pays a ~5% royalty out every year.
  • Sharper competition — Van Heusen up-market and D2C brands (XYXX, DaMENSCH, Zivame/Clovia) chipping at premium mindshare, especially online and in women's.
  • Urban-discretionary sensitivity — a premium, urban-skewed customer feels consumption slowdowns; execution in women's (fit/design-heavy) is unproven at scale.
The bottom line

Page Industries is a structurally advantaged, elite-return premium-consumer franchise that happens to be built on a licence rather than an owned brand. The business risk is low; the valuation-and-growth risk is the whole story. Over the next few years it will be settled by two numbers each quarter — volume growth (is the compounder genuinely back, or is ~single-digit the ceiling?) and the value-vs-volume mix (is premiumisation lifting revenue per piece?). Get those right and a 60× multiple slowly earns its keep; disappoint, and a legendary business at a still-premium price has room to disappoint the share price too. And humming quietly beneath it all: a magnificent Indian consumer empire built on an American brand it rents until 2040.
