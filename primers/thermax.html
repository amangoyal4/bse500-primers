00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Thermax is a diversified energy-and-environment engineering company — the go-to Indian name when a factory needs to heat (boilers, heaters), cool (absorption chillers), power itself (captive and cogeneration plants), or clean up its emissions and water (air-pollution control, water and wastewater treatment). It is a business-to-business capital-goods and EPC (engineering, procurement, construction) company: it sells equipment and builds plants for other companies, so its fortunes track India's industrial-capital-spending cycle. Founded in 1966 as a small boiler-maker in Pune, it is now controlled by the founding Bhathena / Aga family, chaired by Meher Pudumjee, and run day-to-day by MD & CEO Ashish Bhandari.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A steady FY26, with a booming order book. Consolidated revenue was ~₹10,694 Cr in FY26 (year ended Mar 2026), up ~3%; net profit rose ~15% to ~₹720 Cr. The eye-catching number isn't the P&L — it's the order book of ₹13,604 Cr, up 27% year-on-year, with Q4 order intake surging 112%. For a capital-goods firm, the backlog is the future revenue you can already see.
  • The identity is shifting from "boiler company" to "decarbonisation company." Thermax historically sold a lot of coal- and oil-fired boilers. Its defining strategic pivot is toward clean energy and emissions control: green hydrogen (a licensing tie-up with UK's Ceres Power for electrolysers), bio-CNG and biomass, waste-to-energy, solar, flue-gas desulphurisation (FGD) to meet tightening emission norms, and even carbon capture. This is the re-rating narrative.
  • The market already prices it richly. At a ~₹54,900 Cr market cap and a trailing P/E around 80×, Thermax trades at the premium multiple that Indian capital-goods stocks command in an industrial-capex upcycle. The debate is whether execution and the clean-energy pivot can grow into that valuation.
In plain terms

Think of Thermax as the company a factory calls when it needs to make steam, make it cold, make its own electricity, or stop belching pollution. Almost nothing Thermax sells ends up in your home — it's all industrial "plumbing and machinery" that keeps chemical plants, refineries, food factories, steel mills and power stations running. Because its customers only buy this gear when they're expanding or upgrading, Thermax rises and falls with India's investment cycle: when companies build new factories, Thermax's order book swells. The exciting twist is that the same expertise that once built coal boilers is now being pointed at the opposite problem — helping those same factories burn less carbon. That reinvention, if it works, is what turns a solid engineering company into a growth story.

01

What Thermax actually does — heat, cool, power, clean

No engineering degree required — just four verbs that cover the whole company.

Every industrial process needs energy and produces waste. Thermax's entire business is built around four things a factory needs, and it is one of very few companies in the world that does all four under one roof. Understanding these four verbs is the key that unlocks everything else.

The four things Thermax sells — and why a factory buys them

The clever bit: "waste heat is money"

Thermax's engineering DNA is squeezing value out of energy that would otherwise be wasted. Its signature product, the vapour absorption chiller, is a good example: a normal air-conditioner runs on electricity, but an absorption chiller can produce chilling from surplus heat — the hot exhaust a factory was going to throw away. So Thermax sells the boiler that makes the heat, the cogeneration plant that captures it, the chiller that reuses it, and the pollution-control kit that cleans what's left. It's a company built on the idea that in industry, efficiency is a product you can sell — which, conveniently, is exactly the same skill you need to help a factory decarbonise.

The two things that make it a "capital-goods" business

  • It's B2B and order-driven. Thermax doesn't sell to consumers; it sells to companies, usually one large project at a time. It wins an order, then executes it over months or years, recognising revenue as the work progresses. So the health of the business shows up first in order inflow and the order book (backlog), and only later in reported revenue.
  • It lives on the capex cycle. Because customers only invest when they're confident and expanding, Thermax is a leveraged play on India's industrial capital-expenditure cycle — and increasingly on the decarbonisation capex cycle. Good times bring a flood of orders; downturns dry them up. This cyclicality is the single most important thing to understand about the stock.

03

The company and who controls it

From a two-person boiler shop to a diversified engineering major — under one family.

Thermax is a founder-family-controlled engineering company with an unusually respected culture. It began in 1966 as Wanson (India), making small packaged boilers, founded by A. S. Bhathena. Over six decades it added cogeneration, absorption cooling, air-pollution control, water treatment and chemicals — becoming one of India's most trusted names in industrial energy and environment. It listed on the BSE in 1995.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter group: the Bhathena / Aga family (chiefly through RDA Holdings and related entities) holds roughly 62% of Thermax — a high, stable promoter stake that signals long-term alignment. RDA Holdings alone holds ~54%.
  • Leadership: Meher Pudumjee is Chairperson; her mother Anu Aga (a former chairperson and well-known philanthropist) remains a respected figure associated with the group. Ashish Bhandari, an outside professional, has been MD & CEO since September 2020 — a classic "family-owned, professionally run" structure.
  • Reputation: Thermax is known for conservative accounting, low debt, and a strong governance culture — part of why the market awards it a premium multiple. Anu Aga's tenure (including steering the company through a tough late-1990s downturn) is part of Indian corporate lore.
04

The order book & the capex cycle — the number that matters

For a capital-goods firm, the backlog tells you the future before the P&L does.

If you learn one thing about how to read Thermax, learn this: watch the order book and order inflow, not just the quarterly revenue. Because Thermax wins large projects and executes them over time, the order book (backlog) is a preview of revenue still to come, and order inflow is the leading indicator of whether that backlog is growing or shrinking. FY26 was a strong year on exactly this metric.

How the order-book machine works

Picture a conveyor belt. At one end, new orders drop on (inflow); the belt itself is the backlog; at the far end, completed work falls off as revenue. For revenue to grow tomorrow, inflow today must be running faster than revenue — which is exactly what a rising backlog tells you. Thermax's FY26 backlog of ₹13,604 Cr is roughly 1.3 years of revenue already in hand, and the 112% jump in Q4 order intake (helped by a single ~₹1,600 Cr boiler order for an 800 MW ultra-supercritical power plant — one of the largest orders in its history) means the belt is getting fuller. That's why analysts cheered FY26 even though reported revenue grew only ~3%: the future is being booked now.

How to read Thermax each quarter: lead with order inflow and the order book, then execution (is the backlog converting into revenue on time?), then margins. A quarter with soft revenue but a bulging order book is a good quarter for a capital-goods company; strong revenue with collapsing inflow is a warning sign. Also watch the mix — a book skewed toward large low-margin EPC can dilute profitability even as it swells the headline backlog.

The working-capital catch

Big EPC projects tie up cash: Thermax often buys materials and does work before it gets fully paid, and may hold retention money and inventory against long project timelines. So a booming order book is great for future revenue but can strain working capital and cash flow in the near term. A well-run capital-goods company grows its order book and keeps working capital under control; a poorly-run one books revenue it struggles to collect. Thermax's conservative balance sheet is a genuine advantage here.

05

Industrial Products: the profit core

The biggest, steadiest and most profitable engine — the reason the group holds together.

Industrial Products is Thermax's heart: the standardised (or semi-standardised) equipment it designs and manufactures — boilers and heaters, absorption chillers, and air-pollution-control systems (electrostatic precipitators, bag filters, scrubbers, FGD). In FY26 it was the largest segment by revenue at ~₹5,096 Cr (up from ~₹4,530 Cr) and delivered the best profitability, with segment PBIT of ~₹540 Cr — a margin comfortably in double digits, well above the group average.

Why products earn more than projects

Making repeatable equipment is a higher-margin, lower-risk business than building one-off custom plants. A boiler or chiller is a product Thermax has built hundreds of times: the engineering is refined, the supply chain is set, and the pricing carries a healthy margin because the product is differentiated on efficiency and reliability, not just price. Contrast that with a giant EPC project, where a single execution slip — a delayed delivery, a cost overrun, a client dispute — can wipe out the margin. This is why an analyst wants to see the product mix hold up: the more of Thermax's revenue that comes from products rather than lump-sum projects, the higher and more dependable the group's margins.

Why this segment is also the decarbonisation front line

  • Air-pollution control rides tightening norms. As India enforces stricter emission standards, thermal power plants and heavy industry must install FGD and particulate-control systems — a structural tailwind for this segment. Thermax has commissioned FGD systems for government power plants and competes for the large NTPC and utility FGD retrofit pipeline.
  • Boilers are going multi-fuel and green. Instead of pure coal boilers, Thermax now sells multi-fuel and biomass-capable boilers that let a customer switch away from fossil fuels — turning a decarbonisation headache into a product line.
  • Data-centre cooling is a new frontier. Thermax has begun winning data-centre orders (cooling and boiler components), including in the US — riding the global data-centre build-out, where its heat-and-cool expertise translates directly.
06

Industrial Infra: the big-ticket EPC engine

The revenue heavyweight — larger and lumpier, thinner-margined, higher-risk.

Industrial Infra is Thermax's projects / EPC business: it doesn't just supply equipment, it designs, procures and builds entire plants — large captive-power and cogeneration facilities, environment (water and air) projects, and turnkey energy solutions. In FY26 it generated revenue of ~₹4,348 Cr (down slightly from ~₹4,694 Cr) but its profitability improved sharply: segment PBIT roughly doubled to ~₹218 Cr (from ~₹110 Cr), as better project selection and execution flowed through.

The EPC trade-off, explained

EPC is a volume game with thin margins and real risk. You win a large fixed-price contract, then live or die on execution: procure steel and equipment, manage sub-contractors, hit milestones, and hope input costs and timelines behave. Get it right and you book big revenue; get it wrong and a single troubled project can swallow a year's profit for the segment. That's why Infra's margins are structurally lower than Products' — and why FY26's doubling of Infra PBIT was such a positive signal: it suggests Thermax is choosing projects more selectively and executing better, rather than chasing revenue at any margin. For a capital-goods investor, disciplined EPC growth is worth far more than fast EPC growth.

07

Chemicals: the quiet, high-return niche

Small in revenue, historically outsized in returns — and a genuinely different business.

Tucked inside an engineering company is a real specialty-chemicals business. Thermax makes ion-exchange resins (the beads that soften and purify water), performance chemicals, and other specialty products — largely consumable, recurring-revenue lines that fit its water-treatment franchise. In FY26 the segment was small by revenue (~₹758 Cr, roughly flat) but had historically been a high-return business. FY26 was a tough year for it: PBIT fell to ~₹54 Cr from ~₹122 Cr, hit by higher input costs and an adverse product mix.

Why a chemicals arm matters more than its size suggests

Chemicals is the opposite of EPC in character: instead of a few giant lumpy projects, it sells lots of consumable product that customers re-order — closer to a branded-goods business, with better and more stable returns on capital when it's running well. It also diversifies Thermax away from pure project cyclicality and plugs neatly into the water-treatment story (resins are what water plants consume). The FY26 profit drop is a reminder that even this steadier business isn't immune to raw-material swings — but management has guided that Chemicals' margin should recover meaningfully as costs and mix normalise, potentially back toward the low-teens EBITDA range. It's a small line to watch for a disproportionate swing in group profit.

08

The energy-transition pivot — the defining growth story

The single most important reason to own (or avoid) the stock at today's valuation.

Here is the heart of the Thermax investment case. For most of its life, Thermax's biggest business was coal- and oil-fired boilers and captive power — squarely in the fossil-fuel world. Its defining strategic bet is to reposition itself as a decarbonisation and clean-energy company, using the very same engineering skills (heat, combustion, gas handling, water, emissions) to help industry burn less carbon. If this works, a cyclical capital-goods stock becomes a structural growth story — and that is precisely the re-rating the market is paying an ~80× P/E to bet on.

The pieces of the pivot

  • Green hydrogen — the headline bet. In 2024 Thermax signed a global licence with the UK's Ceres Power to make solid-oxide (SOEC) electrolysers in India. The pitch: SOEC is up to ~25% more efficient than conventional electrolysis and can use waste steam — ideal for decarbonising hard-to-abate industries (ammonia/fertiliser, steel, refining, chemicals). Thermax plans to build a manufacturing facility and localise the supply chain, aiming at India's target of 5 million tonnes of green hydrogen by 2030.
  • Bio-CNG & biomass. Thermax converts agricultural and organic waste into bio-CNG and clean steam — a fast-growing decarbonisation niche where it can offer both the plant and (via TOESL, see §09) run it as a service.
  • Solar & waste-to-energy. Solar EPC and energy-from-waste round out the renewable offering, often bundled into a customer's decarbonisation plan.
  • FGD & emissions control. Tightening norms force power and industrial plants to install flue-gas desulphurisation — a multi-year, regulation-driven order pipeline where Thermax has proven EPC capability.
  • Carbon capture. Management has flagged excitement about the Indian government's proposed ~₹20,000 Cr carbon-capture outlay, citing its R&D and partnerships to offer capture EPC — an early-stage but potentially large future market.
Why the pivot is elegant — and why it's a call option, not a certainty

The logic is beautiful: decarbonisation doesn't require Thermax to become a different company; it requires it to point the same skills at the opposite problem. The engineer who knows how to burn coal efficiently also knows how to burn biomass, capture CO₂, scrub flue gas, and build a hydrogen plant. So the transition protects Thermax's core (it isn't left holding obsolete coal-boiler skills) and opens new growth. The caution: most of this — green hydrogen especially — is still early, capital-hungry, and unproven at scale. Green Solutions is still tiny (~₹732 Cr revenue, roughly break-even). So the pivot is best understood as a call option: heads, it becomes a large new growth engine and justifies the premium multiple; tails, it stays a small, break-even side-business and the stock is left leaning on a cyclical core. The next few years of order wins and profitability in Green Solutions are the proof points to watch.

What to watch: green-hydrogen electrolyser manufacturing go-live and first commercial orders; the FGD order pipeline as emission norms bite; Green Solutions moving from break-even toward real segment profit; and any large decarbonisation or carbon-capture contract wins. Progress here re-rates the stock; stagnation leaves the ~80× P/E exposed.
09

TOESL & the annuity model — selling steam, not boilers

A quiet business-model shift that could smooth out the whole cyclical company.

One of the most interesting things Thermax is doing gets little attention: through Thermax Onsite Energy Solutions (TOESL), it has moved from selling energy equipment to operating it as a service. Under a build-own-operate (BOO) model, TOESL invests in, builds and runs utility plants inside the customer's premises — and sells the customer the output (steam, heat, treated water, solar power) on a long-term contract, rather than selling the plant itself.

"Steam-as-a-service," explained

Normally a factory buys a boiler (a big one-time capital outlay) and runs it. Under TOESL's model, Thermax pays for and owns the boiler, and simply sells the factory steam by the tonne — the way a utility sells electricity by the unit. The customer gets guaranteed steam with no upfront capex and no operational headache; Thermax gets a 10-to-25-year contract with stable, annuity-like cash flows, plus it manages the fuel (often clean biomass) and the emissions. It's the "software-as-a-service" idea applied to industrial utilities — and it fits the decarbonisation story perfectly, because TOESL can quietly convert a customer's dirty captive boiler into a clean biomass one and charge for the green steam.

Why this matters more than its size

  • It smooths the cycle. Equipment sales are lumpy and cyclical; annuity revenue is recurring and predictable. Every BOO contract Thermax signs adds a stream of steady cash flow that doesn't depend on the next capex boom — gradually making the whole company less cyclical.
  • It deepens customer lock-in. A 10–25 year utility contract embedded inside a customer's plant is extremely sticky — far stickier than a one-off equipment sale.
  • It rides decarbonisation. Because TOESL can supply green utilities (biomass steam, bio-CNG, solar, treated water), it lets customers outsource both their energy and their emissions problem — expanding as more industries commit to net-zero.
  • It's going international. TOESL has begun building installations in Southeast Asia (first in Indonesia), extending the annuity model beyond India.
The trade-off: BOO ties up Thermax's own capital in owned assets, so it consumes balance-sheet capacity and takes time to earn back — the opposite of the capital-light equipment sale. It's a deliberate bet that recurring, decarbonisation-linked annuity cash is worth more, over time, than lumpy project revenue. Watch how large TOESL's owned-asset base and annuity book grow relative to the capital they absorb.

The valuation debate in one idea

On the face of it, an ~80× P/E for a company earning ~13% return on equity and growing profit ~15% looks expensive — and the bears say exactly that. The bull's answer is that you're not paying for FY26; you're paying for the capex-cycle upturn plus the decarbonisation pivot: a record order book that converts into faster revenue, EPC margins that are improving, a Chemicals margin poised to recover, and Green Solutions / TOESL as call options on top. The whole question is whether growth accelerates enough to grow into the multiple. If order-book conversion, margin recovery and the clean-energy ramp all deliver, today's price is forward-looking; if growth stays in the mid-teens, the stock is priced for perfection and vulnerable to any capex-cycle wobble.

Competition & the moat

Who Thermax fights — and why it usually wins the profitable end.

Thermax competes across each of its verbs, and the competitive set changes by product. Its edge isn't being the cheapest — it's being the broadest, most integrated and most trusted energy-and-environment engineer, able to bundle heat, cool, power and clean into one relationship, increasingly with a decarbonisation wrapper.

What actually protects Thermax

Thermax's moat is trust, breadth and switching cost, not a patent. When a factory buys mission-critical energy equipment, it wants a supplier that will still be around in 20 years to service it — and Thermax's six-decade reputation and conservative culture make it that safe pair of hands. Its breadth (few rivals do heat and cool and power and clean) lets it win whole-plant relationships and cross-sell. And the shift toward TOESL's long-term BOO contracts deepens that lock-in further. The clearest evidence the moat is real: Thermax consistently earns respectable margins in Products even against tough competition, and it wins marquee international work (US data centres, Middle East, Africa, SE Asia — international is now ~29% of sales). The risk to the moat is the newest arena: in green hydrogen and large clean-energy EPC, it faces far larger, deeper-pocketed conglomerates (Reliance, Adani, L&T) — a fight where breadth alone won't be enough.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality, well-run engineer — priced for its reinvention to succeed.

Thermax is one of India's most respected engineering companies: conservative, diversified, family-anchored, with a record order book and a genuinely thoughtful clean-energy strategy. The debate isn't about quality — it's about whether growth and the decarbonisation pivot can justify a premium capital-goods multiple, through the ups and downs of the capex cycle.

The bull case

  • Record order book — ₹13,604 Cr (+27%) with +112% Q4 inflow gives strong forward revenue visibility.
  • Capex-cycle leverage — a direct play on India's industrial and decarbonisation capex upturn.
  • The decarbonisation pivot — green H₂ (Ceres), bio-CNG, FGD, carbon capture and TOESL turn the energy transition into a growth engine, not a threat.
  • Improving quality of profit — Infra margins doubled in FY26; Chemicals margin set to recover; TOESL adds recurring annuity cash.
  • Fortress balance sheet & aligned owner — low debt, conservative culture, ~62% family promoter holding, respected governance.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~80× P/E on ~13% ROE and mid-teens growth leaves little room for disappointment.
  • Cyclicality — orders and margins swing with the capex cycle; a slowdown hits both the book and the multiple.
  • EPC execution risk — thin-margin, lumpy projects where one bad contract can dent segment profit; working-capital strain from a big book.
  • Unproven clean-energy bets — green hydrogen and much of Green Solutions are early, capital-hungry and near break-even; the pivot is a call option, not a guarantee.
  • Bigger rivals in the new arena — Reliance, Adani and L&T bring far deeper pockets to the hydrogen/clean-energy fight.
The bottom line

Thermax is a high-quality, conservatively run engineering major at an inflection point: a strong industrial-capex order cycle in hand, and a credible attempt to reinvent itself from a coal-linked boiler company into a decarbonisation and clean-energy business. The business risk is low; the valuation risk is not — the stock already prices in years of the pivot paying off. The next chapters will be written in a handful of numbers: order-book conversion (does the record backlog turn into faster revenue?), margin trajectory (do Infra and Chemicals margins hold and recover?), and Green Solutions (does clean energy grow from a break-even side-business into a real profit engine?). Get those right and a premium multiple is deserved; stumble on the capex cycle or the clean-energy ramp, and a fine company is left looking expensive.
