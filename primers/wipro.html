Wipro, the big-four IT major that keeps trying to catch up

A complete walk through India's fourth-largest IT-services firm — how a business of people, billed by the hour, earns money; why Wipro has structurally lagged TCS, Infosys and HCLTech on growth for the better part of a decade; the turnaround now under CEO Srini Pallia; the big-deal and consulting push (the $1.45 bn Capco bet); the generative-AI question that hangs over the whole industry; a serial-buyback capital machine; and the standout feature — the Premji family owning nearly three-quarters of it, most of that pledged to charity.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Wipro is an IT-services company: like its bigger rivals, it supplies large global enterprises — banks, manufacturers, retailers, healthcare and telecom firms across the US and Europe — with armies of software engineers and consultants who build, run and modernise the technology those businesses depend on. It is India's fourth-largest IT-services firm, behind TCS, Infosys and HCLTech, with FY26 IT-services revenue of $10.48 billion (total gross revenue ₹92,600 crore) and around 230,000 employees.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The chronic growth laggard. This is Wipro's defining trait. Where TCS, Infosys and HCLTech have generally grown revenue year after year, Wipro has repeatedly stalled — and FY26 was another such year: IT-services revenue actually fell 1.6% in constant currency, versus HCLTech +3.9%, Infosys +3.1% and TCS roughly flat. Through multiple CEO changes and turnaround plans, closing that growth gap has been the whole story.
  • A turnaround underway, led by deals. New CEO Srini Pallia (since April 2024) is trying to break the pattern with a leaner, consulting-and-AI-led model. The early evidence is in the order book, not yet the revenue: FY26 large-deal bookings hit $7.8 billion (+45% YoY), a record pipeline that management hopes converts into growth in FY27–FY28.
  • An owner unlike any peer, and a cash machine. The Premji family owns ~72.7% of Wipro — one of the highest promoter holdings in Indian large-caps, and the opposite of promoter-less Infosys and government-linked TCS. Founder-chairman Azim Premji has pledged the bulk of his economic interest to the Azim Premji Foundation for philanthropy. And, like its peers, Wipro hands cash back aggressively — most recently a ₹15,000 crore buyback in June 2026.
In plain terms

Think of Wipro as a global outsourcing partner that rents out brainpower — the same basic business as TCS and Infosys. When a US bank or a European insurer wants to rebuild its systems, move to the cloud, or now bolt AI onto its operations, it can hire Wipro to supply the engineers and run the programme, paying per hour or per outcome. The catch is that Wipro has spent years being the weakest grower of the big Indian IT names — a mix of a tougher client portfolio, repeated leadership churn and patchy execution. So the entire Wipro question, as an investment, is simpler than for its peers: can it finally start growing like the others? The record deal pipeline says maybe; the shrinking FY26 revenue says not yet.

01

IT-services economics 101: how Wipro earns

No engineering degree required — just the levers that turn people into profit.

An IT-services firm is, financially, a labour-arbitrage business: it employs a large pool of engineers at (largely Indian) cost, places them on client projects, and bills the client at a higher rate. Profit is the gap between what it pays its people and what it charges for them — multiplied across hundreds of thousands of employees. Wipro plays exactly this game as TCS and Infosys; the difference is in the results, not the model. A handful of numbers drive the whole machine.

The only levers you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters)
HeadcountThe number of billable employees — the raw capacity. Wipro ended FY26 with ~230,000 people. More heads can mean more revenue, but only if they're billed. Wipro's headcount, like the sector's, has been flat-to-down as AI lifts productivity.
UtilisationThe share of employees actually working on (billable) client projects vs sitting "on the bench". Higher utilisation = more revenue on the same payroll — a key margin lever, and one Wipro has leaned on hard to hold margins while revenue stalled.
Billing rate / pricingWhat the client pays per hour or per outcome. Rises with skill (cloud, AI, data, consulting) and falls when clients squeeze. Wipro's push into higher-value consulting (via Capco) is partly about lifting this.
PyramidThe mix of cheap juniors to expensive seniors. A "fat" base of freshers under fewer senior architects keeps average cost low — managing the pyramid is core to margin.
AttritionHow fast people quit. High attrition means costly re-hiring and training; a stable, moderate rate protects both cost and delivery quality. Wipro's FY26 voluntary attrition was 13.8% (trailing 12 months) — low and steady, one bright spot of the Pallia era.
Constant currency — the phrase you'll see everywhere

Wipro earns in dollars, pounds and euros but reports in rupees, so currency swings can flatter or hurt headline growth. To strip that out, the industry quotes growth in "constant currency" (cc) — what growth would have been if exchange rates hadn't moved. That's why Wipro's FY26 gross revenue rose 4.0% in rupees (helped by a weaker rupee) while its IT-services revenue actually fell 1.6% in constant currency — and the cc number is the one analysts care about, because it measures real volume and pricing, not the currency tailwind. For Wipro, the cc figure is unflattering: it is the number that exposes the growth gap with peers. Always read the cc figure as the "true" growth.

Time-and-material vs fixed-price — two ways to bill

  • Time-and-material (T&M) — the client pays for hours worked at agreed rates. Lower risk for Wipro, but revenue is directly tied to bodies on the project.
  • Fixed-price / outcome-based — Wipro commits to deliver a defined outcome for a set fee. Higher margin if delivered efficiently (and the natural home for AI-driven productivity), but Wipro carries the execution risk. The mix is steadily shifting toward fixed-price as automation rises.

Two structural things matter about the customer base. First, Wipro is heavily concentrated in a few large clients and in banking — it discloses its number of $100 m+ accounts precisely because winning and keeping a handful of mega-clients moves the whole company. Second, it splits reporting into a huge IT Services segment (the ~$10.5 bn business this primer is about) and a much smaller IT Products plus India State Run Enterprise (ISRE) business — so when you read "IT Services revenue," that is the number that matters for the growth story.

02

The growth-laggard problem — Wipro's defining story

The one thing you must understand: for years, Wipro has grown slower than every big peer.

Here is the single most important thing to understand about Wipro as a business: it has structurally lagged TCS, Infosys and HCLTech on growth for the better part of a decade. In a sector where the big-three routinely add revenue each year, Wipro has repeatedly stalled — and FY26 was a stark example. While HCLTech grew IT-services revenue +3.9% cc, Infosys +3.1% cc and TCS held roughly flat, Wipro's IT-services revenue shrank −1.6% cc. It was, once again, the weakest grower of the big four.

Why has Wipro lagged? Three structural reasons

  • Portfolio & client mix. Wipro carried a heavier weighting toward businesses and clients that were slower-growing or more prone to spending cuts — parts of its book (certain telecom, energy and consumer accounts) were shrinking just as peers' portfolios were tilted toward faster-growing digital and cloud work. It has fewer of the very large, sticky, expanding relationships that let TCS compound.
  • Execution & leadership churn. This is the recurring theme. Wipro has cycled through CEOs — T. K. Kurien, Abidali Neemuchwala, Thierry Delaporte, and now Srini Pallia — each with a fresh strategy and reorganisation. Constant strategic resets, plus a stretch of heavy senior-leadership attrition, disrupted client continuity and sales momentum in a way steadier peers avoided.
  • Big-deal conversion. Wipro has often won respectable deal volumes but struggled to convert them into reported revenue growth as fast as peers — a signal of ramp-up delays, deal leakage, or existing accounts shrinking underneath the new wins. The gap between "bookings" and "revenue" is Wipro's chronic frustration.
In plain terms

Imagine four restaurants on the same street selling the same food to the same customers. Three of them fill more tables each year; the fourth keeps re-hiring its head chef, reshuffling the menu, and losing regulars out the back door as fast as it wins new ones out the front. That fourth restaurant is Wipro. The food isn't the problem — the execution and consistency are. Because Wipro sells essentially the same services as TCS and Infosys, investors don't ask "is the business good?"; they ask "why does it keep growing slower than the others, and can new management fix it?" Every turnaround plan of the last decade has been an attempt to answer that — and so far, the gap has proven stubborn.

Why the gap matters for the stock: because Wipro grows slower, it trades at a persistent discount to TCS and Infosys (roughly ~18× forward earnings vs ~24–28× for the leaders). That discount is the market pricing in "execution risk." The bull case is that the discount closes if growth converges to peers; the bear case is that the discount is deserved because the gap never closes.
03

The Pallia turnaround — the current attempt

A 30-year insider, a leaner model, and a record order book that hasn't yet reached the P&L.

Every few years Wipro launches a plan to close the growth gap. The current one is led by Srini Pallia, who became CEO & Managing Director in April 2024 — notably, a 30-year Wipro veteran and insider, a deliberate contrast with the outsider-heavy hires of the past. Under Rishad Premji (Azim Premji's son) as executive chairman, Pallia's mandate is blunt: stabilise the ship, stop the leadership bleed, and get Wipro growing at least in line with the sector again.

What the Pallia strategy actually is

  • Internal stability first. Pallia's early, visible win was stemming senior-level exits — the churn that had plagued the previous era. A steadier leadership bench means steadier client relationships, which is the precondition for everything else.
  • Fewer, bigger bets — a leaner, consulting-led model. Rather than chasing every deal, Wipro is concentrating on large, integrated transformation deals that bundle consulting + technology + AI, playing to the Capco consulting capability (see §05). Decision-making has been pushed down to regional/market heads to be closer to clients.
  • AI at the centre. The pitch is now explicitly "AI-first": a $1 bn AI investment programme, an "AI Native Business & Platforms" unit, and a shift toward services-as-software — selling outcomes powered by AI rather than pure headcount. (More in §07.)
  • Prove it in deals, then revenue. Management's honest framing is that the turnaround shows up in the order book before the income statement. FY26's record large-deal bookings are the leading indicator; converting them into positive cc revenue growth in FY27–FY28 is the test that matters.

The turnaround, honestly framed

A turnaround has two clocks: the fast clock of bookings (deals signed) and the slow clock of revenue (deals delivered and billed). Pallia's Wipro has clearly moved the fast clock — a record $7.8 bn of large-deal wins in FY26 is genuine progress, and attrition and leadership stability have improved. But the slow clock hasn't caught up: revenue still fell in constant currency, and Q1 FY27 guidance of −2.0% to 0% says the near-term is still soft. The bet is that the big deals now in the book ramp through FY27 and push growth positive. Investors have seen Wipro "almost turn the corner" before, so the market is watching for one thing above all: the first few quarters of cc revenue growth that actually matches HCLTech and Infosys. Until then, the turnaround is a promising order book, not yet a proven recovery.

What to watch each quarter: (1) does cc revenue growth turn positive and close the gap to peers? (2) is the record deal book converting to revenue, or leaking? (3) does senior-leadership attrition stay low? (4) does the Q1-FY27 soft guide give way to a better H2? A turnaround is confirmed only when the slow clock (revenue) finally matches the fast clock (bookings).
04

The company and the Premjis

From cooking oil to code — and the most concentrated, most philanthropic ownership in Indian IT.

Wipro's origin is one of Indian business's great pivots. It began in 1945 as Western India Vegetable Products Limited — a maker of cooking oil (vanaspati). When founder M. H. Premji died in 1966, his 21-year-old son Azim Premji left Stanford to run it, and over the following decades transformed a soap-and-oil company into a global technology giant. That reinvention, and the family that drove it, still defines Wipro today.

Ownership & control — the standout feature

Wipro's ownership is the mirror image of its Infosys peer. Where Infosys has no controlling promoter, Wipro is one of the most tightly promoter-held large-caps in India: the Premji family and its entities control roughly 72.7% of the company (as of the latest disclosures around mid-2025). This is unusually high — most of the free float that trades is barely a quarter of the company.

  • Premji family ~72.7%. Held through a web of entities (Azim Premji, family members, and partnership/investment vehicles such as those associated with Premji Invest and Zash Traders). Rishad Premji, the son, is executive chairman; Azim Premji remains chairman emeritus and the guiding presence.
  • The philanthropy angle — this is what makes it special. Azim Premji has irrevocably pledged the bulk of his economic interest in Wipro (widely reported at ~67% of the company's economic ownership) to the Azim Premji Foundation. In effect, a majority of Wipro's economic value is dedicated to charity — funding one of India's largest philanthropic efforts, focused on school education, teacher training and rural development. Premji is often called India's most generous businessman; Wipro's dividends and buyback proceeds partly fund social spending at scale.
  • Institutions & retail own the rest. With ~73% locked up by the promoter group, foreign and domestic institutions plus retail investors share the remaining ~27% free float — a thin float that can make the stock move sharply on flows.
  • Balance sheet: net cash, effectively debt-free at the operating level, with strong free-cash generation that funds both the turnaround investment and the recurring buybacks.
Why the ownership matters to an investor

Two implications. First, alignment and stability: a family that owns ~73% and pledges most of it to charity is not going anywhere and thinks in decades, not quarters — that patience is a genuine asset for a multi-year turnaround. Second, the flip side: with control so concentrated, minority shareholders have little sway over strategy or the pace of CEO changes, and the thin ~27% float can amplify volatility. The philanthropy pledge also means Wipro's capital returns aren't just enriching owners — a large slice ultimately flows to the Foundation's social programmes, which is a distinctive (and, to many, admirable) feature of owning this particular IT major.

05

Big deals & consulting — the Capco bet as the fix

Move up the value chain from "supply engineers" to "advise, then transform" — and win bigger, integrated deals.

If the disease is slow growth, one of the prescribed cures is moving up the value chain into consulting — sitting at the front of the client relationship (advising on what to change) rather than just the back (building what someone else specified). The centrepiece of that strategy was Wipro's $1.45 billion acquisition of Capco in 2021 — its largest deal ever — a UK-headquartered management-and-technology consultancy specialised in banking, capital markets and insurance.

Why Capco, and what it was supposed to do

  • The logic — "consult, then deliver." Capco brought ~5,000 domain consultants who advise banks and insurers at board level on strategy, risk and regulation. The idea: Capco wins the high-level advisory conversation, then pulls in Wipro's cheaper, larger delivery machine to execute the resulting transformation — a single, integrated, end-to-end deal at higher margin and stickier than pure staffing.
  • Doubling down on BFSI. Capco added ~30 new large banking/financial-services clients and deep sector expertise, reinforcing Wipro's biggest vertical (financial services). It was a bet that the future is industry-specific, consulting-led transformation, not commodity IT.
  • The integrated-deal shift. Under Pallia, this has hardened into a strategy of chasing fewer, larger integrated deals — consulting + cloud + data + AI + run — rather than many small T&M engagements. The record FY26 large-deal book is the intended output of that shift.

The honest verdict on Capco so far

Capco was strategically sound but harder to make work in practice than hoped. Integrating a premium, high-cost consulting culture into a large, cost-driven IT-services firm is genuinely difficult — pay scales, incentives and ways of working differ — and in Capco's early Wipro years the promised "consult-then-deliver" flywheel was slow to spin, arriving during exactly the stretch when Wipro's overall growth was weakest. The Pallia strategy essentially re-commits to the original Capco logic — integrated, consulting-led, AI-infused deals — while trying to execute it better. The record FY26 deal book suggests the model is finally producing bigger wins; whether those wins convert to the revenue growth that has eluded Wipro for years is the open question the next several quarters will answer.

06

Verticals and geography — where the revenue comes from

A banking-led, Americas-heavy book — the same shape as peers, with some of the same cyclical exposure.

Wipro sells across most major industries, but its revenue leans on banking & financial services (BFSI) and on the Americas. Those concentrations are its deepest relationships — and the channels through which a Western spending slowdown hits it. Wipro's book is broadly similar in shape to Infosys's, though with a somewhat heavier tilt to sectors (parts of energy, telecom and consumer) that have been under pressure.

07

The generative-AI question

The industry-wide force — does AI deflate the people model or expand the opportunity? For Wipro it's both cure and risk.

No question matters more to the whole Indian IT sector — and to Wipro specifically — than this: does generative AI help these firms or hollow them out? They earn by selling human effort. AI can write code, test software and run processes — exactly the work they bill for. So AI is simultaneously a tailwind (clients hire Wipro to build their AI; productivity lifts margins) and a threat (if the same output needs fewer people, revenue-per-project and the headcount model could deflate). For Wipro there's an extra twist: AI is both the vehicle for its turnaround and, if it deflates the model, a risk that could keep it behind.

Wipro's answer: ai360, Lab45 and "AI-first"

  • Wipro ai360 — launched in 2023 with a headline commitment to invest $1 billion in AI over three years and train the entire workforce. It's the umbrella for embedding AI across Wipro's platforms, tools and client solutions, and for building industry-specific AI offerings.
  • AI Native Business & Platforms + services-as-software. Under Pallia, Wipro created a dedicated unit and is pushing a "services-as-software" model — selling AI-powered outcomes (a working capability, priced to value) rather than a pile of billable hours. This is the strategic answer to deflation: if AI shrinks the hours, sell the result instead of the hours.
  • AI embedded in deals. Like peers, Wipro increasingly bakes AI into large transformation deals rather than selling it as a standalone line. Management points to AI as a driver of the record FY26 large-deal pipeline — clients wanting both AI-led transformation and AI-driven cost take-out.
The bull/bear of AI, in one frame

Bull: AI is the biggest enterprise-tech shift in a generation, and someone has to implement it — re-platform data, build the agents, integrate, secure and run them. That's services work, and it could reset client budgets upward, giving a laggard like Wipro a fresh race where past positioning matters less and where its Capco-plus-AI, consulting-led pitch can win share. AI also makes Wipro's own delivery cheaper, helping margins. Bear: if AI lets a client achieve the same outcome with a fraction of the engineers, the industry's core "bill people by the hour" model shrinks — pricing and headcount could deflate faster than new AI work replaces it, and a sub-scale #4 with a weaker growth record has the least cushion to absorb that. The honest answer is that both are true; the question is the net and the timing.

The signal to track: for Wipro specifically, the tell is whether the AI-heavy deal book converts to positive cc revenue growth without margins cracking. Rising revenue on flat/falling headcount would mean AI is a productivity win. Falling revenue-per-employee with shrinking deal sizes would mean the deflation thesis is winning — and Wipro, as the smallest of the big four, would feel it first.
08

Margins and the buyback machine

Holding margins while growth stalls — and giving cash back as a serial buyback payer.

Two things about Wipro's finances stand out. First, despite the growth struggles, it has held its IT-services operating margin steady — FY26 came in at 17.2% (up ~0.2 points YoY), defended through utilisation, pyramid management and cost discipline. Second, Wipro is a serial buyback payer: a cash-rich, slow-growth business that repeatedly returns large sums to shareholders — most recently a ₹15,000 crore buyback in June 2026.

Margins — steady, but structurally below the leaders

Wipro's ~17% IT-services margin is a respectable, defended number — but it sits meaningfully below TCS (~25%+) and Infosys (~21%). That margin gap is a direct echo of the growth gap: less scale, a costlier consulting layer (Capco) to absorb, and less pricing power all weigh on profitability. Holding 17% while revenue shrank in FY26 is a genuine cost-discipline achievement — but closing the margin gap to peers really requires closing the growth gap first.

The capital-return / buyback machine

Because a people business is light on capital and Wipro throws off far more cash than a slow-growth firm can reinvest, the question becomes what to do with the cash — and the answer, repeatedly, is to hand it back. Wipro has run multiple large buybacks over the years and pays dividends on top. The buyback route is tax-efficient in India and shrinks the share count, supporting per-share metrics even when total revenue is flat.

In plain terms

A slow-growing, cash-rich company faces a choice: hoard cash, spend it on risky acquisitions, or give it back. Wipro (after the mixed experience of its biggest acquisition, Capco) leans firmly toward giving it back — big, repeated buybacks plus dividends. For a shareholder this puts a floor under the stock and steadily shrinks the share count, flattering earnings-per-share even when revenue is flat. But it's also a tell: heavy cash returns are, in part, an admission that explosive organic growth is not the near-term story. And because the Premji family owns ~73%, a large share of every buyback and dividend flows to the family and, through Azim Premji's pledge, ultimately toward the Foundation's philanthropy.

09

Financials & valuation

Cash-rich and cheap versus peers — but cheap for a reason (the growth record).

Wipro's financial profile is that of a stable, cash-generative business that simply won't grow. Margins are steady at ~17%, cash conversion is strong, the balance sheet is net cash — but IT-services revenue has been flat-to-down for years. The result is a persistent valuation discount to TCS and Infosys: the market pays up for growth, and Wipro hasn't delivered it.

How to think about the discount

Wipro trades at roughly 18× forward earnings versus ~24–28× for TCS and Infosys — a persistent discount of about a quarter to a third. Bulls read this as value: you're buying a debt-free, ~17%-margin, cash-gushing franchise with a record deal book and turnaround optionality, at a cheap price — and if growth converges to peers, the multiple re-rates and you make money twice (earnings up, multiple up). Bears read the same discount as fair, even generous: Wipro has earned its lower multiple through a decade of trailing growth, and paying 18× for a business that keeps shrinking in constant currency is not obviously cheap. The swing factor is identical to the whole thesis: does the record bookings pipeline finally convert into peer-level revenue growth? (Note: market-cap and P/E figures are indicative around 1 Jul 2026 and move daily.)

10

Catch-up vs the big-3 — the competitive frame

Wipro sells the same thing to the same clients as three larger, faster, higher-margin rivals.

Indian IT at the top is a group of four selling broadly the same services to broadly the same Western clients. TCS leads, Infosys is #2, HCLTech is #3, and Wipro is a clear #4 — smaller, slower-growing and lower-margin than all three. The entire Wipro investment case is whether it can converge toward the pack.

Why "catch-up" is the whole game

Because all four sell essentially the same thing, the contest is fought on execution, growth and margin discipline — not on a fundamentally different product. On all three, Wipro has been behind: it grows slower, earns a lower margin, and has changed strategy more often. That's the burden. The opportunity is the mirror image — Wipro has the most room to improve and the lowest expectations baked into its price. If the Pallia turnaround simply gets Wipro growing in line with HCLTech and Infosys, the stock could re-rate meaningfully from its discount, because so little success is currently priced in. The bet is not that Wipro beats the big-three — it's that it stops losing to them. Convergence, not leadership, is the realistic prize.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, cash-rich #4 with a record order book — priced for the growth gap to persist.

Wipro is a stable, well-capitalised, family-controlled IT major with a serial buyback habit and a genuine record-deal pipeline — trading at a clear discount to its bigger rivals. The investment debate is almost entirely one question: can the turnaround finally close the growth gap, or is the discount permanent?

The bull case

  • Record deal book — FY26 large-deal TCV of $7.8 bn (+45% YoY) and $16.4 bn total bookings give the best revenue visibility in years; conversion could turn growth positive in FY27–FY28.
  • Cheapest of the big four — ~18× forward earnings vs ~24–28× for TCS/Infosys; convergence to peer growth would re-rate the multiple and lift earnings.
  • Turnaround traction — Pallia has stemmed senior attrition, steadied leadership, and re-committed to the integrated, consulting-led (Capco + AI) model.
  • Cash machine & patient owner — net cash, ~113% cash conversion, serial buybacks (₹15,000 Cr in FY26), and a ~73% family owner that thinks in decades — a floor under the stock.
  • AI reset — a fresh, budget-expanding AI wave could let a laggard win share in a race where past positioning matters less.

The bear case

  • Chronic growth gap — FY26 cc revenue fell 1.6% while all three peers grew; the pattern has survived multiple CEOs and turnaround plans.
  • Bookings ≠ revenue — Wipro's recurring failing is converting deal wins into reported growth; the record TCV may leak again.
  • Soft near-term — Q1 FY27 guidance of −2.0% to 0% cc signals the recovery is still not visible in the P&L.
  • Structurally lower margin — ~17% vs ~21–25%+ for peers; less scale and pricing power, harder to close without growth.
  • AI deflation risk — as the smallest major, Wipro has the least cushion if AI shrinks the billable-hours model faster than new AI work replaces it.
The bottom line

Wipro is the value-and-turnaround corner of Indian IT: the #4 major, cheaper than its peers because it has grown slower than them for years, now betting that a record deal book, a leaner consulting-and-AI-led model, and a steadier leadership team under Srini Pallia can finally close the gap. The story over the next few years will be told in three numbers each quarter — constant-currency revenue growth (does it turn positive and match HCLTech/Infosys?), bookings conversion (does the $7.8 bn pipeline reach the income statement?), and the margin (does it hold ~17% and begin to climb?). Get those right and a cheap, cash-rich laggard re-rates toward its peers; get them wrong and the discount — earned over a decade — endures. For once, the question isn't whether the business is good. It's whether Wipro can finally do what the others already do: grow.

Wipro's whole story fits in one sentence: it sells the same thing as TCS and Infosys — the only mystery is why it keeps growing slower, and whether this turnaround is the one that changes it.
