Greaves Cotton, old engines funding a new current

A full walk through one of India's oldest engineering houses — a company that quietly powers a large share of the country's auto-rickshaws with its small diesel and CNG engines, now spending that cash to build the electric scooters and three-wheelers (the "Ampere" brand) that are meant to replace them. The whole story lives in a single subtraction: a profitable 166-year-old engine maker, minus a loss-making EV arm, equals a group crawling to break-even.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Greaves Cotton is a diversified engineering company founded in 1859 — one of the oldest industrial names in India — that is midway through a hard pivot. Its historic core is small engines: the compact diesel, CNG and petrol powertrains that sit inside a huge share of India's auto-rickshaws, plus gensets, agricultural pumps and tillers, and a large spares-and-service aftermarket sold through Greaves Retail. That business is cash-generative but structurally challenged, because the three-wheeler is exactly the vehicle now electrifying fastest. So Greaves has placed the opposite bet with the same hand: it owns Greaves Electric Mobility (GEM), maker of the Ampere electric scooter and electric three-wheelers — a loss-making, cash-burning growth arm meant to become the future.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The profitable old business is subsidising the loss-making new one. In FY26 the standalone parent — essentially the legacy engine, industrial and retail business — earned a ~₹200 Cr net profit. But the group's Electric Mobility & Other Vehicles segment lost ~₹182 Cr. Net the two against everything else and consolidated profit was just ~₹35 Cr on ~₹3,437 Cr of revenue. The engine business is quietly financing Ampere's climb.
  • The EV arm is scaling fast but still burning. Ampere sold past its 4 lakh cumulative scooter mark, logged ~61,600 electric-two-wheeler registrations in FY26 (+51%) and lifted its share of the e-2W market to ~4.4%. The loss is shrinking — ₹223 Cr in FY25 to ₹182 Cr in FY26 — but it is still a loss, and the path to break-even is the whole debate.
  • The plan to spin the EV arm out just changed. Greaves had filed for a Greaves Electric Mobility IPO and even secured a regulatory nod, then withdrew it in July 2026 and instead raised ~₹530 Cr through a rights issue. How the EV bet is funded — IPO, rights, or strategic capital — is now a live, moving part of the story. The stock trades near 45× earnings, a multiple that only makes sense if the EV arm eventually turns.
In plain terms

Two companies live inside one share. The first is a 166-year-old maker of small engines — the humming diesel or CNG motor in the auto-rickshaw you rode last week — a steady, profitable, unglamorous cash machine that is slowly shrinking as those rickshaws go electric. The second is Ampere, an electric-scooter brand still losing money as it fights Ola, TVS and Bajaj for share. Greaves is deliberately using the cash from the first to build the second, hoping to walk across the bridge from combustion to electrons without falling off it. The reward is a reinvented mobility group; the risk is that the old engine erodes faster than the new one turns profitable.

01

The small-engine business, explained

No mechanical background needed — just where a company like this actually makes money.

Say "engine maker" and most people picture car or truck engines. Greaves is not that. Its heartland is the small single- and twin-cylinder engine — typically 1.5 to a few hundred horsepower — the kind that powers a three-wheeler, a diesel water pump on a farm, a portable generator, or a small piece of construction equipment. These are unglamorous, high-volume, ruthlessly cost-driven machines, and the economics are very different from a car business. To follow Greaves, you need a handful of ideas.

The terms that run this business

The economics that decide everything: the installed base

A small-engine business does not live only on the machines it sells this year. It lives on the millions of engines already running — because every one of them keeps buying spare parts and service for a decade or more. That installed base is the real asset: it throws off steady, higher-margin aftermarket revenue that is far less cyclical than new-engine sales. It is also why a legacy engine maker can stay cash-generative even as new-engine volumes soften — the field population keeps paying. For Greaves, that annuity is precisely what funds the electric adventure. The danger is that as the three-wheeler fleet electrifies, the future flow of new engines into that base slows, and eventually the annuity itself thins.

Who buys Greaves engines and vehicles

  • Three-wheeler manufacturers — the OEMs that build auto-rickshaws and small cargo carriers and buy Greaves powertrains to put inside them. The traditional bread-and-butter customer.
  • Farmers & rural businesses — buyers of diesel pumpsets, small tillers and portable power for irrigation and agri work, sold deep into rural India through dealer networks.
  • Industrial & institutional buyers — firms needing gensets and non-auto engines for backup power, firefighting pumps, and even defence applications.
  • Everyday two-wheeler riders — a brand-new customer class Greaves reached only via Ampere: the urban and semi-urban commuter buying an electric scooter to beat fuel costs. This is the growth frontier.
02

What Greaves sells today: the revenue engines

Read this once and the whole conglomerate clicks into place.

Greaves is not one business but a portfolio, and the pieces have wildly different economics. The simplest way to hold it in your head is to sort every rupee of revenue into one of four buckets — legacy powertrain, aftermarket & retail, industrial & other engineering, and electric mobility — and then notice that the first three make money while the fourth spends it.

The four buckets, decoded

  • Legacy powertrain (engines) — the historic core. Small diesel, CNG and petrol engines for three-wheelers, plus non-auto power. This is where Greaves' brand and scale sit, and where a large chunk of the group's profit is generated. It is also the segment most exposed to electrification of the three-wheeler.
  • Retail & aftermarket — Greaves Retail and the e-spares platform sell parts and service across the installed fleet, and increasingly to multi-brand vehicles, not just Greaves-powered ones. Management has been integrating sales and service, and the aftermarket has been a standout grower (up ~35% in the latest year). This is the annuity, and its margins help carry the group.
  • Industrial & other engineering — gensets, pumps, firefighting and non-auto engines serving agriculture, industry and defence. Steadier, less headline-grabbing, but genuinely diversified demand — and the reason Greaves is more than a three-wheeler play.
  • Electric mobility (Ampere / GEM) — the electric two- and three-wheeler business. Fast-growing revenue, rising market share, but structurally loss-making today as it invests in products, dealers and battery capability. The future is meant to live here.
Why the portfolio shape is the point

A pure ICE component maker would be a slow-declining value stock; a pure EV start-up would be a cash-burning growth stock. Greaves is neither, because it is both at once, under one balance sheet. That is the crucial distinction from every clean comparison: its combustion business is not a stranded liability to be run off, it is the funding source for the electric one. When you value Greaves you are not valuing an engine maker or a scooter maker — you are valuing a self-financed transition, and pricing the odds that the internal transfer of cash from old to new actually works before either side breaks.

03

The company: 166 years, and who owns it

A colonial-era engineering house, restructured for an electric future.

Greaves Cotton traces to 1859, when James Greaves and George Cotton set up a trading and engineering partnership in Bombay — making it, by a wide margin, one of the oldest continuously operating industrial companies in India. Over more than a century and a half it has been a distributor, an agency house and finally a focused engineering manufacturer. Today it is controlled by the Thapar family (through the Karun Carpets / promoter group), and it has spent the last few years deliberately reshaping itself around clean mobility.

Timeline

  • 1859
    Greaves Cotton founded in Bombay as an engineering and trading house — the roots of one of India's oldest corporate names.
  • 20th century
    Evolves into a diversified engineering manufacturer; builds a dominant position in small single-cylinder diesel engines, especially for three-wheelers, pumps and gensets.
  • 2017–18
    Reads the electrification writing on the wall and moves early: acquires a majority stake in Ampere Vehicles, a Coimbatore electric-two-wheeler start-up founded in 2008 — the seed of the entire EV strategy.
  • 2021–2023
    Consolidates its EV assets into Greaves Electric Mobility (GEM); brings in strategic investor Abdul Latif Jameel Green Mobility Solutions; builds a large integrated EV plant and expands the Ampere range across scooters and electric three-wheelers.
  • FY24–FY25
    EV losses peak as the whole industry resets on subsidy changes; the aftermarket and industrial businesses grow; consolidated profit swings to a small loss in FY25.
  • FY26
    Consolidated revenue ~₹3,437 Cr (+18%); group returns to profit (~₹35 Cr) as the EV loss narrows; standalone PAT ~₹200 Cr; ₹2/share dividend; Ampere crosses 4 lakh cumulative scooters.
  • Jul 2026
    Greaves withdraws the planned GEM IPO and instead funds the EV arm with a ~₹530 Cr rights issue; also completes buyout of components maker Excel Controlinkage as a wholly owned subsidiary.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Thapar family / Karun Carpets promoter group hold the controlling stake (broadly in the ~50%+ range; check the latest shareholding filing for the exact figure and any pledge). The rest is public — institutions and retail — and the free float is meaningful, so this is a genuinely listed, widely tracked company.
  • The group map (worth keeping straight): Greaves Cotton Ltd (GREAVESCOT) is the listed parent — engines, industrial, retail. Greaves Electric Mobility (GEM) is the EV subsidiary that houses the Ampere brand, and is co-owned with strategic investor Abdul Latif Jameel. Other arms include Greaves Retail (aftermarket), Greaves Finance / Greaves Technologies (financing and engineering services), and, newly, Excel Controlinkage (control cables and components).
  • The co-ownership nuance: because GEM is not 100% owned by Greaves Cotton, the electric business's value and its losses are shared with its other backers. When you assess the EV bet, you are looking at Greaves' economic slice of GEM, not the whole thing — an important subtlety the headline "Ampere is a Greaves brand" can obscure.
Why the structure keeps changing: a legacy manufacturer trying to fund a capital-hungry EV start-up faces a basic tension — the electric arm needs far more growth capital than a mature engine business comfortably spins off. That is why Greaves keeps re-engineering the plumbing: bringing in a strategic partner, planning an IPO, then pivoting to a rights issue. Each move is really the same question in a new form — how do we keep pouring capital into Ampere without starving or over-leveraging the parent?
04

The 3-wheeler cash cow — and its slow tide out

The single most important thing to understand about the legacy engine business.

For decades, Greaves' quiet superpower was that a very large share of India's auto-rickshaws ran on a Greaves engine. It rarely appeared on the vehicle — the rickshaw wore a Bajaj, Piaggio or Mahindra badge — but under the seat sat a Greaves powertrain. That made Greaves a picks-and-shovels supplier to one of the highest-volume vehicle categories in the country. The problem is that the three-wheeler is now electrifying faster than almost any other segment, which means the very product that made Greaves is being replaced by the very product Greaves is trying to sell next.

The same electrification that threatens the Greaves engine is the opportunity the Greaves scooter is chasing. The company is on both sides of its own disruption.

This is why the legacy powertrain business is best understood as a declining but still-valuable annuity, not a growth engine. It generates cash today from three sources at once: new engines still going into diesel and CNG three-wheelers (CNG in particular has extended the ICE runway, as fleets switch from diesel to cleaner gas before they go electric); non-auto engines for gensets and agriculture; and the fat, recurring aftermarket on the millions of engines already in the field. But the direction of travel for the auto-engine piece is down, and everyone — Greaves included — knows it.

The buffer most people miss: CNG bought Greaves time

The lazy version of the Greaves story is "diesel engines are dying, therefore Greaves is dying." Reality is more forgiving in the near term, because the three-wheeler didn't leap straight from diesel to electric — a very large slice went to CNG first. That intermediate step matters enormously: it kept fuel-burning engines selling, kept the aftermarket flowing, and gave Greaves years of extra cash-generation to fund the EV build-out. The transition is real, but it is a gradual tide, not a cliff — and CNG is the reason the cash cow keeps giving milk while Ampere learns to walk.

05

Old business funding the new: the whole company in one subtraction

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is unique to this company.

Every diversified company reports a consolidated profit, and most of the time that number tells you roughly how the business is doing. Greaves is different: its consolidated profit is a net of two large, opposite forces, and if you only read the ₹35 Cr bottom line you miss the entire story. The legacy engine parent makes real money; the electric arm loses real money; the group result is what survives the subtraction. Watch how far apart the two sides sit.

This is Greaves in one picture. The standalone parent — the engines, gensets, industrial and retail operations — is a genuinely profitable, cash-generating business worth a solid, if unexciting, multiple on its own. The electric arm is a growth investment that, like almost every EV maker at this stage, spends more than it earns. The consolidated number sits in between because the parent's profit is literally absorbing the subsidiary's loss. A reader who wants to understand whether Greaves is improving should not track group PAT alone; they should track the two numbers separately and watch the gap close.

The device that only fits Greaves Cotton: read the two halves, not the total

Most companies you can judge on one profit line. Greaves needs two, held side by side. The legacy PAT tells you how much cash the transition can be funded with; the EV segment loss tells you how much the transition still costs. The whole thesis is the trajectory of that second number toward zero — from ₹223 Cr of loss in FY25 to ₹182 Cr in FY26 — measured against the durability of the first. If the EV loss keeps narrowing while the engine profit holds, the two lines converge and consolidated earnings inflect sharply upward. If the EV loss stalls while the engine cash erodes, the subtraction squeezes the group from both ends. Everything else in this primer is detail hanging off that single equation: ₹200 made, ₹182 burned, ₹35 kept.

06

Ampere and the electric bet

The growth arm that is meant to justify the multiple — and is still losing money.

Ampere is the face of Greaves' future. Housed inside Greaves Electric Mobility (GEM), it makes affordable electric scooters aimed squarely at the mass-market commuter — the rider trading a petrol two-wheeler for lower running costs — plus a growing range of electric three-wheelers. The business has done the hard part of any EV brand: it has scaled volume and taken share. What it has not yet done is make money.

After a flat, subsidy-disrupted FY25, Ampere's e-2W registrations jumped ~51% in FY26 to ~61,600 and its market share climbed to ~4.4%. Cumulatively, Ampere has crossed 4 lakh scooters sold. FY24/FY25 figures are approximate.

Two things are genuinely working. First, volume and share are rising in a market that shook out weaker players after subsidy rules tightened — Ampere survived the reset and came out with more share, not less. Second, the loss is narrowing: the Electric Mobility & Other Vehicles segment lost ~₹182 Cr in FY26 against ~₹223 Cr the year before, even as its revenue grew to ~₹786 Cr. That combination — more revenue, smaller loss — is exactly the shape you want to see from a business marching toward break-even.

Why affordability is Ampere's chosen battlefield

Ampere has not tried to out-tech Ola or out-premium Ather. It has planted itself at the value end of the electric-scooter market — lower-priced, practical commuter models — betting that the largest, most durable slice of Indian two-wheeler demand is price-first, not spec-first. That positioning fits Greaves' DNA as a cost-driven, mass-market engineer, and it is a defensible niche if battery costs keep falling. But it is also the most crowded, most margin-thin part of the market, where every rupee of price matters and brand loyalty is fragile — which is a large part of why the segment still loses money despite real volume.

A genuine surprise: the company most associated with the diesel engine inside India's auto-rickshaws is now also one of the players trying to replace that rickshaw's engine with a battery. Greaves sells the combustion powertrain that moves the three-wheeler and, through GEM, an electric three-wheeler that competes to make that powertrain obsolete. Very few incumbents are this literally positioned on both sides of their own disruption — it is either the smartest hedge in the sector or a company competing with itself, and which one it turns out to be is exactly what the next few years decide.

07

How the EV arm gets funded: the IPO that wasn't

The financing plan is itself a live, moving part of the story.

A loss-making EV business cannot fund itself; it needs external capital until it reaches break-even. For a couple of years the plan was clean and public: list Greaves Electric Mobility separately, raise growth capital from the market, let Ampere stand on its own balance sheet, and unlock a visible value for the EV arm that the conglomerate structure hides. Greaves filed for the IPO and even secured a regulatory nod. Then, in July 2026, it pulled the offer — and raised ~₹530 Cr through a rights issue instead.

Why the change matters: the funding route is not a footnote, it is central to how much of Ampere's future value accrues to Greaves Cotton shareholders. An IPO would have brought in outside money but also sold down and separately priced the EV stake — crystallising value, but diluting the parent's economic share and handing the market a clean, standalone verdict on Ampere. A rights issue keeps the funding inside the existing ownership group, lets the current backers (Greaves and its strategic partner) top up their commitment rather than cash out, and avoids listing the EV arm into what may have been an unfavourable market window. It is a vote of continued conviction — and a bet that a better moment to monetise Ampere lies ahead.

What the pivot signals — read carefully

Withdrawing a filed, approved IPO is not a neutral event, and it cuts two ways. The generous reading is confidence: the owners would rather keep funding Ampere themselves and list it later at a richer valuation than sell a slice cheaply now — you don't put fresh rights-issue money into a business you're about to abandon. The sceptical reading is that the market appetite, or the price on offer, wasn't there — that a standalone Ampere could not yet command the valuation the parent wanted, so the listing was shelved rather than tested. Both interpretations are alive, and the truth shows up later, in whether GEM eventually lists at a strong price or keeps quietly drawing capital from the parent. For now, the takeaway is simpler: Greaves is still all-in on the EV arm, and it just wrote another cheque to prove it.

Track the funding cadence: because Ampere consumes cash, the rhythm of capital raises is a live signal. A widening gap between fresh capital going in and losses coming down would be a warning; a rights issue that proves to be the last one before break-even would be the bullish tell. The next capital event — another raise, a strategic sale, or a revived IPO at a strong price — will say more about the EV thesis than any single quarter of scooter volumes.
08

The aftermarket annuity and the quiet diversification

The steadier, less-watched half that keeps the group honest.

Beneath the loud EV story runs a quieter, more dependable one: the aftermarket and retail annuity, plus a deliberate spread into non-auto engineering. These are the parts of Greaves that do not depend on winning a scooter price war — they compound slowly, and they are a large reason the standalone parent stays comfortably profitable while the EV arm burns cash.

The aftermarket works on a simple, powerful mechanism. Every engine Greaves has ever sold is a future customer for spares and service — filters, pistons, gaskets, maintenance — for years after the sale. Through Greaves Retail and its e-spares platform, the company monetises that installed base, and it has been broadening the offer to service multi-brand vehicles and parts, not only Greaves-powered ones. The result has been one of the group's fastest-growing, highest-margin lines — aftermarket grew roughly 35% in the latest year as sales and service operations were integrated. This is the closest thing Greaves has to a recurring, cycle-resistant profit stream.

Why the annuity is the ballast

New-engine sales are cyclical and, in the auto segment, structurally softening. The aftermarket is neither — a diesel three-wheeler engine sold five years ago still needs servicing whether or not anyone buys a new one this quarter. That steadiness is what lets Greaves keep funding a loss-making EV arm without wobbling: the annuity underwrites the adventure. It also quietly future-proofs the retail arm, because a spares-and-service network that already reaches deep into semi-urban India is exactly the distribution Ampere needs to sell and service electric scooters. The old business's plumbing becomes the new business's channel.

The engineering diversification

  • Industrial & non-auto engines — gensets, pumps, firefighting and defence-linked applications give Greaves demand that has nothing to do with three-wheelers or scooters. In FY26 this segment grew modestly but steadily, drawing on agriculture, firefighting and defence orders.
  • Excel Controlinkage — Greaves completed the buyout of this control-cables and components maker, folding it in as a wholly owned subsidiary. It deepens the group's engineering base and adds a component business that sells to a range of vehicle makers, widening Greaves beyond its own powertrains.
  • Greaves Finance & Technologies — financing (which can help retail customers buy engines, spares and, increasingly, electric vehicles) and engineering-services arms that round out the group into something broader than a single-product manufacturer.
The diversification cuts both ways: a spread of engineering businesses makes Greaves more resilient than a pure three-wheeler-engine play — but it also makes it harder to value, because the market must price a slow-declining engine annuity, a growing aftermarket, a stable industrial book, a components acquisition, and a cash-burning EV start-up, all inside one share. Conglomerate breadth is a hedge against any single business failing and a discount for the complexity of holding them together.
09

The industry and the rivals

Two very different competitive worlds, fought at once.

Greaves competes in two arenas that barely resemble each other. In legacy engines, the contest is a slow, share-defending grind against other powertrain suppliers and, more fundamentally, against the electrification of the three-wheeler itself. In electric two-wheelers, it is a bruising, fast-moving fight for share against far larger and better-funded rivals. The second arena is where the market's attention — and the valuation — really sits.

The competitive truth to hold onto

Greaves is a giant in a shrinking pond and a minnow in a growing one. In legacy three-wheeler engines it is a scaled incumbent, but that pond is slowly draining as vehicles electrify. In electric two-wheelers the pond is filling fast, but Greaves is a small fish among whales — Ola, TVS and Bajaj bring vastly more capital, brand and distribution. The entire strategic gamble is to carry the strength from the draining pond into the filling one before the first runs dry. Ampere's ~4.4% share is not a throwaway number; it is the scoreboard for whether an incumbent engine maker can actually earn a durable place in the electric future, or is merely renting one with the parent's cash.

10

Financials & valuation

A real turnaround at the group level — priced as if the EV half is already fixed.

FY26 was a genuine step up: consolidated revenue grew, the group swung back to profit, the standalone engine business earned solid money, and the EV loss narrowed. The market has already priced a lot of that recovery — and, more importantly, a lot of the EV future — into a ~45× multiple. The debate is whether the transition delivers fast enough to earn a valuation set on the destination, not the present.

The one number that settles the thesis: the EV segment's path to break-even

At ~45× trailing earnings, Greaves is not priced as a slow-declining engine maker; it is priced as a company whose electric arm will stop losing money and start making it. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the EV segment's operating loss, tracked toward zero — read alongside three supports: Ampere's market share (is it holding and gaining, not buying volume with unsustainable discounts?), segment contribution per vehicle (is each scooter losing less?), and the standalone engine profit (is the funding source still intact?). If the EV loss keeps shrinking — ₹223 Cr, ₹182 Cr, then toward break-even — while the engine business holds its ~₹200 Cr, consolidated earnings inflect and the multiple can be defended. If the loss plateaus while engine cash softens, the group is left paying a growth price for a business earning ₹35 Cr. Aftermarket growth and CNG resilience are the cushions; the EV break-even line is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A profitable old engine, a promising new one, and the narrow bridge between them.

Greaves enters the next phase in a defensible spot: a profitable, dividend-paying legacy business, a fast-growing aftermarket, a broadening engineering base, and an electric arm that is scaling and narrowing its losses. The real debate is one of timing — whether the EV business reaches self-sustaining profitability before the legacy three-wheeler engine, its main funding source, erodes too far.

The bull case

  • Self-funded transition — a profitable, cash-generative engine and aftermarket business bankrolls the EV build-out, so Ampere doesn't depend solely on capital markets to survive.
  • EV loss narrowing — the segment loss fell from ₹223 Cr to ₹182 Cr as revenue grew; the trajectory points toward break-even, the single event that re-rates the stock.
  • Ampere is a real player — ~4.4% e-2W share, past 4 lakh cumulative scooters, growing ~51% and holding a defensible value niche after the industry shakeout.
  • CNG buffer + aftermarket annuity — the CNG shift extends the ICE runway and the spares/service business is a fast-growing, high-margin, cycle-resistant profit stream.
  • Optionality in the structure — a future GEM listing or strategic deal could crystallise Ampere's value separately from the parent.

The bear case

  • The engine business is structurally declining — three-wheeler electrification erodes the very cash cow funding the pivot; the tide is slow but the direction is not in doubt.
  • EV competition is brutal — Ola, TVS and Bajaj outspend and outscale Ampere; the value segment is the thinnest-margin, most crowded corner of the market.
  • Rich valuation on thin earnings — ~45× on ~₹35 Cr of consolidated profit prices the EV turnaround as near-certain; any stall in loss-narrowing risks a sharp de-rating.
  • Funding uncertainty — the withdrawn IPO and reliance on rights issues show the EV arm still needs external capital; the route and price of the next raise are unresolved.
  • Conglomerate complexity — engines, aftermarket, industrial, components and a co-owned EV start-up under one share make the business hard to value and prone to a holding discount.
The bottom line

Greaves Cotton has done something rare for a 166-year-old engine maker: it read its own obsolescence early, bought an electric brand before it had to, and is now using the profits of the business being disrupted to build the business doing the disrupting. Whether that works comes down to a race that is already measurable, quarter by quarter — the EV segment loss shrinking toward zero while the standalone engine profit holds its ground. Watch those two lines converge. The moment Greaves can report a consolidated profit that no longer needs the words "despite the EV drag" — the quarter Ampere stops subtracting and starts adding — is the moment this reinvention is complete, and the market will not wait to be told twice.
