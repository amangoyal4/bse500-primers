00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Aditya Infotech Limited is the company behind CP PLUS — India's largest and best-known brand of CCTV cameras, video recorders (DVRs/NVRs) and surveillance systems. It designs, sources, increasingly manufactures and sells security cameras to homes, shops, factories, banks, hospitals, schools and government (smart-city / safe-city) projects, distributed through a vast partner network. By revenue it is India's No. 1 video-surveillance player, with ~20.8% market share (FY25) under the CP PLUS brand.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A fast-growing, newly listed market leader. Revenue reached ₹4,221 Cr in FY26 (+35.6%), EBITDA more than doubled to ~₹566–579 Cr (~13.4–13.7% margin), and adjusted profit (PAT) jumped ~166% to ~₹368 Cr. The company IPO'd in August 2025 and the stock has since more than doubled off its ₹675 issue price.
  • The whole story is China-substitution. India is deliberately pushing Chinese surveillance majors (Hikvision, Dahua) out of the market on national-security / data-security grounds — from 1 April 2026, every internet-connected camera sold in India must carry STQC certification, which Chinese-chipset cameras can't get. That hands a structural gift to a domestic, STQC-certified, "Make-in-India" leader like CP PLUS.
  • It is racing from importer-assembler to real manufacturer. Its Kadapa (Andhra Pradesh) plant — India's largest and the world's third-largest surveillance factory, ~2.2 million units/month — is taking Make-in-India content from ~85% toward ~98%, and it is backward-integrating into housings and lenses. That localisation is both a margin lever and a policy shield. The catch: the same policy is ending its 16-year Dahua distribution business, and at ~115× earnings the stock prices in near-flawless execution.
In plain terms

Think of CP PLUS as the Indian answer to the Chinese camera giants. For years India's CCTV market was ruled by cheap Chinese hardware (Hikvision and Dahua). Now the government — worried those cameras could quietly beam footage to foreign servers — is effectively banning Chinese-chipset cameras and forcing everyone to buy locally-tested, certified kit. CP PLUS spent years building an Indian brand, an Indian factory, and its own certified products, so it is walking into a market where its biggest rivals are being shown the door. The complication: CP PLUS was also India's distributor for Dahua, so it has to grow its own brand faster than it loses the Chinese business it used to resell — and investors are paying a very rich price for it to pull that off.

01

What CP PLUS actually sells

A camera is only the start — the money is in the recorder, the software and the service.

A modern surveillance system is not one product; it is a stack. CP PLUS sells the whole stack — from the camera on the wall to the box that stores the footage to the AI software that watches it and the service contract that keeps it running. Understanding that stack explains where the margins and the moats live.

The surveillance stack, layer by layer
LayerWhat it isWhy it matters
CamerasAnalog and IP (internet-protocol) network cameras — the "eyes". IP cameras are ~75% of the portfolio (Q3 FY26) and rising.The volume driver. IP cameras carry higher value and are where the AI/analytics premium sits — the mix shift from analog to IP lifts revenue per unit.

Products + solutions + services — three business models in one

  • Products — the core: designing/branding/assembling-and-increasingly-manufacturing cameras, recorders and accessories, sold through the channel. This is the bulk of revenue.
  • Solutions — integrating those products into complete systems for an enterprise or a government project (a bank's branch network, a city's traffic cameras, a factory's perimeter). Higher value per deal, more services attached.
  • Services — installation, maintenance, AMC (annual maintenance contracts) and the OnVigil remote-monitoring service — a foothold in recurring revenue.
Why the recorder and software matter more than the camera

A CCTV camera is close to a commodity — anyone can source a cheap sensor. What is harder to copy is the full system: the recorder that reliably stores weeks of footage, the software that lets a shop owner watch 20 stores from a phone, the analytics that flag an intruder at 2am, and a service network that fixes a dead camera the next day. That is why CP PLUS keeps pushing "up the stack" — from selling cameras to selling integrated security. The camera gets you in the door; the recorder, the software and the AMC are what keep the customer and protect the margin.

02

How the business earns: the channel machine

CP PLUS doesn't sell to you directly — it sells through an army of partners. Read this once and the model clicks.

Aditya Infotech is, at heart, a brand-and-distribution machine. It does not run showrooms or an army of salespeople knocking on doors. Instead it pushes product through a vast layered channel: distributors, dealers, and — critically — system integrators (the firms that actually design and install a CCTV setup for an end customer). Win the channel, and the channel sells for you.

The system integrator is the kingmaker: they choose which brand to install for the customer. A deep, well-supplied, well-supported channel is the real moat — far harder for a rival to replicate than any single camera.

The four levers of channel economics

  • Reach — 30,000+ channel partners, 1,800+ system integrators, 40+ branches and 55+ service centres across India. This density is what lets CP PLUS put product on a shelf in a small town where a Chinese import can't easily follow with service.
  • Brand pull — CP PLUS is a recognised name, so end customers and integrators ask for it. Brand contributed ~86–87% of FY26 revenue (the rest being distributed third-party product, chiefly Dahua — see section 06).
  • Service & support — a CCTV system fails silently; whoever fixes it fastest keeps the customer. The service network is a genuine differentiator versus grey-market imports.
  • Working capital — a channel business runs on inventory and receivables. Distributors need stock and credit; that ties up cash. Managing this working-capital cycle (and the memory/DDR component costs that swing with global chip prices) is central to the margin story.
Why "asset of the channel" is the point

You can copy a camera in a weekend. You cannot copy 30,000 partners and 1,800 integrators who trust your brand, stock your product and know they'll get service — that takes 25 years to build. This is CP PLUS's real competitive advantage, and it is exactly the thing a Chinese major loses when it's banned: even a superior, cheaper camera is useless if no Indian integrator will install it, no distributor will stock it, and no service network stands behind it. The channel is the moat; the ban removes the rivals who could contest it.

Aditya Infotech is a promoter-controlled family business. Founded in 1994 by the Khemka family, it grew from an IT/electronics trading house into India's leading surveillance brand under Aditya Khemka, now Managing Director. 04

The China-substitution story — the defining tailwind

The single force an analyst leads with. Read this section twice.

For a decade, India's CCTV market was Chinese-dominated: Hikvision and Dahua — the world's two largest surveillance makers — together with a long tail of Chinese-chipset imports controlled the bulk of the hardware. India is now deliberately dismantling that dependence on national-security and data-security grounds — and a domestic leader like CP PLUS sits directly in the path of the money that has to move.

Why the government is acting

Security agencies flagged that internet-connected cameras — especially those built on Chinese chipsets — could allow unauthorised remote access, covert data transmission to foreign servers, and surveillance of sensitive installations. Millions of Chinese cameras watching Indian streets, offices, airports and power plants became a strategic liability. The policy answer was a certification wall.

The regulatory wall being built around Chinese cameras
RuleWhat it doesEffect
Essential Requirements (ER) normsMeitY rules (from Apr 2024, 2-year runway) requiring surveillance cameras to meet Indian security/quality standards.Set the framework; gave Indian brands two years to certify and scale.
Mandatory STQC certificationFrom 1 April 2026, every internet-connected CCTV camera sold in India must be tested and certified by a government lab (STQC).The hard gate. No certificate, no legal sale.
Chinese-chipset exclusionThe government has effectively refused to certify products using Chinese-origin chipsets — targeting Hikvision, Dahua and TP-Link.Dahua reportedly has no STQC-certified models; Chinese majors are barred from new sales from Apr 2026.
Government procurement preferencePublic projects (smart-city, safe-city, PSU, defence) already require STQC and favour Indian brands.The largest, stickiest demand pool tilts hard toward certified domestic vendors.
Why this is such a big deal for CP PLUS

India's surveillance market is roughly a US$2 billion+ market growing ~15–17% a year, and Chinese-origin brands historically took a very large slice of it. When policy removes those rivals from new sales, that demand doesn't vanish — it has to be re-supplied by someone who is certified and domestic. CP PLUS spent the two-year window swapping its camera chips to Novatek (Taiwan) and other non-Chinese silicon, getting dozens of models STQC-certified, and building Indian manufacturing. It is arguably the single best-positioned beneficiary of the reshuffle. That is precisely why its market share leapt from ~20.8% (FY25) toward the ~40%+ range through FY26 as buyers and integrators pre-emptively shifted to a compliant Indian leader.

05

Make-in-India & the Kadapa plant

The pivot from importer-assembler to manufacturer — a margin lever and a policy shield at once.

Being an Indian brand is not the same as being an Indian manufacturer. For years, much of the CCTV sold in India — including by domestic brands — was really Chinese-made hardware re-badged. CP PLUS is trying to change that with real, deep, local manufacturing at Kadapa, Andhra Pradesh, and it is the second pillar of the whole investment case.

What "backward integration" actually means here

Assembling a camera from imported parts is shallow localisation. The deeper — and more valuable — move is making the components in India. CP PLUS is doing exactly that:

  • Housings & enclosures — a dedicated unit adjacent to the Kadapa plant to make its own camera housings, cables and adapters, reducing dependence on external (often imported) supply. Expected operational by mid-2026.
  • Lens assembly — a CCTV-lens line commissioned, with commercial production slated from ~Q1 FY27, scaling from ~300,000 to ~1 million units/month by Q4 FY27. Lenses are a high-value, previously-imported component.
  • Chip realignment — swapping camera SoCs (system-on-chips) from Chinese to non-Chinese suppliers (Taiwan's Novatek and others) — the change that makes STQC certification possible in the first place.
Why localisation pays twice

Deep local manufacturing does two things at once. First, margin: every component made in-house instead of imported cuts cost, reduces forex exposure, and improves design control — a direct lever on the gross margin that helped EBITDA margin expand ~540 bps in FY26. Second, protection: the more genuinely "Make-in-India" the product is, the more it qualifies for government-procurement preferences, STQC certification and public-tender eligibility — turning localisation into a moat that policy actively rewards. This is why the Kadapa build-out is not just a factory; it is the physical embodiment of the entire China-substitution thesis. The risk is execution and capital intensity: building the world's third-largest surveillance factory and then filling it is a bet on demand showing up on schedule.

06

The Dahua unwind — the tailwind's hidden cost

The same policy that helps CP PLUS is killing a business it used to depend on. This is the nuance most miss.

Here is the twist that makes Aditya Infotech more interesting — and riskier — than a simple "China-ban winner". For ~16 years, CP PLUS was India's exclusive distributor for Dahua, the world's second-largest surveillance maker. That distribution business was a meaningful chunk of revenue. The very ban that boosts the CP PLUS brand is destroying the Dahua distribution business at the same time.

In FY25, Dahua-supplied product was ~24.7% of revenue (~₹770 Cr). Management has said the Dahua business contracted ~80%. Shares are approximate; the mix is shifting decisively toward the owned CP PLUS brand (~86–87% of revenue).
Why the unwind is (mostly) good — but not free

Losing a quarter of your revenue base sounds alarming. Two reasons it is manageable — and one reason it isn't. (1) It's low-margin revenue being replaced by high-margin revenue. Distributing someone else's product earns a thin distributor spread; selling your own branded, increasingly-Indian-made product earns a full manufacturer's margin. Swapping Dahua rupees for CP PLUS rupees is margin-accretive — which is exactly why EBITDA more than doubled in FY26 even as the Dahua line shrank. (2) The demand doesn't disappear — customers who used to buy Dahua through CP PLUS can now buy CP PLUS instead. But (3) the transition must be executed cleanly: if CP PLUS-brand growth ever lagged the Dahua decline, top-line growth would stall. So far it hasn't — FY26 grew 35.6% through the unwind — but it is the key thing to watch.

The real risk to keep in view: the Dahua unwind is a one-time headwind on a shrinking base, but it also removes a supplier relationship and forces CP PLUS to fill the gap entirely with its own capacity and its own R&D. Any stumble in owned-brand supply, certification, or component sourcing would bite harder now that the Chinese fallback is gone.
07

The market and the rivals

A market being re-drawn by policy — and CP PLUS starts it as the largest domestic name.

India's electronic-surveillance market has historically been concentrated at the top and fragmented below: the three biggest brands — CP PLUS, Dahua and Hikvision — together held ~70–75% share, with a tail of Bosch, Panasonic, Uniview, Godrej, Zicom and others. The STQC wall is now redrawing that map by removing the Chinese two from the top — and the fight for the vacated share is the industry's central story.

The competitive tension to watch

Two forces pull against each other. In CP PLUS's favour: it enters the reshuffle as the largest domestic brand with the deepest channel and real Indian manufacturing — the hardest package for anyone to replicate quickly. Against it: the same tailwind is visible to everyone. Chinese majors will try to survive by localising through Indian partners (Prama for Hikvision), other Indian brands will chase the freed-up share, and cheap grey-market imports never fully disappear. The result is likely share gains with pricing discipline required — CP PLUS can win volume, but must keep raising the value of each sale (more IP cameras, more solutions, more services) rather than just riding a one-time policy windfall. Whoever converts the policy moment into durable brand and service loyalty keeps the share once the dust settles.

08

Demand drivers and the AI shift

Surveillance is going everywhere — and getting smarter. Both trends lift revenue per camera.

Even without the China-substitution windfall, the underlying market is growing fast. Two structural forces drive it: surveillance is spreading into every corner of Indian life, and cameras are shifting from "record footage" to "understand footage" (AI/analytics) — a shift that raises the value of each device sold.

The three demand engines, decoded

  • Ubiquity — cameras are now expected everywhere: every new shop, apartment, factory, school, highway and toll plaza. India's Smart Cities Mission and safe-city command-and-control centres institutionalise this at the government level. The base of "places that need a camera" keeps widening.
  • The analog-to-IP-to-AI shift — old analog cameras just recorded; modern IP cameras stream, and AI cameras interpret — detecting intrusion, counting people, reading number plates, spotting fire. IP cameras are already ~75% of CP PLUS's portfolio, and AI-enabled cameras carry higher prices and margins. Each step up the ladder lifts revenue per unit.
  • Services & recurring revenue — VSaaS and remote-monitoring (OnVigil) turn hardware buyers into subscription customers, the highest-growth slice of the market and the one that could, over time, smooth CP PLUS's hardware-cyclical revenue.
Why "smarter cameras" is a quiet margin story

The obvious growth story is more cameras. The subtler — and more valuable — story is better cameras. When a customer upgrades from a ₹1,500 analog camera to a ₹6,000 AI-enabled IP camera with analytics, CP PLUS earns far more from the same install, and the software/analytics layer is far harder for a commodity importer to match. Layer on rising services (AMC, VSaaS), and revenue per customer can grow even if the number of customers grows slowly. This mix-shift toward IP + AI + services is the internal engine that lets CP PLUS keep growing margins after the one-time China-substitution boost fades — provided it stays ahead on the technology.

The technology risk is the flip side: the AI shift is an opportunity only if CP PLUS leads it. If global players bring superior on-device AI, or if the market commoditises AI features faster than CP PLUS can differentiate, the premium erodes. Staying at the technology frontier — not just the manufacturing frontier — is a permanent requirement, not a solved problem.

How to think about the ~115× multiple

At ~₹42,300 Cr market cap on ~₹368 Cr of FY26 adjusted profit, CP PLUS trades around 115× trailing earnings — an extremely rich price even for a fast-growing leader. That number only makes sense if you look forward: on FY27 guidance of ₹6,000–6,500 Cr revenue at 8.5–9.5% PAT margin, profit could roughly double again, pulling the forward multiple down sharply. The bull case is that the China-substitution windfall, the mix-shift to IP/AI, and localisation-driven margin gains compound for several years, so today's price is really ~40–50× a much larger future profit. The bear case is that (a) a chunk of the recent surge is a one-time policy re-rating that normalises, (b) memory/component cost swings and channel working-capital can dent margins, and (c) at 115× trailing / ~22× book, any disappointment — a soft quarter, a guidance cut, a lock-in expiry — can de-rate the stock hard. Both can be true at different points: the growth may be real and the entry price demanding.

On the "adjusted" PAT: FY26 profit is quoted as adjusted (excluding IPO-related and one-off items); reported PAT and adjusted PAT can differ, and margin/EPS figures vary slightly by source (Screener shows ~₹368 Cr net profit, ~₹31.2 EPS). Cross-check against the audited annual report before relying on any single figure.

The growth runway

Guidance to ~₹6,000 Cr+, deeper localisation, and a services layer on top.

Management is guiding to ₹6,000–6,500 Cr of revenue in FY27 (roughly ~45%+ growth) at 14–15% EBITDA and 8.5–9.5% PAT margins — an unusually confident outlook that leans on three stacked levers.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine structural winner — priced for near-flawless execution.

CP PLUS is one of the cleaner "policy-plus-execution" stories in Indian capital goods: a domestic surveillance leader handed a large structural tailwind, with real manufacturing and a deep channel to convert it. The debate is almost entirely about how much is already in the price and whether the one-time windfall becomes durable growth.

The bull case

  • Structural tailwind — the STQC wall pushes Chinese majors out of a growing market and hands share to the domestic leader.
  • Channel & brand moat — 30,000+ partners, 1,800+ integrators, a recognised brand and a national service network that rivals can't quickly copy.
  • Real Make-in-India manufacturing — the Kadapa plant + backward integration into housings/lenses lift margins and deepen the policy moat.
  • Margin inflection — EBITDA margin up ~540 bps in FY26 as low-margin Dahua revenue is replaced by high-margin owned brand; more to come.
  • Long runway + optionality — ubiquity, AI mix-shift, VSaaS/OnVigil, exports (14+ markets) and multi-brand (NEXIVUE/EYRA) on top of a strong balance sheet.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — ~115× trailing / ~22× book leaves no room for error; a soft quarter or guidance cut can de-rate hard.
  • One-time vs. durable — part of the surge is a policy re-rating; if share gains prove less sticky than hoped, growth normalises.
  • Dahua unwind & supply risk — a ~₹770 Cr low-margin business is disappearing; the owned brand must fill the gap flawlessly with its own supply and R&D.
  • Component / forex / working capital — memory/DDR cost swings, import content in the supply chain, and channel inventory/receivables can squeeze margins and cash.
  • Competition & tech shift — other Indian brands and localising Chinese majors chase the same share; the AI/analytics frontier must be led, not just followed. Plus a tightly-held, founder-run company (~75% promoter) and post-IPO lock-in dynamics.
The bottom line

Aditya Infotech is a genuine structural beneficiary of India's push to de-risk its surveillance infrastructure from China — with a real brand, a real factory and a real channel to capture the share the policy frees up. The business case is strong and the FY26 numbers are spectacular. The valuation case is the entire debate: at ~115× trailing earnings, the market is already paying for the windfall and for it to become durable, compounding growth. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers — CP PLUS brand revenue growth (is it outrunning the Dahua decline?), EBITDA margin (is localisation and mix delivering?), and the durability of market share (does the share stick once the ban is old news?). Get those right and the premium can be grown into; stumble, and a 115× stock has a long way to fall.
