00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Linde India makes industrial and medical gases — oxygen, nitrogen, argon, hydrogen, carbon dioxide and specialty gases — and builds the giant cryogenic plants that produce them. It is the listed Indian arm of Linde plc, the world's largest industrial-gases company (formed by the 2018 Praxair–Linde merger). Linde plc, through The BOC Group, owns 75%; the remaining 25% floats on the NSE and BSE.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It literally sells the air. The core business separates atmospheric air into its components (oxygen, nitrogen, argon) in refrigerated "air-separation units", plus makes process gases (hydrogen, CO₂) and specialty/medical gases. FY26 (year ended March 2026) revenue was ₹2,531 Cr, net profit ₹551 Cr (+23%), at a ~36% operating margin — a fat margin that reflects an oligopoly and hard-to-replicate assets.
  • The economics are annuity-like. Its biggest plants are built next to a customer's steel mill or refinery and locked into 15–20-year "take-or-pay" contracts: the customer pays whether or not it lifts the gas. That makes a chunk of revenue utility-like and sticky — capital-intensive to build, but predictable for decades once running.
  • Quality business, vertiginous price. Near debt-free (₹79 Cr borrowings against ~₹4,100 Cr reserves), ~18% ROCE, a clean parent — and yet the stock trades around 100×+ trailing earnings, one of the richest multiples on the exchange. Part of that is genuine scarcity value; part is the recurring hope that a related-party/governance catalyst re-rates the minority stake.
In plain terms

Think of Linde India as a toll booth on modern industry's need to breathe. Steel mills, refineries, chemical plants, hospitals, food packers and fabricators all consume vast amounts of oxygen, nitrogen and argon — and almost none of them want to make their own. Linde builds the plant, pipes or trucks the gas over, and collects a fee for years. It's a boring, essential, oligopolistic business with a global parent behind it. The catch for a public-market investor is twofold: the parent controls 75% and has a history of moving assets around the group in ways minorities have challenged, and the shares are priced as if perfection is guaranteed.

01

Industrial gases 101: making money out of thin air

No chemistry degree required — just what these gases are and why every factory needs them.

Air is roughly 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen and ~1% argon, plus traces of other gases. An industrial-gases company's central trick is to separate those components and sell them individually — because a purified stream of oxygen or nitrogen is worth far more than the free air it came from. The main way to do this at scale is an Air Separation Unit (ASU): air is filtered, compressed, cooled to about −185 °C until it liquefies, then distilled (like an oil refinery, but at cryogenic temperatures) into pure oxygen, nitrogen and argon.

Why this is a great business to own

Three structural features make industrial gases unusually attractive. (1) The product is essential but a tiny fraction of the customer's cost — a steel mill can't run without oxygen, but oxygen is a small line item, so it won't risk supply to save a little. (2) It's expensive and pointless to self-supply — building and running an ASU is capital- and know-how-intensive, so factories outsource it. (3) Gases are heavy and costly to transport — you can't economically ship liquid oxygen across the country, so supply is regional, and whoever owns the plant nearest the demand effectively owns that pocket of the market. Put together, you get high margins, high switching costs and natural local monopolies. That's why the global industry has consolidated into a handful of giants earning utility-like returns.

02

The three supply modes — and why "on-site" is the crown jewel

This one distinction explains the whole economics of the business. Read it once and the model clicks.

Industrial gas is delivered in three fundamentally different ways, and they have completely different economics. Which mix a company skews toward tells you almost everything about how capital-intensive, how sticky and how predictable its revenue is.

The same ASU can feed all three channels: piped gas to the anchor customer next door, liquid to the tanker fleet, and cylinders from the leftover output.

Mode 1 — On-site / tonnage (the annuity)

  • What it is: Linde builds a huge dedicated ASU right next to a big customer — a steel plant, refinery or fertiliser unit — and pipes oxygen/nitrogen straight over the fence. The Jindal Stainless plant at Kalinganagar (a 1,450 tonnes-per-day oxygen ASU commissioned in FY26) is a textbook example.
  • Why it matters: These are locked into 15–20-year "take-or-pay" contracts — the customer commits to pay a minimum whether or not it actually lifts the gas, plus pass-throughs for power (the ASU's biggest running cost is electricity). That converts a big up-front capex into a decade-plus annuity with an investment-grade customer. It's capital-hungry to build but utility-like once running, and the customer is effectively locked in — moving suppliers means building a new plant.

Mode 2 — Merchant / bulk liquid (the flywheel)

  • What it is: Surplus output is liquefied and trucked in cryogenic tankers to mid-size customers who need more than cylinders but can't justify their own plant — filled into on-site storage tanks that Linde installs.
  • Why it matters: Higher margin per unit than tonnage but more cyclical and price-sensitive (spot pricing moves with supply/demand and freight). It's the swing volume that keeps the ASUs running near full and captures upside when industrial demand is hot.

Mode 3 — Packaged / cylinder gases (the retail tail)

  • What it is: Compressed gas in cylinders delivered to thousands of small customers — fabricators, workshops, labs, hospitals, welders.
  • Why it matters: Smallest volumes, highest price per unit, most fragmented — brand, safety reputation and distribution reach matter. It's the sticky, high-touch "long tail" that also carries specialty and medical gases.
The one mental model to keep

Picture a single ASU as a refinery for air with three taps. The first tap (on-site) is a pipeline into a captive customer on a 15-year contract — boring, safe, annuity-like, but capital-heavy. The second (merchant) is a tanker fleet selling the surplus at spot prices — punchier, more cyclical. The third (cylinders) is a retail network — small, sticky, premium-priced. A gases company's quality comes from the mix: heavy on-site gives predictability and defensiveness; merchant/packaged give margin and growth. Linde India runs all three, anchored by big tonnage plants bolted to India's steel and refining base.

03

The company and who controls it

A 90-year-old British-founded firm, now a 75%-owned arm of a global giant.

Linde India is one of India's oldest industrial companies. It began in 1935 as the Indian Oxygen & Acetylene Company, promoted by the UK's BOC Group. It traded for decades as BOC India, and was renamed Linde India in February 2013 after Germany's Linde AG acquired BOC globally. The 2018 Praxair–Linde merger folded it into today's Linde plc — the world's largest industrial-gases company by revenue.

Timeline

  • 1935
    Founded as the Indian Oxygen & Acetylene Company, promoted by BOC Group (UK) — among the first industrial-gas firms in India.
  • 1960s–90s
    Grows across India as Indian Oxygen then BOC India, building air-separation plants for the country's expanding steel and heavy-industry base.
  • 2006
    Germany's Linde AG acquires BOC Group globally, making BOC India part of the Linde group.
  • Feb 2013
    Renamed Linde India Limited, adopting the global brand.
  • Oct 2018
    Praxair–Linde merger creates Linde plc. In India, Linde India and the (privately held) Praxair India become sister companies — triggering a geographic/product split and years of related-party scrutiny (Section 08).
  • FY26
    Commissions the 1,450 TPD ASU at Jindal Stainless, Kalinganagar; PAT rises 23% to ₹551 Cr; declares a ₹12/share dividend (incl. an 80% special).

Ownership & control

  • Promoter — Linde plc (via The BOC Group), ~75%. The maximum a promoter can hold and stay listed (25% minimum public float). This is a controlled company: the parent sets strategy, supplies technology and can transact with the group. That's a double-edged sword — global scale, technology and balance-sheet backing on one side; the risk of related-party decisions favouring the parent on the other (Section 08).
  • Public float ~25%. Domestic institutions hold ~7%, foreign investors ~2%, and retail/other public ~16% (as of Mar 2026). Because 75% is locked up, the free float is thin — which amplifies both scarcity-premium and volatility.
  • Balance sheet: essentially net cash — only ~₹79 Cr of borrowings against ~₹4,100 Cr of reserves. The company self-funds its plant-building from internal cash and can draw on parent support if needed.
The parent is the whole story here. Unlike a founder-led firm, Linde India's destiny is set in Guildford and Danbury, not decided independently. The parent decides which projects flow to the listed entity versus the private Praxair India, sets the technology/royalty terms, and controls capital allocation. For a minority investor, understanding Linde plc's intentions toward its Indian minorities matters as much as understanding the gases business.
04

The two segments: gases and the plants that make them

Linde India is really two businesses stapled together — a gas utility and a plant builder.

Linde India reports in two segments. Gases & related products — the recurring utility business of selling oxygen, nitrogen, argon and other gases across the three modes — is the larger and steadier engine. Project Engineering — designing and building air-separation and other cryogenic plants (for itself, for group companies, and for third parties) — is lumpier, tied to the capex cycle, and swings the group's revenue and margins from year to year.

Segment 1 — Gases & related products (the utility)

  • What it is: the day-to-day sale of atmospheric, process, specialty and medical gases across on-site, merchant and packaged modes. This is the annuity heart of the business — the take-or-pay tonnage contracts, the tanker network, the cylinder distribution.
  • Why it matters: it's the recurring, defensive revenue that justifies a utility-like valuation. Margins here are driven by plant utilisation, power-cost pass-throughs and merchant pricing.

Segment 2 — Project Engineering (the builder)

  • What it is: Linde India designs and builds cryogenic air-separation units and other process plants. Critically, in the post-merger India carve-up, Linde India got exclusivity over the group's Project Engineering business in India (Praxair India took HyCO / hydrogen / CO / CO₂).
  • Why it matters: it makes Linde India the group's Indian engineering hub — building plants both for its own gas business and for third parties/exports — but it's project-timing dependent: revenue and margins jump when large plants are executed and dip between them. This is a big reason reported earnings (and the P/E) can look distorted quarter to quarter.
Why the split explains the wobbly financials

When you see Linde India's revenue fall one year and rise another with no obvious pattern, the culprit is usually Project Engineering. A gas utility grows steadily; a plant-builder's revenue is lumpy — it recognises big chunks as major projects hit milestones, then goes quiet. So FY24 revenue (₹2,769 Cr) was actually higher than FY25 (₹2,485 Cr) and FY26 (₹2,531 Cr), largely on project timing — even as profit kept climbing (PAT ₹426 → ₹448 → ₹551 Cr) because the higher-margin gases mix and operating efficiency improved. The takeaway: judge Linde India on profit and margin trends and the gases annuity, not on any single year's top line.

05

The industry: an industrial-gases oligopoly

Globally three giants; in India, essentially a two-horse race.

Industrial gases is one of the most consolidated industries on earth. Because gas can't travel far and plants are capital-intensive, competition is regional and the barriers to entry are enormous — so a handful of players dominate everywhere. Globally the "big three" are Linde plc, Air Liquide (France) and Air Products (US). In India, the market is effectively a duopoly at the top: Linde and INOX Air Products (a JV of the INOX group and Air Products) each hold roughly a quarter of the market, with the leading players together taking ~80% of it.

The competitive dynamic that actually matters

Because the market is carved into regional monopolies, the fight isn't day-to-day price war — it's winning the next big tonnage contract. When a new steel mill or refinery is planned, Linde and INOX Air Products bid to build and own the captive gas plant, and the winner locks in that customer for two decades. So Linde India's growth is really a function of how much heavy-industry capex is being built in India, and its share of the plants that get won. That makes its fortunes tightly bound to India's steel-and-refining investment cycle — the subject of the next section.

06

The demand engine: steel, and everything else

One customer industry towers over the rest — and India is expanding it fast.

Industrial-gas demand is downstream of heavy industry, and no single sector matters more than steel. Modern steelmaking consumes enormous volumes of oxygen (to blast into molten iron) and nitrogen/argon (for inerting and metallurgy). India is the world's second-largest steel producer and has an official target to grow crude-steel capacity toward ~300 million tonnes by 2030 — a huge structural tailwind, because every new integrated steel mill needs a captive oxygen plant, and Linde competes to build and own it.

The medical-oxygen chapter — the COVID spotlight

For most of its history, medical oxygen was a quiet, low-margin corner of Linde's business. The 2021 COVID second wave changed that overnight: India faced acute medical-oxygen shortages, and companies like Linde became front-page infrastructure — diverting industrial oxygen to hospitals, building generation and logistics. The crisis passed, but it left two lasting marks: it hard-wired oxygen resilience into hospital and government planning (more captive plants, PSA units, storage), and it reminded everyone that industrial gases are essential public infrastructure, not just an industrial input. Healthcare is now a structurally larger, stickier slice of demand.

Why steel capex is the number to watch

Here's the mechanism: India's push to grow steel capacity toward ~300 Mt by 2030 means a wave of new integrated steel plants — and every one needs a large, dedicated oxygen plant it would rather outsource than build and run itself. That's exactly the on-site tonnage contract Linde lives on. So Linde India's medium-term growth is, to a first approximation, a bet on Indian steel and heavy-industry capex plus its win-rate against INOX Air Products. The Jindal Stainless Kalinganagar ASU is the model: build the plant, sign the 15–20-year contract, add an annuity. The risk is the flip side — if the steel cycle stalls, the pipeline of new tonnage plants thins out.

07

The hydrogen option — the long-dated call on decarbonisation

The one part of the story that could be much bigger in 10 years than it is today.

Beyond the steady gases utility sits an optionality that gets analysts excited: hydrogen. As the world tries to decarbonise heavy industry, hydrogen — especially green hydrogen (made by splitting water with renewable electricity) and blue hydrogen (from natural gas with carbon capture) — is one of the few credible routes to clean steel, clean refining and clean ammonia. And industrial-gas companies sit at the centre of it, because producing, purifying, storing and piping hydrogen is exactly what they already do.

Why Linde is a natural hydrogen winner

  • It already makes hydrogen at scale. Hydrogen and syngas (CO/CO₂) are long-standing Linde products for refining and chemicals — the group globally operates a large hydrogen network and dozens of electrolyser plants.
  • The whole value chain overlaps. Electrolysis needs power and water and produces hydrogen and oxygen; Linde already builds cryogenic plants, handles high-pressure/cold gases, and runs the logistics. Green hydrogen is a natural adjacency, not a new business.
  • India's National Green Hydrogen Mission is pushing to build a domestic green-H₂ industry (targeting millions of tonnes of annual capacity), backed by incentives — a policy tailwind for whoever can build and operate the plants.
Keep it in proportion. For Linde India specifically, note a crucial wrinkle from the 2018 merger carve-up: the group handed HyCO / hydrogen / CO / CO₂ exclusivity in India to the private Praxair India, while the listed Linde India kept Project Engineering. So the biggest Indian hydrogen gas opportunities may accrue to the unlisted sister company, not the listed one — though Linde India can still win the engineering and plant-building for hydrogen projects. This is a real example of why the related-party structure (next section) matters: the most exciting growth theme may sit on the other side of the group wall.
How to value optionality you can't yet size

Hydrogen is a call option, not a base-case cash flow. It could be transformational for the Linde group over 10–20 years, and Linde India can participate through engineering and (where allowed) supply. But the volumes and profits are years out, policy-dependent, and — for the listed entity — partly walled off to Praxair India. The honest way to hold it: don't pay up today for hydrogen profits that may never land in this ticker, but recognise that a global parent at the frontier of clean-energy gases gives Linde India credible long-term optionality that a standalone Indian gas firm wouldn't have. It's the icing, not the cake.

08

The Praxair merger & the governance overhang

The single most important company-specific issue for a minority shareholder — and a recurring source of both risk and hope.

This is the section that separates Linde India from a plain-vanilla gases play. When Linde AG and Praxair merged in 2018, their two Indian arms — the listed Linde India and the privately, wholly-owned Praxair India — became sister companies under the same parent. That created a structural conflict: the parent controls both, but only one has 25% outside minority shareholders. Every transaction between them potentially shifts value from the listed minorities to the private parent-owned entity.

The carve-up

To satisfy competition regulators, the group divided India between the two companies:

How India was split post-merger
DimensionLinde India (listed)Praxair India (private, 100% parent)
GeographyNorth, East and West-2 regionsSouth, Central and West-1 regions

In the process, certain Linde India assets were transferred to Praxair India. Because Linde India is listed with public shareholders, moving assets to a private sister company at the "wrong" price would enrich the parent at minorities' expense — precisely the related-party risk regulators exist to police.

The SEBI / SAT saga

  • The complaint: minority shareholders and observers argued the asset transfers to Praxair India were material related-party transactions that required independent valuation and minority (shareholder) approval — and hadn't been properly put to a vote.
  • SEBI's order: the regulator directed that the transferred assets be independently valued, and that if the transactions crossed the materiality threshold (broadly ~10% of turnover), they must go to shareholders for approval — with related parties abstaining.
  • The Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT) subsequently upheld SEBI's stance (rulings reported around Dec 2024/2025), keeping the pressure on the company to complete valuations and seek approval.
Why this cuts both ways — risk and hope

The risk: a 75%-owner with a private sister company has a standing incentive to route the best assets, contracts and hydrogen opportunities to the wholly-owned entity, and to charge the listed one royalties/technology fees — steadily leaking value from minorities. That "parent-extraction" fear is a permanent discount factor. The hope (the bull twist): if the regulator forces a fair, independent valuation and a minority vote, the parent may have to compensate Linde India in cash for under-priced past transfers — a one-off windfall that flows partly to the very minorities who were disadvantaged. Some investors hold the stock explicitly for this catalyst: a governance resolution that re-rates or pays out the listed entity. It's genuinely uncertain which way it lands, and how much, and when.

The honest read: the related-party/royalty structure is the biggest reason to be cautious on Linde India as a minority — and, paradoxically, part of why the stock carries such a rich multiple (some of the price is a bet on a governance catalyst, not on the gases business). Watch for: the outcome of the asset-transfer valuation and any minority vote; the size and terms of royalty/technology-fee payments to the parent; and any move toward, or away from, fairer treatment of the listed minorities.

How to think about a ~100× multiple

Let's be blunt: on the base gases-and-engineering earnings, ~100× is very hard to justify on cash-flow grounds — it implies decades of high growth with no stumbles, from a business whose top line is essentially flat and cyclical. So why does it trade there? Three reasons stack up. (1) Scarcity: a thin ~25% float in the only listed pure-play on Indian industrial gases, a global-parent name — index and quality-hungry money chases the few available shares. (2) The governance/asset-transfer catalyst: some of the price is a bet that a fair valuation of the Praxair transfers forces a cash payout or re-rating to minorities. (3) The long-dated call options: steel-capex growth and hydrogen. None of these is a normal DCF input — which is exactly why the P/E looks "distorted". The bear's rejoinder is simple: a ~100× stock priced on hopes and scarcity has a very long way to fall if any hope disappoints.

New tonnage plants, a steel-capex wave, and the group's global toolkit.

The quiet compounding underneath

Even without dramatic new projects, Linde India compounds quietly: existing tonnage contracts have built-in price escalators and power pass-throughs, merchant pricing firms in a tight market, and the mix keeps shifting toward higher-value gases. Add a debt-free balance sheet that self-funds new plants, and you get a business that can grow earnings mid-teens for years without heroics — if the steel-capex cycle cooperates and the parent lets the value accrue to the listed entity rather than the private sister.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely good business — with a governance asterisk and a valuation that leaves no room for error.

Linde India is a high-quality, defensive, oligopoly business with a world-class parent and a debt-free balance sheet, riding India's industrial expansion. The investment debate is almost entirely about two things that have nothing to do with whether the gases business is good: the price, and how the controlling parent treats the minorities.

The bull case

  • Oligopoly moat — regional monopolies, high barriers, sticky 15–20-year take-or-pay tonnage contracts.
  • Structural demand — India's ~300 Mt-by-2030 steel push plus refining, chemicals and post-COVID healthcare oxygen.
  • Fortress balance sheet — near net-cash, ~36% margins, ~18% ROCE, self-funded plant-building.
  • World-class parent — Linde plc's technology, engineering and hydrogen frontier give long-dated optionality.
  • Governance catalyst — a fair Praxair-transfer valuation / minority vote could force a cash payout or re-rating to the listed entity.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — ~100×+ earnings on flat, cyclical revenue; priced for perfection, huge downside if hopes fade.
  • Related-party leakage — a 75% parent with a private sister (Praxair India) can route the best assets/hydrogen and charge royalties, bleeding minority value.
  • Steel-cycle dependence — growth in new tonnage plants hinges on a capex cycle that can stall.
  • Lumpy earnings — Project Engineering makes reported results (and the P/E) jump around and hard to read.
  • Thin float & power/energy cost risk — ~25% float amplifies volatility; ASUs are power-hungry, exposing margins to energy costs and pass-through timing.
The bottom line

Linde India is a textbook high-quality industrial-gases franchise — the kind of essential, oligopolistic, annuity-heavy business investors dream of owning. The problem isn't the business; it's that you're being asked to pay ~100× earnings for a 25% slice of a company whose 75% owner has its own private vehicle in the same market and a track record of transactions minorities have had to fight in front of SEBI. The story over the coming years turns on three things to watch each year: the pace of new tonnage-plant wins (is the steel-capex annuity growing?), the resolution of the Praxair asset-transfer / royalty question (does value flow to minorities or leak to the parent?), and whether any of the priced-in hopes actually convert to cash. Get those right and a scarce, compounding gases utility can grind higher; get them wrong and a triple-digit multiple has an awfully long way to fall.
