Mahanagar Gas, the pipe that owns Mumbai's fuel

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Mahanagar Gas Limited (MGL) is the company that pipes natural gas across Mumbai. It holds the exclusive licence to distribute gas within its city geography — Greater Mumbai plus the adjoining Thane, Navi Mumbai and Raigad belt — and it does so in two forms. It sells CNG (compressed natural gas) to vehicles: the auto-rickshaws, taxis, buses and cars that pull into its network of over 500 filling stations. And it sells PNG (piped natural gas) through underground pipes to homes (cooking gas that never runs out mid-meal), to commercial users (hotels, restaurants, hospitals) and to industry. It is one of India's original city-gas monopolies, floated by state-owned GAIL, listed since 2016, cash-rich and dividend-paying.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a geographic monopoly selling an essential utility. Nobody else may lay a gas pipe to a Mumbai kitchen or open a rival CNG pump on MGL's turf for the licence's protected life. Demand is steady, sticky and grows with the city. In FY26 MGL moved roughly 4.59 million cubic metres of gas a day (mmscmd) — up ~8% — of which CNG for vehicles was about 71% of the volume and the profit core.
  • The whole business is one sum: volume × margin-per-unit. MGL buys gas, ships it down its pipes, and keeps a spread on every standard cubic metre (scm) it sells — its EBITDA per scm, around ₹8.3 in FY26. Multiply that by ~1.67 billion scm of annual throughput and you have almost the entire operating profit. It is a volume-times-per-unit-margin annuity, and both halves are trackable each quarter.
  • Its margin lives and dies on a government allocation. MGL's priority segments (CNG and household PNG) are meant to be fed by cheap, government-priced domestic "APM" gas. In 2024 the government cut that cheap-gas allocation, forcing MGL to buy pricier imported LNG to fill the gap — and the per-unit margin took the hit. FY26 net profit fell ~19% to ~₹847 Cr for exactly this reason. The stock trades on a modest ~13× earnings — cheap for a monopoly, precisely because the market is pricing that policy risk and a longer-term EV threat.
In plain terms

Picture a toll booth on the only road into a large, growing city — except the toll is on fuel, and the road is a network of buried gas pipes and CNG pumps that only MGL is allowed to build. Every rickshaw fill, every restaurant burner, every apartment stove that runs on gas pays MGL a small margin on the way. The volume is dependable and rises with the city. The catch is what the toll keeper pays for its own gas: a chunk of it is meant to come cheap from a government quota, and when the government quietly shrank that quota in 2024, MGL's margin per unit — the height of its toll — got squeezed even as traffic kept growing. That single tension is the whole story.

01

City gas distribution 101: how a pipe becomes a business

No energy background needed — just the handful of terms that drive the whole model.

"City Gas Distribution" (CGD) is the business of taking natural gas — piped in from far-away fields and import terminals on high-pressure trunk lines — and delivering it to the doorsteps of an urban area: to vehicles as CNG and to buildings as PNG. A CGD company is, at heart, a last-mile network operator plus a fuel retailer. It doesn't drill for gas; it distributes it and keeps a margin. To follow MGL, you need six terms.

The only vocabulary you need

The economics that decide everything: the spread per unit

A CGD company's profit is not complicated. It buys gas at a blended cost (a mix of cheap APM and dearer RLNG), it sells that gas at a retail price it largely sets, and it keeps the difference — its margin — on every cubic metre, after running the pipes and pumps. Because the network is already built and mostly fixed-cost, growth in volume drops through handsomely, and a wider per-unit spread lifts profit almost one-for-one. So the two numbers that matter above all others are how much gas MGL sells and how much margin it keeps per unit. Everything else in this primer is really a story about one of those two levers.

Why natural gas, and why a monopoly

  • It's cleaner. Natural gas burns far cleaner than petrol, diesel or coal — less particulate pollution, lower carbon per unit of energy. In a city choking on vehicle exhaust, that is a policy tailwind, not just a marketing line.
  • It's cheaper for the user. A rickshaw or taxi running on CNG spends materially less per kilometre than one on petrol — often 40%+ less. That running-cost gap is what pulls vehicle owners to convert, and it is the structural engine under MGL's CNG volumes.
  • It's a natural monopoly. You cannot sensibly have three companies digging up the same street to lay competing gas pipes. So the regulator awards exclusive distribution rights over a geography for a long protected period — which is exactly why a CGD licence is such a prized asset.
02

What MGL actually sells: one gas, four customers

The segment map — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

MGL buys a single commodity — natural gas — and sells it to four kinds of customer through two delivery methods. The volumes and the margins differ sharply across them, and the mix is the key to understanding both the profit and the risk. Follow the gas from source to burner.

One purchase, one network, two retail channels. The margin MGL keeps per cubic metre — after the buried pipes and pumps are paid for — is the whole game; the mix of who it sells to decides how defensible that margin is.

The four customers, decoded

  • CNG for vehicles — the biggest and best. Around 71% of MGL's volume is CNG dispensed to auto-rickshaws, taxis (Mumbai's famous kaali-peelis), buses, cabs and private cars. It is a priority segment entitled to cheap APM gas, it carries a healthy margin, and its demand is anchored by the running-cost saving over petrol/diesel. This is where MGL makes most of its money.
  • Domestic PNG for homes — the sticky annuity. Piped cooking gas to residential kitchens. Once a household is connected, it almost never leaves — no cylinder booking, no running out mid-meal — so this base only grows. MGL served roughly 3.2 million domestic PNG households by FY26. Also a priority segment on cheap gas, though small per household in volume.
  • Commercial PNG — hotels, restaurants, hospitals, canteens. Higher realisation per unit than domestic, but sold at market-linked gas cost. A steady, growing mid-segment.
  • Industrial PNG — factories using gas as fuel or feedstock. The most price-sensitive and most exposed to competition from alternate fuels (furnace oil, LPG, propane) — when imported gas is dear, industrial customers can switch away. Lower margin, more cyclical, but useful volume.
Why the mix is the risk map

The two priority segments — CNG and domestic PNG — are the crown jewels precisely because they are supposed to be fed with cheap APM gas, so their margins are fat and protected. The two non-priority segments — commercial and industrial — run on costlier market gas and face substitutes, so their margins are thinner and shakier. That is why MGL's health is measured not just by total volume but by how much of its volume sits in the protected CNG-plus-domestic core. When the government trims the APM quota, it is trimming the cheap gas that feeds exactly those crown jewels — which is why an allocation cut hits harder than the headline gas price alone would suggest.

03

The company, the parent, and who controls it

A GAIL-founded joint venture, now widely held and cash-rich.

MGL was born as a joint venture: state-owned gas transmission giant GAIL (India) teamed up with what was then British Gas to build out Mumbai's gas grid in the mid-1990s. Over time the foreign partner exited and MGL was listed. Today GAIL remains the single largest promoter, but the company is broadly held by the public and institutions — a professionally run utility rather than a family concern.

Timeline

  • 1995

  • 2000s

  • 2016
    IPO on NSE & BSE — a heavily oversubscribed listing that made MGL one of the market's favourite "utility monopoly" stories. British Gas's successor stake is sold down over time.
  • 2018–2022
    Steady volume growth; network and station expansion; MGL acquires Unison Enviro to add CGD geographies beyond Mumbai (Ratnagiri, and areas in Karnataka and Punjab).
  • Oct 2024

  • FY26
    Volume ~4.59 mmscmd (+8%); consolidated revenue ~₹9,185 Cr; net profit ~₹847 Cr (down ~19% on the margin squeeze); EBITDA/scm defended near ~₹8.3; ₹30/share dividend; balance sheet net-cash.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter: GAIL (India) Ltd — the state-owned gas major — holds roughly 32.5%. GAIL is both MGL's largest shareholder and a key upstream supplier and transporter of its gas, an unusually intertwined relationship for a listed company.
  • Free float: the remaining ~67% is widely held by foreign and domestic institutions, mutual funds and retail investors. There is no dominant private promoter family — MGL trades and is run like a professionally managed public utility.
  • Subsidiary: Unison Enviro Pvt Ltd (UEPL) extends MGL's CGD footprint outside Mumbai into newer geographies. It is why the consolidated revenue (~₹9,185 Cr) runs above the standalone Mumbai business (~₹8,240 Cr).
  • Balance sheet: essentially debt-free / net-cash, with strong free cash flow and a consistent dividend record (₹30/share for FY26, ~2.8% yield). A utility that funds its own expansion and still returns cash.
The GAIL relationship cuts both ways: as promoter, supplier and transporter, GAIL's interests are largely aligned with MGL's — but it also means MGL's gas sourcing and pipeline tariffs are partly negotiated with its own largest shareholder and with the same government that sets APM allocations. MGL is a monopoly at the retail end, yet a price-taker at the wholesale end, on terms shaped by the state. That asymmetry is the root of the margin risk explored in section 06.
04

The monopoly licence: the moat, spelled out

Why the exclusive distribution right is the single most valuable thing MGL owns.

The heart of MGL's investment appeal is a piece of paper: an authorisation from the sector regulator (PNGRB) to be the sole distributor of natural gas within its geographic area. For the protected life of that licence, no rival may lay competing pipes to homes or open competing CNG stations on MGL's turf. It is, quite literally, a legal monopoly over an essential utility in one of the world's densest, richest urban markets.

Two protections layer on top of each other. First, an infrastructure exclusivity — the right to be the only one building the network for a long initial window. Second, a marketing exclusivity — the right to be the only one selling gas to customers for a defined period. Together they hand MGL years of protected, competition-free operation in which to build density and a loyal installed base — the deep, sticky customer relationships that persist even after the exclusivity formally lapses, because switching a buried pipe network is not something a competitor can easily replicate.

Why "monopoly" here is a real moat, not a slogan

Plenty of businesses call themselves moated; a CGD network genuinely is. Three reinforcing barriers: the legal one (an exclusive licence competitors cannot bid against during exclusivity), the physical one (you cannot dig up Mumbai's streets a second time to lay a rival grid — the capital and disruption are prohibitive), and the behavioural one (a household or rickshaw driver on gas has no reason and no easy way to switch to a network that doesn't exist). The result is an essential-service utility with pricing power, low churn and demand that grows with the city — the closest thing to a toll road that the Indian market offers. The one thing the moat does not protect is the cost of the gas MGL itself must buy — which is precisely where the risk lives.

05

The one equation: volume × EBITDA-per-scm

The single most important idea in the whole case — and it fits MGL and almost no one else.

You can hold a great deal of MGL in your head with one deliberately crude equation. Operating profit ≈ total volume × the margin kept per cubic metre. That's it. A CGD monopoly is a volume-times-per-unit-margin machine, and the beauty — and the danger — is that both terms are small, simple numbers you can watch move each quarter. Now watch how the two levers behave differently. Volume is the slow, dependable one: it grinds up ~6–8% a year as more rickshaws convert to CNG, more homes get piped, and the city expands. It rarely lurches. EBITDA per scm is the volatile one: it can swing meaningfully within a year on the cost of gas, the retail price MGL is able to charge, and — the big one — how much cheap APM gas the company is allocated. In FY26, volume did its steady ~8% job, but the per-unit margin was under pressure, which is why volume grew yet profit fell. That divergence is the entire tension.

The device that only fits MGL: track the two numbers, and the wedge between them

Most companies you value on a dozen moving parts. MGL you can pressure-test with two: volume growth and EBITDA/scm. Draw them as two lines. When both rise, profit compounds beautifully — a monopoly annuity re-rating. When volume rises but EBITDA/scm falls (FY26), you get the frustrating picture of a growing business earning less — the classic sign that the cost lever, not the demand lever, is in control. And EBITDA/scm has a floor MGL now openly defends: management has signalled it will hold the line around ~₹8/scm even while spending to expand. So the checkable question every quarter is brutally specific: is volume still compounding at 6–8%, and is EBITDA/scm holding the ~₹8 floor — or is the APM squeeze dragging it below? Get a fix on those two prints and you have priced the stock.

06

The APM lever: when a government pen-stroke sets your margin

The dominant, policy-driven risk — and the genuine surprise of this whole business.

Here is the fact that surprises most people meeting MGL for the first time: the biggest swing factor in this monopoly's profit is not demand, not competition, and not even the global gas price directly. It is a bureaucratic allocation decision — how many cubic metres of cheap, government-priced domestic gas the state chooses to hand MGL for its CNG and household customers. Change that one number in Delhi, and MGL's per-unit margin moves in Mumbai. In late 2024, the government did exactly that.

How the cheap-gas plumbing works

India produces some natural gas domestically from old, state-controlled fields, priced administratively (the "APM" price) far below imported gas. For years, the government's deal with CGD companies was simple: your priority uses — CNG for vehicles and gas for household kitchens — get first call on this cheap domestic gas, so ordinary citizens enjoy affordable clean fuel. That cheap APM supply is what made CNG both cheap for the rickshaw driver and profitable for MGL at the same time. It was the golden goose.

Then domestic gas fields aged and their output declined, while CNG demand kept rising. The cheap gas could no longer cover the priority segments. So in October 2024 the government cut the APM allocation to CGD firms — reducing the share of a CNG customer's gas that came from the cheap domestic pool, and forcing companies like MGL to buy the shortfall as pricier imported RLNG (or newer, higher-priced domestic gas categories). The blended cost of gas went up. And because a monopoly cannot fully and instantly pass every rupee of cost to price-sensitive rickshaw drivers without denting demand and inviting scrutiny, MGL absorbed part of the hit in its own margin.

The genuine surprise: a rickshaw driver in Andheri filling up for the day, and a policy official in Delhi adjusting a gas-allocation percentage, sit on the same profit line for MGL — and the official's pen moves it more violently than the driver ever does. MGL's Q4 FY26 profit fell sharply (net profit for the quarter down roughly 46% year-on-year) largely because of this cost squeeze, even though the number of rickshaws filling up kept rising. A textbook monopoly whose margin is set, in large part, by an act of government. That is not how most "moaty" businesses work — and it is the reason MGL trades cheap.
Why this is the dominant risk, above the gas price itself

It is tempting to file MGL's margin risk under "commodity prices," but that misreads it. A pure commodity risk MGL could largely pass through — retail gas prices adjust. The APM-allocation risk is worse because it is discretionary and policy-driven: the government can shrink (or, in principle, restore) the cheap-gas quota at will, and the direction over the coming years is more likely down than up as old fields deplete. So MGL's per-unit margin depends on a variable it does not control and cannot hedge — the single biggest reason a monopoly this good does not command a monopoly's premium multiple. The offset: MGL has some pricing power and the government has an incentive to keep CNG affordable, so a total collapse of the model is unlikely. But the ceiling on the margin is set in Delhi, and every investor in MGL is, whether they realise it or not, taking a view on future APM allocation policy.

07

The CNG conversion engine: the humble rickshaw as profit core

Why the least glamorous vehicle on Mumbai's roads drives most of the value.

Strip MGL down and the beating heart is not a gleaming corporate PNG contract — it is the auto-rickshaw, the taxi and the fleet vehicle pulling into a CNG pump. CNG for vehicles is ~71% of MGL's volume and the bulk of its profit. Understanding why that vehicle owner chooses gas — and keeps choosing it — is understanding the demand engine.

The logic is pure economics. A CNG vehicle costs meaningfully less to run per kilometre than a petrol or diesel one — often on the order of 40% or more cheaper on fuel — because gas is cheaper per unit of energy and taxed more lightly. For someone who drives for a living, covering hundreds of kilometres a day, that daily saving is not a rounding error; it is the difference between a viable livelihood and a squeezed one. So Mumbai's commercial vehicles converted to CNG en masse, and new ones increasingly come CNG-ready. Each conversion is a customer locked to MGL's network for the life of the vehicle, filling up several times a week, for years.

The unglamorous truth worth sitting with

An investor drawn to MGL's clean-energy, smart-city framing should anchor on something plainer: this is, at its core, a business that earns a small margin every time a three-wheeler tops up its tank. The profit is not in a boardroom PNG deal — it is in millions of routine, repeat, low-drama rickshaw and taxi fills, each one a few kilograms of gas, adding up to the largest and most defensible slice of the whole company. That is a strength (it is diversified across countless tiny, sticky, price-driven customers, not a handful of big contracts that can walk) and a tell (the demand is only as strong as CNG's running-cost edge over the alternatives — which is exactly what the next section puts under threat).

08

The EV question: the long shadow over CNG

The structural bear case — and why the timing, not the direction, is the real debate.

If the APM allocation is MGL's near-term margin risk, the electric vehicle is its long-term existential one. CNG's entire value proposition to a driver is "cheaper and cleaner than petrol." An electric rickshaw, taxi or bus can be cheaper and cleaner still — no fuel bill at all, zero tailpipe emissions. If the city's three-wheelers and cabs go electric over the coming decade, they stop pulling into MGL's CNG pumps. The profit core is, in principle, disruptable.

This is not hypothetical. Electric two- and three-wheelers are already selling in volume in India; electric buses are entering city fleets; battery costs keep falling and charging networks keep growing. The direction of travel for urban transport is, over a long horizon, electric. For a company whose largest, most profitable segment is selling fuel to vehicles, that is the deepest structural question on the table, and no serious primer can wave it away.

The way to actually frame it

The debate is not whether EVs eventually pressure CNG — they will — but when, and how gracefully MGL's other legs grow in the meantime. A CGD network does not vanish when CNG plateaus: the domestic-PNG household base keeps rising, industrial and commercial gas demand persists, new licensed geographies add volume, and the company can even turn its real-estate-rich CNG stations into EV-charging sites, converting a threat into an adjacency. The bearish read is that CNG is a melting ice cube on a policy-supported subsidy; the constructive read is that it is a durable cash cow with a decade-plus runway that funds MGL's pivot to a broader energy-distribution utility. Which is right depends on the pace of electrification in commercial fleets specifically — the segment that matters most to MGL — and that pace is the genuine unknown at the centre of the long-term case.

09

Network expansion: the growth runway beyond Mumbai

Where the next volumes come from — deeper penetration plus new geographies.

A monopoly on a fixed city could, in theory, grow slowly once it saturates. MGL's answer is two-fold: go deeper in its core Mumbai geography (more CNG stations, more household connections, more industrial hook-ups) and go wider into new licensed areas won in the regulator's periodic bidding rounds and through its Unison Enviro subsidiary.

  • Deeper in the core: Mumbai is not yet saturated. Millions more households can be piped for domestic PNG; more auto-rickshaws, cabs and private cars can convert to CNG; commercial and industrial customers in the adjoining Thane–Navi Mumbai–Raigad belt keep being added. Each new connection is incremental volume on an already-built, mostly fixed-cost network — high-margin growth that needs modest capital.
  • Wider into new geographies: through Unison Enviro, MGL has extended CGD operations into areas such as Ratnagiri (Maharashtra), parts of Karnataka and Punjab — brand-new monopoly footprints in their own right, in earlier stages of the same build-density-then-harvest cycle Mumbai went through decades ago.
  • Adjacencies: LNG for long-haul trucking, compressed biogas (CBG), and hosting EV charging at existing CNG station sites are all optionalities that let MGL redeploy its network, land and brand as the fuel mix shifts.

The reinvestment maths is what makes a CGD monopoly attractive to a long-term owner. MGL throws off strong free cash flow from its mature Mumbai base, pays a healthy dividend, and still has enough left to fund network extension without taking on debt — which is why it stays net-cash even as it grows. The constraint on growth is rarely capital; it is the pace at which new areas can be built out and new customers connected, plus the ever-present margin question of what the gas to serve them costs.

The nuance on "expansion": new geographies dilute before they accrete. A freshly won CGD area burns capital laying pipes and building stations for years before its volumes and margins mature — so aggressive expansion can weigh on near-term consolidated returns even as it builds long-term value. When MGL's management talks about prioritising infrastructure over margin (and holding EBITDA/scm near a floor rather than maximising it), this is what they mean: spending today to widen the annuity tomorrow. Whether that trade pays off is judged over years, not quarters.
10

The industry and the rivals

A field of regional monopolies — they rarely compete head-on, but the market compares them constantly.

The peculiar thing about CGD "competition" is that the companies almost never compete for the same customer — each holds an exclusive geography. The rivalry is really for investor capital and for the next batch of licensed areas, and the natural yardstick is how each monopoly's volume growth and EBITDA/scm stack up. MGL's most-compared peer is Indraprastha Gas (IGL), the mirror-image monopoly serving Delhi.

The comparative shape to hold in mind

Two takeaways. First, MGL sits at the defensive, high-quality end of the CGD spectrum: a dense, mature, CNG-anchored monopoly with the sector's better returns and a net-cash balance sheet — less racy than Adani Total Gas's expansion, less industrially cyclical than Gujarat Gas, most directly comparable to IGL. Second, because all CGD players draw on the same national APM pool and the same regulator, the 2024 allocation cut hit the whole sector together — this is not an MGL-specific wound but an industry-wide reset of the cheap-gas deal. So the relevant question for an investor is less "is MGL better than IGL this quarter?" and more "is the CGD model itself — monopoly volume plus a policy-dependent per-unit margin — worth owning at these multiples?" MGL is simply the cleanest, cheapest way to take that view.

Financials & valuation

A cash-rich monopoly on a utility multiple — cheap for a reason the market can name.

FY26 was the year the APM squeeze showed up in the numbers: revenue rose as volumes and gas costs climbed, but profit fell as the per-unit margin compressed. The market has responded by keeping MGL on a modest ~13× earnings — a low multiple for a monopoly, and an explicit price for the policy and EV risks. The debate is whether that discount is too harsh or exactly right.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A wonderful monopoly with two clouds over it — one policy, one technological.

MGL is, on almost every operational measure, an enviable business: a legal monopoly on an essential, growing utility, cash-rich, high-return, paying a real dividend, and expanding without debt. The reason it trades cheap is not the quality of the toll booth — it is a fair question about who sets the toll (the government, via APM) and whether the traffic (CNG vehicles) eventually reroutes (to EVs). The honest case weighs a superb franchise against those two clouds.

The bull case

  • Genuine monopoly annuity — exclusive licence over Mumbai's gas; sticky, essential-utility demand that compounds 6–8% a year with the city.
  • Cheap and cash-generative — ~13× earnings, net-cash, ~2.8% yield; a monopoly priced like a troubled cyclical.
  • CNG cost edge intact — gas remains far cheaper per km than petrol/diesel, keeping the conversion engine running for years.
  • Margin defended at a floor — management holding EBITDA/scm near ~₹8 even while investing; the squeeze may be near its worst, not its start.
  • Runway beyond Mumbai — deeper penetration plus new Unison geographies extend the annuity; optionality in CBG, LNG trucking, EV charging.

The bear case

  • APM / allocation risk — the dominant, policy-driven threat; further cuts to cheap domestic gas force costlier RLNG and squeeze the per-unit margin, as proven in 2024.
  • The EV shadow — electrification of rickshaws, taxis and buses could erode the CNG profit core over the long term; timing is the only real unknown.
  • Price-taker on gas, FX-exposed — the imported-RLNG portion carries crude-linked price and currency volatility MGL cannot hedge away.
  • Regulatory & licence risk — retail-price scrutiny, exclusivity-period expiry and eventual open-access/competition as protected windows end.
  • Industrial & substitution — price-sensitive industrial customers switch to alternate fuels when gas is dear; expansion into new areas dilutes near-term returns.
The bottom line

MGL is the rare Indian utility that behaves like a toll on a growing city's fuel — dependable volume, sticky customers, real cash returns, no debt — and it can be bought on a single-digit-to-low-teens multiple precisely because the market has (fairly) noticed that the toll's height is set partly in Delhi and its traffic could someday go electric. Neither cloud is imaginary; neither is fatal tomorrow. The next chapter of the story will be told not in the revenue headline but in one recurring print: whether EBITDA/scm holds its ~₹8 floor as volumes keep climbing and the government's next APM allocation decision lands. Watch that per-unit margin and the cheap-gas share behind it each quarter — hold the floor while the city keeps converting, and a monopoly this cheap re-rates; lose the floor to a shrinking APM quota, and a growing business simply runs to stand still.
