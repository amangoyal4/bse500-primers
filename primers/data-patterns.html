00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Data Patterns (India) is a Chennai-based company that designs and builds complete electronic systems for India's armed forces and space programme — radars, electronic-warfare (jamming and eavesdropping) suites, aircraft avionics, communication sets, the electronics inside missiles, satellite electronics, and the automatic test rigs that qualify all of it. It is one of a very small number of fully vertically integrated private defence-electronics firms in India: it does the whole chain in-house, from the circuit board and the chip-level logic up to the finished radar or jammer pod.

Four facts frame everything:

  • Small revenue, outsized quality. FY26 revenue was only ~₹925 Cr (up 31%), yet it earns a ~40% EBITDA margin and a ~40% net-profit margin — figures that would be extraordinary for a factory business, and are almost unheard of among defence suppliers, who are usually thin-margin and cash-strapped.
  • Net cash, self-funded, high-return. Borrowings are a rounding error (~₹5 Cr), it sits on ~₹1,725 Cr of reserves, and it earns a ~22% return on capital employed — a defence supplier that funds its own growth instead of leaning on the government's cash cycle.
  • The order book is turning from "invent" to "build." The firm order book was ~₹926 Cr at March 2026, and the pipeline including negotiated orders was ~₹2,062 Cr — an all-time high. The mix is shifting from one-off development work toward repeat series production.
  • Priced for a lot. At ~₹23,900 Cr market cap on ~₹271 Cr of profit, the stock trades near ~95× earnings and ~14× book. That is what the market pays for a scarce, IP-owning, indigenisation-linked compounder — and the central tension of the whole story.
In plain terms

Most defence suppliers are like a tailor who sews to someone else's pattern — they bend metal or stuff boards to a design the government or a foreign firm hands them, and they earn a modest cutting-and-stitching fee. Data Patterns is the firm that drew the pattern in the first place: it conceives the radar, writes the signal-processing software, lays out the boards, designs the logic down to the chip, builds the hardware, and then also builds the test bench that proves the thing works. Because it owns the design and the intellectual property, it prices on the value of the capability, not on cost-plus — which is why a ₹925 Cr revenue business throws off hospital-grade margins. The catch is that this kind of work is lumpy, slow to qualify, and almost entirely dependent on one customer: the Indian state.

01

Defence electronics 101: how this business actually earns

No engineering degree required — just the shape of a defence-electronics contract.

Defence electronics does not behave like normal manufacturing. You do not make a product and sell thousands of units next quarter. Instead, revenue arrives in a slow, staged sequence tied to a government programme, and the economics change completely as a system moves through that sequence. Understanding the sequence explains almost everything about Data Patterns' margins, lumpiness and risk.

The life of a defence-electronics contract

→
Stage 2 · Prove

→
Stage 3 · Repeat

Make many units of the qualified design. Bigger revenue, thinner margin.
→
Stage 4 · Sustain
Spares & upgrades
Decades of maintenance, obsolescence fixes, mid-life upgrades. Sticky.
A programme can take 3–7 years to travel from Stage 1 to Stage 3. Once a firm owns a qualified design, it is extremely hard to displace — the switching cost for the military is a fresh multi-year re-qualification.

The four features that define the business

  • Long gestation, then "design-win-then-production." Money is committed early for development, but the large revenue only lands years later when the same design goes into repeat production. Patience is structural.
  • High-mix, low-volume. Data Patterns runs hundreds of distinct part-numbers across dozens of programmes, in small batches — the opposite of a consumer-electronics line churning one product by the million. This is why it needs deep design capability rather than a giant factory.
  • Indigenous-content mandates. India's defence-buying rules now heavily favour home-designed content — positive-indigenisation lists that ban import of specified items, higher domestic-content thresholds, and a "Buy Indian" tilt in procurement. A firm that owns the Indian IP is exactly who these rules are written to reward.
  • Qualification is the barrier. Military hardware must survive vibration, heat, cold, shock and electromagnetic chaos, and be certified to exacting standards before it can fly or sail. That certification — not the soldering — is the moat; it takes years and cannot be shortcut by a new entrant.
The economic punchline of the sequence

The fattest margins live at the design and development end, where Data Patterns is being paid for scarce engineering IP that only a handful of firms possess. As a programme matures into series production, it becomes more of a manufacturing job — bigger rupee volumes, but a lower percentage margin, because you are now being paid to build a known design rather than to invent it. Hold that thought: it is the single most important and least intuitive fact about this company, and Section 07 is built entirely around it.

02

What "vertically integrated" means here — and why it's the moat

The one phrase that explains the margins, the IP and the qualification speed.

"Vertically integrated" is used loosely everywhere, so here is precisely what it means for Data Patterns: it performs every layer of a defence-electronics system in-house, instead of assembling modules bought from others. Walk down the stack and Data Patterns owns each rung:

The full design-and-build stack — all done in-house
LayerWhat it involvesWhy owning it matters
System & architectureConceiving the whole radar / jammer / avionics box and how its parts fitCaptures the highest-value engineering; sets the IP that everything else hangs from.
Chip / FPGA logicDesigning the programmable-chip logic down to gate levelVery few Indian firms design at this level — it removes dependence on foreign black-box parts.
Board & hardwareRF/microwave and digital circuit boards, power suppliesControls performance, cost and the ability to re-spin a design fast.
Embedded software & firmwareThe signal-processing and control code that makes the hardware "think"In a radar or EW suite, the software is most of the capability.
Mechanical & environmentalRugged enclosures, cooling, vibration/heat survivalLets it certify to military standards without waiting on an outside vendor.
Test & qualification (ATE)Building the automatic test equipment that proves the system worksIt literally builds the rigs that qualify its own systems — and sells them to others.

Contrast that with the more common model: a supplier buys a foreign radar processor, a foreign RF front-end and third-party firmware, screws them into a box, and adds a margin on the assembly. That supplier is fast to start but owns no IP, captures little value, and is hostage to the foreign vendor's price, lead-time and export licence. Data Patterns deliberately chose the harder, slower path of owning the whole stack.

Why the whole stack is the moat, four ways

(1) It captures more value. When you design the system and build it, you earn the design fee and the production margin, not a thin assembly cut. (2) It controls the IP. The qualified design belongs to Data Patterns, so repeat orders, spares and upgrades flow back to it for the platform's decades-long life. (3) It qualifies faster. When the customer asks for a change, one team can re-spin the board, the firmware and the enclosure together, rather than negotiating across three outside vendors — a real edge in a business where re-qualification is the bottleneck. (4) It isn't hostage to foreign sub-systems. In a world of export controls and sanctions, "we designed it here, down to the chip logic" is exactly what the Ministry of Defence wants to hear — and increasingly what the rules require.

The flip side of integration: owning every layer means Data Patterns must fund a large, expensive engineering team continuously, whether or not orders are flowing that quarter. Integration converts vendor risk into fixed-cost and utilisation risk — the design bench has to be kept busy. That is affordable at 40% margins; it would be punishing if margins ever compressed toward the sector's usual thin single digits.
03

The company and who controls it

A founder-engineer, a single Chennai campus, and a 2021 listing.

Data Patterns is a founder-built engineering house. Srinivasagopalan Rangarajan — an IIT-Madras post-graduate — has run the business since the mid-1980s, growing it from a small electronics-design outfit into a listed strategic supplier to the armed forces. He and Rekha Murthy Rangarajan are the promoters. The whole operation is anchored on a single Chennai campus (a ~100,000 sq ft facility on ~5.8 acres), with a larger new facility being built to house the production ramp.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: S. Rangarajan (Chairman & Managing Director) and family held ~42.4% at March 2026 — engaged, technically-fluent founder control, though a notch below the two-thirds many Indian promoters keep.
  • Institutional interest: FIIs held ~12.5% and domestic institutions ~11.7% at March 2026 — meaningful, growing institutional ownership for a company this size.
  • Balance sheet: net cash / effectively debt-free (~₹5 Cr borrowings against ~₹1,725 Cr reserves), funding the expansion from internal accruals and IPO proceeds rather than debt.
Why a founder-engineer matters in this specific business

In a design-IP company, the promoter is not just a capital allocator — the accumulated engineering judgment is much of the asset. Rangarajan spent three-plus decades assembling the rare thing that cannot be bought off a shelf: a team that can take a defence radar from a blank sheet to a certified, flying system. That is a genuine edge and a genuine key-person concentration at the same time. The moat is people, and the founder is the one who built and holds that bench together.

04

The product arsenal, broken into parts

Seven product families — the "brains, eyes and ears" of India's platforms.

Data Patterns' catalogue spans essentially the entire electronic content of a modern defence platform. A useful way to picture it: on any fighter, ship, missile or satellite, Data Patterns can supply the parts that sense (radars, electro-optics), the parts that think and fly (avionics, mission computers), the parts that listen and fight the spectrum (EW, comms, IFF), the parts that strike (missile/weapon electronics), and the parts that test it all (ATE).

The self-reinforcing trick most people miss

Look again at that last row. Data Patterns builds the automatic test equipment (ATE) — the rigs and software that put a radar or avionics box through its paces and certify it fit for service. Because it makes the test benches, it can qualify its own products faster and cheaper, and it also sells those test systems to other primes who are qualifying their kit. The tester and the tested come from the same house. Few competitors have both halves.

Why the breadth is strategic, not scattered

A first look might read this catalogue as a company that does a bit of everything. It is the opposite. Every family shares the same underlying design skills — RF/microwave engineering, high-speed digital, signal-processing software, ruggedisation and test. Master that toolkit once, and you can point it at a radar this year, a jammer next year, a satellite payload the year after. The breadth is one capability applied across many platforms, which is exactly why a single ~₹925 Cr company can credibly bid on radars, EW, avionics, missiles and space at the same time — and why its addressable market is the whole electronic content of Indian defence, not one niche.

06

The order book and the shift from "invent" to "build"

The single most important operational transition happening right now.

Data Patterns is at an inflection point that recurs in the life of every successful defence-design firm: the programmes it spent years developing are now entering series production. That transition is the reason revenue is accelerating — and, paradoxically, the reason margins are guided lower (Section 07). First, the order book itself.

Two things about this order book matter more than its size. First, the pipeline is more than double the firm book — management sees a large body of negotiated and expected work that has not yet hardened into contracts. Second, the composition is tilting toward production: qualified designs from earlier years are being ordered in repeatable quantities, which gives more predictable, higher-volume revenue than the stop-start rhythm of pure development work.

Why "development-to-production" is the growth engine

Development revenue is capped by how many new systems a firm can invent at once — it is engineering-hours-limited. Production revenue is capped by how many units the customer will buy of a design you already own — it is far more scalable. As Data Patterns' library of qualified, owned designs grows, each one becomes an annuity: order after order for units, spares and upgrades across a platform's 20–30 year life, with the hard engineering already paid for. The bull case is essentially that Data Patterns has spent a decade filling a shelf with qualified designs, and is now starting to ring the till on them repeatedly. That is why management guides to 20–25% revenue growth even as it warns on margins.

The company spent its first decades as a listed-scale business earning the right to build. It is now starting to build at scale — and discovering that building pays a different margin than inventing did.
07

The margin inversion — why growing lowers the margin here

The one idea in this primer that could not be copied onto any other company.

Here is the fact that should surprise a smart generalist. For an ordinary manufacturer, scaling up production raises margins — you spread fixed costs over more units, and operating leverage kicks in. At Data Patterns it runs the other way. In May 2026, alongside a record quarter, management guided FY27 EBITDA margins down to ~35–40%, and the stock fell about 11% in a day. Not because business was bad — the order book was at an all-time high — but because the better the production ramp goes, the thinner the blended margin becomes.

Production revenue carries more bought-in material and less pure design content, so its percentage margin is structurally lower — even though it adds large absolute profit. The mix, not any loss of pricing power, moves the blended margin.

Put it in this company's own numbers. In FY26 the blended EBITDA margin was ~40%, and gross margin in the standout fourth quarter touched ~73%. Management's message was that those levels reflect a mix still heavy with high-margin development work, and that as the revenue base shifts toward large-scale hardware production, a sustainable margin is nearer 35–38%. Crucially, they framed the lower number as healthier, not weaker: production revenue is more predictable and lower-risk than lumpy development billing.

The device: same company, same skill, two different margins

Take one qualified radar design. Sold as a development contract, Data Patterns is paid mostly for scarce engineering IP — margins in the 40s or higher. Sold as a production batch of the identical design, the same company earns a manufacturing margin on hardware it now largely buys in and assembles — margins in the 30s. Nothing about the firm's quality changed; only which stage of the same programme is billing this quarter. So the counter-intuitive truth is that a falling reported margin can be a sign the strategy is working — it means the design shelf is converting into a production annuity. The bear reads the same fact as "the extraordinary margins were always a development-mix artefact, and normalisation has begun." Both are looking at the identical inversion.

Why this is the genuine surprise: most investors instinctively cheer a defence supplier "moving into production" and expect margins to rise on scale. At Data Patterns, the more successful that move, the lower the headline margin — a company where good news and margin compression arrive in the same press release. Anyone modelling this firm as a normal operating-leverage story will misread every quarter.
08

The industry and the rivals

A giant, a cluster of specialists, and a once-in-a-generation indigenisation tailwind.

Data Patterns rides one of the strongest structural themes in Indian industry: defence indigenisation. Decades of importing weapons are being deliberately reversed through positive-indigenisation lists, higher domestic-content rules, a rising defence budget and an export push. High-value electronics — processors, radios, radars — can be 30–40% of a modern platform's total cost, so the electronics slice of that indigenisation wave is unusually rich. The question is where Data Patterns sits in the field.

Where Data Patterns actually competes — and where it doesn't

The key nuance is that DRDO, ISRO, HAL, BEL and BDL are simultaneously customers, collaborators and occasional competitors. Data Patterns rarely goes toe-to-toe with BEL for a whole warship's electronics; instead it wins the sub-systems and complete boxes where deep, fast design work matters more than sheer scale — radars, EW suites, avionics, test systems. Its edge in that lane is the vertical integration from Section 02: when a programme needs a bespoke radar re-spun and re-qualified quickly, a single integrated house beats a chain of module vendors. The sector's average EBITDA margin sits around the low-30s; Data Patterns runs well above that, which is the quantitative proof that it is playing a design game, not an assembly game.

The tailwind, stated plainly: India's defence-modernisation and "design-and-make-in-India" drive is a genuine multi-year demand story, and it is written to favour exactly the firm that owns Indian IP down to the chip. The risk hidden inside the tailwind is that a lucrative, high-margin niche attracts capital — well-funded rivals and private entrants are moving into defence electronics, and the state can always route more work to BEL. The moat is qualification and IP, not a legal monopoly.

The metric that settles the thesis

Do not get lost in the P/E debate. For this specific company, the number that decides the outcome is the production-phase EBITDA margin — does it hold at ~38% as revenue tilts to hardware, or slide below? Pair it with book-to-bill (fresh orders divided by revenue): above ~1.0 and the ~₹2,062 Cr pipeline is refilling faster than it drains; below it, growth is borrowing from the backlog. If production margins stabilise in the high-30s and book-to-bill stays above one, the compounding is intact and the multiple can be carried. If production margins drift toward the sector's low-30s while order intake cools, the earnings base and the multiple correct together. Everything else — a strong quarter here, a soft one there — is noise around those two dials.

10

The lumpiness and the working capital

Why one quarter can carry half the year — and why cash lags profit.

Two features make Data Patterns' results jerky in ways a first-time reader must expect, or every quarter will look like a shock. The first is revenue lumpiness concentrated in the fiscal year-end; the second is heavy working capital baked into how the government pays.

The Q4 mountain

Defence revenue is recognised when systems are formally accepted and deliveries signed off — and in India that clusters heavily into the March quarter as annual budgets and acceptance milestones close out. The FY26 shape shows it starkly:

Q4 FY26 net profit (~₹138 Cr) was more than double Q3's (~₹58 Cr), with revenue up ~99% sequentially — a single quarter delivered roughly half the full-year profit. This is normal for the business, not an aberration.

The practical lesson: judge Data Patterns on trailing-twelve-months, never on a single quarter. A "weak" September quarter and a "blowout" March quarter can be the same programme's revenue landing on the far side of an acceptance signature. Reading the seasonality as a trend — in either direction — is the most common error in this stock.

Cash lags profit

  • Long receivables. Debtor days ran near ~290 in FY26 — the government pays slowly, and milestone billing stretches collection. Reported profit is real, but the cash arrives well after.
  • Heavy inventory. Inventory days near ~290 reflect long-lead specialised components and work-in-progress on multi-year programmes.
  • Net working-capital days approaching a full year. This is the structural cost of serving the defence customer, and it is why growth consumes cash — the faster Data Patterns scales production, the more working capital it must fund up front.
Why net cash is the quiet hero here

Now the balance sheet earns its keep. A working-capital-heavy defence supplier that also carried debt would be perpetually squeezed — exactly the trap that keeps most defence-ancillary margins thin and their balance sheets stretched. Data Patterns funds a near-year-long cash cycle out of its own reserves and net cash, so it can chase the production ramp without a lender's foot on the brake. The 40% margin and the net-cash sheet are not two separate virtues; the first is what makes the second affordable, and the second is what lets the first scale.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A rare-quality business at a price that assumes the ramp goes right.

Data Patterns is one of the highest-quality names in Indian defence: a founder-built, IP-owning, net-cash, ~40%-margin designer-builder riding a genuine multi-year indigenisation wave. The debate, honestly, is not whether it is a good business — it is whether the production transition holds margins near the top of the range while the valuation already prices years of flawless execution.

The bull case

  • Owned-IP moat — full-stack design down to chip logic, certified and hard to displace; qualification is a years-long barrier for any challenger.
  • Structural tailwind — indigenisation rules, a rising defence budget, and 30–40% electronics content per platform aim demand straight at this firm.
  • Production annuity forming — a decade's worth of qualified designs converting into repeatable orders, spares and upgrades.
  • Balance-sheet superpower — net cash and ~22% RoCE let it self-fund a working-capital-hungry ramp that would strangle a leveraged rival.
  • Optionality — exports (from a ~₹53 Cr base) and new programmes are upside not yet in the numbers.

The bear case

  • Very rich valuation — ~95× earnings and ~14× book bake in years of high growth and sustained top-tier margins.
  • Margin normalisation — management itself guides margins down as production scales; the market punished the reset once already.
  • Single-customer dependence — almost all revenue rides on Indian government budgets and procurement timing; exports still tiny.
  • Lumpiness & working capital — Q4-loaded revenue and ~290-day debtor/inventory cycles make cash flow jumpy and growth cash-hungry.
  • Execution risk — scaling into large production is a different discipline from bespoke design; slippage in the ramp hits both revenue and the multiple.
The bottom line

Data Patterns has done the genuinely hard thing in Indian defence: it built a shelf of qualified, home-designed systems that it owns outright, and it is now starting to sell them by the batch. The next chapter is not about winning new science projects — it is about proving that the same integrated house that invented the radar can build it at volume while holding a ~38% margin, and that its order intake keeps outrunning its billing. Watch two concrete markers as the tell: whether the LCA Tejas Mk1A avionics ramp and the conversion of the ~₹2,062 Cr negotiated pipeline into firm orders land on schedule, and whether the production-phase margin settles in the high-30s rather than sliding into the low-30s. Those milestones — not this quarter's headline — are where the case is won or lost.
