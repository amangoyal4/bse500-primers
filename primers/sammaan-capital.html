00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Sammaan Capital is a non-bank housing-and-retail lender (an NBFC/HFC) that mostly makes home loans and small secured business loans, borrows its money wholesale from banks and bond markets, lends it to households and small firms, and earns the gap between the two rates. You almost certainly knew it under its old name: until 2024 this was Indiabulls Housing Finance — for a while one of India's largest mortgage NBFCs, built by the Indiabulls group of founder Sameer Gehlaut. It was hammered by the 2018–19 NBFC liquidity crisis, a failed bank merger, and a heavy tail of stressed developer loans. What you are reading about is the survivor: smaller, cleaner, retail-focused, renamed, and — as of March 2026 — owned by an Abu Dhabi sovereign investor.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • It changed its name to change its story. In July 2024 the company dropped "Indiabulls Housing Finance" and became Sammaan Capital Limited — sammaan is Hindi for "respect" or "honour". A rebrand does not by itself fix a balance sheet, but here it marked a genuine reset: a new board-run structure with no founder, a run-down of the risky legacy book, and a pivot to plain-vanilla retail lending.
  • It deliberately posted a giant loss. FY26 (year ended March 2026) produced a headline net loss of ₹7,145 crore — after the company had actually earned ₹957 crore in the first nine months. The swing was self-inflicted: it reclassified ₹14,953 crore of legacy non-core loans and investments, revalued them, sold tranches to an asset reconstruction company, and stacked on a ₹1,850-crore extra provision — clearing the Indiabulls-era wreckage in one quarter. Remarkably, the stock rose on the news.
  • It has a new owner and a target. Abu Dhabi's International Holding Company (IHC) put in ₹8,850 crore ($1 billion) and became the promoter in March 2026 — bringing capital, credibility, and a controlling shareholder to a company that had none for three years. Management now guides to a return to profit (~₹1,400 Cr) and ₹70,000 crore+ of AUM in FY27.
In plain terms

Picture a large old house that was structurally sound in places and rotten in others. The previous owner walked away; for a while nobody owned it, and a professional caretaker crew quietly demolished the rotten wing rather than keep patching it. FY26 was the demolition — expensive, ugly on paper, but done on purpose. What remains is a smaller, sturdier house (a retail home-loan book worth around ₹44,000 crore), a new deed in Abu Dhabi's name, and a fresh nameplate on the door reading "Sammaan." The whole investment question is whether the renovated house is worth more than the demolition cost — and whether the new owner can extend it profitably.

01

Housing-finance economics 101: how a mortgage NBFC makes money

No finance degree required — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

A housing-finance company (HFC) is a specialised NBFC: a lender that is not a bank, so it cannot take ordinary savings deposits. That single fact shapes everything. Because it has no cheap public deposits, it must buy its money wholesale — from banks, from bond investors, and by packaging loans and selling them on — and then re-lend that money as mortgages and secured business loans at a higher rate. The difference between what it earns on loans and what it pays for funding, minus the loans that go bad and the cost of running the operation, is the profit. Six terms tell you almost the whole story.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and Sammaan's number)
AUM"Assets Under Management" — the total value of loans the company manages, on its own books plus those it has co-lent or sold to banks but still services. Sammaan's total AUM is ~₹53,160 Cr (Mar 2026), of which roughly ₹44,000 Cr is retail.
NIM / spread"Net Interest Margin" — the gap between the interest it earns and the interest it pays, as a share of the book. Wide is good; Sammaan's NIM is ~5.3%, healthy for a mortgage lender.
GNPA"Gross Non-Performing Assets" — the slice of loans where borrowers have stopped paying. Lower is safer. On the retail book Sammaan reports GNPA of only ~1.2% — the legacy trouble sat elsewhere (see §04–05).
Credit costThe money set aside each year for expected bad loans. For Sammaan this is the whole drama: FY26 credit cost and one-off provisions ran to thousands of crore as it cleaned the legacy book.
RoA / RoE"Return on Assets / Equity" — how much profit the book and shareholders' capital generate. Sammaan's RoE was negative in FY26 because of the reset; the case is whether it climbs back above ~10%.
Co-lending / assignmentPartnering with a bank so Sammaan originates the loan but keeps only part of it on its own books, earning a fee on the rest. The capital-light lever the whole strategy now leans on — see §02 and §07.
Why "asset-light" is the word that matters here

An old-style HFC keeps every loan on its own balance sheet, which means every rupee it lends consumes its own capital — grow the book, and you constantly need to raise more equity or debt. Sammaan is deliberately doing the opposite: it originates a home or MSME loan, keeps a slice, and hands most of the rest to a partner bank (which is delighted to own safe, priority-sector mortgages). Sammaan collects an origination-and-servicing fee and frees its capital to make the next loan. The prize is that it can grow the managed book without ballooning its own balance sheet or its own credit risk — which is exactly what a company nursing legacy scars and rebuilding trust wants. The price, which belongs in the risk column, is that a large part of its growth now depends on banks staying willing partners.

Who actually borrows from Sammaan?

  • Home-loan buyers — the core: mostly affordable and mid-market home loans to salaried and self-employed borrowers, secured by the property itself. Long tenure, well-secured, low loss rates.
  • LAP / MSME borrowers — small businesses taking loans against property (LAP) or secured MSME credit for working capital and expansion. Higher yield than home loans, still secured.
  • (Legacy) developer & wholesale borrowers — the Indiabulls-era book of large loans to real-estate developers and corporates. This is the part being deliberately run down and written off — the source of nearly all the pain (see §04–05).
02

The money machine: funding, lending & the asset-light twist

Follow a rupee from the bond market to a homebuyer and back, and the model clicks.

Because an HFC cannot fund itself with cheap public deposits, its whole existence is an arbitrage: raise money as cheaply as it can, lend it as safely as it can, and manage the gap. Sammaan's post-reset twist is that it increasingly does not hold the whole loan — it shares a large slice with a partner bank and earns a fee for originating and servicing it. Here is the cycle.

The co-lending / assignment step (Step 3) is the growth lever: by keeping only a slice of each loan, Sammaan can originate far more volume than its own capital alone would allow — earning fees on money that sits on a bank's balance sheet, not its own. That is the "asset-light, lean balance sheet" model in one picture.

Co-lending and sell-down, decoded

In a co-lending arrangement, Sammaan and a partner bank fund one loan together — typically the bank puts up the larger share and Sammaan the rest — with Sammaan sourcing the customer, doing the paperwork, and collecting the instalments. In a direct assignment or securitisation, Sammaan makes the whole loan and then sells a pool of such loans to a bank outright, retaining the servicing role. Either way the bank gets the priority-sector retail assets it struggles to originate itself, and Sammaan gets to grow without using up its own scarce capital for the full loan amount. The company has been building out these tie-ups — from roughly ten toward a dozen bank partners, largely mid-sized public- and private-sector banks — as the spine of the new model.

The surprise most people miss

For a company this size, Sammaan runs a strikingly small physical footprint: around 200+ branches and 8,000+ channel partners. The old Indiabulls machine was a sprawling, balance-sheet-heavy lender chasing scale; the new Sammaan is a leaner origination engine that deliberately hands the heavy lifting — the capital, and much of the risk — to bank partners. That is the whole reinvention in one contrast: the same franchise that once grew by piling loans onto its own books is now trying to grow by keeping loans off them.

03

The company and its three governance eras

Founder-built, then owner-less, now Abu-Dhabi-owned — a rare three-act ownership story.

Most Indian NBFCs have one identity: a founder and a family who control them. Sammaan is unusual because it has lived through three completely different governance regimes in a few short years — and understanding them is the fastest way to understand the whole business.

The three eras

  • Era 1 — the promoter-led Indiabulls (to ~2020/23). The company was the housing arm of Sameer Gehlaut's Indiabulls group, one of India's high-growth NBFCs of the 2010s. Gehlaut ran it as a classic founder-promoter business — aggressive growth, heavy on wholesale and developer lending.
  • Era 2 — the owner-less interregnum (2023 – early 2026). Gehlaut stepped down as chairman in 2020 and ceased to be a promoter entirely by February 2023, divesting his whole stake. For roughly three years the company had no identifiable promoter at all — a genuine rarity in India. It was board-controlled and professionally managed, chaired by former RBI Deputy Governor S. S. Mundra and led by long-time CEO Gagan Banga, with ownership spread across institutions and the public.
  • Era 3 — the IHC-owned Sammaan (March 2026 onward). Abu Dhabi's International Holding Company (IHC), via its affiliate Avenir Investment, invested ₹8,850 crore, completed an open offer, and in March 2026 was classified as the promoter — the company's first identifiable controlling owner since Gehlaut left (see §06).

Ownership & management (mid-2026)

  • Promoter — IHC (Avenir Investment). Post the preferential allotment and open offer, IHC holds roughly 28.4% of equity, rising toward ~41% on a fully-diluted basis once its ₹307-crore-warrant tranche converts over ~18 months. IHC exercises control through nominee directors and board sub-committees.
  • Institutions still dominate the float. Foreign investors (FIIs) held ~46% and domestic institutions (DIIs) ~12% at March 2026 — a heavily institutionally-owned company, a legacy of the promoter-less years.
  • Management continuity. Gagan Banga (23+ years with the group) remains MD & CEO through all three eras — the human thread of continuity between old Indiabulls and new Sammaan; S. S. Mundra (ex-RBI Deputy Governor) chairs the board.
A quirk worth knowing: for roughly three years Sammaan was one of the very few large Indian NBFCs with zero promoter holding — an ownership vacuum that is unusual, and cuts both ways. No promoter meant no dominant family agenda and a professional, board-led reset; it also meant no anchor to backstop the company or lend its name in a crisis. The IHC deal fills that vacuum — swapping "no owner" for a deep-pocketed sovereign-linked one.
04

The Indiabulls fall: how a giant ended up needing a reset

You cannot value Sammaan without knowing what happened to Indiabulls — this is the "why."

Sammaan's low valuation and its whole strategy are a direct answer to a period of near-death. In the late 2010s Indiabulls Housing Finance was flying: a top-tier mortgage NBFC growing fast, funded cheaply in the wholesale markets, and lending heavily not just to homebuyers but to real-estate developers and corporates in large-ticket "wholesale" loans. Then the ground gave way.

What broke, in order

  • The IL&FS shock (2018). When the giant infrastructure lender IL&FS defaulted in September 2018, it triggered a system-wide NBFC liquidity crisis. Wholesale funders — mutual funds and banks — suddenly refused to roll over short-term paper for any NBFC seen as risky. For a lender that had funded long-dated developer loans with short-dated market borrowing, that is an existential squeeze: the money you owe comes due faster than the money owed to you.
  • The developer-loan scare. Indiabulls' large exposure to real-estate developers — an inherently cyclical, illiquid, hard-to-value book — became the market's focus. Short-sellers and critics circulated allegations about the quality and governance of that lending. Whether or not every claim held up, the trust the wholesale market extends to an NBFC evaporated, and trust is an NBFC's true raw material.
  • The failed bank merger (2019). Indiabulls' escape plan was to merge with Lakshmi Vilas Bank and become a bank — which would have given it access to cheap, sticky public deposits and a stable funding base. In October 2019 the RBI rejected the merger. The lifeline was gone, and the company was left to survive as an NBFC dependent on skittish wholesale money.
  • The long shrink. With no cheap funding and a stressed wholesale book, the only survivable path was to deliberately shrink — stop chasing growth, run down the developer/wholesale loans, provision against the bad ones, and pivot toward smaller, safer, granular retail lending. That contraction, and the founder's exit, is the bridge from Indiabulls to Sammaan.
The counter-intuitive core: a lender's instinct is to grow; Sammaan's survival depended on doing the opposite. From its peak, the company spent years making its balance sheet smaller — running off wholesale loans faster than it added retail ones — because a shrinking, de-risking book was the only thing that could rebuild the market's confidence enough to keep lending to it. A once-giant NBFC that chose to shrink to live is the single most important fact about this company.
The generalist's takeaway

The Indiabulls episode is a textbook illustration of the one risk that defines NBFCs and has no equivalent in an ordinary company: an asset-liability mismatch feeding on a collapse in trust. Nothing was physically destroyed — no factory burned, no product failed. What happened is that the wholesale market simply stopped believing in the developer book, refused to refinance it, and a profitable lender was forced into years of retreat. Every strategic choice Sammaan makes today — asset-light, retail-only, run-down-the-legacy, invite a sovereign anchor — is an attempt to make sure that particular movie can never play again.

05

The great reset: the FY26 loss its shareholders cheered

The single most important recent event — a deliberate, self-inflicted ₹7,145-crore loss. Read this to understand the stock.

Here is the number that captures the entire Sammaan story, and it is genuinely strange: through the first nine months of FY26 the company earned a ₹957-crore profit. For the full year it reported a ₹7,145-crore loss. A single quarter — Q4 FY26, an ₹8,101-crore loss — erased three profitable quarters and then some. And it was done on purpose.

What actually happened in that quarter

As it finalised the IHC deal, Sammaan chose to take every remaining Indiabulls-era skeleton and dispose of it at once — a "kitchen-sink" clean-up, timed so the new owner would inherit a scrubbed balance sheet rather than a slow-bleeding one. The mechanics:

  • ₹14,953 crore of non-core loans and investments reclassified. The company changed the business model for this legacy pool — largely the old wholesale/developer exposures — moving it into a "held for sale / fair value" bucket, which forces it to be marked to what it is really worth today, not what it was booked at.
  • ₹6,499 crore of exceptional losses. The fair-value hits plus the sale of tranches of this pool to an asset reconstruction company (ARC) — a specialist buyer of distressed loans — crystallised a one-off ₹6,499-crore exceptional charge.
  • A ₹1,850-crore management-overlay provision. On top of the model-driven expected-credit-loss numbers, management added an extra ₹1,850-crore cushion (under the Ind AS 109 framework) against macro and sector risks — a belt-and-braces buffer so the cleaned book is conservatively covered.
  • Impairment charges surged. Impairment on financial instruments jumped to roughly ₹2,958 crore in the quarter, versus a small write-back the prior quarter — the accounting recognition of the legacy pain.
Why the stock rose ~7% on an ₹8,101-crore quarterly loss: because the market read it exactly as intended — not as new bad news, but as the end of old bad news. A one-time, self-chosen write-down that sells the toxic legacy to an ARC and over-provisions the rest removes the single biggest overhang on the company: the fear of what the developer book was really worth. Investors traded a known, one-off paper loss for the removal of a chronic uncertainty — and marked the shares up, not down.
The device to hold in your head

Sammaan's FY26 P&L is best read as two different companies stapled together. Company A is the ongoing retail lender that earned ~₹957 crore in nine months at ~5.3% margins and ~1.2% retail GNPA — a perfectly normal, mildly profitable HFC. Company B is a one-quarter demolition crew that booked ₹6,499 crore of exceptional losses and ₹1,850 crore of extra provisions to bury the Indiabulls estate. The reported ₹7,145-crore loss is the sum of a healthy A and a deliberately explosive B. The entire investment question is whether, from FY27, only Company A shows up — and Company B never has to be summoned again.

06

The IHC takeover: Abu Dhabi buys the reset

A $1-billion vote of confidence that gave a promoter-less company an owner — and a name to lean on.

The clean-up and the takeover are two halves of one plan. In October 2025 Abu Dhabi's International Holding Company (IHC) — a sprawling, sovereign-linked conglomerate and one of the largest listed companies in the Gulf — agreed to invest roughly ₹8,850 crore ($1 billion) in Sammaan through its affiliate Avenir Investment. By March 2026 the deal and the follow-on open offer were complete, and IHC was formally classified as the company's promoter and controlling shareholder.

The deal, decoded

  • Structure. A preferential allotment of ~330 million equity shares plus ~307 million convertible warrants at ₹139 per share. An initial ₹5,652 crore was infused up front, with the remaining ₹3,198 crore to arrive as the warrants convert over roughly 18 months.
  • Stake. After the preferential allotment and open offer, IHC holds about 28.4%; on full warrant conversion its holding rises toward ~41% on a fully-diluted basis — a clear controlling position.
  • Control. IHC gains direct operating oversight through nominee directors and board sub-committees, while continuity management (Banga, Mundra) stays in place. Sammaan describes itself, post-deal, as an "IHC Group company."
Why a sovereign anchor is worth more than the money

The ₹8,850 crore matters, but the credibility matters more. For an NBFC, funding cost is the business — and funding cost is a function of trust. A company still carrying the Indiabulls-era stigma borrows at a penalty; a company backed by a AAA-grade Abu Dhabi sovereign investor can, over time, borrow cheaper, because lenders believe someone deep-pocketed stands behind it. That is the real prize of the IHC deal: it swaps a three-year ownership vacuum for a strategic owner whose very name lowers the cost of the raw material — wholesale money — that the whole model runs on. The open question is execution: a Gulf conglomerate with no prior Indian-lending operating history now controls a mid-sized HFC, and turning capital and credibility into a durably profitable retail franchise is a different skill from writing the cheque.

07

The asset-light, co-lending pivot: the crux strategy

Grow the managed book without swelling the balance sheet — the engine of the new Sammaan.

Every piece so far — the shrink, the rebrand, the reset, the sovereign anchor — exists to enable one strategy: rebuild as a retail-focused, tech-enabled, asset-light lender. The idea is to originate plain, secured retail loans (home loans and LAP/MSME), keep only a slice on its own books, and push most of each loan onto partner banks through co-lending and sell-down — growing the business it manages while keeping its own balance sheet lean and its own credit risk contained.

The three moving parts

  • Run down the legacy, grow the retail. The wholesale/developer book is being actively shrunk (helped by the FY26 ARC sale); the retail book — now roughly ₹44,000 crore, ~80% of AUM — is the growth engine. The mix shift is the de-risking: every rupee that moves from developer to retail lowers the book's volatility.
  • Co-lend and sell down. Partnerships with a growing roster of banks (targeting around a dozen) let Sammaan distribute loans off its books for fees, keeping a "lean balance sheet" while still serving borrowers at scale through 200+ branches and 8,000+ channel partners.
  • Broaden the product shelf. Management's stated ambition is to move from a mostly-mortgage lender to a diversified retail NBFC with 12+ products and AUM of ₹70,000 crore+ by FY27 — using the new capital base and IHC's backing to re-accelerate origination.

The strategy removes the old risks; it does not remove the ordinary difficulty of growing a lender well.

A retail-led mortgage book, a healthy margin, and a legacy tail in run-off.

Strip away the drama and the underlying lender is now fairly simple: a mostly-mortgage book, well-secured, with a modest margin and — on the retail side — clean asset quality. The complexity is almost entirely in the legacy tail that is being retired.

Reading the asset-quality number correctly

A ~1.2% gross NPA looks reassuringly clean — but on its own it flatters the picture, because the point of the FY26 reset was precisely to remove the assets that would have shown up as NPAs and park them with an ARC or in the fair-valued pool. The fair way to read Sammaan's quality is not "GNPA is 1.2%, therefore all is well," but "the ongoing retail book is genuinely low-risk, and the legacy has been aggressively provisioned and sold down — so the residual question is whether the marks taken in FY26 were conservative enough." Given the ₹1,850-crore overlay stacked on top, the company has chosen to err toward over-provisioning; the proof will be whether any further legacy top-ups are needed in FY27.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded housing-finance market where banks and specialists both press in.

Home loans in India are contested from two directions at once: dedicated housing-finance companies fight for the affordable and self-employed segments, while banks — with their far cheaper deposit funding — increasingly muscle into mainstream mortgages. Sammaan sits among the mid-to-large HFCs, no longer the giant it once was, competing on reach and origination rather than the lowest rate.

The most useful comparison — and the twist

The rival to watch is not the biggest but the most similar: PNB Housing Finance. It, too, was a large HFC dragged down by a stressed corporate/wholesale book, spent years running it off and rebuilding a clean retail franchise, and was rewarded with a re-rating once the market believed the clean-up was real. Sammaan is attempting the same arc — with two differences that cut in opposite directions. In its favour: a fresh sovereign owner and a bigger, one-shot ARC clean-up rather than a slow grind. Against it: PNB Housing always had a strong bank parent's name; Sammaan is still shaking off the Indiabulls one. Sammaan's re-rating case is essentially "PNB Housing, but with Abu Dhabi instead of Punjab National Bank behind it."

Financials & valuation

A book-value stock: cheap on assets, unproven on returns.

Sammaan is the kind of stock where the income statement misleads and the balance sheet tells the truth. FY26's ₹7,145-crore loss is a one-off demolition charge, not the earning power of the business — so valuing it on that loss is meaningless. The market instead prices it on book value: how much net worth the company owns, and how much return it can eventually squeeze from it.

The one number that settles the thesis

For Sammaan, valuation reduces to a single question: can the cleaned, retail-led book earn a double-digit return on equity — and does that RoE actually show up in the reported numbers, starting with the FY27 guide of ~₹1,400 crore of profit? At ~1× book, the market is paying for the assets and assuming nothing about the returns — which is why a P/B near one is not really a "discount to book" so much as a verdict that the return on that book is, for now, close to zero. That is the whole re-rating mechanism: a lender that trades at 1× book and earns 4% RoE deserves 1× book; the same lender earning 13% RoE deserves closer to 1.5–2× book. Sammaan does not need a new story or a multiple expansion granted on faith — it needs the retail engine to convert into a mid-teens RoE, quarter after quarter, with no fresh legacy top-ups. Every other debate about this stock collapses into that one line: does RoE cross back into double digits and stay there.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cleaned-up survivor with a new owner and an unproven return — priced for exactly that.

Sammaan enters its next chapter having done the hard, ugly things: shrunk a dangerous book, exited a founder, taken the write-downs, and handed the keys to a sovereign owner. What it has not yet done is prove that the surviving franchise earns a good return. The investment case is a straight contest between a de-risked, sovereign-backed, retail HFC trading at book value and a company whose earning power is still a projection on a slide, over a legacy it only just finished burying.

The bull case

  • The legacy is largely gone — the ₹14,953-Cr non-core pool has been reclassified, marked down and sold to an ARC, with a ₹1,850-Cr overlay on top; the biggest overhang is off the table.
  • A real owner, deep pockets — IHC's $1 bn and its name can lower funding cost and restore trust after three promoter-less years.
  • Clean retail core — ~₹44,000 Cr retail book, ~5.3% NIM, ~1.2% GNPA, ~₹19,000 Cr net worth: the raw materials of a healthy HFC are all present.
  • Cheap on assets — ~1× book for a large, recapitalised lender; any credible return on equity makes that look inexpensive versus 2–5× peers.
  • A concrete, near-term test — the FY27 ~₹1,400-Cr profit and ₹70,000-Cr AUM guidance is specific and soon-checkable, not a vague long-term dream.

The bear case

  • Return is unproven — RoE is negative today; a large net worth earning nothing is worth exactly book, and the recovery to double digits is a plan, not a fact.
  • Legacy tail risk — if the FY26 marks prove too optimistic, fresh provisions could reappear; the ARC sale is done but the residual pool still exists.
  • Trust discount lingers — the Indiabulls-era stigma and short-seller history don't vanish with a new name; re-rating requires years of clean results.
  • Execution & partner reliance — asset-light growth depends on banks staying willing co-lenders and on a Gulf owner with no Indian-lending track record steering well.
  • Never the leader — out-scaled by LIC Housing, out-valued by Bajaj Housing, out-nimbled by affordable specialists; a rebuilt mid-tier HFC rarely earns a premium multiple quickly.
The bottom line

Sammaan Capital is a large housing lender that survived a near-death by deliberately making itself smaller, changed its name to shed its past, and has just finished paying the bill for that past in a single explosive quarter — a bill its new Abu Dhabi owner effectively funded. Everything toxic has been quarantined; everything hopeful is still a forecast. At about book value, the shares carry a fair reading of exactly that: the assets are real and recapitalised, the returns are a promise. This is not a story about a cheap multiple waiting to expand — it is a story about whether a rebuilt retail engine can earn its keep. Buy the assets today and you are underwriting management to turn ~₹19,000 crore of net worth into a mid-teens return; the moment that RoE arrives, the book value it sits on is worth far more than 1×.

The company spent one quarter burying Indiabulls and one signature handing the keys to Abu Dhabi. The next milestone that matters isn't a slogan — it's the FY27 result landing near the ₹1,400-crore profit management has promised, with the legacy pool never asking for another rupee.
