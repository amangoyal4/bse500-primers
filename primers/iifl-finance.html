00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

IIFL Finance is a non-bank lender (NBFC) that makes retail loans across four main products at once — loans against household gold, affordable home loans, tiny rural microfinance loans, and business/MSME credit. It borrows money wholesale (from banks, bond markets and by selling packages of loans to banks), lends it out to millions of small borrowers, and keeps the gap between the two rates. It is part of the Mumbai-based IIFL group founded by Nirmal Jain and R. Venkataraman in 1995, and after a 2019 restructuring it is the group's dedicated lending arm, separate from its broking and wealth cousins.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • Big, and genuinely diversified. Consolidated loan assets under management (AUM) crossed ₹1.08 lakh crore at March 2026, up 38% in a year, spread across gold (~49%), home loans (~30%), microfinance (~10%) and business/MSME/other (~11%). That product spread is the whole design: no single book is supposed to sink the group.
  • The regulator froze its core engine. In March 2024 the Reserve Bank of India ordered IIFL to stop sanctioning and disbursing gold loans entirely, after an inspection found breaches in how it assayed gold purity, monitored loan-to-value, ran auctions and handled cash. The ban lasted until September 2024. Its gold book collapsed by roughly 40% in a single quarter, the company had to raise emergency capital, and FY25 profit fell 71%.
  • A textbook recovery — so far. With the ban lifted and gold prices at record highs, IIFL rebuilt the gold book to ₹52,581 crore (well above its pre-ban peak), FY26 profit rebounded 214% to ₹1,817 crore, and asset quality (GNPA 1.46%) is the best in years. The open question is whether trust — and the RBI's tolerance — has fully returned.
In plain terms

Picture a retail lending supermarket. Where a specialist like Muthoot sells only gold loans, IIFL runs several lending counters under one roof: a gold counter (money against your bangles in minutes), a home-loan counter (small housing loans for first-time buyers), a microfinance counter (tiny loans to groups of rural women), and a business counter (working capital for shopkeepers). Each counter earns a spread; spreading across them is meant to smooth the ride when any one product hits trouble. The drama in the IIFL story is what happened when a regulator walked in and padlocked the busiest counter — the gold one — and told the company it could not open it until the paperwork was fixed.

01

Diversified-NBFC economics 101: how a multi-product lender makes money

No finance degree required — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

An NBFC is a lender that is not a bank: it cannot take ordinary savings deposits, so it has to buy its money wholesale — from banks, from bond investors, and by packaging loans and selling them on — and then re-lend that money to households and small businesses at a higher rate. The difference between what it earns on loans and what it pays for funding, minus the loans that go bad and the cost of running branches, is the profit. Six terms tell you almost the whole story.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and IIFL's number)
AUM"Assets Under Management" — the total value of loans the company manages. IIFL's consolidated loan AUM is ~₹1,08,180 Cr, up 38% in FY26.
NIM / spread"Net Interest Margin" — the gap between the interest it earns and the interest it pays, as a share of the book. A wide spread is why lending is profitable; IIFL's net interest income was ~₹4,304 Cr in FY26.
GNPA"Gross Non-Performing Assets" — the slice of loans where borrowers have stopped paying. Lower is safer. IIFL's GNPA fell to 1.46% (FY26) from 2.23% a year earlier.
Credit costThe money set aside each year to cover expected bad loans. It swings with the cycle — and it is what turned IIFL's microfinance arm from profit to loss in 2024–25.
RoA / RoE"Return on Assets / Equity" — how much profit the book and the shareholders' capital generate. IIFL printed ~2.4% RoA and ~13.1% RoE in FY26, recovering from a ban-hit low.
Co-lending / assignmentPartnering with a bank so IIFL originates the loan but keeps only part of it on its own books, earning a fee on the rest. A capital-light growth lever — see §02.
Why lend across four products instead of one

A single-product lender lives and dies by one market. Muthoot is ~90% gold, so its fate rides on the gold price and gold-loan rules. IIFL deliberately built a wider base: gold loans (secured by metal, low-risk, high-yield, very short tenure), home loans (large, long, ultra-safe, thin-margin), microfinance (tiny, unsecured, high-yield, high-risk), and business/MSME loans (medium everything). The bet is that these cycles do not all turn at once — when microfinance sours, gold and housing carry the group; when gold is frozen, the others cushion the blow. That is the theory of diversification. The catch, which IIFL learned the hard way, is that more products means more moving parts, more regulators watching more counters, and more places for something to break — and the "safe" secured gold book turned out to be the one that got shut down.

Who actually borrows from IIFL?

  • Gold borrowers — households and small traders who pledge jewellery for quick, no-questions cash, average ticket ~₹86,000, redeeming the gold within months.
  • Affordable-home buyers — first-time, often self-employed borrowers in smaller towns buying modest homes that mainstream banks find fiddly to underwrite.
  • Microfinance borrowers — groups of low-income rural women taking joint-liability loans of a few thousand rupees for small enterprise, through the Samasta arm.
  • Small businesses — shopkeepers and MSMEs needing working capital, secured against property (loan-against-property) or cash-flow.
02

The money machine: funding, lending & the co-lending twist

Follow a rupee from the bond market to a borrower and back, and the model clicks.

Because an NBFC cannot fund itself with cheap public deposits, its whole existence is an arbitrage: raise money as cheaply as it can, lend it as widely and safely as it can, and manage the gap. IIFL's twist is that it increasingly does not hold the whole loan — it shares a large slice with banks and earns a fee for originating and servicing it. Here is the cycle.

The diversified-NBFC money machine — funding to spread
Step 1 · Raise
Borrow wholesale
Bank lines, bonds/NCDs, securitisation. No retail deposits.
→
Step 2 · Lend
Across four books
Gold, home, microfinance, business — millions of small tickets.
→
Step 3 · Share
Co-lend / assign
Keep part on-book; sell/co-lend the rest to a bank for a fee.
→
Step 4 · Earn
Spread + fees
Interest income minus funding cost minus bad loans; plus fee income.
→
Step 5 · Recycle
Free the capital
Off-loaded loans free up capital to originate the next batch.
The co-lending / assignment step (Step 3) is the growth lever: by keeping only part of each loan, IIFL can originate far more volume than its own capital alone would allow — earning fees on money that sits on a bank's balance sheet, not its own.

Co-lending, decoded — and why it matters

In a co-lending arrangement, IIFL and a partner bank fund one loan together — typically the bank puts up ~80% and IIFL ~20% — with IIFL sourcing the customer, doing the paperwork, and collecting the instalments. In a direct assignment, IIFL makes the whole loan and then sells a pool of such loans to a bank outright, retaining a servicing role. Either way, the bank gets priority-sector or retail assets it struggles to originate itself, and IIFL gets to grow without using up its own scarce capital for the full loan amount.

The surprise most people miss

Roughly a third of the loans IIFL originates do not sit on IIFL's own balance sheet at all. As of the first half of FY26, its off-book portfolio was about 34% of total AUM — split ~39% co-lending and ~61% direct assignment — with the co-lending book alone at ~₹14,384 crore, up 36% in a year. Partners span SBI, Central Bank of India, ICICI, PNB, IDBI, DCB and others. This is a genuinely capital-light way to look big: IIFL reports a ₹1.08-lakh-crore franchise, but it carries materially less than that as its own credit risk. The flip side — and the reason it belongs in the risk column — is dependence on those bank partners: if regulators tighten co-lending rules, or a partner pulls back, a chunk of IIFL's growth engine loses its fuel.

03

The company and who controls it

From a 1995 research-and-broking start-up to a three-headed listed group.

IIFL — originally "India Infoline" — was founded in 1995 by Nirmal Jain, a former Hindustan Lever manager and chartered accountant, who was joined by co-founder R. Venkataraman. It began as an equity-research and broking house, rode India's retail-investing boom, and steadily built a lending arm. In 2019 the group split into three separately-listed companies to sharpen focus and satisfy regulators: IIFL Finance (this company — lending/NBFC), IIFL Wealth (asset & wealth management, since rebranded 360 ONE), and IIFL Securities / IIFL Capital (broking and capital-markets). This primer is only about the lender.

Ownership & management

  • Founders / promoters: Nirmal Jain (chairman) and R. Venkataraman (managing director) remain the promoters, holding roughly ~24.9% between them (no pledged shares) as of March 2026.
  • Anchor investor: Fairfax Financial (Prem Watsa's group, via Fairfax India) is the largest institutional shareholder — a long-standing backer that stepped up with a $200m liquidity line and participated in the 2024 rights issue when the gold ban hit.
  • Structure: IIFL Finance (the listed parent) directly runs the gold and business/MSME books; IIFL Home Finance (the housing arm) and IIFL Samasta Finance (microfinance) are separately-capitalised subsidiaries — so the "diversified NBFC" is really three lenders stacked under one holding company.
  • Reach: a large physical network of roughly 4,700+ branches across India, concentrated in the gold and microfinance businesses where in-person handover and collection are essential.
A quirk worth knowing: gold loans are ~91% of the standalone parent's AUM but under half of the consolidated group's — because home finance and microfinance live in their own subsidiaries. So when you read "IIFL Finance," check whether the number is standalone (very gold-heavy) or consolidated (genuinely diversified). This primer uses consolidated figures unless stated.

The four loan books: what IIFL actually lends

One company, four very different lending businesses — and a big chunk sitting off-balance-sheet.

The clearest way to understand IIFL is to open its four counters one by one. Each has a different risk shape, tenure and margin, and each grew (or shrank) very differently in FY26 — gold roaring back after the ban, home loans deliberately flat, microfinance shrinking through a stress cycle. The four counters, decoded

  • Gold loans — the biggest and most profitable book: money against pledged jewellery, average ticket ~₹0.86 lakh, yield ~18.1%, average LTV ~63%. Very short tenure, very low loss rate (the gold sits in the vault). AUM ~₹52,581 Cr, up 150% in FY26 on the rebuild plus a gold-price surge.
  • Home loans (IIFL Home Finance) — affordable and "emerging-market" housing loans, deliberately held roughly flat (~1% growth) while the business was repositioned toward smaller-ticket, self-employed borrowers. The cleanest, safest, longest-tenure book.
  • Microfinance (IIFL Samasta) — tiny unsecured joint-liability loans to low-income women. AUM ~₹10,501 Cr, shrinking through the 2024–25 sector stress but healing (see §07).
  • Business, LAP, MSME & digital — loan-against-property, small-business and new-age digital loans; the company has leaned into MSME as a fresh growth engine, disbursing ~₹9,430 Cr of new MSME loans in FY25.
The diversification paradox — and how it actually played out

IIFL diversified precisely so that no single shock could floor it — and in FY24–25 the design was tested twice, from opposite directions. First the secured gold book, supposedly the safe one, was frozen by a regulator; the home and business books kept the company alive through the freeze. Then the unsecured microfinance book blew up in an industry-wide cycle; the now-recovered gold book carried the group. So diversification did its job — the company never came close to failing — but the episodes exposed a blunt truth: in a four-product lender, at any given time at least one book is usually in trouble. The reward for spreading out is resilience; the price is that management is permanently fighting a fire somewhere.

05

The RBI gold-loan embargo: the day the core engine was switched off

The single most consequential event in IIFL's recent history — read this to understand the whole stock.

On 4 March 2024, the Reserve Bank of India did something rare and severe: it ordered IIFL Finance to immediately stop sanctioning, disbursing, assigning, securitising or selling any gold loans. Not a fine, not a warning — a full halt on the company's largest, most profitable business, with immediate effect. For a lender, being told to stop lending is roughly the corporate equivalent of a surgeon being told to stop operating. This is the crux of the IIFL story, and it is worth understanding in detail.

Why the RBI acted

The RBI said a supervisory inspection (as of March 2023) had found "material supervisory concerns" in how IIFL ran its gold-loan business. The specific breaches it cited:

  • Purity & weight assaying. "Serious deviations" in how the purity and net weight of the pledged gold were certified — both at the moment the loan was sanctioned and at auction when a defaulted loan's gold was sold. If you mis-measure the collateral, every loan sits on a false foundation.
  • Loan-to-value (LTV) breaches. Lending more against the gold than the rules permit — eroding the safety cushion that makes gold lending low-risk in the first place.
  • Cash limits. Disbursing and collecting loan amounts in cash far in excess of the statutory limit — a compliance and anti-money-laundering red flag.
  • Auction & charges. Non-adherence to the standard auction process, and a lack of transparency in the charges levied on customers.
Why this was so damaging: gold loans were the profit heart of the company. The RBI froze not just new lending but also IIFL's ability to sell or assign its existing gold loans — cutting off a funding and capital-recycling channel at the same time. The order said IIFL could keep servicing its existing gold book, but could originate nothing new. With gold loans being ultra-short-tenure, an existing book that cannot be replenished simply runs off and shrinks, fast.

What it cost — and how IIFL responded

  • The book collapsed. Gold-loan AUM fell from roughly ₹24,000 crore (March 2024) to ₹14,727 crore by June 2024 — a ~38% drop in a single quarter, down ~33% year-on-year, as loans matured and could not be re-lent.
  • Profit cratered. Full-year FY25 consolidated profit fell 71% to ₹578 crore (from ~₹1,994 crore in FY24) as the highest-margin engine sat idle for half the year.
  • Emergency capital. Anchor shareholder Fairfax pledged $200 million of liquidity support within two days; IIFL then raised ₹1,271 crore via a rights issue in May 2024 (₹300/share, 1:9, oversubscribed 135%) to steady the balance sheet during the freeze.
  • A process overhaul. IIFL engaged a special external audit, rebuilt its gold assaying, LTV-monitoring, cash-handling and auction processes, and strengthened internal governance to satisfy the RBI.
  • The lift. After remediation and a special audit, the RBI lifted the restrictions on 19 September 2024 — pointedly just before the festive gold-buying season — letting IIFL restart gold lending.
The lesson a generalist should take away

The IIFL episode is the cleanest case study you will find of a specific NBFC risk that has no equivalent in most industries: a single regulator's letter can switch off a lender's best business overnight — no default, no fraud conviction, no market crash required. The RBI did not seize IIFL or question its solvency; it found process and compliance failures and simply revoked its permission to originate one product. That is enough to shrink a ₹24,000-crore book by 40% in ninety days and halve annual profit. For any NBFC, therefore, compliance is not overhead — it is the licence to keep the doors open. IIFL's recovery is real and impressive, but the scar it leaves is that the market now prices a permanent question: could it happen again?

Footnote (2025): the saga had a small coda — the RBI later levied a token ₹3.10-lakh penalty tied to a specific lapse (failure to remit surplus auction proceeds to some borrowers), a reminder that supervisory scrutiny of the gold book did not simply vanish when the ban lifted.

The gold book's round-trip: from ₹14,727 Cr to ₹52,581 Cr

One book, two years, a full collapse and a more-than-triple rebound — the number that captures the whole story.

If you want one chart that explains IIFL, it is the path of its gold-loan AUM. The same business — same branches, same appraisers, same product — went from a pre-ban peak, to a regulator-induced trough, to a record high, in the span of two years. The collapse was a compliance order; the rebound was remediation plus one of the biggest gold-price rallies in living memory.

Why the rebound was so violent — the gold-price tailwind

A gold lender's book is roughly tonnage × gold price × LTV. IIFL did not just win back customers after September 2024; it won them back into a market where the gold price was smashing records. Domestic gold ran from roughly ₹48,000 per 10 grams a few years ago to well over ₹1.5 lakh per 10 grams by early 2026. The same bangle that once backed a ₹50,000 loan could now back a far larger one at the same LTV — so every new customer, and every rolled-over old one, carried more rupees of loan. The whole gold-NBFC sector's AUM was projected to swell toward ₹15 lakh crore by March 2026 on this dynamic. IIFL's 150% gold-AUM jump in FY26 is part real market-share reclaim and part pure gold-price arithmetic.

07

Samasta and the microfinance stress cycle

The book that turned a recovery year sour — and is now healing.

While the gold engine roared back, another book was going the other way. IIFL Samasta Finance, the microfinance arm — tiny, unsecured, joint-liability loans to low-income rural women — was caught in an industry-wide microfinance downturn in 2024–25: years of over-lending left many borrowers over-leveraged, defaults rose across the sector, and the RBI leaned on the whole industry to tighten pricing and conduct. Samasta was not spared.

What happened

  • The sector cracked. Across India, microfinance defaults spiked in 2024–25 as borrowers who had taken loans from multiple lenders at once could no longer service them all — a self-inflicted, cyclical blow-up the whole industry shared.
  • Samasta shrank on purpose. IIFL deliberately ran down and wrote off bad microfinance loans; the book contracted to ~₹10,501 crore, with elevated credit costs dragging the arm's profitability to a near-breakeven ~₹21 crore PAT for FY26.
  • The turn is visible. Asset quality is mending: Samasta's gross NPA fell to 3.87% in Q4 FY26 from 4.93% the prior quarter, and disbursals are being restarted more cautiously. The worst of the drag appears to be passing.
Why this matters beyond one book: microfinance is the sharpest illustration of the diversification trade-off. It is high-yield when times are good and unsecured when they turn — so it whipsaws the group's credit cost in a way the gold vault never does. The Samasta stress is exactly the kind of pain the gold-heavy standalone parent avoids, and exactly the kind the diversified consolidated group has to absorb.
The read to hold in your head

The microfinance stress was largely a cyclical, industry-wide event, not an IIFL-specific failure — peers across the sector took similar hits, and IIFL's response (shrink, provision, clean up) was the textbook one. The reassuring signal is that the group could absorb a microfinance downturn and a gold-loan ban within eighteen months of each other and still print a ₹1,817-crore FY26 profit — which is diversification earning its keep. The watch-item is whether Samasta's clean-up holds as it re-accelerates, because a second microfinance leg-down would test the group's patience with unsecured lending altogether.

08

Home finance and the affordable-housing pivot

The quiet, ballast book that held the company steady through the storm.

Overshadowed by the gold drama and the microfinance mess is IIFL's most stable business: IIFL Home Finance, its affordable-housing lending arm. Long-tenure, well-secured mortgages to first-time and self-employed buyers in smaller towns — a book that grows slowly, loses little, and provides the ballast that let IIFL survive the gold freeze.

  • Deliberately flat, by design. Home-finance AUM grew only ~1% in FY26 as management repositioned the book toward smaller-ticket, affordable and "emerging-market" borrowers and away from chunkier loans — prioritising quality and granularity over headline growth.
  • The safest book. Housing loans are backed by the home itself and repaid over many years; loss rates are low, which is why home finance anchors the group's asset quality and why lenders and rating agencies view it as the reliable core.
  • Capital-light too. A large slice of the home book is co-lent or assigned to banks (which love priority-sector affordable-housing assets), so IIFL earns origination and servicing fees while parking much of the loan on a partner's balance sheet — the reason its managed home book (~₹40,000 Cr) is larger than the slice it carries on its own consolidated balance sheet.
  • Growth ambition intact. Management guides to renewed home-loan growth of ~15–20% as the repositioning completes — a return to expansion once the book is re-shaped.
Why the boring book is strategically vital

During the six-month gold freeze, home finance was one of the businesses that kept IIFL's lights on: it kept originating, kept collecting, and kept generating fee income while the gold counter was padlocked. That is the entire case for diversification in one real-world stress test — the slow, low-margin, low-drama book is worth most precisely when the exciting, high-margin book is in trouble. A pure gold lender under the same RBI order would have had nothing to fall back on; IIFL had two other engines still turning.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded field — because IIFL competes in four markets at once, it faces a different rival in each.

Being diversified means IIFL has no single competitor; it has a different one at every counter. In gold it faces the specialists; in housing, the affordable-home lenders and banks; in microfinance, the MFIs; and across everything, the giant diversified NBFCs and the banks with cheaper funding. That is both a strength (no one rival can attack it everywhere) and a weakness (it is rarely the number one in any single product).

The awkward middle — and the twist

IIFL's competitive position has a genuine tension: it is rarely the leader in any one product — Muthoot out-golds it, Bajaj out-diversifies it, the big MFIs out-scale its microfinance. A specialist can pour everything into being the best at one thing; a generalist spreads its focus. But the same banks that pressure IIFL's yields are also its co-lending partners — SBI, ICICI, Central Bank and others fund alongside it. So IIFL's answer to "banks are cheaper" is not to out-price them but to partner with them: originate the customer the bank can't reach, and share the loan. Its edge is reach and origination in under-served pockets — gold in small towns, affordable homes for the self-employed, credit for the unbanked — not the lowest rate.

The one number that settles the thesis

For IIFL, forget the flashy growth rates — the number that decides the case is consolidated RoE, and whether it climbs back to and holds its pre-ban mid-teens level with a clean RBI inspection file. FY26's ~13.1% RoE is the recovery becoming visible; the pre-ban franchise earned more like the high-teens. At ~3.1× book, the market is already paying for a return to that pre-ban profitability — so the stock does not need a re-rating so much as it needs the RoE to show up and stay up, quarter after quarter, without a fresh supervisory surprise. If RoE settles durably in the mid-to-high teens and no new RBI order lands, the multiple is defensible and the earnings compound into it. If RoE stalls in the low teens — or a regulator re-appears at any of the four counters — a ~3.1× book looks like it front-ran a recovery that only half arrived. Every other debate about IIFL resolves into that single line: durable, clean, mid-teens-plus RoE, or not.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A recovered franchise with a spotless-again gold book — and a regulatory scar it can't fully erase.

IIFL enters its next chapter having survived a stress test few lenders face: a six-month freeze of its best business by the regulator, plus a microfinance downturn, inside eighteen months — and it came out the other side with AUM above ₹1 lakh crore, GNPA at 1.46%, and profits at a record. The investment case is a genuine tug-of-war between a proven, diversified, capital-light lender trading below the sector's leaders and a company whose core engine a regulator has already switched off once.

The bull case

  • Recovery is real, not hoped-for — gold book rebuilt above its pre-ban peak, PAT above FY24, GNPA down to 1.46%: the franchise demonstrably works again.
  • True diversification — four books meant no single shock (gold ban, MFI cycle) could floor the group; that resilience is now battle-tested.
  • Capital-light growth — a third of AUM off-balance-sheet via co-lending/assignment lets it grow faster than its own capital, with fee income on top.
  • Gold & MSME tailwinds — record gold prices lift the core book; MSME is a fresh, secured growth engine with 20–30% guidance.
  • Cheaper than the leaders — ~13× earnings and below Muthoot/Bajaj on quality-adjusted value, with a Fairfax anchor behind it.

The bear case

  • Regulatory scar — the RBI has frozen its core business once; the possibility it recurs at any of four counters is now a permanent part of the price.
  • Gold-price dependence — a sharp, sustained gold crash would reverse the AUM boom and force loss-making auctions.
  • Unsecured drag — microfinance can whipsaw credit costs; a second MFI leg-down would sting the group again.
  • Co-lending reliance — a chunk of growth depends on bank partners and favourable co-lending rules that regulators could tighten.
  • Never the leader — out-golded by Muthoot, out-diversified by Bajaj; a generalist rarely earns a specialist's premium multiple.
The bottom line

IIFL Finance is a large, genuinely diversified retail lender that has just walked back from the edge — a company whose most valuable engine a regulator padlocked for six months, and which rebuilt it bigger than before while a second book (microfinance) was on fire. That it earned a record ₹1,817-crore profit through both shocks is the diversification thesis vindicated. What it cannot buy back is the memory: the market now knows, in specific rupee terms, exactly what an RBI order can do to this company — subtract ~₹9,000 crore of book in a single quarter. At ~3.1× book, the recovery is largely in the price; from here the stock is a bet on clean, durable, mid-teens-plus RoE and, above all, on the RBI inspectors never having cause to write another letter like the one dated 4 March 2024.

The gold counter that the RBI switched off on 4 March 2024 reopened on 19 September. The next milestone that matters isn't a growth number — it's an entire inspection cycle that passes without a single fresh adverse remark.
