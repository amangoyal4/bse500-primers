Cummins India, the engine that powers the backup

A complete walk through India's dominant diesel-engine and power-generation company — the maker whose gensets keep hospitals, factories and, increasingly, AI data centres running when the grid fails. This primer explains how a debt-free, cash-rich, ~40%-return-on-capital engine business actually makes money; why a single regulatory change (CPCB IV+ emission norms) reset the entire genset industry's pricing; how servicing a giant installed base of engines is a quiet, high-margin annuity; why the American parent uses India as an export hub for the world; and how a diesel company is hedging toward hydrogen — all against a premium ~65× valuation that leaves no room for error.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Cummins India makes diesel (and increasingly gas) engines and the generator sets ("gensets") built around them, plus the spare parts and service that keep those engines alive for decades. It is the listed Indian arm of Cummins Inc., the ~US$34bn American engine giant, which owns 51%. Think of it as the company that supplies the backup power for the country — the diesel generator humming behind a hospital, a mall, a telecom tower, a factory, and, more and more, a hyperscale data centre. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) it earned ₹11,950 crore of revenue and ₹2,330 crore of net profit, at a ~21.4% operating margin — and did it debt-free, throwing off cash, and earning a return on capital near 40%.

Four facts frame everything about this company:

  • It is the market leader in Indian gensets, not a bit player. When India's unreliable grid fails, someone has to supply the diesel power that fills the gap. Cummins India is the biggest, most trusted name in the higher end of that market — from a small shop's back-up set all the way to the multi-megawatt units that keep an AI data centre from ever going dark.
  • Its profit runs on four engines. Power Generation (gensets — the big growth driver), Industrial (engines for mining, rail, marine, construction, defence), Distribution (spare parts & service — the high-margin annuity), and Exports (India as a global sourcing hub for the American parent). They behave very differently, and knowing which is firing tells you the whole story of any quarter.
  • Two structural forces are supercharging it right now. A data-centre building boom (AI needs vast, uninterruptible power) is driving genset demand, and a new emission standard (CPCB IV+) has reset the entire industry's technology and pricing — pulling demand forward and lifting the value of every genset sold. Both play to the strengths of the technology leader.
  • It is priced like a compounder, not a cyclical. The stock trades near 65× earnings at a ~₹1.57 lakh crore market cap — a rich multiple for a capital-goods company. Bulls say a debt-free, 40%-ROCE, structurally-growing power franchise deserves it; bears say it is an industrial cyclical priced for perfection.
In plain terms

Picture India's power problem: the grid is far more reliable than it used to be, but for a hospital, a factory, a bank's server room — or a data centre running billions of AI queries — "far more reliable" isn't good enough. A few seconds of darkness can cost a fortune or a life, so these customers buy their own on-site diesel power plant that switches on automatically the instant the grid drops. Cummins India builds that machine, and it is the brand serious buyers trust. But the clever part isn't just selling the engine — it's that every engine it has ever sold keeps needing filters, oil, overhauls and genuine spare parts for 15–20 years, and only Cummins can supply them. So the company sells a machine once, then earns a steady, high-margin income stream servicing it for two decades. That "sell the razor, then sell the blades forever" dynamic — layered on top of a once-in-a-generation data-centre boom and a regulation that just made every genset more valuable — is why the market treats a heavy-engineering company like a premium consumer brand.

01

How an engine maker actually earns

No engineering degree required — just the handful of forces that drive the profit.

A genset business is, at heart, a volume-and-mix machine grafted onto an annuity. It earns in two very different ways, and understanding the split is the key to the whole company: there is the one-time sale of the equipment, and the forever sale of what keeps that equipment running. The equipment sale is cyclical and competitive; the service stream is sticky and fat-margined.

The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (units sold × price per unit × margin) + (installed base × spend-per-engine-per-year × margin). The first bracket is the cyclical equipment business — it swings with the economy and with one-off drivers like the data-centre boom and emission-norm changes. The second bracket is the annuity: as long as Cummins keeps selling engines, its installed base grows, and that base throws off ever more parts-and-service revenue at high margins regardless of the cycle. The genius of the model is that a good equipment year isn't just good today — it plants a service crop that compounds for two decades. That's why the "boring" distribution business is the one analysts quietly love most.

The three levers of the equipment business

  • Volume — how many gensets and engines it ships. Driven by construction, infrastructure, manufacturing capex, and now data centres. This is the cyclical lever.
  • Realisation (price/mix) — the average selling price per unit, which jumps when the mix shifts to bigger, higher-horsepower machines, or when a regulation (like CPCB IV+) forces more technology into every unit. Higher realisation lifts revenue with no extra volume.
  • Operating leverage — because a big chunk of costs (plants, engineering, R&D-sharing with the parent) is fixed, once volumes rise, extra revenue drops disproportionately to profit. This is exactly what drove FY26's margin expansion: revenue grew ~17–18% while costs grew ~14–15%, so margins widened ~140 basis points.
02

The company and the American parent

A 60-year-old joint venture that became an export hub — and a governance nuance worth understanding.

Cummins India is one of the oldest and most successful foreign joint ventures in Indian industry. It began in 1962 as "Kirloskar Cummins" — a partnership between the American engine maker Cummins Inc. and India's Kirloskar Group — and over six decades evolved from a licensed local manufacturer into a global manufacturing and sourcing base for its parent. Cummins Inc. lifted its stake to 51% in 1997 and the company was renamed Cummins India Limited in 1998.

Timeline

  • 1962

  • 1980s–90s
    Launches emission-compliant engines; sets up Fleetguard (filters) and, in 1993, Tata Cummins (engines for Tata trucks) — deepening the India ecosystem.
  • 1997–98
    Cummins Inc. raises its stake to 51%; the company is renamed Cummins India Limited — the parent takes control.
  • 2011
    Inaugurates the Phaltan Megasite in Maharashtra — a 225-acre campus consolidating engine, genset and high-horsepower plants, an export base for the group.
  • 2020–23
    Positions for the future: Destination Zero low-carbon strategy, hydrogen-ICE for Tata Motors, and gears up for the CPCB IV+ emission transition.
  • FY26
    Record year — revenue ₹11,950 Cr, PAT ₹2,330 Cr; data centres drive powergen; margins expand to ~21.4%; stock hits all-time highs at ~65× earnings.

Ownership & who controls it

  • Cummins Inc. (US) — 51% promoter. The American parent controls the company, supplies its core engine technology and platforms, funds >US$1bn/year of group R&D (which Cummins India accesses without paying for it directly), and directs the export mandate. This is both the company's greatest strength and a governance nuance (see below).
  • Institutions — ~40%. Foreign institutional investors (~19%) and domestic institutions/mutual funds (~21%) hold the bulk of the free float — a high-quality, widely-owned large cap.
  • Retail — ~9%. Public shareholders hold the rest. Shares are pledge-free; the company confirmed zero promoter encumbrance for FY26.
Why the parent is a moat — and a nuance

The Cummins Inc. relationship is a genuine competitive advantage: Cummins India gets access to world-class engine technology and billions of dollars of R&D it could never fund alone, plus a ready-made global export channel — the parent routes engine manufacturing to India and sells the output worldwide. That's how a mid-sized Indian company competes at the technology frontier. The nuance: when a controlling MNC parent both supplies technology and buys the exports, related-party pricing matters — how much of the value stays in the listed Indian entity versus the parent is a fair minority-shareholder question. In practice Cummins India has been a clean, well-governed, dividend-generous company; but "the parent decides" is a permanent feature to keep in mind. Minority holders own a great business — they just don't steer it.

The four segments, broken into parts

Power, Industrial, Distribution, Exports — four engines with four very different rhythms.

Cummins India reports along four business lines. Roughly 80% of sales are domestic (split across Power Generation, Industrial and Distribution) and ~17–20% are exports. In FY26, powergen and distribution did the heavy lifting, exports recovered, and industrial stayed roughly flat — a mix that tells you exactly where the growth is coming from.

04

Power generation and the data-centre supercycle

Why the AI boom shows up as a genset order — and why it's the biggest single force on the stock.

The most important thing happening to Cummins India is not, on the surface, about engines at all — it's about artificial intelligence. Every AI query, every model trained, every cloud service runs in a data centre, and data centres consume enormous, absolutely-uninterruptible amounts of power. India is in the early innings of a massive data-centre build-out, and each new facility needs a bank of large diesel gensets as backup. That demand has become the single biggest driver of Cummins India's powergen segment.

Why a data centre needs so many diesel generators

A data centre can never lose power — even a momentary blackout can corrupt data, crash services and cost millions. Batteries (via a UPS) bridge the first few seconds and minutes; but for a sustained grid outage, the facility falls back on diesel gensets that spin up automatically and can run for hours or days. A single large data centre campus can need tens of megawatts of backup — that's a fleet of the biggest, high-horsepower gensets Cummins makes, each a multi-crore machine. So India's AI ambitions translate, very directly, into orders for exactly the product Cummins India leads in. And because reliability is non-negotiable, buyers pick the most trusted brand — they don't shop on price for the thing that stops their business going dark.

Demand is led by "colocation" (colo) operators — companies that build data-centre space and rent it out — with global hyperscalers (Microsoft, AWS, Google, etc.) increasingly adding to it. Management sees a multi-year (3–4 year+) runway for this demand.
  • It's a genuine supercycle, not a spike. India is expected to add gigawatts of data-centre capacity through 2030 as global cloud and AI players localise. Powergen revenue grew ~24% in FY26 and analysts model a ~20% growth rate continuing for years — unusually visible demand for a capital-goods company.
  • The high-horsepower mix lifts margins and realisation. Data-centre gensets are among the largest, most complex units Cummins builds ("HHP" — high horsepower). Selling more big machines lifts the average price per unit and pulls in the parent's advanced technology — a mix shift that helps margins.
  • It seeds a huge future annuity. Every data-centre genset installed today becomes decades of service and parts revenue tomorrow. The AI boom isn't just a one-time equipment sale — it's planting the highest-value crop the distribution business has ever seen.
The other side: data-centre demand is real but concentrated in a handful of large buyers, and it is lumpy — big orders land in bursts, so any single quarter can look strong or soft on timing alone. And the same AI enthusiasm that drives the demand also inflates the valuation: if the data-centre build-out slows, or shifts to grid/renewable-plus-battery backup faster than expected, a premium-priced stock has a lot to give back.
05

The CPCB IV+ reset: how a rule changed the whole market

A single emission standard rewired the genset industry's technology, pricing and demand timing — read this once and a lot of the last two years clicks.

In July 2023, India enforced a new, far tougher emission standard for diesel gensets called CPCB IV+ (from the Central Pollution Control Board), replacing the old CPCB II norms. It sounds like dry regulation, but it did three powerful things to the industry at once: it raised the technology bar, reset prices upward, and pulled demand forward — and every one of those favoured the technology leader, Cummins.

What actually changed, technically

CPCB IV+ demands roughly a 90% reduction in particulate matter and NOx versus the old CPCB II standard. Meeting that isn't a tweak — it turns a genset from a largely mechanical machine into a sophisticated electronic one: electronic controls, advanced after-treatment (selective catalytic reduction and diesel particulate filters), remote monitoring and predictive maintenance. In other words, the government forced far more content and technology into every single unit.

The three mechanisms — why this matters for earnings

(1) The technology moat widens. Meeting CPCB IV+ needs exactly the electronic engine controls and after-treatment that Cummins' global parent has spent billions developing for stricter Western/emission norms. Small, mechanically-focused genset makers struggle to meet it; the technology leader was first to market with compliant products. A tougher standard is a barrier that shakes out weaker rivals.

(2) The price of every genset resets higher. Because each unit now carries far more expensive technology (after-treatment, sensors, controls), the average selling price rises — the cost is passed to customers. So even at the same volume, the industry sells the same number of machines for more money. That structurally lifts realisation. (Pricing took a few quarters to settle as the industry digested the new architecture.)

(3) Demand gets pulled forward — the "pre-buy." Whenever a costlier standard is coming, buyers rush to purchase cheaper, soon-to-be-banned equipment before the deadline — a "pre-buy" surge — then demand dips, then normalises on the new (higher-priced) product. This creates a bumpy transition but, on the other side, a structurally higher-value market. By late FY25, CPCB IV+ units were already ~40% of Cummins' genset sales.

  • It even feeds the annuity. The new electronic gensets need specialised service, and the standard also created a retrofit market — states (e.g. Maharashtra) mandating emission-control devices (RECD) on older CPCB II gensets, which flows straight into the high-margin distribution business.
  • Warranty tailwind ahead. The first CPCB IV+ machines begin exiting warranty from ~FY27 — at which point their owners start buying paid service and parts, adding another leg to distribution growth.
  • Net effect: a regulation that looked like a compliance headache became a durable tailwind for the leader — higher prices, a wider moat, and a fresh service pipeline. This is the classic way a standards change reshapes an industrial market in favour of the strongest player.
06

The distribution annuity — the quiet cash machine

Why servicing a giant installed base of engines is the most valuable, least appreciated part of the company.

If power generation is the exciting front of the shop, distribution is the vault at the back. It is the business of keeping every Cummins engine ever sold in India running — supplying genuine parts, oil, filters, overhauls, service contracts and, now, telematics and emission retrofits. It grew ~22–26% in FY26, it is the highest-margin, stickiest, most recession-resistant revenue in the company, and it gets bigger every single year the equipment business sells more machines.

The razor-and-blades logic, in plain English

Selling a genset is like selling a razor — a one-time, competitive sale. But that genset then needs genuine Cummins parts, filters, coolant and periodic overhauls for its entire 15–20-year life — the "blades." And here's the lock-in: for a critical machine that must not fail — a hospital's backup, a data centre's power — owners overwhelmingly use genuine Cummins parts and Cummins-authorised service, because a cheap counterfeit filter that causes a failure at the wrong moment is unthinkable. So Cummins effectively has a captive, price-insensitive customer for two decades after each sale. The bigger its installed base grows, the larger and more predictable this blade-revenue becomes — a compounding annuity that doesn't care much about the economic cycle. That is why a razor-thin genset order today is worth far more than it looks: it's the entry ticket to twenty years of high-margin blades.

Why the annuity keeps getting bigger

  • A growing installed base. Every genset sold in the powergen boom — especially the big, high-value data-centre units — adds to the pool of machines Cummins will service for decades. Good equipment years compound the annuity.
  • New revenue layers. Beyond traditional parts, distribution now sells telematics (remote monitoring of engine health), RECD emission-retrofit devices on older gensets (mandated in some states), and new-product service — widening the wallet per installed engine.
  • Geographic and channel expansion. More service points, more geographies, and deeper penetration of the existing base all lift distribution revenue without needing a single new engine sale.
  • Margin ballast. Because distribution is higher-margin and non-cyclical, its growing weight in the mix steadily lifts and stabilises the company's overall profitability — a quiet reason FY26 margins expanded.
The equipment business wins the customer; the distribution business keeps him for twenty years. That second sentence is where the durable value lives.
07

The export engine — India as the world's workshop

How the parent turned its Indian arm into a global manufacturing base — a moat with a governance twist.

Cummins India doesn't just serve India — it makes engines, gensets and parts for the world. Because it has world-class, low-cost manufacturing (the vast Phaltan Megasite is the parent's largest high-horsepower engine plant anywhere), Cummins Inc. routes global production and sourcing through India and exports the output to 60+ countries. Exports were ~₹1,989 crore in FY26 (~17% of sales, +12%) — a meaningful, if volatile, fourth engine.

  • India as a sourcing hub. The parent deliberately uses Cummins India as a global manufacturing base — combining Indian cost efficiency with Cummins' global technology and quality standards. This gives the Indian entity scale, technology transfer and a demand source beyond the domestic cycle.
  • A currency and diversification tailwind. Exports earn foreign currency, so a weaker rupee helps realisations (though a portion of the benefit is typically shared with customers over time). Exports also diversify the company beyond India's construction and infrastructure cycle.
  • But it is lumpy and geopolitically exposed. Export demand swings with global capex, tariffs and geopolitics — it fell ~6% in Q4 FY26 even as the full year grew ~12%, and management stays "cautious amid geopolitical developments." It's the least predictable quarter-to-quarter line.
The moat-and-nuance of being an MNC's export arm

Being the parent's global sourcing hub is a real advantage: it hands Cummins India scale, cutting-edge platforms and a worldwide customer base it could never build alone. But it also means the parent decides how much manufacturing to place in India, and at what transfer price the intra-group exports are booked. For minority shareholders, that's the governance twist: a large share of export economics is set by a controlling parent whose interests (spread across many global entities) don't always perfectly align with the listed Indian company's. In practice the relationship has been value-additive and the company well-run — but "exports depend on the parent's global allocation and pricing" is a structural dependence, not a fully independent growth lever.

08

Destination Zero — a diesel company hedging its own disruption

How the maker of diesel engines is positioning for a world that wants to burn less diesel.

Here is the existential question for any engine maker: what happens to a diesel business in a decarbonising world? Cummins' answer — globally and in India — is a strategy called Destination Zero: a deliberate, two-track plan to keep improving today's engines while investing in the low- and zero-carbon powertrains of tomorrow. It's how a company whose core product burns diesel is trying to avoid being disrupted by the energy transition rather than run over by it.

The two tracks

  • Track 1 — clean up the engine we have. Make today's diesel engines dramatically cleaner and more efficient (exactly what CPCB IV+ forces), and pivot toward natural gas — the most mature, available alternative fuel, aligned with India's push to raise gas in its energy mix. This buys time and revenue in the near-to-medium term.
  • Track 2 — build the zero-carbon powertrains. Invest in hydrogen internal-combustion engines, hydrogen fuel cells, and battery-electric platforms for when the economics and infrastructure arrive. The centrepiece is a "fuel-agnostic" engine: one common engine block that can be configured to run on diesel, natural gas, or hydrogen — so a single platform hedges across whichever fuel wins.
Why "fuel-agnostic" is the clever hedge

Nobody knows exactly which clean fuel wins, or when. Betting the whole company on hydrogen, or on batteries, or on gas would be reckless. So Cummins builds one base engine and swaps the fuel system and cylinder head to run diesel today, gas tomorrow, hydrogen later — reusing most of the machine across every scenario. That protects its manufacturing investment and lets it follow customers into whatever fuel the market and regulation choose, without a costly bet-the-company gamble. It's the industrial equivalent of a diversified portfolio: don't predict the winner, be ready to serve all of them.

  • Real, early moves. Cummins began producing hydrogen internal-combustion engines (the B6.7H) in India for Tata Motors trucks in 2024, and showcases hydrogen and fuel-cell technology at Indian expos — this is a live programme, not a slide.
  • But it's optionality, not earnings — yet. Hydrogen and fuel cells are tiny today and depend on green-hydrogen cost and refuelling infrastructure that don't exist at scale. For now, Destination Zero is a hedge and a narrative that protects the long-term story; the profits still come from diesel and gas.
  • The realistic base case: diesel and gas engines remain the cash engine for a long time (backup power especially stays diesel), while these clean-fuel bets mature slowly. The value of Destination Zero is that it stops the market pricing Cummins as a "stranded diesel asset" — it credibly positions the company on the right side of the transition.

How to think about the ~65× multiple

At roughly ₹1.57 lakh crore market cap on ~₹2,330 crore of FY26 profit, Cummins India trades near 65× trailing earnings — nearly double its own ten-year median of ~35× and rich for any industrial. That price is paying for three things: (1) the visible multi-year data-centre and CPCB IV+ tailwinds; (2) the exceptional quality — ~40% ROCE, debt-free, a compounding service annuity; and (3) scarcity — few listed ways to own India's power-and-AI-backup theme this cleanly. Bulls argue a structurally-growing, capital-light-ish, 40%-return franchise deserves a premium and can grow into the multiple as powergen and distribution compound ~20%. Bears counter that this is still a cyclical industrial with a lumpy export line and a diesel core, priced for perfection — and that at 65× any stumble (a data-centre air-pocket, an export shock, a margin wobble) invites a sharp de-rating. Both are right at different points in the cycle, which is why the segment mix each quarter matters so much.

10

The industry and the rivals

A market where brand, technology and service — not price — decide the winner.

India's genset and industrial-engine market is competitive but stratified: at the low end (small kVA sets) it's crowded and price-driven; at the high end (large, high-horsepower, critical-power) it narrows to a few technology leaders where reliability and brand trump price. Cummins is the dominant force in that higher-value tier — precisely where the data-centre and CPCB IV+ growth is.

Why Cummins wins where it matters

In the segments that are growing fastest — large gensets for data centres, hospitals and critical infrastructure — the buyer's first question isn't "what's cheapest" but "what will never fail, and who will service it fast for the next twenty years." That question favours the player with the best engine technology (from a global parent that outspends everyone on R&D), the strongest brand, and the widest service network. CPCB IV+ deepened this advantage by forcing electronic, after-treated technology that smaller mechanical-genset makers can't easily match. So even as the low end stays a price war, Cummins is entrenching itself at the profitable, high-technology top of the market — exactly where the structural demand is heading.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely excellent business — and a price that assumes it stays excellent.

Cummins India is one of the cleaner quality stories in Indian capital goods: a debt-free, ~40%-ROCE, market-leading power franchise, riding two rare structural tailwinds (AI data centres and an emission-norm reset), backed by a global technology parent, and compounding a high-margin service annuity underneath. The investment debate is almost entirely about price and cyclicality, not business quality.

The bull case

  • Data-centre supercycle — a multi-year, high-value demand driver for exactly the big gensets Cummins leads in; ~20% powergen growth visibility.
  • CPCB IV+ tailwind — higher realisation per unit, a wider technology moat, and a fresh retrofit/warranty-exit service pipeline.
  • Distribution annuity — a growing installed base compounding a sticky, high-margin, recession-resistant service stream.
  • Fortress economics — ~40% ROCE, debt-free, strong cash conversion, generous dividends; margins expanding on operating leverage.
  • Parent + optionality — global technology access, an export hub, and Destination Zero positioning for the energy transition.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~65× earnings (2× its history) leaves no room for error; a cyclical priced like a compounder.
  • Cyclicality & lumpiness — industrial and export lines swing with capex and geopolitics; data-centre orders arrive in bursts.
  • Diesel core — the long-term energy transition is a genuine (if slow) structural question; clean-fuel bets aren't yet profitable.
  • Parent dependence — the MNC controls technology, export allocation and related-party pricing; minorities don't steer.
  • Demand concentration — data centres and a few large buyers now drive powergen; a slowdown there would hurt disproportionately.
The bottom line

Cummins India is a structurally advantaged, exceptionally profitable power franchise at a genuinely interesting moment — the AI-driven data-centre boom and the CPCB IV+ reset are real, durable tailwinds, and the distribution annuity quietly compounds beneath them. The business risk is low; the valuation risk is real. The story over the coming quarters will be written in three numbers: powergen growth (is the data-centre demand still compounding?), distribution growth (is the annuity widening?), and margins (is operating leverage still delivering?). Get those right and a ~40%-return, debt-free leader can grow into its premium; stumble on any of them — a data-centre air-pocket, an export shock, a cyclical industrial dip — and a 65× stock has a long way to fall.
