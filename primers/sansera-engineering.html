Sansera Engineering, the engine-parts maker racing to outgrow the engine

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Sansera Engineering makes precision metal parts — the small, complex, safety-critical components buried inside an engine, a gearbox, a suspension or a braking system. It takes a lump of steel or aluminium, forges it into a rough shape under enormous pressure, then machines it on computer-controlled tools to micron tolerances, and ships a finished, ready-to-fit part to a vehicle maker. Think connecting rods, rocker arms, crankshafts, gear-shift forks and a long tail of others. Its customers are the world's big engine and vehicle makers — Indian and global, across two-wheelers, passenger cars and commercial vehicles — plus a fast-growing book of non-automotive work in aerospace, defence and off-road machinery.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a Tier-1 precision supplier, not a bulk forger. Sansera doesn't sell tonnes of rough steel; it sells finished, qualified, safety-critical parts that are designed in to a customer's engine or platform and stay there for the model's whole life. That makes the revenue sticky and the margins tied to complexity, not commodity prices. FY26 revenue was ~₹3,498 Cr (+16%), EBITDA ~₹632 Cr at an 18.1% margin, and PAT jumped ~51% to ~₹327 Cr.
  • A big chunk of its "auto" content dies with the combustion engine — and it knows it. Connecting rods and rocker arms exist because there is a piston engine to serve. If the car goes electric, those parts vanish. So Sansera is deliberately shrinking its exposure to internal-combustion engine (ICE) parts and growing everything that survives electrification: tech-agnostic auto parts, electric-vehicle (xEV) components, and non-auto aerospace and defence work. In FY26 the mix was roughly 68.5% Auto-ICE / 13% tech-agnostic + xEV / 18.5% non-auto.
  • The aerospace bet is bigger than it looks. Sansera's Aerospace, Defence & Semiconductor (ADS) arm did only ~₹315 Cr of revenue in FY26 — under a tenth of the company — but it grew ~155% and sits on an unexecuted order backlog of ~₹4,464 Cr, which is larger than the entire company's FY26 revenue. That backlog is the clearest signal that the diversification is real, not a slide in an investor deck.
In plain terms

Sansera is a precision-parts workshop for the world's engine and vehicle makers — it forges and machines the metal bits that have to be exactly right or something breaks. The uncomfortable truth at its core is that a large slice of what it sells only exists because engines burn fuel, and the world is slowly moving away from that. So the company is in a race against its own product: grow the parts that don't need an engine — for electric cars, for aircraft, for defence — fast enough to replace the engine parts before they fade. The whole investment question is not "how big is Sansera?" but "how quickly is the non-engine half of Sansera growing, and is it as profitable as the half it's replacing?"

01

Forged-and-machined 101: how a lump of metal becomes a con-rod

No engineering degree needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

Every complex machine — a car engine, an aircraft actuator, a truck gearbox — is a stack of precisely shaped metal parts that must fit together and survive years of stress. Two manufacturing steps turn raw metal into those parts, and Sansera does both. Understand them and you understand what the company sells and why it is hard to copy.

The only process terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
ForgingShaping metal by hammering or pressing it under huge force, usually hot. The metal's internal grain flows to follow the shape, which makes a forged part far stronger and more fatigue-resistant than a cast or machined-from-bar one. That is exactly what you want in a part that flexes millions of times, like a connecting rod.
Machining (CNC)Cutting the forged blank to exact size on computer-controlled (CNC) tools — drilling, milling, grinding to tolerances measured in microns. This is where a rough forging becomes a finished, fit-ready component. It is the step that adds most of the value and the difficulty.
Safety-criticalA part whose failure could cause an accident or destroy the machine — a con-rod, a crankshaft, an aircraft bracket. These parts face the toughest qualification and can't be sourced casually, which is what makes the supplier relationship sticky.
ToleranceHow little variation a part is allowed — often a few microns (thousandths of a millimetre). Tight tolerances demand better equipment and process control; they are the moat between a precision supplier and a general job-shop.
Aluminium vs steelSteel forgings dominate engine internals (strength). Aluminium forgings are lighter — prized for electric vehicles and aerospace, where every kilogram saved matters. Sansera does both, and its aluminium capability is central to its future mix.
The economics that decide everything: value-add per part

Sansera's profit is not in the metal — steel and aluminium are commodities anyone can buy. It is in the transformation: taking a cheap blank and turning it into a certified, micron-accurate, safety-critical part that a customer will pay a large multiple of the raw-material cost for, and will keep buying for the life of the vehicle. The more complex the part and the tighter the tolerance, the more of that price is Sansera's engineering rather than pass-through metal. This is why the company chases complexity — high-precision, high-difficulty parts — rather than volume. A more complex part with an aerospace-grade finish carries far more margin per kilogram than a simple stamping, even though both start as the same metal.

Where these parts end up

  • Inside engines & transmissions — connecting rods, crankshafts, rocker arms, gear-shift forks. The traditional heart of the business, and the part most exposed to electrification.
  • In the "rolling chassis" — suspension, braking and steering components that a vehicle needs whether it runs on petrol, a battery or a hybrid. These are the tech-agnostic parts that survive the switch to EVs.
  • In aircraft, defence and off-road machines — precision-machined brackets, structural and actuation parts for aerospace; components for defence platforms; parts for agricultural and off-highway equipment. The non-auto book Sansera is scaling hard.
02

What Sansera actually sells: three books under one roof

The product families — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

It is easy to see Sansera as "an auto-parts maker" and stop there. The more useful lens — and the one management itself uses — is to split the company into three books of business defined not by what the part is but by whether it needs a combustion engine to exist. That single distinction is the spine of the investment case, so it is worth drawing the value chain around it.

The three books, decoded

  • Book 1 — Auto-ICE (internal-combustion) parts — the historical core: connecting rods (Sansera is among the world's larger makers), rocker arms, crankshafts, gear-shift forks and other engine/transmission internals for two-wheelers, cars and commercial vehicles. High-quality, sticky, cash-generative — but structurally tied to the piston engine. This is the ~68.5% of FY26 revenue that faces the long, slow headwind of electrification.
  • Book 2 — Tech-agnostic auto + xEV — parts a vehicle needs regardless of what powers it: suspension, braking, steering and other chassis components, plus components designed specifically for electric and hybrid powertrains (motor and battery-system parts, EV-specific structural and driveline pieces). ~13% of FY26 and growing. Crucially, this book turns the EV threat into an opportunity — an EV still needs a suspension arm and a brake component; some even need more precision aluminium than an ICE car.
  • Book 3 — Non-automotive (ADS & off-road) — the diversification engine: precision-machined aerospace components, defence parts, semiconductor-related and industrial work (grouped as "ADS"), plus agricultural and off-highway machinery components. ~18.5% of FY26, the highest-margin ambition, and the book with a multi-year order backlog behind it.
Why splitting the company this way matters

A normal auto-ancillary is valued on the auto cycle: when vehicle sales rise, it earns more; when they fall, it earns less. Sansera is asking to be valued on something different — a deliberate migration from a shrinking pool (engine parts) to growing ones (everything engine-free). If you only track total revenue, you miss the entire point: the company could grow revenue modestly while quietly transforming what that revenue is made of, and it is the composition, not the total, that decides whether Sansera is a melting ice-cube or a re-rating growth story. That is why every section that follows keeps returning to one figure — the share of revenue that does not depend on a combustion engine.

03

The company, the founders, and who controls it

A Bengaluru precision house, founder-built — with a surprisingly small promoter stake.

Sansera Engineering was founded in Bengaluru in 1981 by S. Sekhar Vasan (Chairman & Managing Director) and F.R. Singhvi (Joint Managing Director), who between them have run the business for over four decades. From a single precision-machining operation it grew into a multi-plant forging-and-machining group with facilities across India and a footprint in Sweden, and it listed on the NSE and BSE in September 2021.

  • 2000s–2010s

  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters (approx. ~29%): the founding families around S. Sekhar Vasan and F.R. Singhvi. Note this carefully — the promoter holding is unusually low for an Indian founder-run company (many peers sit at 50–75%). A large part of the register is institutional and public, a legacy of the private-equity backing that pre-dated the IPO and the offer-for-sale at listing. It means the founders run the business but do not hold a commanding equity majority — worth tracking in the shareholding filings.
  • Institutional base: domestic mutual funds and foreign investors hold a substantial slice, reflecting the market's appetite for the diversification story. This also means the stock trades on institutional expectations — and can be punished quickly if the mix-shift narrative wobbles.
  • Operating footprint: a network of manufacturing plants across India (Karnataka, Maharashtra, Uttarakhand and others) plus a Sweden aluminium-forging facility, giving it both low-cost Indian machining and a European presence close to global customers.
The ownership quirk worth noticing: a promoter stake near ~29% is low enough that Sansera behaves more like a professionally-run, institution-owned company than a typical family-controlled ancillary. That cuts both ways — it aligns management with public shareholders and keeps the register liquid, but it also removes the deep promoter cushion that steadies many Indian small-caps when a cycle turns. The founders' operating record, not a controlling block, is what underwrites the story.
04

The EV question: the mix shift that is the whole thesis

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to this company.

Here is the problem Sansera cannot escape and does not pretend to. A connecting rod exists to connect a piston to a crankshaft. A rocker arm exists to open and close an engine valve. A crankshaft is the engine's spine. Take away the combustion engine — as electrification eventually will, at some pace, in some markets — and those parts have no reason to be manufactured at all. There is no EV version of a con-rod. This is not a cyclical headwind that passes; for the engine-parts book it is a slow, structural sunset. Every serious question about Sansera reduces to one thing: how fast can the engine-free half of the company grow to replace the engine half before that sunset arrives?

This is why total revenue is the wrong scoreboard. Imagine Sansera grows revenue 12% next year but every rupee of that growth comes from ICE engine parts — the company got bigger and more exposed to the very thing that threatens it. Now imagine flat total revenue but the non-ICE books doubling their share — the company stood still on size while transforming its terminal value. The market is not paying ~₹20,000 Cr for the first outcome; it is paying for the second. The number that separates them is the non-ICE mix and its rate of change.

The device that only fits Sansera: the crossover clock, not the revenue line

Most manufacturers you can judge by whether the top line grows. Sansera you have to judge by a race between two curves inside the top line. Curve one: the ICE engine-parts book, sticky and cash-rich today, on a multi-decade fade as engines slowly disappear from new vehicles. Curve two: the engine-free books (tech-agnostic + xEV + aerospace/defence), small today at ~31.5% but compounding fast. The entire equity value hinges on the crossover — the point where the growing curves out-earn the shrinking one and the fade in engine parts stops mattering. FY26's ~31.5% non-ICE share, up from a lower base, is the clock reading. If that share marches toward 40% and then a majority while margins hold, Sansera earns its multiple by becoming a different company. If the share stalls in the low-30s, the market is paying a growth price for a business quietly running down its own core.

05

The aerospace & defence pivot: the hedge being built in plain sight

The smallest book today, the biggest strategic lever — and the one with a backlog behind it.

The most striking thing about Sansera is not what it sells today but what it is building. Grouped as ADS — Aerospace, Defence & Semiconductor — this vertical takes the same forging-and-machining skill Sansera honed on engine parts and points it at components that have nothing to do with cars: precision-machined structural and actuation parts for aircraft, components for defence platforms, and industrial/semiconductor-related work. It is small — ~₹315 Cr in FY26, under a tenth of revenue — but it is where the company's future optionality lives.

That is multi-year execution visibility for a business most investors still file under "auto components."

Why does this matter so much for such a small book today? Because aerospace and defence work is the strongest possible answer to the EV question. It is completely engine-agnostic — an aircraft bracket doesn't care what powers cars. It is higher-margin — aerospace tolerances and certifications command premium pricing. And it is extraordinarily sticky: once a part is qualified onto an aircraft or defence programme, it is designed in for the platform's life, often decades, with switching costs that dwarf anything in autos. A large qualified backlog in ADS is therefore not just revenue-in-waiting; it is proof that global aerospace primes have accepted Sansera as a supplier — the hardest gate in the industry to pass.

Why the backlog is the tell, not the current revenue

Aerospace has a brutal on-ramp: qualifying a single part can take years of audits, test articles and certification before the first rupee of production revenue appears. That lag is exactly why the backlog, not today's ~₹315 Cr, is the number to watch. A ~₹4,464 Cr book of orders already won means the hard, slow qualification work is largely done and the revenue is now a question of ramping production, not winning trust. For a company racing a crossover clock, having a multi-year, high-margin, engine-free order pipeline already banked is the single most valuable asset it owns — it converts the diversification story from "we hope to grow ADS" into "we have to execute an ADS book we've already signed."

The India angle: the timing of Sansera's aerospace-and-defence push lines up with two national tailwinds — India's drive to localise defence manufacturing and its ambition to become a global aerospace machining hub as Western OEMs diversify supply chains away from single-country dependence. A qualified, low-cost, precision-machining supplier in India is exactly what that shift needs. It means Sansera's ADS ramp is riding a structural sourcing move, not just its own sales effort.
06

Tech-agnostic & xEV parts: the auto content that survives

The overlooked middle book — why not all of "auto" is at risk from electrification.

Between the shrinking engine book and the glamorous aerospace book sits the quietly important middle: automotive parts that either don't care what powers the vehicle (tech-agnostic) or are made specifically for electric and hybrid vehicles (xEV). Together ~13% of FY26 revenue, this book is the reason "Sansera is an ICE company" is too crude a summary — a large part of its automotive expertise carries straight across the electric transition.

Split it in two. Tech-agnostic parts are the ones every vehicle needs whatever is under the bonnet: suspension components, braking-system parts, steering and other chassis pieces. A battery-electric car still has four wheels, a suspension that has to absorb bumps, and brakes that have to stop it — often needing heavier-duty versions because EVs are heavier. xEV parts go further: components designed for the electric powertrain itself — motor and gearbox parts, battery-housing and structural aluminium, driveline pieces unique to EVs. Here the switch to electric is not a threat at all; it is net-new demand Sansera can win precisely because it already knows how to forge and machine precision aluminium.

Why aluminium is the through-line

The single capability that ties Sansera's future together is precision aluminium forging and machining. Aluminium is light, and lightness is what both electric vehicles and aircraft are desperate for — an EV wants to offset its heavy battery, an aircraft wants to burn less fuel. So the same aluminium skill feeds three of Sansera's growth vectors at once: xEV structural parts, tech-agnostic lightweight components, and aerospace machined parts. A company that spent decades forging steel con-rods for engines has been retooling toward the material that the post-engine world runs on. That is the connective tissue beneath the three-book story — one core competency, pointed at wherever the metal is going.

The nuance to hold: tech-agnostic and xEV content is genuinely defensive against electrification, but it is not automatically high-margin. Some chassis and EV parts are more commoditised than a complex engine internal, and EV programmes can be fiercely price-negotiated. So this book protects volume through the transition, but whether it protects margin depends on Sansera winning the complex, high-value pieces rather than the simple ones. Watch the mix within the mix.
07

Why the parts are sticky: the qualification moat

The economics of a designed-in Tier-1 precision supplier.

Precision forging-and-machining is not a business you win on price alone, and that is the whole reason Sansera can earn respectable margins on what looks, from a distance, like metal-bashing. The moat is qualification: the long, exacting process by which a customer certifies a part and a supplier before trusting them with something safety-critical. Once through it, the supplier is embedded for years.

Consider what it takes to become the con-rod supplier for a global engine, or a machined bracket for an aircraft. The customer audits your plant, your metallurgy, your process controls. They run your sample parts through fatigue and stress testing. They validate that you can hold micron tolerances batch after batch, for millions of parts, without a single failure — because a failed con-rod destroys an engine and a failed aircraft part can be catastrophic. That process takes months in autos and years in aerospace. Having invested all that, a customer does not casually re-shop the part to save a few percent; the risk and cost of re-qualifying a new supplier dwarf the saving. So the part stays with the incumbent for the model's or platform's entire life.

Why "designed-in" is the phrase that matters

The best precision parts are not bought off a shelf; they are designed in — engineered jointly with the customer for a specific engine, chassis or aircraft, and locked into the bill of materials before the product ever launches. A designed-in part carries three gifts: pricing power (the customer can't easily substitute it), longevity (it sells for the whole product cycle, often 5–10 years in autos and decades in aerospace), and a head-start on the next programme, because the incumbent supplier is first in line. Sansera's decades of designed-in engine parts are what funded its diversification; its designed-in aerospace and xEV wins are what will fund the next decade. The moat isn't a factory — anyone can buy CNC machines — it's the accumulated trust that gets you designed in.

The flip side is that this stickiness works against Sansera too. Winning new content — especially in a fresh vertical like aerospace — means running that same slow qualification gauntlet before earning a rupee. That is why the ADS ramp takes years and why the backlog matters more than current revenue: it represents qualification gates already passed. And it is why customer concentration is a real risk — losing a large designed-in programme, or a big customer losing share, hits hard precisely because the relationships are so deep.

08

Exports and the customer book

A genuinely global supplier — with the concentration that implies.

Sansera is not an India-only ancillary that happens to export a little. A large share of its revenue comes from global customers, and it supplies engine and vehicle makers across Europe, North America and Asia alongside the big Indian names. That international footprint is both a strength — access to the world's most demanding, highest-value programmes — and a source of currency and concentration risk.

But a heavily export-and-large-customer book means the revenue is concentrated: a handful of big programmes and customers drive a large slice of sales, so a model going out of production, a customer losing share, or a demand air-pocket in one region lands directly on Sansera. And because much of the revenue is in foreign currency, the rupee's moves swing reported results even when volumes are steady. The global footprint is what makes the diversification credible; it is also what makes the earnings lumpier than a purely domestic ancillary's.

09

The industry and the rivals

A scaled forging-and-machining field — where Sansera is the precision-and-diversification specialist.

Forging and precision machining in India is a business of a few serious, scaled players, because the capital, metallurgy and qualifications needed to make safety-critical parts keep out the casual entrant. Sansera competes with the country's forging majors, but it has staked out a distinct position: higher-complexity precision components and a faster tilt into non-auto, rather than sheer forging tonnage.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, Sansera is not trying to out-tonnage Bharat Forge — it is competing on precision and complexity, the harder-to-copy, higher-value end of forging-and-machining, and on how quickly it can move that skill into engine-free work. Second, almost every domestic peer faces the same electrification question at once, which makes the diversification pace the real differentiator: the forger that grows its non-auto and xEV share fastest, at the best margin, wins the re-rating; the one that stays anchored to ICE tonnage gets valued as a fading cyclical. Sansera is betting that being smaller and more focused lets it pivot faster than the giants — but it is pivoting against rivals who are far better capitalised.

The one number that settles the thesis: the non-ICE share and its margin

At roughly ~₹20,300 Cr of market value on ~₹327 Cr of FY26 profit, Sansera trades at a growth-stock multiple — the market is not pricing an engine-parts cyclical, it is pricing a company that will transform its mix. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the combined non-ICE revenue share (tech-agnostic + xEV + aerospace/defence) and its growth, at a held-or-rising EBITDA margin. Watch it alongside the ADS order backlog converting to revenue (is the ~₹4,464 Cr book turning into sales on schedule?) and EBITDA margin holding ~18%+ as the mix changes. If the ~31.5% non-ICE share climbs toward 40% and beyond while margins stay firm, the multiple is defensible and Sansera really is becoming an engine-free precision company. If the share stalls in the low-30s or the new books come in at thinner margins, the market is paying a growth price for a business still tethered to a fading engine core. Total revenue is the distraction; the non-ICE share at margin is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A precision supplier engineering its way out of its own core market — asked to prove it can do so at pace and at margin.

Sansera enters the next phase with a rare setup: a cash-generative engine-parts base funding a deliberate migration into engine-free, higher-margin work, a multi-year aerospace-and-defence backlog already banked, and a management team that has run the business for four decades. The real debate is not whether the diversification exists — the ADS backlog settles that — but whether it scales fast enough, and profitably enough, to outrun the slow sunset of the engine-parts book before the market's patience with a premium multiple runs out.

The bull case

  • Real, banked diversification — a ~₹4,464 Cr ADS backlog and a non-ICE share already ~31.5% mean the pivot is executing, not aspirational.
  • Margin expansion with mix — FY26 EBITDA margin rose to 18.1% as higher-value work grew; the richer the mix, the better the margin should get.
  • One skill, three growth vectors — precision aluminium forging feeds xEV, tech-agnostic and aerospace at once, so the core competency compounds across markets.
  • Qualification moat — designed-in, safety-critical parts are sticky for a platform's life; new aerospace wins are decade-long annuities.
  • Structural tailwinds — India's defence localisation and global aerospace supply-chain diversification pull demand toward exactly Sansera's ADS offering.

The bear case

  • Electrification erodes the core — ~68.5% of revenue is ICE engine content with no EV equivalent; the pivot must outrun a structural, not cyclical, decline.
  • Rich valuation — a growth multiple on a component maker prices the pivot working; a stall in the non-ICE share or a margin slip can de-rate it sharply.
  • Execution & scale-up risk — aerospace qualification takes years; converting the ADS backlog to margin-accretive revenue on schedule is unproven at scale.
  • Cyclicality & concentration — a few large OEMs and programmes drive much of revenue; a lost platform, a demand air-pocket, or FX swings hit hard.
  • Capex, working capital & well-funded rivals — diversification is capital-hungry, and Bharat Forge and others chase the same non-auto and aerospace pools with deeper pockets.
The bottom line

Sansera is that unusual thing: a company knowingly building the replacement for its own best business. Its engine-parts book is excellent and doomed in the same breath — cash-rich today, structurally fading tomorrow — and every rupee it throws off is being redeployed into the parts a post-engine world will still buy: brake and suspension components an EV needs, aluminium for electric powertrains, and machined structures for aircraft and defence. The setup is genuinely attractive; the catch is the arithmetic of a race. The next few years answer it in a single, checkable figure — the pace at which the non-ICE share climbs from ~31.5% toward a majority while the EBITDA margin holds at 18%-plus, with the ~₹4,464 Cr aerospace backlog as the leading indicator of whether that climb is on track. Watch that mix number and that backlog conversion each quarter; together they tell you whether Sansera is out-running the engine's sunset or merely spending richly to postpone it.
