Smartworks Coworking, offices as a managed service

A complete walk through India's largest managed-campus operator by area — a company that leases bare-shell office floors from landlords, fits them out, and re-lets them as fully-serviced offices to large enterprises. Why it is not the coworking-for-freelancers brand you might picture, how a fit-out and a lease turn into a profit, and why the same year produces a 64%-margin operating line and a rounding-error net profit at the bottom.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Smartworks Coworking Spaces Limited runs fully-managed, fully-serviced offices for companies that don't want to build and run their own. It signs long leases on large, bare-shell office floors from property owners, spends its own money designing and fitting them out — desks, cabins, cabling, cafeterias, meeting rooms, reception, security — and then sub-licenses the finished, staffed workspace to client companies on a per-seat or per-floor basis. Despite the word "coworking" in its legal name, its customer is not the freelancer with a laptop: roughly 92% of its revenue comes from mid-to-large enterprises, many taking whole floors or campuses of 300-plus seats.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a spread business dressed as a real-estate business. Smartworks does not own the buildings and is not trying to. It captures the gap between the rent it pays a landlord for raw space and the higher, all-inclusive fee it charges an enterprise for a running office — plus a thin layer of amenity and fit-out income on top. The whole model is that arbitrage, scaled across ~16.9 mn sq ft of secured floor space.
  • The accounting makes a cash-positive business look barely profitable. Under Ind-AS 116, the long leases Smartworks signs are pulled onto its balance sheet as a right-of-use asset and a lease liability, and the rent it pays is re-expressed as depreciation plus interest rather than a simple rental cost. The effect: a reported operating (EBITDA) margin north of 60%, but a net line dragged almost to zero by that depreciation and finance charge. FY26 delivered ~₹1,796 Cr of revenue, ~₹1,155 Cr of reported EBITDA — and just ~₹11 Cr of net profit. That ~₹840 Cr chasm between the two is the single most important thing to understand here.
  • FY26 was the turn. Revenue rose ~31%, normalised (post-rent) EBITDA jumped ~75% to ~₹314 Cr, and the company posted its first full-year net profit (~₹11 Cr) after a ~₹63 Cr loss the year before. Yet the stock trades on a valuation — a P/E in the low hundreds, ~11× book — that assumes this is only the first step of a long ramp, not the destination. The market is buying the maturing footprint, not the FY26 profit.
In plain terms

Picture a middleman that rents empty concrete floors by the decade, spends a few hundred crore fitting them into polished offices, and rents them back out — fully staffed and serviced — to Infosys-sized tenants who would rather pay one monthly bill than manage a lease, a contractor, a facilities team and a cafeteria vendor themselves. Smartworks earns the difference. The difficulty is timing: it pays the fit-out cash and the first landlord rent long before a new centre fills up, so a fast-growing Smartworks looks loss-making precisely when it is investing hardest. FY26 is the year enough of its older centres matured to tip the whole company into the black — barely — while the newest ones were still ramping. The investment question is whether the next 16.9 mn sq ft fills the way the last 10 did.

01

Managed workspace 101: flex office, not a café for freelancers

The category, the jargon, and why the "coworking" label undersells what this actually is.

"Coworking" conjures an open loft where freelancers buy a hot-desk by the day. That is one end of the flexible-workspace market — and it is not where Smartworks lives. Smartworks sits at the opposite, enterprise end: it builds and operates whole managed campuses for large companies that want a turnkey office without signing a construction contract or a 15-year lease themselves. To follow the business you need five terms.

The only flex-office terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Managed / flex officeA fully fitted, serviced office that a company occupies on a flexible licence instead of building it out and signing a long lease itself. The operator (Smartworks) handles fit-out, facilities, IT, F&B and maintenance; the client just moves in and pays a single per-seat fee. This is Smartworks' entire product.
Bare shellRaw, un-fitted floor space — just structure, no interiors. Smartworks leases bare shell from landlords (cheap per sq ft) and spends capital turning it into finished office (which it can charge much more for). The value it adds is that transformation.

GCCGlobal Capability Centre — the in-house tech/operations arm a multinational runs in India. GCCs are exploding in number and are Smartworks' sweet-spot client: large, credit-worthy, long-tenure, and happy to outsource the office itself.
The distinction that changes the whole risk profile

A freelancer-coworking brand lives on thousands of tiny, cancel-anytime memberships — churny, price-sensitive, recession-fragile. Smartworks deliberately does the opposite: it courts a smaller number of large enterprises signing multi-year commitments for whole floors. That makes its revenue far stickier and its client concentration higher. When 92% of your income comes from corporates on long licences, you are much less exposed to a downturn in the gig economy — but much more exposed to a handful of big tenants' expansion or exit decisions, and to how disciplined you are about not over-building space before those tenants sign. Same industry as WeWork's café image; a genuinely different animal underneath.

The two sides of every deal

  • Upstream — the landlord. Smartworks signs long leases (often 9–15 years) on bare-shell floors, frequently from non-institutional owners — HNIs and family offices are said to hold ~76% of its landlord base. This is the cost side: fixed rent obligations stretching years into the future.
  • Downstream — the enterprise client. Smartworks re-licenses the finished, serviced office at an all-in per-seat rate. Client tenures are typically shorter than its own head-leases, which is the structural mismatch every flex operator manages: long commitments out, somewhat shorter commitments in.
  • The layer in between — services. On top of the core rent, Smartworks sells food courts, gyms, crèches, medical rooms, smart stores and design/fit-out services, monetising the campus as a platform, not just a lease.
02

The landlord-to-enterprise spread: where the money is made

The unit economics — fit-out capex, the rent gap, and the payback clock.

Every Smartworks centre is one repeated bet: spend fit-out capital today to earn a rent spread for a decade. The economics rest on three numbers — what it costs to build a seat, what the rent gap is once the seat is occupied, and how long it takes a new centre to fill. Get the reading on those three and you understand the entire company, because it is the same bet made 70 times over.

The capex per seat, and why it is a moat

Smartworks reports a fit-out capex of about ₹1,350 per sq ft — which management flags as among the lowest in the industry, roughly 43% below typical norms — and an operating cost of only ~₹30–35 per sq ft. That efficiency is not a rounding detail; it is the moat. In a business where you sink cash into every new floor before a rupee of client revenue arrives, building a seat for two-thirds of what a rival spends means you break even sooner, recycle capital faster, and can out-grow peers on the same balance sheet. The lower the fit-out cost, the shorter the payback — and payback is the whole game.

This J-curve is why a growing flex operator's headline losses can be misleading. Every new centre is a fresh dip in the curve; a portfolio that is mostly young reports thin or negative margins even if each centre is destined to be highly profitable at maturity. FY26 is the first year Smartworks' base of mature centres grew large enough to outweigh the drag from the newest ones — which is exactly why the whole company nudged into profit even as it kept expanding aggressively.

The mismatch that keeps a flex CFO awake

Here is the structural tension. Smartworks commits to its landlord rent for 9–15 years — a fixed, long, non-cancellable obligation. It lets space to clients on shorter licences it must keep re-winning. In a strong office market that mismatch is a gift: rents to landlords are locked while client demand and pricing rise, widening the spread. In a weak market it bites the other way — the lease clock keeps ticking whether or not a client renews. The discipline that matters, then, is not signing new landlord leases faster than enterprise demand can absorb them. Over-building bare shell ahead of committed tenants is precisely how flex operators have blown up before; under-building leaves growth on the table. The spread is only as good as that judgement.

03

What Smartworks actually sells: campuses, clients, and the platform

A revenue base that is ~94% rent, tilted hard toward large, multi-city enterprises.

Strip Smartworks to its revenue lines and it is overwhelmingly a rent business with a services garnish. Around 94% of income is the core managed-office licence fee; food courts, gyms and other amenities add ~3.6%; design and fit-out services for clients contribute ~2.5%. The strategic story is less in that mix than in who is paying and how the campuses are built to hold them.

The more of Stage 3 Smartworks monetises — amenities, services, add-ons — the more it lifts revenue per seat above the raw rent spread set in Stage 1. But Stage 1's fixed lease is the obligation that outlives everything to its right.

The client base — big, multi-city, and concentrated by design

  • Large enterprises (~92% of revenue). MNCs, Indian corporates and fast-scaling companies taking whole floors or campuses — the deliberate core. At the last full disclosure Smartworks served 738 enterprise clients across a footprint of ~9 mn sq ft.
  • GCCs and multi-city clients. Around 35% of revenue comes from clients taking space in more than one city — a sign Smartworks is becoming a national workspace partner, not a single-building landlord. Global Capability Centres, expanding their India headcount, are a structural demand engine here.
  • The platform layer. Food courts, gyms, crèches, medical rooms, smart stores and fit-out services — the ~6% of revenue that turns a lease into a stickier, higher-margin campus experience and raises switching costs for the enterprise tenant.

The concentration in rent is the point: Smartworks is a rent-spread machine first, a services platform second. Growing the thin non-rent slices — amenities at ~3.6% of income, design and fit-out at ~2.5% — is how it lifts revenue per seat over time, but the ~94% rent line will always dominate the picture.

Why the enterprise tilt is the smartest thing management controls

Almost everything about a flex operator's fate — office-market rents, interest rates, corporate hiring — is set outside its walls. The one big dial Smartworks genuinely owns is client selection. By chasing large, credit-worthy enterprises on multi-year licences rather than a churny membership crowd, it trades some pricing upside for a far more predictable revenue base and longer average tenure. That predictability is what lets it underwrite decade-long landlord leases in the first place: you can only promise a landlord 12 years of rent if you are reasonably sure your own tenants aren't leaving next quarter. The enterprise focus and the long-lease model are therefore two halves of one design — remove either and the other stops working.

A founder-led business, a Singapore strategic backer, and a July-2025 IPO.

Smartworks is a relatively young, founder-driven company that scaled fast, took strategic capital from a Singapore property major, and listed only in mid-2025. Understanding the cap table explains both its growth ambition and the governance texture a new public-market investor should watch.

05

The Ind-AS 116 illusion: why the profit line lies in both directions

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a lease-heavy operator.

Most people judge a company by its net profit. For Smartworks, the net profit is the least informative number on the page — because an accounting standard, Ind-AS 116, systematically distorts it. The same standard that makes the operating line look gloriously fat makes the bottom line look anaemic. Understand the mechanism once and the entire financial statement reorganises itself into sense.

Before Ind-AS 116, a company that rented its premises simply expensed the rent, and its P&L was intuitive. Under Ind-AS 116, a long lease is treated almost like a purchase on credit: Smartworks puts a right-of-use asset and a matching lease liability on its balance sheet, then replaces the single "rent" line with two others — depreciation on the asset and interest on the liability. Crucially, rent vanishes from the costs above EBITDA. So reported EBITDA balloons — because the single biggest real cost of the business, the landlord rent, has been moved below the EBITDA line and split into depreciation and finance charges.

The gap between the bars is Ind-AS 116.

Now the reverse distortion. Because a new centre's right-of-use asset and lease liability land in full on day one, its depreciation and interest hit the P&L immediately — long before the centre fills up and earns. So a fast-expanding Smartworks reports depressed or negative net profit precisely because it is growing, not because the centres are bad. The FY25 loss of ~₹63 Cr was substantially this: a young footprint carrying full lease charges before its occupancy caught up. FY26's swing to a ~₹11 Cr profit is the mirror image — enough centres matured that their earnings finally overtook the accounting drag from the newer ones.

The number that only fits a lease-heavy flex operator: normalised EBITDA

Forget both the fat reported EBITDA and the thin net profit; the honest operating figure for Smartworks is normalised (post-rent) EBITDA — reported EBITDA with the landlord rent put back in as the real cost it is. In FY26 that was ~₹314 Cr on ~₹1,796 Cr of revenue, a ~17.5% margin, up ~75% year on year from ~₹172 Cr. That single adjustment cuts through the accounting in both directions: it strips out the Ind-AS flattery of the EBITDA line and it removes the growth-drag distortion of the net line. If you track one profitability number for this company, track that one — and watch it climb as occupancy on the secured footprint rises.

The surprise to sit with: a business most people assume is thin-margin real-estate arbitrage reports an operating margin above 60% — and it means almost nothing, because that margin is calculated before its single largest cost. Conversely, its near-zero net margin also means almost nothing, because that number is inflated-down by carrying full lease charges on centres that haven't filled yet. Two headline profitability figures, both technically correct, both actively misleading. The company's true trajectory lives in a third number, ~₹314 Cr, that appears on neither line of a standard P&L.
06

The occupancy ramp: the footprint is bought before it is filled

Secured area, operational area, and the contracted-rental book that pre-sells FY27.

Smartworks reports three different footprint numbers, and the gap between them is the whole growth story. Secured area is space it has leased or committed to (~16.9 mn sq ft). Operational area is space actually open and running (crossed ~10 mn sq ft in FY26). And within the operational base, occupancy (~83%) is the share actually let. Growth flows from left to right: secure the floor, open it, fill it.

The ~7 mn sq ft gap between secured and operational area is the visible growth runway — floors already committed that will open and then fill over the coming years, each walking its own J-curve. Filling that gap at good occupancy is the single biggest lever on future normalised EBITDA.

The most reassuring number for a J-curve business is a forward order book, and Smartworks has one: it entered FY27 with contracted rental revenue of over ₹5,200 Cr — client commitments already signed, waiting to be recognised as centres fill and licences run. Against ~₹1,796 Cr of FY26 revenue, that is a multi-year cushion of visibility unusual for a company still often described as loss-making. It does not guarantee the spread — costs and new-lease discipline still matter — but it de-risks the top line materially.

The counter-intuitive truth about a "profitable" quarter here

For Smartworks, a burst of new-centre openings is bad for this quarter's reported profit and good for the business. Each opening loads full lease charges onto the P&L while the centre is near-empty, so aggressive expansion mechanically depresses near-term margins — even though every one of those centres is a future profit engine climbing its ramp. The mistake a newcomer makes is reading a soft profit quarter as weakness when it is often the opposite: the company investing into a demand pipeline. The right question each period is not "did net profit rise?" but "is occupancy on the maturing centres tracking up, and is the secured-to-operational gap converting on schedule?" Those tell you whether the ramp is working; the net line won't.

07

Reading the balance sheet: the ₹4,778 Cr that isn't really debt

Why the biggest "borrowing" on the books is an accounting artefact — and where the real leverage sits.

Glance at Smartworks' balance sheet and a scary number jumps out: total borrowings of ~₹4,778 Cr against equity of a few hundred crore. Taken at face value it looks like a dangerously leveraged company. It isn't — and knowing why is essential to not mis-pricing the stock.

The overwhelming majority of that ~₹4,778 Cr is lease liability: the Ind-AS 116 present value of all those future landlord rents, capitalised onto the balance sheet. It is not money owed to a bank that can call it; it is the accounting shadow of the same long leases that generate the right-of-use assets (~₹5,238 Cr of fixed assets) on the other side. In economic terms it is a committed rent schedule, not financial gearing. The company's actual financial debt — the bank borrowings that carry real refinancing and covenant risk — is a small fraction of that headline, and management has cut it hard: gross real debt is down more than 50% since the IPO, with the company describing itself as having moved to a net-cash position on that real-debt measure after the listing raised primary capital.

How to read leverage for a flex operator without being fooled

The trap is treating the lease liability as if it were bank debt and concluding the company is over-geared; the opposite trap is ignoring it entirely and pretending the future rent obligations don't exist. Neither is right. The lease liability is a real, non-cancellable commitment — those rents must be paid whether or not centres fill — but it is matched by right-of-use assets that generate the revenue to pay them, and it carries no bank covenant or refinancing wall. So judge Smartworks' financial risk on two separate tracks: its real debt (small, now net cash — genuinely reassuring) and its lease obligations (large, long, and only safe if occupancy and the spread hold). The IPO's primary proceeds went substantially to clearing the first; nothing can clear the second except filling the space.

This is also why cash flow tells the story better than profit here. Because depreciation and the interest-heavy early lease charges are non-cash or front-loaded, Smartworks generates operating cash well ahead of what its net-profit line suggests — the very definition of a business that is "EBITDA and cash-flow positive while loss-making at PAT." Read the cash-flow statement and the normalised EBITDA together, and the company looks far healthier than the ~₹11 Cr net profit implies.

The tailwind: hybrid work, GCCs, and enterprise flex

Why demand for managed offices is structurally rising, not merely recovering.

Smartworks is riding a genuine, multi-year shift in how large companies consume office space. Three forces push in its favour at once — and unlike the cyclical variables, these are structural. They are why the secured footprint keeps growing and why the contracted-rental book runs to thousands of crore.

DriverWhat it isWhy it helps Smartworks
1 · The GCC boomMultinationals building large in-house tech/operations centres in India, adding hundreds of thousands of seats a year.GCCs are the ideal client: big, credit-worthy, long-tenure, and keen to outsource the office itself so they can focus on their core work. They want turnkey campuses fast — exactly Smartworks' product.
2 · Hybrid / flexible workCompanies wanting the ability to scale space up and down without decade-long leases or their own fit-out risk.Flex converts a fixed real-estate commitment into a variable, managed service. The demand nuance a first-timer should hold

The pandemic-era fear was that hybrid work would shrink office demand. For enterprise flex specifically, it did close to the opposite. Companies that no longer want to sign their own 15-year leases, or to run their own cafeteria and IT desk, hand the problem to an operator instead — which grows the addressable market for managed offices even if total square footage occupied per employee falls. Add the GCC wave, where global firms are opening India campuses at record pace and want them running in months, not years, and Smartworks sits in front of demand that is expanding for structural, not merely cyclical, reasons. The risk is not that the tailwind reverses; it is that every rival sees the same tailwind and over-supplies into it.

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, newly-listed cohort — and a shared accounting quirk that hides who is actually profitable.

India's flexible-workspace industry produced a wave of IPOs, and Smartworks now sits in a listed cohort with distinct strategies. It is the largest by managed-campus footprint, but not the largest by revenue, and — importantly — not among the clearly profitable. The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, this is not a price war of the obvious kind — operators compete on location, fit-out quality, service, capital efficiency and the ability to land whole-campus enterprise mandates, not on undercutting a per-desk rate. Smartworks' edge is its low fit-out cost and its concentration on large-format enterprise campuses; Awfis' edge is a lighter, broader network; WeWork India's is brand. Second, the whole cohort shares the same accounting fog, so the discipline that separates winners is invisible on the reported EBITDA line and shows up only in occupancy, normalised margin, cash generation and — crucially — restraint in not over-leasing ahead of demand. The operator who signs the fewest empty floors in the next downturn wins the cycle.

Financials & valuation

A business at its inflection, priced for the ramp to keep ramping.

FY26 was the company's coming-of-age year: revenue up ~31% to ~₹1,796 Cr (from ~₹1,374 Cr in FY25 and ~₹1,039 Cr in FY24), normalised EBITDA up ~75% to ~₹314 Cr, and the first full-year net profit of ~₹11 Cr. But the valuation does not reward FY26's profit — it reaches past it. On a headline P/E in the low hundreds and roughly 11× book, the market is explicitly paying for the next several years of the occupancy ramp, not the trailing earnings. The question is not whether the price bakes in optimism; it plainly does. The question is whether the maturing footprint delivers the normalised-margin expansion that optimism requires.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A newly-profitable category leader, asked whether it can fill what it has already committed to.

Smartworks enters FY27 in its strongest position yet: the largest managed-campus footprint in India, a first full-year profit banked, a ~₹5,200 Cr contracted-rental cushion, real debt slashed to net cash, a Singapore beachhead, and structural GCC and enterprise-flex demand at its back. What no flex operator can promise is the one thing that decides everything — that the space it has already leased fills at the occupancy and spread the model assumes, without a downturn catching it with empty floors and non-cancellable rent. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Category leader on a real tailwind — the biggest managed-campus footprint, in front of a structural GCC and enterprise-flex demand wave, not a cyclical blip.
  • The J-curve is turning up — FY26's first profit and +75% normalised EBITDA show the maturing base finally outweighing new-centre drag; the ~7 mn sq ft secured-but-not-operational gap is visible growth.
  • Forward book de-risks the top line — >₹5,200 Cr of contracted rental revenue gives multi-year revenue visibility rare for a still-scaling company.
  • Capital-efficient by design — ~₹1,350/sq ft fit-out (43% below norm) means faster payback and more growth per rupee; normalised ROCE already ~16%.
  • Balance sheet cleaned up — real gross debt more than halved since IPO to a net-cash position, removing the financing fragility that sank earlier flex operators.

The bear case

  • Priced far ahead of profit — a P/E in the low hundreds and ~11× book demand years of flawless ramp; a stumble in occupancy re-rates the stock hard.
  • The lease-mismatch risk is permanent — 9–15-year landlord obligations against shorter client licences; a demand air-pocket leaves rent running while floors empty.
  • Thin net margin, real cash needs — net profit is a rounding error, and every new centre still consumes fit-out capital before it earns; growth is not free.
  • Crowded field, shared tailwind — WeWork India, Awfis, IndiQube, Table Space and others chase the same enterprise mandates; over-supply into the demand wave would crush the spread.
  • Young public company — short listed track record, pre-IPO and promoter lock-ups still to unwind, and a model that lives or dies on management's restraint in signing leases.
The bottom line

Smartworks is a capital-efficient operator that has genuinely cracked the enterprise-flex model at scale and just proved it can earn a full-year profit — while carrying an accounting presentation that flatters its operating line and buries its true operating margin in a third number most investors never compute. The self-help is real: low fit-out cost, a maturing base, a cleaned-up balance sheet, a fat contracted-rental book. But the valuation has already paid for the next several turns of the ramp, and the model's defining vulnerability — long fixed rents against a demand cycle it cannot control — never goes away. The next test is concrete and datable: whether the ~7 mn sq ft of secured-but-not-yet-operational space opens and fills toward ~85%+ occupancy through FY27, lifting normalised EBITDA per square foot while real free cash funds the fit-out. Convert that committed footprint at spread and the profit stops being a rounding error; open it into soft demand, and the long leases become the anchor they were always designed to leverage.
