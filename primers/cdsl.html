00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Central Depository Services (India) Limited — CDSL — is one of India's two securities depositories: the electronic registries that hold shares, bonds, mutual-fund units and other securities in dematerialised ("demat") form and record who owns what. When you buy a share on your Zerodha or Groww app, no paper certificate changes hands — the ownership is simply updated in a depository's books. CDSL runs the ledger behind a majority of India's retail investors.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is critical market infrastructure with monopoly-like economics. CDSL and rival NSDL form a regulated duopoly — there are only two depositories in the whole country. CDSL is now the larger by number of demat accounts (~18.0 crore, roughly 4 in 5 of all accounts), because it powers most discount-broker sign-ups (Zerodha, Groww, Angel One, Upstox). It is a toll-booth: it takes a small cut of the market's plumbing, at ultra-high margin, with almost no capital tied up.
  • It is the purest listed play on India's retail-investing boom. Every new investor, every new demat account, every IPO and every share transfer feeds CDSL's revenue — and the marginal cost of one more account is close to zero. Revenue grew to ~₹1,239 Cr (FY26 consolidated) at a roughly 50% EBITDA margin, with zero debt and ~₹2,850 Cr of cash and investments. It pays generous dividends (₹12.75/share for FY26).
  • Its profit can fall even as the franchise grows. FY26 net profit actually dropped ~14% to ~₹455 Cr, because a big chunk of revenue is market-linked — transaction charges, IPO and corporate-action fees — and market activity cooled after a frenzied FY25 (and SEBI's curbs on retail F&O speculation bit). That cyclicality, plus a SEBI-regulated pricing regime and a rich ~60× P/E, is the whole debate.
In plain terms

Think of the stock market as a giant shopping mall. The exchanges (NSE, BSE) are the shops where trades happen. But someone has to keep the master record of who owns which goods once the sale is done, and hand ownership from seller to buyer. That record-keeper is the depository. India, by design, has exactly two of them — CDSL and NSDL — both tightly regulated by SEBI. CDSL is the one most new small investors end up on. It doesn't take market risk, doesn't lend, doesn't hold inventory; it just charges tiny fees on an enormous and growing flow of accounts and transactions. That makes it a rare thing: a debt-free, cash-gushing, near-monopoly "picks-and-shovels" business — but one whose fees are set under a regulator's eye and whose busiest quarters depend on how excited the public is about stocks.

01

Demat 101: what a depository actually is

No finance degree required — just one idea about where your shares live.

Before the late 1990s, buying a share in India meant receiving a physical paper certificate. It could be forged, torn, lost in the post, or take weeks to transfer. Settlement was slow and fraud was common. India fixed this by dematerialising securities — converting paper certificates into electronic entries — and creating depositories to hold those entries centrally. CDSL was set up for exactly this, receiving its certificate of commencement from SEBI in February 1999.

The bank analogy that makes it click

A demat account is to your shares what a bank account is to your money. Your bank doesn't keep a specific pile of banknotes with your name on it — it keeps an electronic ledger entry saying you have ₹X. When you pay someone, the bank debits your entry and credits theirs; no cash physically moves. A depository does the identical thing for securities: it holds an electronic entry saying you own 100 shares of Reliance, and when you sell, it debits your account and credits the buyer's. CDSL is, in effect, a bank for shares — the record-keeping utility that makes fast, safe, paperless trading possible. And like a bank running the payments rails, it earns a small, steady toll on the flow.

What sits in the vault

It isn't only equity shares. CDSL holds equities, bonds and debentures, mutual-fund units, government securities, treasury bills, commercial paper, certificates of deposit and more — and, via subsidiaries, even insurance policies in electronic form. Anything that can be "held" as a financial asset can, increasingly, be dematerialised and parked in the depository — which quietly widens the toll-booth's road every year.

02

How CDSL makes money

Six little tolls on the same enormous flow — read this once and the model clicks.

CDSL's revenue is a bundle of small, recurring charges levied at different points of the securities lifecycle. No single fee is large; collectively, on a base of 18 crore accounts and billions of transactions, they add up to a high-margin annuity. Crucially, some of these fees are stable and recurring (they arrive whether or not markets are busy), and others are activity-linked (they rise and fall with trading, IPOs and market mood). Knowing which is which explains the whole earnings profile.

The tolls, decoded

  • Annual issuer charges — every company whose securities are held with CDSL pays a yearly fee (scaled to its number of folios / market value). This is the gold-standard annuity: it recurs regardless of whether anyone trades, and it grows as more companies list and more securities are dematerialised.
  • Transaction charges — a small fee each time securities are debited from a demat account (i.e. on sales/transfers). This is the biggest activity-linked line: busy, speculative markets mean more transactions and more fees; quiet markets mean fewer.
  • IPO & corporate-action fees — CDSL earns when new shares are issued (IPOs, rights, bonuses) and when corporate actions (dividends, splits, mergers) are processed. A red-hot IPO market is a direct tailwind; an IPO drought is a direct headwind.
  • Online data charges — fees for data and technology services CDSL sells to market participants, including KYC-verification pulls. A fast-growing, sticky, tech-flavoured line.
  • Account-maintenance & e-CAS — charges tied to holding and servicing accounts (statements, e-CAS consolidated account statements). Recurring and account-base-driven.
  • e-voting, insurance repository & other — CDSL runs the dominant e-voting platform companies use for shareholder resolutions, an insurance repository (policies held electronically), and other data/tech services — small today but optionality on top.
Why the mix matters more than the total

The single most important thing to understand about CDSL's revenue is the split between annuity and activity. Roughly a third to a half of the core depository revenue (transaction charges + IPO/corporate-action fees) is directly geared to how active the market is. In a euphoric year — heavy trading, a flood of IPOs — those lines surge and profits look spectacular. In a cooler year they shrink, and profit can fall even though the account base keeps rising (exactly what happened in FY26). The recurring lines — issuer charges, account maintenance, data — provide a rising floor. Bulls focus on the growing floor; bears focus on the swinging roof.

03

The company and who controls it

A stock exchange built it — which creates one genuinely unusual quirk.

CDSL was promoted by BSE Limited (the Bombay Stock Exchange) in the late 1990s, alongside a group of leading banks, to bring a second depository and lower-cost demat services to Indian investors. It went public on 30 June 2017 — the first depository to list anywhere in the Asia-Pacific region. Ownership is deliberately dispersed, because SEBI's rules for market infrastructure sharply cap how much any one shareholder can own.

The listing quirk — read this carefully: Because CDSL's promoter is BSE, CDSL is listed only on the NSE (symbol CDSL) — it does not trade on the BSE. It would be awkward for a depository to list on the very exchange that owns it. So CDSL has no BSE listing — "NSE: CDSL" is the only real, tradable line. (The similar-looking BSE scrip code 543328 in fact belongs to an unrelated company, Krsnaa Diagnostics.)

Ownership & the SEBI cap

CDSL is a Market Infrastructure Institution (MII), a category SEBI treats as a public utility. Under the Depositories & Participants Regulations, no single shareholder may own too much: broadly 5% for individuals/most entities and up to 15% for approved institutions (an exchange, bank, insurer, another depository). This is deliberate — it prevents any one owner from controlling a piece of critical national plumbing.

  • BSE Limited — the promoter, holding at/near the 15% institutional cap. It cannot own more.
  • Other large holders — a spread of institutions including Standard Chartered Bank, HDFC Bank, and domestic funds such as PPFAS Mutual Fund and LIC. No dominant controller; the free float is large.
  • Governance — as an MII, CDSL runs under an unusually strict SEBI governance code (independent "public-interest" directors, conduct rules, fee oversight). This is a double-edged sword: it protects the franchise's integrity and its licence, but it also means SEBI, not CDSL, has the final say on much of its pricing.
04

The CDSL–NSDL duopoly

Two vaults, one country — and they specialise in very different customers.

India has exactly two depositories: CDSL and the older NSDL (National Securities Depository Limited, set up in 1996, promoted by NSE/IDBI). No third is licensed. This is a textbook regulated duopoly — high barriers (you need a SEBI licence, deep trust, and national-scale technology), rational competition, and no price war to zero. But the two are not mirror images: they dominate opposite ends of the market.

Why CDSL's "worse" AUC share is actually its strength

At first glance NSDL looks bigger — it custodies six times the rupee value. But CDSL's revenue leans on volume of accounts and transactions, not the value of assets sitting still. It is the retail investor — opening an account on a phone app, trading small lots, participating in IPOs — who generates the transaction, IPO and data fees. That is precisely the segment growing fastest in India, and the one CDSL owns: it captures the large majority of every new account opened. NSDL's institutional custody is enormous but grows slowly and generates comparatively little per-transaction fee. So CDSL, the "smaller" depository by assets, is the higher-growth, higher-margin, more retail-geared franchise — which is why the market has historically valued it richly. NSDL's own listing in August 2025 now gives investors a direct, comparable yardstick for the first time.

05

The retail-investing boom — the growth engine

The single structural force behind the whole investment case.

CDSL is, more than anything, a leveraged bet on Indians putting savings into the stock market. For most of the country's history, households kept wealth in gold, property and bank deposits. Over the last decade — cheap smartphones, zero-brokerage discount apps, UPI-easy money movement, rising incomes, and a long bull market — a structural shift began: tens of millions of first-time investors opened demat accounts. CDSL sits directly under that firehose, because the discount brokers driving it (Zerodha, Groww, Angel One, Upstox) overwhelmingly route new accounts to CDSL.

The mechanism that makes this so powerful for shareholders is operating leverage. CDSL's costs — its technology platform, its people, its data centres — are largely fixed. Once the system is built, servicing one more demat account costs almost nothing. So as the account base swells from a handful of crore toward 20+ crore, the recurring fee streams (account maintenance, issuer charges, data) scale up while costs barely move, and the incremental revenue drops almost straight to profit.

Under-penetration is the long runway

Even at 18 crore accounts, India remains strikingly under-invested in equities. Accounts belong to a modest share of the population, many households still hold most wealth in property, gold and deposits, and the number of unique investors is smaller still (many people hold multiple accounts). Compared with developed markets, the share of household savings in equities is low and rising. If that "financialisation of savings" continues for another decade — the base case most bulls hold — the demat account base keeps compounding, and CDSL, as the default rail for new retail investors, keeps collecting a bigger toll. The engine isn't the stock market's level; it's the number of participants, which has a much longer secular runway.

06

Toll-booth economics: why the margins are so high

Capital-light, cash-rich, and structurally hard to attack.

CDSL is close to a textbook example of a capital-light compounder. It doesn't build factories, carry inventory, extend loans, or take market risk. It runs a technology platform and a licence. That produces a financial profile most businesses can only dream of: EBITDA margins around 50%+, minimal capital expenditure, negative working capital, and a balance sheet with no debt and ~₹2,850 Cr of cash and investments.

The four features of the moat

  • Regulatory barrier — you cannot simply start a depository. It requires a SEBI licence, national infrastructure, and years of trust. Only two exist. That is the deepest moat of all.
  • Network effects & switching costs — brokers, companies and investors are all wired into CDSL's system; moving is disruptive and rare. Companies list their securities once; investors keep accounts for years.
  • Extreme operating leverage — fixed technology cost base; near-zero marginal cost per extra account or transaction. Growth in volume flows to profit.
  • Capital-light cash generation — little reinvestment needed, so almost all profit becomes free cash flow, funding fat dividends and a growing cash pile rather than capex or debt service.

The flip side of "capital-light" is that CDSL has limited ways to redeploy its cash at high returns — it can't build ten new hospitals or a giant plant. So it returns most profit as dividends and accumulates cash. That's shareholder-friendly, but it also means growth is tethered to the market's own expansion (more accounts, more securities, more transactions) rather than to reinvestment the company controls.

07

Beyond the core: KYC, insurance & data

The subsidiaries that widen the moat — especially a quiet KYC near-monopoly.

CDSL is not only the depository. Through subsidiaries it runs several adjacent market-utility businesses that ride the same rails and deepen its grip on India's capital-market plumbing. The most important is CDSL Ventures Limited (CVL), a dominant KYC registry.

Why CVL is the underrated jewel

Every investor in India must be KYC-verified before they can invest — and doing that check individually for every broker and fund would be enormously wasteful. So the system created KRAs (KYC Registration Agencies): verify once, store centrally, reuse everywhere. CVL was the first and is the biggest, sitting on 10+ crore KYC records. It's the same business logic as the depository — a central, regulated, trust-based registry that every new market entrant must pass through — and it grows off the identical retail-investing wave. In effect, CDSL taxes the retail boom twice: once when you open a demat account, and again when your identity is verified to invest at all. That layering is a quiet, powerful part of the moat that headline "depository" descriptions miss.

09

Valuation: the premium debate

A wonderful business at a demanding price — the crux of the whole story.

At roughly ₹28,700 Cr market cap on ~₹455 Cr of FY26 profit, CDSL trades around 60× trailing earnings — a rich multiple by any standard, and richer still against a year in which profit fell. The bull–bear argument is almost entirely about whether that premium is deserved.

How to think about the ~60× multiple

The bull case for the premium: CDSL is a scarce, regulated near-monopoly with ~50% margins, no debt, high return on capital, and a decade-plus structural tailwind (financialisation of Indian savings). There are only two depositories, and only one is this geared to retail growth. Businesses of that quality — moaty, capital-light, compounding — trade at premiums the world over, and a scarce one in a fast-growing market attracts a "there is no substitute" bid. The bear case: at 60× a cyclically-sensitive earnings stream, the price bakes in years of uninterrupted account and activity growth; a cooler market (like FY26), a SEBI fee cut, or simply a de-rating toward more normal multiples could hurt even if the business is fine. And with NSDL now listed, investors have a direct comparable that can pull CDSL's multiple around. Both sides are right in different environments — which is why the price you pay, and the point in the market cycle, matter as much here as the business quality.

10

The risks — read these honestly

A great business is not the same as a great investment at any price.

CDSL's risks are not about the franchise falling apart — the duopoly and the moat are secure. They are about the swing in earnings, who controls the pricing, and how much you pay. Four deserve real weight.

1 · Market cyclicality

A large slice of revenue — transaction charges plus IPO and corporate-action fees — is directly geared to market activity. In a bear market, or simply a quieter year, trading volumes drop, IPOs dry up, and new-account growth slows. FY26 was a live demonstration: profit fell ~14% despite a bigger account base. On top of ordinary cycles, SEBI's curbs on retail futures-and-options speculation (aimed at protecting small traders) indirectly cool the frenetic activity that fattens CDSL's busiest quarters. The recurring floor cushions the fall — but the roof genuinely swings.

2 · Regulatory & pricing risk

This is the subtle one. CDSL does not fully control its own prices. As a SEBI-regulated MII, its fee structures are set within a regulatory framework, and SEBI has both the mandate and the inclination to keep market-access costs low for investors. A regulator that decides depository charges should fall — to benefit the retail investors it champions — could cap or cut a fee stream with the stroke of a circular. That is a permanent, structural feature of owning a regulated utility: the same rules that protect the monopoly also constrain how much it can charge.

3 · Competition & the listed rival

NSDL is the other half of the duopoly and now a listed company, sharpening the competition for share (especially if it pushes harder into retail) and giving the market a direct valuation comparable. Duopoly economics are usually stable — neither side benefits from a price war — but any aggressive move by NSDL, or regulatory nudges toward "interoperability," could pressure CDSL's growth or pricing.

4 · Operational, cyber & valuation risk

  • It is critical infrastructure — a serious technology failure, cyber-attack or data breach would be reputationally and operationally severe, and invite regulatory action. Trust is the product.
  • Concentration on retail activity — CDSL's fortunes are tied to the retail-investing wave continuing; a prolonged risk-off phase in Indian equities would hit it more than a diversified financial.
  • Valuation — at ~60× earnings, the stock is priced for continued compounding. Any disappointment — a weak market year, a fee cut, a growth wobble — can trigger a sharp de-rating even with the business intact. The valuation itself is a risk.
The honest tension in one line: CDSL is a wonderful, moaty, monopoly-like toll-booth — and it is market-cyclical, regulated on price, and richly valued. Both descriptions are true. The investment question is not "is it a good business?" (it plainly is) but "is a cyclical, regulated cash machine worth ~60× at this point in the cycle?" Reasonable people disagree.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely high-quality utility on the rails of India's savings shift.

Look past any single year and CDSL is one of the cleanest structural stories in Indian financials: a debt-free, capital-light, ~50%-margin near-monopoly that earns a growing toll every time an Indian household moves savings into securities. The long arc — financialisation, more investors, more securities dematerialised, more data and KYC — points up. The debate, as ever, is price and cycle.

The bull case

  • Regulated near-monopoly — one of only two depositories; the deepest possible moat, plus a KYC near-monopoly (CVL) on top.
  • Purest retail-boom play — captures ~4 in 5 new demat accounts; grows with every new investor and IPO.
  • Exceptional economics — ~50% margins, zero debt, ~₹2,850 Cr cash, high ROE, huge operating leverage, generous dividends.
  • Long runway — India remains under-invested in equities; financialisation of savings has a decade-plus to run.
  • Optionality — KYC, insurance repository, e-voting and data services widen the toll-booth.

The bear case

  • Market cyclicality — a big chunk of revenue swings with trading, IPOs and market mood; FY26 profit fell ~14%.
  • Regulated pricing — SEBI, not CDSL, has the final say on many fees; a fee cut is a permanent risk.
  • Rich valuation — ~60× earnings leaves little margin for error and is vulnerable to de-rating.
  • Listed rival & F&O curbs — NSDL now public and competing; SEBI's retail-speculation curbs cool activity.
  • Capital-light cuts both ways — little to reinvest in; growth is tethered to the market's own expansion.
The bottom line

CDSL is a structurally advantaged, monopoly-like utility riding one of India's most durable trends — the movement of household savings into financial markets. The business risk is genuinely low: the duopoly is protected, the balance sheet is pristine, the margins are extraordinary. The risks that matter are cyclicality (profit will wobble with the market), regulation (its prices are not fully its own), and valuation (a scarce compounder is priced like one). Watch two things each year: the net new demat accounts (is the retail engine still humming?) and the mix of recurring vs activity-linked revenue (is the annuity floor rising fast enough to matter when the activity roof swings?). Get comfortable with a cyclical, regulated cash machine at a premium multiple, and CDSL is about as high-quality as toll-booths come. Balk at the price or the cycle, and the same facts read very differently.
