00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

360 ONE WAM is India's largest listed, pure-play wealth-and-asset manager for the very rich. It looks after the money of ultra-high-net-worth families (UHNIs) and high-net-worth individuals (HNIs) — think promoters who've just sold a business, industrial dynasties, senior executives and family offices — and it also runs a fast-growing asset-management arm (mutual funds, PMS and alternative funds). It was born as IIFL Wealth Management, demerged from the IIFL group, and rebranded to 360 ONE in 2022.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Scale and scarcity. It oversees ₹6.74 lakh crore of total AUM (assets under management) as of March 2026 — the largest of the listed Indian wealth managers — serving 8,500+ ultra-rich families and corporates. As essentially the only listed pure-play UHNI wealth manager at this scale, it is a scarce asset the market prices at a premium (~37× earnings, ~4.6× book).
  • The quality of the earnings is the whole story. Revenue is shifting decisively toward ARR — Annual Recurring Revenue — sticky, asset-based annuity fees that recur year after year. ARR revenue hit ₹2,289 Cr in FY26, up 34.5%, now ~75% of operating revenue. The rest is lumpy transactional/brokerage income. More recurring = more predictable = a higher multiple.
  • It is compounding fast and paying you along the way. FY26 profit (PAT) rose 20.7% to ₹1,225 Cr; ARR-AUM grew 26% to ₹3.12 lakh Cr; the firm runs a ~50% cost-to-income ratio, a ~19% tangible RoE, and has been a generous dividend payer (₹6/share interim declared for FY27). The debate is not the business — it's whether a rich multiple is safe as it spends aggressively to expand.
In plain terms

Picture a private bank crossed with a fund house, built for India's billionaires. When a family suddenly has ₹500 crore to look after — after an IPO, a business sale, or an inheritance — they don't want a retail bank branch; they want a trusted adviser who manages the whole fortune: investments, estate planning, tax, borrowing against assets, the lot. 360 ONE is that adviser, at the top of the market. It earns a small annual fee on the pile it manages (that's the sticky "recurring" money everyone loves), plus one-off fees when clients trade or when it distributes products. The game is to keep pulling in more money from more rich families each year — and to make more of that money the recurring, dependable kind.

01

Wealth management 101: how you earn money by minding money

No finance degree required — just the handful of numbers that drive everything.

A wealth manager is, financially, an asset-gathering business. It doesn't own factories or sell products; it accumulates a large pool of clients' money — Assets Under Management (AUM) — and earns a slice of it as fees. The economics rest on a few simple ideas.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and 360 ONE's number)
AUMThe total pool of client money the firm oversees. 360 ONE's total AUM is ~₹6.74 lakh crore (Mar '26). This is the raw material — the bigger it is, the more fees it can earn.
ARR-AUMThe slice of AUM that generates recurring, asset-based fees — advisory mandates, discretionary/managed portfolios, the AMC's funds. ~₹3.12 lakh Cr and growing fastest. This is the "quality" AUM.
Net flowsNew money in minus money withdrawn, over a period — the single best gauge of whether clients are choosing you. FY26 ARR net flows were ₹55,875 Cr; organic net flows ₹35,199 Cr (+36%).
Yield / retentionThe fee rate earned on assets (a fraction of a percent per year). Small in percentage terms, but on lakhs of crores it compounds into serious revenue.
The simple revenue equation

Roughly: Recurring revenue ≈ ARR-AUM × fee yield. So the firm grows in three ways — gather more assets (net flows), let markets lift the assets it already holds (a rising stock market inflates AUM "for free"), and improve the mix toward higher-fee recurring products. Crucially, once a client and their ₹500 crore are on the platform, servicing an extra rupee costs almost nothing — the relationship-manager, technology and compliance are largely fixed costs. Past a break-even, most incremental fee revenue drops to profit. That's operating leverage, and it's why net flows are the number to watch above all others.

Why "sticky" matters more here than almost anywhere

  • Trust is the product. A UHNI family isn't buying a fund; they're handing over their life's fortune to an adviser they trust. That relationship, once earned, is extraordinarily hard for a rival to prise away — switching means re-papering an entire complex financial life.
  • Assets don't churn like customers. A satisfied family stays for decades, adds more money over time, and refers peers. Low attrition on a huge, growing base is the definition of an annuity.
  • The wealth keeps compounding. Even with zero new clients, a UHNI's assets grow with markets — so a stable book quietly gets bigger every year the market rises.

Why recurring income earns a premium multiple

A rupee of recurring profit is worth more than a rupee of one-off profit, because it's more likely to be there next year — and the year after. Investors pay up for predictability: they can forecast an annuity book with confidence, so they capitalise it at a higher price-to-earnings. As 360 ONE converts its earnings from lumpy brokerage into sticky annuity fees, two good things happen at once — profits grow, and the multiple the market is willing to pay on those profits can expand. That double-benefit ("re-rating") is the quiet engine behind why a scarce, ARR-heavy wealth manager can trade at 35–40× earnings while a brokerage trades at 12–15×.

Watch the quality, not just the size: a bumper quarter driven by transactional spikes (a hot market, lots of trading) flatters headline profit but is low-quality and won't repeat. The number that matters for the long-term thesis is ARR-AUM and ARR net flows — the recurring base that will still be paying fees when the trading frenzy fades.
03

The business model: one platform, many fee lines

How a single ultra-rich relationship gets monetised again and again.

360 ONE runs an integrated wealth platform: it captures a wealthy family through a relationship manager, then serves that family across advisory, lending, distribution and its own manufactured funds — turning one relationship into many, recurring fee lines. The magic is that the same pool of client assets can be monetised through several products at once.

Each stage to the right adds another fee on the same client, raises switching costs, and — for the AMC and advisory pieces — is recurring. Owning both the advice (distribution) and the product (manufacturing) is the structural edge over a pure distributor.

The four businesses, in plain English

  • 360 ONE Wealth — the flagship. Relationship managers advise UHNI/HNI families on their whole balance sheet. Its centrepiece is the 360 ONE Plus discretionary/advisory offering — the firm actively manages the money for a recurring, asset-based fee, rather than just selling products for a commission. This is the highest-quality, stickiest revenue.
  • 360 ONE Asset — the in-house asset manager (AMC). A leader in alternatives (AIFs — Alternative Investment Funds), plus PMS and a growing mutual-fund book. Manufacturing its own products means it earns the management fee and keeps the client, instead of handing both to a third party.
  • 360 ONE Prime — the lending arm (an NBFC). It lends to clients against their portfolios ("loan against securities") and other structured credit — a way to monetise the relationship further and earn a spread. (Note: this is why the balance sheet carries borrowings — see §10.)
  • 360 ONE Capital / distribution — capital-markets, transactional distribution and, post-acquisition, institutional broking & research (via B&K). This is where much of the transactional revenue lives.
Why owning the whole stack matters

A pure distributor (like some rivals) only earns a commission for selling someone else's fund — a thinner, more contestable slice. 360 ONE both advises the client and manufactures the product, so it captures fees at two levels and controls the relationship end-to-end. That integration is why its operating margins (~61%) sit above peers, and why its earnings are stickier: the client's money is invested in 360 ONE's own funds, advised by 360 ONE's own team, custodied on 360 ONE's own platform. Unwinding all of that to leave is a genuine hassle — which is exactly the point.

04

The company and who controls it

From an IIFL subsidiary to a Bain-backed, founder-led scarce asset.

360 ONE is a founder-led firm with a marquee institutional cap table. Incorporated in 2008 as IIFL Wealth Management, it was demerged from the IIFL group and separately listed in 2019, took on private-equity heavyweight Bain Capital as an anchor, rebranded to 360 ONE in 2022, and — unusually — has since reduced promoter holding sharply, leaving it majority-owned by institutions. That ownership shape is itself part of the story.

Ownership & management

  • Founders / promoters: Karan Bhagat (Founder, MD & CEO) leads; Yatin Shah co-founded and built the asset-management side. Notably, the classified promoter holding is now only ~6.2% (down from ~8–9%) — the founders trimmed stakes, and a portion is under a retention/profit-sharing arrangement. This is a genuinely founder-run but not founder-controlled company.
  • Institutional pedigree: Bain Capital remains the single largest holder (~18% as of late FY26, down from ~25%); overall FII holding is ~63% and DIIs ~13% (Mar 2026) — an institution-heavy register that includes Bain within the "public"/FII bucket. UBS holds warrants for ~4.95% via the 2025 deal.
  • What the cap table tells you: low promoter ownership plus heavy, blue-chip institutional backing means the company is run for scale and returns, professionally managed, and closely watched — but it also means no dominant family owner, and some sensitivity to large holders selling down (Bain has been paring its stake over time).
A subtlety worth noting: because founders trimmed their stakes and Bain sits outside the "promoter" label, the reported ~6% promoter holding understates how founder-driven the firm is day-to-day. The flip side of a de-promoterised, PE-anchored structure is periodic overhang — the market watches every Bain block-deal, since a large sponsor exit can pressure the stock regardless of business performance.

A dominant wealth arm, with a leading alternatives-heavy AMC bolted on.

360 ONE's recurring engine runs on two AUM pools. The Wealth business is the larger and the identity of the firm; the Asset Management business is smaller but strategically vital — it's where 360 ONE manufactures the products (especially alternatives) that keep the client's money, and the fees, in-house.

360 ONE Wealth — the flagship

  • Who it serves: the very top of the pyramid — 8,500+ UHNI/HNI families and corporates, typically with investible wealth from a few crore to thousands of crore. Family offices, promoter households, senior professionals.
  • The 360 ONE Plus proposition: a shift from selling products for commission to managing the whole portfolio for a recurring, asset-based fee — aligning the firm with the client (both win when the portfolio grows) and converting one-off distribution income into annuity revenue. This ARR conversion is the strategic spine of the wealth business.
  • Why it wins: deep relationships, a full-stack platform (advice + lending + products), and a brand synonymous with Indian private wealth. Winning a family is slow; keeping one is easy.

360 ONE Asset — the manufacturing arm

  • Alternatives leadership: 360 ONE Asset is one of India's largest alternatives (AIF) managers — private equity, private credit, listed-equity and structured strategies aimed at sophisticated investors. Alternatives carry higher, stickier fees than plain mutual funds.
  • Broadening the base: a growing mutual-fund and PMS book extends the AMC beyond alternatives toward a wider investor set — including, via ET Money, the mass-affluent.
  • Why it matters strategically: manufacturing lets 360 ONE capture the full fee stack on client money and reduces reliance on distributing rivals' products. It's the difference between renting shelf space and owning the shop.
Wealth + Asset = a self-reinforcing loop

The two arms feed each other. The Wealth business has the rich clients; the Asset business has the products those clients want (especially exclusive alternatives). So 360 ONE can source capital cheaply for its own funds from its own client base, and offer those clients differentiated products no pure distributor can. Money raised by the AMC is often advised by the Wealth arm; money advised by the Wealth arm often flows into the AMC's funds. Each rupee is monetised twice, and neither arm is easy to replicate alone — it's the combination that's the moat.

Net flows: the single most important number

The KPI an analyst checks first, every single quarter.

AUM can rise for two very different reasons: the market went up (which flatters everyone equally and can reverse), or clients chose to bring you more money. Only the second — net flows — tells you the franchise is winning. Net flows are new money in minus money withdrawn, and for a wealth manager they are the closest thing to a truth serum.

Why "organic" flows are the honest number

There are two ways to grow AUM: organically (win new client money through the front door) or inorganically (buy another firm and absorb its book, as with UBS's India wealth business). Both add assets, but only organic net flows prove the core franchise is healthy — that relationship managers are still winning families and existing clients are still adding money. 360 ONE's organic ARR net flows of ₹35,199 Cr, up 36%, is therefore the cleanest evidence the machine is working. When you read the results, separate the two: strong organic flows = a healthy engine; growth that leans on acquisitions = a company buying its numbers.

What drives flows — and what threatens them

  • Wealth creation: every IPO, business sale, ESOP windfall and inheritance mints new UHNIs who need managing — a structural tailwind (see §07).
  • Relationship-manager productivity: flows ultimately come through people. Hiring, retaining and equipping top RMs is the operational lever — and RM poaching is the operational risk.
  • Performance & trust: good outcomes and clean conduct bring referrals; a scandal or serial underperformance triggers outflows — and in FY26 the firm did flag outflows in its discretionary PMS, a reminder that flows can go negative in pockets even as the whole grows.
  • Market direction: in a sharp bear market, clients de-risk and AUM shrinks on both price and flow — the cyclicality baked into every asset-gatherer.
Markets give you AUM; only clients give you flows. Watch the flows.
07

The UHNI wealth-creation story — the secular tailwind

The structural force that makes the whole category a long-duration growth story.

Behind every wealth manager sits one enormous, slow-moving tailwind: India is minting rich people faster than almost anywhere on earth, and their wealth is professionalising. This is the demand engine that lets a firm like 360 ONE grow flows for years even without taking share — and it is the single biggest reason the category commands premium multiples.

Three structural forces, stacked

  • The wealth explosion. India's population of high-net-worth and ultra-high-net-worth individuals is growing rapidly, driven by a decade of equity-market and start-up wealth creation, promoter liquidity events, and a maturing economy. Each new UHNI is a potential multi-crore, multi-decade relationship.
  • Formalisation of wealth. Historically, rich Indian families managed money informally — through a trusted CA, a bank RM, real estate and gold. The shift toward organised, advised, professionally-managed wealth is a penetration story that dwarfs the raw growth in the number of rich people. Organised players are taking share from the informal market for at least a decade to come.
  • Complexity demands advice. As portfolios globalise (offshore assets, alternatives, private markets), tax and succession get harder, and the next generation takes over, families increasingly need a full-service adviser — exactly 360 ONE's offering. Complexity is a moat that deepens with wealth.
Why this is the whole bull case in one idea

A wealth manager's growth is the product of three things: more rich people × more of their money getting professionally managed × the firm's share of that. In India, the first two are both compounding structurally — so even a firm that merely holds its share grows for years. 360 ONE, as the scale leader at the top end, is positioned to grow flows and take share simultaneously. That's why analysts treat this as a long-duration compounder rather than a cyclical broker: the tailwind is demographic and structural, not dependent on any single year's market. The scarcity of a listed, pure-play way to own this theme is what pushes the multiple higher still.

The honest caveat: the tailwind is real but not immune to markets. UHNI wealth is disproportionately in equities and alternatives, so a prolonged bear market shrinks both AUM and appetite for new investing — flows slow, transactional income dries up, and the "structural" story feels very cyclical for a year or two. The tailwind bends the long-run curve up; it does not remove the cyclicality along the way.
08

The inorganic land-grab: UBS, ET Money & B&K

Three deals that, together, are an attempt to break out of the UHNI niche.

For years 360 ONE was a focused, top-of-market UHNI specialist. Over FY24–26 it made a cluster of acquisitions that, read together, signal a deliberate strategy: broaden beyond the ultra-rich, add distribution reach and product breadth, and plug into a global brand. This is the most important strategic shift in the company's recent history — and the biggest execution question.

The strategic logic — and the tension

The through-line is expanding the addressable market. UHNI is lucrative but finite; to keep compounding, 360 ONE is reaching down (ET Money → mass-affluent), across (B&K → institutional/capital-markets) and out (UBS → global/cross-border). Done well, this turns a niche UHNI house into a full-spectrum wealth platform with far more flow sources. The tension: these segments have different, often lower, margins and different economics than the ultra-rich core, and integrating acquisitions (cultures, RMs, platforms, brands) is where wealth managers most often stumble. The market is paying a premium for the pristine UHNI annuity engine — the risk is that aggressive diversification dilutes the very quality that earned the premium.

Watch how the deals show up in the numbers: the healthy signal is organic flows and ARR mix staying strong while the acquisitions add reach. The warning signal would be growth that increasingly leans on bought AUM, a falling ARR share, or a rising cost-to-income ratio as integration costs bite. The UBS partnership in particular is a call option — potentially transformative if the referral pipeline delivers, but unproven until the flows actually arrive.

The industry and the rivals

A scarce listed cohort — and 360 ONE sits at the top-end of it.

India's organised wealth-management market is large, under-penetrated and consolidating around a handful of scaled, listed players. What makes 360 ONE distinctive within this cohort is its pure-play focus on the ultra-rich and its ownership of both advice and product — which shows up in sector-leading margins.

The competitive edge, decoded

Two things set 360 ONE apart. First, positioning: it fishes in the deepest, most lucrative pool — the ultra-rich — where relationships are stickiest and ticket sizes largest, rather than competing on price in the crowded mass-HNI segment. Second, integration: by owning both the advice and the products (unlike a pure distributor such as Anand Rathi), it captures more of each client's fee stack, which is why its ~61% operating margin sits above Nuvama (~55%), Motilal (~52%) and Anand Rathi (~45%). The strategic question is whether its recent down-market and capital-markets diversification erodes that margin edge — trading premium purity for larger, but thinner, addressable markets.

The regulatory backdrop: wealth & asset management is regulated by SEBI across AIF, PMS, mutual-fund, RIA (advisory) and broking rules — and the regime is steadily pushing the industry from opaque commissions toward transparent, fee-based advice. That structural shift actually favours 360 ONE's advisory/ARR model, but regulatory changes to fee structures, expense ratios or distribution commissions are a permanent swing factor worth watching every year.

How to think about the numbers

Three lenses. (1) Cost-to-income (~50%) — for every ₹100 of income, ₹50 goes to costs (mostly people); the lower it trends as AUM scales, the more operating leverage is showing up. (2) RoE — read the tangible version. Headline RoE looks modest (~14%) because acquisitions have loaded the balance sheet with goodwill/intangibles; the tangible RoE of ~19% better reflects the real economic return on the operating business. (3) The multiple. At ~37× earnings and ~4.6× book, the market is paying up for a scarce, high-margin, structurally-growing annuity franchise — a price that only holds if ARR flows and margins keep compounding.

On the "borrowings" you'll see: a screener shows large borrowings (~₹16,000 Cr FY26). Don't read this as a leveraged, risky wealth manager. Most of it funds 360 ONE Prime's lending book (loans against securities) — a spread business where borrowing to on-lend is the model, not corporate distress. The pure wealth/asset operation is capital-light; the leverage sits in the deliberately-run NBFC arm.
Capital return

360 ONE has been a generous, consistent dividend payer — a capital-light, cash-rich model throws off far more cash than it needs to grow, and much is returned. It declared a ₹6/share interim dividend for FY27, with a dividend yield around ~1%. For a compounder still reinvesting into expansion, steady payouts on top of ~20% earnings growth are a meaningful part of the total-return case.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely high-quality franchise — priced for it to keep executing.

360 ONE is one of the cleaner ways to own India's structural wealth-creation story: the scale leader in UHNI wealth, with a rising recurring-revenue mix, peer-leading margins and a founder-led team. The investment debate is almost entirely about valuation, cyclicality and integration — not business quality.

The bull case

  • Scarce scale leader — the largest listed pure-play UHNI wealth manager; a rare, clean way to own the theme.
  • Rising earnings quality — ARR now ~75% of revenue and growing 34%+; annuity mix earns a premium multiple.
  • Structural tailwind — India's UHNI wealth explosion + formalisation = years of flows even without share gains.
  • Peer-leading economics — ~61% operating margin, ~50% cost-to-income, ~19% tangible RoE, capital-light, generous dividends.
  • Optionality — the UBS partnership, ET Money down-market reach and B&K capital-markets can widen the flow engine.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~37× earnings / ~4.6× book leaves little room for error; vulnerable to de-rating.
  • Market cyclicality — AUM, flows and transactional income all sag in a bear market; the "structural" story is cyclical along the way.
  • Integration & dilution risk — diversifying into lower-margin mass-affluent / capital-markets could erode the premium purity; acquisitions can misfire.
  • Ownership overhang — low ~6% promoter holding and a large Bain stake being pared down create periodic block-deal pressure.
  • People & conduct risk — flows walk out with star RMs; PMS outflows and a ₹336 Cr tax demand (under appeal) are reminders that pockets can go wrong.
The bottom line

360 ONE is a structurally advantaged, high-margin annuity franchise riding one of India's most durable growth themes, run by the people who built it. The business risk is low; the valuation and execution risk is real. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter — organic net flows (is the core engine still winning?), the ARR share of revenue (is earnings quality still improving?), and the cost-to-income ratio (is the diversification paying off or just adding cost?). Get those right and the premium is deserved; stumble, and a 37× multiple has room to fall.
