Entero Healthcare, consolidating India's medicine supply

A complete walk through one of India's three largest pharmaceutical & healthcare-product distributors — the tech-enabled middleman that moves medicines, devices and consumables from thousands of manufacturers to more than a lakh of retail pharmacies and hospitals. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Entero Healthcare Solutions is a B2B pharmaceutical and healthcare-product distributor — the plumbing between the companies that make medicines and the shops and hospitals that dispense them. It does not manufacture a single tablet, and it does not run a chain of pharmacies. It buys medicines, medical devices, surgical consumables, nutraceuticals and diagnostics from ~3,300+ manufacturers, warehouses them across 136 distribution centres in more than 500 districts, and delivers them — often same-day — to over 1.05 lakh retail pharmacies and ~3,600 hospitals. Its own pitch is simple: be the national, technology-driven backbone of a market that is otherwise served by tens of thousands of tiny regional stockists.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Its product is reach, not a drug. Entero earns a small distribution margin on somebody else's medicine. On roughly ₹6,591 Cr of FY26 revenue it kept a gross margin of only ~9.8%, an EBITDA margin of ~4.0%, and a net margin of just ~2.2% — about ₹146 Cr of profit. That is the nature of distribution everywhere: a thin percentage on enormous turnover, where scale, inventory turns and delivery reach are the entire game.
  • Its strategy is a roll-up. India's pharma-distribution market is estimated near ₹3.3 lakh crore and is astonishingly fragmented — served by tens of thousands of small stockists, with national platforms holding under 10% between them. Entero has grown by buying regional distributors — 50+ acquisitions since 2018 — and stitching them onto one warehouse, technology and purchasing platform. Roughly half of FY26's 29% growth came from acquisitions, not organic volume.
  • Profitability is young, thin and improving. Entero lost money as recently as FY23, turned a small profit around its February 2024 IPO, and has since scaled fast: PAT rose ~170% in FY25 and a further ~36% in FY26. The stock trades on a very high headline multiple (~60× earnings) because the market is pricing the consolidation, not the current 2%-margin reality.
In plain terms

Every medicine strip in an Indian pharmacy has quietly passed through a distributor's warehouse. For most of the country that distributor is a small, family-run stockist covering a few towns. Entero's bet is that a single national operator — one that buys in bulk from 3,300 manufacturers, runs 136 warehouses on shared software, and delivers to a lakh of pharmacies — can do that job cheaper, faster and more reliably, and slowly absorb the small players by buying them out. The catch is that distribution is a low-margin, cash-hungry business: Entero touches ₹6,591 Cr of medicine to keep ₹146 Cr of profit. The whole investment question is whether growing bigger makes that thin slice fatter — or just bigger.

01

Pharma distribution 101: who moves the medicine

The value chain from factory to pharmacy — and exactly where Entero sits in it.

A medicine's journey from factory to patient passes through more hands than most people realise, and each hand takes a cut. Entero occupies one specific, unglamorous, indispensable link: the distributor / stockist — the wholesaler that carries inventory, extends credit, and physically ferries product from the manufacturer's depot to the neighbourhood chemist. To follow the company you first need to see the chain and the handful of terms that describe it.

The service a distributor actually sells

Ask why a manufacturer doesn't just ship straight to pharmacies, and the whole reason Entero exists appears. A pharma company making 500 products cannot economically deliver a dozen strips of one drug to each of ten lakh tiny shops, chase payment from all of them, and manage returns of near-expiry stock. So it hands that mess to distributors. The distributor's real products are three: availability (carry 76,000 SKUs so a pharmacy gets everything in one order), credit (pay the manufacturer sooner than the pharmacy pays you, and finance the gap), and logistics (deliver fast and often, sometimes same-day). Entero is trying to industrialise all three — and charge a slightly better margin for doing them better than a small stockist can.

Who Entero serves, and how demand behaves

  • Retail pharmacies — the bulk of customers, ordering small quantities frequently. Sticky once Entero becomes their fill-rate leader, because a pharmacy prizes a distributor that never runs out.
  • Hospitals & clinics — larger, more predictable institutional demand, including surgical consumables and devices; a growing, higher-value channel for Entero.
  • The manufacturers — not customers but suppliers, yet the relationship cuts both ways: the more pharmacies Entero reaches, the more a manufacturer wants it as a channel, which improves Entero's buying terms. Reach begets reach.
02

The razor-thin spread: what a distributor actually keeps

The single most important idea in the case — and it is specific to distribution, not to any drug.

People instinctively size Entero by its revenue — ₹6,591 Cr sounds enormous. It is the wrong number to admire. A distributor does not own the value of the medicine flowing through it; it keeps a thin toll on the flow. The entire company can be read from what happens to a single ₹100 of medicine as it passes through Entero's warehouse. Watch how little sticks.

Of every ₹100 of medicine Entero handles, about ₹90 flows straight back to the manufacturer. Of the ~₹9.8 gross slice, running the network eats most of it — leaving ~₹4.0 of operating profit and just ~₹2.2 of net profit. The business is the two-rupee sliver on the bottom bar, not the hundred-rupee flow.

That thinness is not a flaw to be fixed; it is the definition of distribution. But it makes two things matter far more than they would in a normal company. First, inventory turns: because the margin per rupee of stock is tiny, a distributor only makes real money by turning that stock over many times a year — the same warehouse rupee earning its ~2% again and again. Second, operating leverage: with gross margin fixed near 9.8%, every extra rupee of sales that flows over the same fixed warehouse-and-delivery cost drops disproportionately into EBITDA. That is precisely what FY26 showed — revenue up 29%, but EBITDA up 55%, because the cost base grew slower than the volume across it.

The margins themselves stay small but the direction is the whole story. Gross margin sits near 9.8%; running the network takes most of it, leaving EBITDA margin at ~4.0% (up ~67 bps from ~3.4% the year before) and net margin at just ~2.2%. Management guides to ~5.0% EBITDA in FY27 — and in a 2%-net-margin business, lifting the EBITDA line from 4% to 5% is a ~25% uplift in operating profit on the very same sales. That is the leverage a thin slice hides.

The device that only fits a distributor: margin is small, but it compounds on turns and scale

Forget the size of the revenue. The number that runs Entero is (gross margin %) × (times inventory turns) − (fixed network cost), and the magic is that none of the three needs a miracle to move the profit a lot. Nudge gross margin up 50 basis points by selling more private-label and devices; turn inventory a little faster with better software; spread the same warehouse cost over 29% more volume — and a company keeping ~₹2.2 of every ₹100 can plausibly keep ₹2.8 or ₹3.0. On ₹6,591 Cr, that half-rupee is ~₹330 Cr of extra flow. This is why a low-margin distributor can still be a growth stock: the thinness that looks like weakness is also enormous leverage, because tiny percentage gains land on a very large base. The risk, of course, runs the other way too — a 50-bps slip erases a quarter of the profit.

03

The platform Entero actually runs

Warehouses, software, and a catalogue of 76,000 things — the machinery behind the margin.

Strip away the finance and Entero is, physically, a logistics-and-technology company: a national grid of warehouses stocked with a vast catalogue, wired together by an ordering app and a purchasing system, delivering small parcels fast to a very large number of small customers. The scale of that grid is the source of whatever cost and buying advantage it has over a regional stockist.

The three things the platform is meant to do better

  • Buy cheaper — a national buyer purchasing for 136 warehouses negotiates better terms and rebates from manufacturers than a single-city stockist can. Scale on the purchasing side is a direct feed into gross margin.
  • Fill faster — a wider catalogue and denser warehouse grid mean a pharmacy gets more of its order, sooner, from one supplier. High fill-rates are what make a pharmacy loyal, and loyalty is what lets Entero grow organically inside an acquired territory.
  • Run leaner — shared software for ordering, inventory and collections spreads a fixed technology cost across ever more volume. This is where the operating leverage of section 02 physically comes from.
Why the ordering app matters more than it looks

A distributor's least visible asset is the app on the pharmacist's phone. Once a chemist routinely places the day's order through Entero's system — seeing live stock, schemes and credit in one place — switching to a rival becomes a nuisance, and Entero gains a running read on real demand it can use to buy and stock smarter. It is a modest moat, not a dramatic one; a pharmacy can and does keep two or three distributors. But in a business where the difference between a good and bad year is half a percent of margin, being the pharmacy's default screen — the first place it looks — is worth real money over thousands of shops. The technology layer is not a gimmick bolted onto a trucking business; it is how a thin-margin distributor earns its slightly-less-thin margin.

04

The company, the founders, and who owns it

A 2018 start-up built by industry insiders and PE capital — young, listed, and still promoter-and-fund-controlled.

Entero is a strikingly recent company for its scale — founded in 2018, not decades ago — and that youth explains a lot: the aggressive acquisition pace, the private-equity DNA, the thin-but-rising profitability, and a share register still dominated by its founders and their financial backer. Understanding who built it and who funds it explains how it grew so fast, and where the governance eyes should rest.

Timeline

  • 2018
    Founded by Prabhat Agrawal (formerly of Alkem Laboratories) and Prem Sethi, backed from the start by global healthcare investor OrbiMed and other PE capital — an explicit plan to build a national distribution consolidator.

  • Feb 2024
    IPO on NSE & BSE (BSE code 544122), raising ~₹1,600 Cr (a large fresh issue plus an offer-for-sale). Fresh proceeds were earmarked to repay debt and fund working capital and acquisitions. The stock listed at a slight discount — a lukewarm debut.
  • FY24
    First clear full-year profit (~₹40 Cr) as scale began to cover the fixed network cost; the model crossed from "buying growth" toward "earning" it.
  • FY25

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter / founder group: the founders together with OrbiMed-linked entities remain the promoter block, holding roughly 52% (approx.) after the IPO. The balance is public float, with domestic institutions (~15%) a notable and growing holder. Confirm the exact split in the latest shareholding pattern.
  • The OrbiMed angle (worth pausing on): OrbiMed is one of the world's largest dedicated healthcare investors. Its backing gave Entero both the capital and the credibility to acquire aggressively for six years before listing — a genuine advantage in a roll-up, where cheap, patient equity is the fuel. It also means a large, sophisticated financial owner whose eventual selling behaviour will shape the float.
  • Founder-operators, not a legacy house: Agrawal and Sethi came out of the pharma and healthcare-data industries, not from a family trading business. Why the ownership shape matters: a PE-seeded, founder-run consolidator is built to grow by acquisition, and that is exactly what Entero has done — brilliantly on the reach, and the market now trusts it to keep going. The flip side is that a young, fast-acquiring company carries execution and integration risk that an older, organically-grown distributor would not, and a large PE holder's exit path sits over the stock. Read the acquisition cadence and the balance-sheet quality (section 06) alongside the growth.
05

The consolidation thesis: buying a fragmented market

Entero's real product is not medicine — it is consolidation of an industry of thousands of tiny distributors.

Here is the whole investment idea in one sentence: India's pharma-distribution market is a ~₹3.3 lakh crore business run by tens of thousands of small, sub-scale, regional stockists, and Entero is trying to be the national platform that gradually absorbs them. Every warehouse, acquisition and app is in service of that single thesis — turning a fragmented cottage industry into something with a few scaled operators, and being one of them.

The fragmentation is the opportunity. A typical Indian pharma distributor covers a district or two, carries a limited range, finances a modest book of pharmacy credit, and has no succession plan or capital to modernise. There are estimated to be tens of thousands of them, and national platforms together hold under 10% of the market. Entero grows two ways at once: it buys these distributors — often at modest valuations of ~0.3–0.35× sales — and then grows organically inside the acquired territory by cross-selling its wider catalogue, better fill-rates and private-label range through the relationships it just bought.

The arithmetic of the roll-up is simple: buy distribution revenue at ~0.3–0.35× sales, fold it onto an existing warehouse-and-tech base, strip out duplicate cost, and cross-sell higher-margin lines through the relationships you just acquired. A deal done that way pays back quickly — if it is truly integrated.

Why buying at a fraction of sales can work — and why it is not free money

The maths of a distribution roll-up is seductive. A regional stockist doing ₹100 Cr of sales might be bought for ~₹30–35 Cr, and once its customers are served off Entero's existing warehouses and buying terms, duplicate costs vanish and a slightly richer product mix flows through — so the acquired revenue can end up more profitable under Entero than it was standalone. Do that fifty times and you have built a national network far cheaper than growing it organically. But the same maths hides the danger: every deal must actually be integrated — systems merged, staff retained, the acquired pharmacies kept loyal — and every rupee paid above the target's tangible assets sits on the balance sheet as goodwill that only justifies itself if the synergies are real. The thesis lives or dies not on buying distributors, which is easy, but on integrating them, which is hard. That is the subject of the next section.

06

The roll-up's hidden ledger: integration and goodwill

The part of a serial acquirer that a first-time reader never checks — and should.

Every company that grows by buying others carries a second, quieter set of books alongside its P&L: the goodwill and intangibles created each time it pays more than a target's tangible worth, and the operational job of actually merging dozens of once-independent businesses into one. For a 50-plus-deal consolidator like Entero, this hidden ledger is where the thesis is really tested — and it is the first thing a sceptic should read.

When Entero buys a distributor for more than the book value of its warehouses, stock and receivables, the difference lands on the balance sheet as goodwill. Across 50-plus acquisitions, a substantial share of Entero's net worth is therefore not tangible assets but the accumulated premium paid for reach, relationships and expected synergies. That is normal and not inherently bad — but it means two things a reader must hold: the book value is soft (much of it is goodwill, not bricks), and if an acquired business underperforms, that goodwill can have to be written down, hitting reported profit in a lump.

The genuine surprise: India's distribution market is the mirror-image of America's

Here is the fact that reframes the whole opportunity. In the United States, pharmaceutical distribution is one of the most consolidated industries anywhere — three giants (McKesson, Cencora and Cardinal) move the overwhelming majority of the country's drugs, an entrenched oligopoly built over decades. India is the exact opposite: the same essential service is delivered by tens of thousands of tiny stockists, with national players holding under 10% between them. Entero's entire premise is that India's fragmented map eventually rhymes with America's consolidated one — that scale economics in distribution are universal, and that the country simply hasn't consolidated yet. If that convergence happens, an early national platform captures enormous share. If India's distribution stays fragmented — because pharmacy relationships are hyper-local, credit is personal, and small stockists are cheap and persistent — then the roll-up grinds against a market that never fully tips. That is the deep question under the stock: does India's medicine supply chain want to become an oligopoly?

What to actually monitor: for a serial acquirer, watch three lines that the headline growth hides — the organic growth rate (is the acquired revenue really compounding, or just being bought?), goodwill and any impairment (is the premium paid holding up?), and the cash conversion of profit (is the growth self-funding, or does it keep needing capital?). Entero's recent numbers on all three have been improving; the thesis needs them to keep doing so through many more deals.
07

The working-capital engine: the real fuel of a distributor

Where a distribution business quietly consumes — or releases — cash, and why it decides the value.

If margin is a distributor's heartbeat, working capital is its bloodstream. A wholesaler pays its manufacturers, holds inventory, and waits for pharmacies to pay — financing that entire gap out of its own capital. In a 2%-net-margin business, how many days of cash are tied up in that cycle matters as much as the margin itself, because the interest cost of funding it comes straight out of the thin profit. This is the least glamorous and most decisive part of Entero's story.

The cycle has three moving parts: inventory days (how long stock sits in the warehouse), debtor days (how long pharmacies take to pay), and creditor days (how long Entero takes to pay manufacturers). Net them together and you get the working-capital cycle — the number of days of sales Entero must finance at any moment. The single most encouraging thing in the company's recent history is that this number has been falling even as it scales: from roughly 98 days in FY20 toward the mid-60s, with management targeting ~60 by FY27.

A distributor that shrinks its cash cycle while growing 29% is doing the hardest and most valuable thing in the model — funding expansion from its own tightening operations rather than from ever-more debt. This single trend line did much of the work in lifting ROCE toward ~14.6%.

Why does this decide the value? Because a distributor that grows while its cash cycle lengthens is a machine that eats capital — every rupee of new sales demands more borrowing, interest devours the thin margin, and returns on capital stay stuck in single digits. A distributor that grows while the cycle shortens is the opposite: it frees cash as it expands, funds acquisitions partly from its own operations, and sees returns on capital climb. Entero has been firmly in the second camp lately — ROCE rose to ~14.6% in FY26 from ~10.7%, and it generated positive operating cash flow (~₹96 Cr) — which is exactly why the market is willing to pay up despite the 2% net margin. The whole quality of the business hides in this one trend.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with

A distributor can grow revenue 30% a year and still be a poor investment if every rupee of that growth is funded by more borrowing at an interest cost that swallows its 2% margin — you would be running faster just to stand still. Conversely, a distributor growing the same 30% while squeezing its cash cycle is compounding genuine value, because the growth increasingly pays for itself. So the profit line alone never tells you whether a wholesaler is winning. You have to read it together with the working-capital cycle and the interest bill: falling days and controlled debt turn thin margins into rising returns; rising days turn them into a capital trap. Entero's improving cycle is the quiet engine behind its recent re-rating — and the first thing that would signal trouble if it reversed.

08

The margin-uplift levers: how a 4% business reaches for 5%+

The handful of dials Entero genuinely controls to fatten a structurally thin slice.

Most of what sets a distributor's margin — the trade structure, drug prices, competition — is fixed by forces outside the company. But not all of it. Entero has three levers it genuinely owns to lift the slice it keeps, and management's ~5% FY27 EBITDA target rests on pulling them. Each one nudges the same razor-thin percentage a little higher on a very large base.

Picture the mix as a ladder. At the bottom sits pure third-party pharma distribution — the thinnest slice. A rung up are medical devices, surgical consumables and diagnostics, which carry fatter margins and lean on the hospital channel. At the top is private label — Entero's own brands — where it captures a share of the manufacturer's margin as well as the distributor's, several times the spread of a commodity strip. Higher-margin lines were only ~4–5% of revenue recently; the three medtech acquisitions of FY26 were chosen precisely to climb that ladder.

The three levers

  • Private label — the richest lever. When Entero sells medicines and products under its own brands (sourced from contract manufacturers), it keeps a chunk of the maker's margin on top of the distributor's — several times the spread of a third-party strip. Growing private-label penetration is the single biggest structural margin opportunity, and the clearest thing management fully controls.
  • Medical devices & consumables — surgical items, consumables and diagnostics carry fatter margins than commodity pharma and lean on Entero's hospital channel. Three of FY26's seven acquisitions were deliberately in medtech, tilting the mix toward this richer category.
  • Scale-driven buying & tech — bigger purchasing volumes win better terms and rebates from manufacturers, while shared software lowers the cost of serving each order. Both feed the EBITDA line without needing a new product at all.
Why the mix shift is the smartest thing management controls

In this business, almost every variable is dictated from outside — the trade margin, the regulated prices, the pharmacy's payment habits, the competition down the street. Product mix is the rare dial Entero genuinely owns. Pushing volume up the ladder — more devices, more diagnostics, and above all more private label — lifts the slice it keeps on each rupee without waiting on anyone's permission. It won't transform a 4% business into a 15% one; distribution is distribution. But moving the blend from ~4% to ~5% EBITDA is a ~25% uplift in operating profit on the same sales, and it is the part of the plan least dependent on the market cooperating. The reach was built by acquisition; the margin will be earned by mix.

09

The industry and the rivals

A vast, fragmented market with a few would-be consolidators — and a deep-pocketed one arriving.

Entero competes on two fronts at once. Against the tens of thousands of small regional distributors that still hold most of the market, it competes on range, reliability and price of service — and it is winning, slowly, by buying them. Against a small set of other scaled and would-be-scaled platforms, it competes for the same acquisitions and the same national ambition — and here the field is getting tougher.

The competitive shape to hold in mind — and the line that must not blur

Keep one distinction clear, because it is the most common confusion about Entero: it is not a drug maker like Sun or Cipla, and it is not a pharmacy chain like Apollo or MedPlus. It makes nothing and dispenses to no patient. It is the wholesaler in between — and its competitive test is neither a blockbuster molecule nor footfall, but cost-to-serve and reach. The real strategic risk is that its two strongest rivals sit at opposite ends: a Reliance-backed platform with deeper capital chasing the same roll-up from above, and the endlessly regenerating small stockist defending its patch from below. Entero's edge is being early, national and listed with the capital to keep buying; its vulnerability is that in distribution, being biggest is not the same as being unassailable — the barriers are real but not high, and a richer competitor can bid the same deals.

10

Financials & valuation

Fast growth and a rich multiple — the whole question is whether the thin margin thickens.

Entero's income statement tells a clean growth story: revenue compounding ~30% a year, profit growing even faster as operating leverage kicks in, and returns on capital climbing as the cash cycle tightens. What complicates the picture is the price. Unlike a cheap cyclical, Entero trades on a high earnings multiple — the market is paying today for the consolidation it expects tomorrow. The debate is not whether the business is growing; it is whether the tiny net margin can widen enough to justify a valuation built on a 2%-margin base.

Revenue roughly doubled in three years — from ~₹3,300 Cr in FY23 to ~₹3,922 Cr (FY24), ~₹5,096 Cr (FY25) and ~₹6,591 Cr (FY26) — a mix of organic growth and 50+ acquisitions. But remember section 02: that top line is the medicine flow, not the value kept. The profit line below is the real story.

The valuation is the crux of the argument. At roughly 60× earnings and a market cap near ₹7,850 Cr — about 1.2× sales — Entero is priced as a compounding platform, not a commodity wholesaler. On the current ~₹146 Cr of profit, that multiple looks daunting. The bull's answer is that profit is early: a 2%-margin business growing volume ~25%+ while lifting margin toward 5% and turning capital faster can grow earnings far faster than revenue for several years, so today's P/E collapses quickly if the plan lands. The bear's answer is that a distributor is still a distributor — thin, competitive, capital-hungry — and paying 60× for one assumes an outcome that a single margin slip or a richer rival can derail.

The one number that settles the thesis: EBITDA margin, read with organic growth and cash conversion

At ~60× earnings, Entero does not need to prove it can grow revenue — it plainly can, by acquisition alone. It needs to prove the growth is quality: that the thin slice thickens. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is EBITDA margin — the move from ~4.0% toward management's ~5.0% — tracked alongside two companions the roll-up can hide: organic growth (is acquired revenue really compounding, per section 06?) and cash conversion / working-capital days (is the growth self-funding, per section 07?). If EBITDA margin climbs to ~5% while organic growth holds double-digits and the cash cycle keeps falling, the ~₹146 Cr of profit becomes several hundred crore within a few years and the multiple is justified in hindsight. If margin stalls at ~4% while a well-capitalised rival bids up deal prices and receivables creep back, the same 60× becomes the whole risk. Margin times turns, funded by its own cash — that is the scoreboard.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A fast-scaling consolidator priced for success — asked whether it can widen the slice as it grows.

Entero enters FY27 with real momentum: a national grid built, margins rising, the cash cycle tightening, a full acquisition pipeline, and a market so fragmented that the runway for consolidation is measured in decades, not quarters. What it must still prove is the hardest thing in distribution — that getting bigger makes the razor-thin margin thicker, not just longer. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • A decades-long runway — national platforms hold under 10% of a ₹3.3 lakh crore market; the consolidation opportunity is vast and barely begun.
  • Operating leverage is real — FY26 EBITDA grew ~55% on ~29% revenue; scale visibly drops more of each rupee to profit.
  • Self-funding growth — a working-capital cycle falling from ~98 to ~66 days and ROCE rising to ~14.6% mean expansion increasingly pays for itself.
  • Margin levers management owns — private label and medtech mix can lift EBITDA toward ~5% without needing the market's help.
  • Non-discretionary demand & a proven acquirer — medicine demand only grows; OrbiMed-backed Entero has integrated 50+ deals at disciplined prices.

The bear case

  • Structurally thin margins — a ~2% net margin leaves little cushion; a small slip in gross margin erases a large share of profit.
  • Rich valuation — ~60× earnings prices years of flawless execution; any stumble in margin or integration re-rates hard.
  • Integration & goodwill risk — 50+ acquisitions mean soft book value and the standing risk of a write-down if a deal disappoints.
  • Well-capitalised competition — Reliance-linked platforms can bid up the same acquisitions and pressure the margins Entero needs to lift.
  • Capital-hungry model & PE overhang — distribution consumes working capital as it grows, and a large PE holder's eventual exit sits over the float.
The bottom line

Entero Healthcare is a well-run attempt to do something genuinely hard and genuinely valuable: build the first national backbone for a medicine supply chain that a hundred thousand pharmacies currently reach through tens of thousands of tiny stockists. The reach has been built impressively and cheaply, the operating leverage is real, and the falling cash cycle is turning a low-margin model into rising returns. But the price already assumes the plan works, and distribution offers no permanent moat — only the compounding advantage of being biggest, tightest and most efficient before anyone else gets there. The next test is concrete and datable: whether FY27 delivers the guided ~5% EBITDA margin while organic growth stays double-digit and the working-capital cycle reaches ~60 days — the three numbers that together prove the slice is thickening as the flow grows. Hit that trio and today's rich multiple looks like an entry point to a consolidator in its early innings; miss it, and the market will remember that a distributor, however large, still keeps only two rupees of every hundred it touches.
