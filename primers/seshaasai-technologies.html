Seshaasai Technologies, the plumbing behind the plastic

A complete walk through the company that makes roughly one in three debit and credit cards issued in India — how a business most people have never heard of became critical infrastructure for the country's banks, why its economics look like a printer's but earn like a software firm's, and why a freshly listed 32-year-old converter of paper forms is quietly reinventing itself into the digital nervous system of Indian finance.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Seshaasai Technologies Limited makes the physical and digital "touch-points" a bank uses to reach its customers — above all the debit, credit and prepaid cards that arrive in your mailbox, but also the PIN mailers, cheque books, account statements, welcome kits and, increasingly, the SMS / e-mail / WhatsApp messages a bank sends. It is one of India's two largest payment-card manufacturers, with a claimed ~31.9% share of debit and credit cards issued in FY25, and it sells almost entirely to banks and the wider BFSI sector. Incorporated in 1993 as "Seshaasai Business Forms," it listed on the NSE and BSE only in September 2025, so it comes to public markets with a long operating history but a very short one as a quoted company.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is regulated infrastructure dressed as a printer. Anyone can print a leaflet; almost no one can legally personalise a live Visa, Mastercard or RuPay card. That takes network certifications, a PCI-DSS-audited secure facility, and years of bank empanelment. Seshaasai has all of it — which is why a business that superficially looks like commodity printing earns ~25% EBITDA margins and a ~28% return on capital. The moat is the licence, not the ink.
  • One card is not one sale — it is a stream. When a bank issues a card through Seshaasai, the company gets paid to manufacture and personalise it, then keeps earning off that same account for years: the PIN mailer, the statements, the renewal card three years later, the customer communications in between. Roughly 75–87% of revenue comes from clients of more than five years' standing — this is a repeat, sticky, annuity-like book, not a series of one-off print jobs.
  • FY25 showed the mix at work. Revenue actually fell about 6% to ~₹1,462 Cr, yet net profit rose ~31% to ~₹222 Cr and EBITDA margin jumped from ~19% to ~25%. That divergence — less revenue, much more profit — is the whole investment question in miniature, and section 07 is about why it happened.
In plain terms

Seshaasai is what sits between a bank's decision to give you a card and the moment that card lands in your hand — and then everything the bank posts to you afterward. It buys secure chip modules and card blanks, encodes and personalises them inside audited, network-certified plants, and ships them to the bank's customers, while a second arm prints and dispatches the statements, cheque books and policy documents that BFSI generates by the billion. It is deliberately un-glamorous plumbing. But the plumbing is licensed, audited and hard to rip out once a bank has trusted you with its card programme — and that is exactly why a low-profile forms-printer from the 1990s can list at a market value north of ₹6,000 crore.

01

The plumbing behind banking: what "phygital BFSI infrastructure" means

No finance degree needed — just the touch-points a bank uses to reach you.

Every bank has a paradox at its heart: it is a digital business that still has to put physical, secure objects into millions of individual hands. A card must be a real piece of certified plastic before it can be tapped. A PIN must travel in a tamper-evident mailer that no one — not even the courier — can read. A cheque must carry security features that defeat forgery. A statement must be printed, folded, sealed and posted to the right address, or delivered digitally to the right inbox. Banks do almost none of this themselves. They outsource it to specialists like Seshaasai. That outsourced layer — half physical, half digital, hence "phygital" — is the industry Seshaasai lives in.

The only terms you need to follow this business
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)

The dependency that shapes the whole sector: banks won't do this in-house, and can't use just anyone

Two forces create this industry. First, no bank wants to run its own card-printing plant or postal-fulfilment operation — it is capital-heavy, low-glamour, and far from banking's core. So it outsources. Second, it cannot outsource to anyone: a vendor handling live card data and customer PINs must be certified by the card networks and audited to global data-security standards, or the bank is breaking the rules of Visa, Mastercard, NPCI and the RBI. The intersection of "banks must outsource this" and "they can only use a certified handful" is precisely the space a company like Seshaasai occupies. It is why the market is consolidated among two or three serious names rather than a hundred jobbing printers, and why demand tracks the relentless growth of India's banked population rather than any single bank's fortunes.

Why the tailwind is structural, not cyclical

  • The card base keeps compounding. India has well over a billion debit cards and a fast-growing credit-card base, and every one is re-issued on a roughly three-to-five-year cycle. Even in a UPI-heavy world, the number of physical cards in circulation has kept rising — each new account, each renewal, each replacement is a unit of demand.
  • Financial inclusion mints new accounts. Jan Dhan accounts, RuPay debit cards, and the steady formalisation of the economy pull tens of millions of first-time customers into the banking system — customers who need a card, a welcome kit, and a stream of communications.
  • Digital communication is additive, not just substitutive. As banks move statements and alerts to e-mail, SMS and WhatsApp, the volume of regulated customer communication explodes — and someone certified has to generate and deliver it. The channel changes; the need for a trusted intermediary does not.
02

What Seshaasai actually sells: three segments, one customer

Payments, communication & fulfilment, and a smaller RFID/IoT arm — all pointed at BFSI.

Seshaasai reports through three product families, but they share one DNA: each is a secure, high-volume, mission-critical output a bank would rather buy than build. The revenue is heavily weighted to payments, with communication & fulfilment the sizeable second leg and IoT/RFID a small, faster-growing option on the side.

1 · Payment solutions — the core

This is Seshaasai's flagship and its identity: manufacturing and personalising debit, credit and prepaid cards for banks, along with cheque books and other secure payment instruments. It is a certified card manufacturer and personaliser for the major networks — Visa, Mastercard and NPCI's RuPay — and its product range now runs well beyond ordinary plastic: EMV chip cards, contactless "tap" cards, metal cards, biometric cards, wearables and payment stickers. Card manufacturing capacity was reported at roughly 11.9 million cards a month as of FY25. This segment is where the certifications, the switching costs and the ~31.9% market share all live.

2 · Communication & fulfilment — the second leg

The heir to the original "Business Forms" company: secure printing and delivery of the documents BFSI generates in bulk — account and portfolio statements, PIN mailers, welcome kits, insurance policy documents, utility and tax notices, and identity cards. Critically, this arm is migrating from paper to digital CCM: the same statement that was once printed and posted is increasingly an e-mail, an SMS or a WhatsApp message, generated and dispatched at scale on the customer's behalf. That shift — from a logistics business to a software-flavoured one — is the margin story of section 06.

3 · IoT / RFID solutions — the option

The smallest and newest arm: RFID tags and IoT-enabled tracking for asset management, logistics and authentication, with a reported capacity around 41.7 million tags a month. At ~7% of revenue it is not yet material to the numbers, but it extends Seshaasai's core competence — secure, personalised, high-volume physical objects with a data layer — into markets beyond banking, and gives the "Technologies" in the new name something to point at.

Why one customer base, three products, is the point — not a weakness

It would be easy to read three segments as three unrelated businesses bolted together. They are not. Every one of them sells the same thing to the same buyer: a trusted, certified, high-volume secure output that a bank needs and cannot safely make itself. Because the customer is the same — a bank's operations team — Seshaasai can walk in already holding the card contract and cross-sell the statement printing, the PIN mailers, the digital communication and the RFID welcome kit. One empanelment, one security audit, one relationship, then several revenue lines. That is why the concentration on BFSI is deliberate: depth inside a hard-to-enter account is worth more than breadth across easy ones.

03

Inside a payment card: the business most people never see

What actually happens between a bank's approval and the card in your wallet.

To understand Seshaasai you have to follow a single card. The journey from "the bank approved you" to "the card is in your hand" passes through a chain of steps that are individually mundane and collectively very hard to replicate — because each one is regulated, audited, and reliant on data no printer is casually allowed to touch.

The single most valuable step is Step 2. Anyone can make a blank; only a certified, audited vendor can legally load live card data onto it. That is the step that keeps rivals out and margins up.

The certifications that gate the whole thing

A vendor cannot simply decide to make cards. It has to be individually certified by each payment network and audited to global data-security standards, then empanelled by each bank client. Seshaasai carries the full set:

  • Network certifications — Visa, Mastercard, NPCI (RuPay). Each network audits and licenses the plant and its personalisation processes separately. Winning and keeping all three is a multi-year, ongoing obligation, not a one-time tick.
  • PCI-DSS and card-personalisation security standards. The plants operate as audited secure facilities — controlled access, surveillance, key management, chain-of-custody over blank stock — because they handle live cardholder data. A single serious breach can cost a vendor its certifications and its clients at once.
  • Product breadth as a moat multiplier. Certifying for ordinary chip cards is table stakes; being certified across contactless, metal cards, biometric cards, wearables and payment stickers lets Seshaasai serve a bank's premium and experimental programmes too — the higher-value work that commodity printers cannot touch.

The genuine surprise: printer-like optics, software-like returns

Look at Seshaasai from a distance and it reads as a printing-and-logistics company — plants, plastic, paper, postage. Those businesses usually earn thin, single-digit margins. Yet Seshaasai posts an EBITDA margin near 25% and a return on capital around 28%. The reason is that the value is not in the printing; it is in the permission to print. A certified, empanelled card personaliser is selling trust and compliance with a physical object attached, and trust priced through a regulated bottleneck earns far more than ink and machinery ever could. The optics say "commodity manufacturer." The returns say "licensed gatekeeper." Reconciling those two is the first thing a new investor has to do.

04

The company, its history, and who owns it

A 1993 forms-printer that renamed itself and listed in 2025.

Seshaasai's name changed only recently, and the change tells the story: "Seshaasai Business Forms" became "Seshaasai Technologies" as the company repositioned from a printer of business stationery into a payments-and-communications technology supplier. It is a promoter-run enterprise that stayed private for three decades and reached the public market via a mostly offer-for-sale IPO in September 2025 — meaning the listing was as much about giving founders liquidity as raising fresh capital.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: founder Gautam Sampatraj Jain (with the company since its 1993 inception) and Pragnyat Pravin Lalwani jointly control Seshaasai. The IPO was structured mainly as an offer for sale by these two, so post-listing they retain a controlling majority while the public float is comparatively thin — confirm the exact promoter percentage in the latest shareholding filing.
  • A closely-held, founder-led business. Unlike a professionally-managed conglomerate, this is a company built and still run by its promoters over three decades. That brings deep operating knowledge and continuity, and the usual governance question for any recently-listed promoter company: capital allocation, related-party dealings and board independence deserve ongoing scrutiny given the short public track record.
  • Fresh to the market. With barely a year of listed history, Seshaasai has limited public quarterly disclosure, no long record of guidance-versus-delivery, and a valuation still being discovered. A first-time reader should treat every multiple and trend here as early data, not an established pattern.
Why the "recent IPO, long history" combination matters: the operating business is mature — 32 years old, nationally certified, deeply empanelled — but its life as a public company is only months long. That is an unusual pairing. It means the business risk is relatively well-understood (this is not a pre-revenue story), while the investment risk sits in things a short listed record cannot yet reveal: how the company behaves under quarterly scrutiny, how it allocates the capital it now has access to, and whether the FY25 margin step-up proves durable or flatters a single strong year.
05

The moat: why a bank almost never switches card vendors

The most important structural fact about this company — understand it and the returns make sense.

The central question about any infrastructure supplier is: how hard is it to replace? For Seshaasai the answer is "very," and the reasons are specific and stackable. A bank choosing a card personaliser is not buying a print job; it is handing a vendor its customers' live payment credentials and its own regulatory exposure. Once that trust is granted and audited, moving it is slow, risky and rarely worth the effort — which is why the same clients stay for years.

The four layers of the switching cost

  • Certification. A replacement vendor must already hold Visa, Mastercard and RuPay certifications and PCI-DSS audits. That instantly narrows the field to two or three credible names — you cannot switch to a firm that isn't already licensed.
  • Empanelment & integration. Even among certified vendors, a bank must empanel the supplier, integrate its card-management and data systems, and run security due diligence. This is months of work with no reward except "the same card, from someone new."
  • Operational trust. Card and PIN fulfilment is zero-defect by nature — a mis-mailed PIN or a data leak is a headline and a regulatory event. A bank that has run millions of clean cards through an incumbent is deeply reluctant to gamble that record to save a few basis points.
  • Cross-sold depth. Once statements, mailers, cheque books and digital communication also run through the same vendor, unwinding the relationship means re-tendering several services at once. The bundle is the lock.
The device that only fits a certified personaliser: the moat and the concentration are the same number

Here is the geometry unique to Seshaasai. The very thing that makes the book defensible — a short list of huge, long-tenured bank relationships, ~66% of revenue in the top ten clients, ~84% in BFSI — is the same thing that makes it concentrated. You cannot have one without the other. A vendor that is genuinely hard to switch away from is, by definition, a vendor whose revenue leans on a few sticky accounts. So the "moat" and the "concentration risk" a bear would flag are not two separate facts to weigh against each other; they are a single structural feature seen from two sides. The right question is not "is it concentrated?" (it must be) but "are these the right accounts — large, growing, unlikely to leave?" On a five-year-vintage book of India's biggest card issuers, the answer has so far been yes.

The stakes on the downside: because the moat and the concentration are the same coin, the one scenario that genuinely hurts is losing a top client — not to price, but to a security incident, a certification lapse, or a large bank deciding to in-source. That is low-probability given the track record, but it is the specific failure mode to watch, and it is why a single serious operational breach would matter far more to Seshaasai than to a diversified manufacturer.
06

The phygital pivot: from posting paper to sending pixels

The mix shift that is quietly re-rating the quality of Seshaasai's earnings.

The lazy fear about a "printing" company is that digital will kill it. For Seshaasai the reality is closer to the opposite: the move from paper to pixels is lifting the business, because the company is positioning to deliver the digital version of the very communications it used to print. A statement e-mailed, a transaction alert texted, a policy document sent over WhatsApp — these still need a certified, high-volume intermediary to generate and dispatch them, and they carry better economics than paper because the postage, print and logistics costs fall away while the per-communication fee and the software layer remain.

Each step to the right keeps the trusted-intermediary role but sheds physical cost — which is why "digital eating print" reads, for Seshaasai, as a margin tailwind rather than a threat.

This is not a hypothetical. It is the most plausible explanation for the FY25 result, where revenue fell yet profit and margin rose sharply. When lower-value physical volume is replaced by higher-value digital and personalisation work, the top line can shrink while the bottom line expands — because you are billing less tonnage of paper and more units of trust-plus-software. A pure print business would have seen falling revenue drag profit down with it. Seshaasai saw the reverse, which is the signature of a mix genuinely shifting up the value chain.

Why "digital kills print" gets Seshaasai exactly backwards

The instinct is that a company printing bank statements must be a melting ice cube as banks go paperless. But Seshaasai does not earn from paper existing; it earns from being the trusted party that delivers a bank's regulated communication, whatever the channel. Move that communication from an envelope to an inbox and the bank still needs someone certified to generate it accurately, securely and at billion-message scale — and that someone now keeps the fee without paying the printer, the folder and the postman. The paper was never the asset. The certified pipe between a bank and its customers is the asset, and digital makes that pipe cheaper to run and wider to fill.

07

The annuity behind the plastic: why one card is many payments

The revenue mechanic a first-timer never sees — and the reason FY25 looked the way it did.

Most people picture a card manufacturer's income as a stack of one-time sales: make a card, get paid, move on. That is wrong, and the error hides the best feature of the business. A single card issued through Seshaasai sets off a multi-year chain of billable events tied to that one account — and it is that chain, not the initial card, that explains both the stickiness of the book and the shape of the FY25 numbers.

How does revenue shrink while profit surges by nearly a third? Two mechanics compound. First, the mix climbed the value ladder (section 06): lower-value physical volume gave way to higher-margin personalisation and digital communication, so each rupee of revenue carried more profit. Second, the input-cost and operating environment eased, letting the certified, hard-to-replicate work drop more cleanly to the bottom line. The non-transferable device: a card issued is an annuity opened

Reduce Seshaasai to one sentence and it is this: the company is paid once to make and personalise a card, and then again and again, for years, off the same account. The PIN mailer that follows the card. The monthly statement — printed, then digital. The transaction alerts. The renewal card at expiry, restarting the cycle. Because ~75–87% of revenue comes from clients of over five years' standing, the installed base of cards Seshaasai has already personalised is itself a forward book of communication and re-issuance revenue. That is why the business can afford to let commodity revenue fall away: it is not selling plastic by the tonne, it is opening annuities one card at a time, and harvesting them long after the first sale is booked. Find-replace the company name into any generic printer and the sentence stops being true.

08

The industry and the rivals

A consolidated, certification-gated market with a short list of serious names.

Because the barriers are regulatory rather than merely financial, the Indian card-and-secure-communication market is consolidated among a handful of certified players, not fragmented across hundreds of printers. Seshaasai claims to be one of the top two in card issuance; its rivals split between global card-technology giants and domestic secure-print specialists, with a separate cluster of pure digital-communication challengers coming at the business from the software side.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two dynamics matter. First, on cards, competition is a two-or-three-horse race decided by certification breadth, capacity, security record and price — not a commodity scramble, because the certified field is small and a bank rarely re-tenders lightly. Here Seshaasai's scale and full-stack certification make it structurally hard to dislodge. Second, on digital communication, the contest is wider and newer: nimble software firms can deliver statements and alerts without ever touching plastic. Seshaasai's edge there is the incumbency — it already holds the bank relationship and the regulated-delivery trust — but this is the flank where it must keep proving it can compete on product, not just on being the printer that was already in the building.

09

Financials & valuation

Strong returns and a rich multiple on a very short listed record — the question is durability.

The financial profile is genuinely attractive on a snapshot basis: ~25% EBITDA margins, ~24% return on equity, ~28% return on capital, and FY25 profit up ~31% on a flat-to-down top line. The market has noticed — the stock trades on a low-to-mid-twenties earnings multiple, richer than the "printer" its cost base resembles and closer to a specialty-industrial or platform rating. With only months of listed history, the valuation is being discovered in real time, and the case rests less on the level of the multiple than on whether FY25's margin is a new floor or a favourable peak.

The one number that settles the thesis: the durability of the ~25% EBITDA margin, read with the digital mix

At a low-twenties multiple, Seshaasai is priced as a high-quality compounder, not a cheap cyclical — so the case does not need a re-rating, it needs the margin to hold. The metric that proves or breaks it is therefore EBITDA margin, tracked quarter by quarter and read against the share of revenue coming from personalisation and digital communication rather than commodity print. If that digital-and-value-added mix keeps rising and the ~25% margin proves a floor, the multiple is justified by durable, cash-generative, hard-to-replicate earnings on a structurally growing card base. If FY25's six-point jump turns out to have flattered a single year — an easy input-cost environment, a favourable mix that normalises — then margin reverts toward the high-teens and a low-twenties P/E on those earnings looks generous. Watch the margin and the mix together; on a company this young to the market, one clean year is data, not proof.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A certified, cash-generative infrastructure play — asked whether the margin step-up and the digital pivot both stick.

Seshaasai enters life as a public company with rare qualities for a "manufacturer": a certification-gated moat, a sticky BFSI book, ~25% margins, ~28% returns, and a structural tailwind from a still-expanding card base and a still-formalising economy. What it must now prove, under public scrutiny for the first time, is that the FY25 margin was a floor rather than a peak, that the digital-communication pivot wins share rather than merely defends it, and that a promoter-led company handles its new access to capital and its client concentration with discipline. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Certification moat — a top-two, fully-certified card personaliser is genuinely hard to replace; the ~31.9% share and five-year-vintage book prove the stickiness.
  • Quality of earnings — ~25% EBITDA margin, ~28% ROCE and a mix climbing toward personalisation and digital give it software-like returns on a physical base.
  • Structural demand — a compounding card base, financial inclusion, and re-issuance cycles keep unit volumes rising even in a UPI world.
  • Digital as tailwind — the shift to omni-channel CCM sheds physical cost while keeping the trusted-delivery fee, lifting blended margin.
  • Cross-sell depth — one empanelment supports cards, communication, mailers and RFID; the bundle deepens the lock and widens the wallet.

The bear case

  • Client concentration — ~66% of revenue in ten clients and ~17% in one; the loss of a top bank, to a breach or in-sourcing, would hurt disproportionately.
  • Margin durability — the six-point FY25 EBITDA jump may reflect a benign year; reversion toward the high-teens would leave the multiple exposed.
  • Digital competition — software-native CCM and onboarding firms attack the growth segment on product, where incumbency is a weaker defence than in cards.
  • Working capital & cash conversion — a receivables- and inventory-heavy model can absorb cash in profitable years; FY25 revenue softness was partly working-capital-driven.
  • Short public record & promoter control — barely a year listed, a mostly-OFS IPO, and a founder-controlled float mean governance and capital allocation are still unproven in public.
The bottom line

Seshaasai is a rare thing: a licensed bottleneck wearing a manufacturer's overalls, quietly personalising a third of India's cards and delivering the communications that follow them, at margins and returns a printer has no business earning. The moat is real and the tailwind is structural. The open questions are equally concrete: whether the digital-communication arm can win on software as well as on incumbency, and — the datable test that comes first — whether the ~25% EBITDA margin holds across Seshaasai's first full run of listed quarters as the digital-and-personalisation mix climbs, rather than sliding back toward FY24's high-teens. Clear that bar over the coming four quarters and the low-twenties multiple reads as fair value for durable, sticky infrastructure earnings. Miss it, and FY25 will be reassessed as the good year that a newly-public promoter company put its best foot forward in. The plastic is everywhere; the proof will be in the margin line.
