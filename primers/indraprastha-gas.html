00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Indraprastha Gas Limited (IGL) is the company that pipes natural gas into Delhi's kitchens and pumps it into Delhi's vehicles. It owns and runs the network of steel-and-plastic pipelines, city-gate stations and CNG filling stations that deliver gas across the National Capital Region — and it holds the government-granted exclusive licence to be that supplier in the areas it serves. It is India's largest city gas distribution (CGD) company by volume, a joint venture promoted by state giants GAIL and BPCL, and it sells two things: CNG (compressed natural gas, the fuel in Delhi's autos, taxis, buses and a large chunk of its cars) and PNG (piped gas to homes for cooking, and to shops and factories).

Three facts frame everything:

  • It earns a thin margin on volume, not a price. IGL buys gas and re-sells it; its profit is the spread per unit — the gap between its blended gas-sourcing cost and its retail price, measured in rupees per standard cubic metre (scm) or per kg of CNG. In FY26 that worked out to roughly ₹5.4 of EBITDA per scm across a record 9.39 mmscmd of gas. Grow the volume, protect the spread, and profit grows. That is the whole machine.
  • Its cheap-gas ration is being cut. IGL historically got a quota of low-cost, domestically-produced "APM" gas to run its CNG business. Since October 2024 the government has repeatedly reduced that allocation, forcing IGL to top up with costlier imported gas — squeezing the very spread the business runs on. FY26 profit fell ~7% to ~₹1,364 Cr standalone even as volumes hit a record.
  • Its home market is being electrified. Delhi's EV Policy 2.0 (approved June 2026) targets 95% of new vehicle registrations electric by 2027. IGL's single biggest cash engine is CNG for vehicles — and CNG cars and taxis alone are ~65% of its CNG volume. The capital is, in effect, drawing up plans to phase out IGL's flagship fuel inside IGL's flagship city.
In plain terms

Picture a toll road for gas molecules. IGL doesn't make the gas and it doesn't own the customer's car or stove — it owns the road in between: the pipes and pumps that carry each unit of gas from a national trunk pipeline to the point of use, plus the exclusive right to be the only toll-collector on that road for years. It earns a few rupees on every unit that flows. For two decades that was a wonderful business — a growing city, a captive network, a fat and stable margin, almost no debt, and generous dividends. Two things now cloud it: the government has started rationing the cheap gas that fed the toll road's best-margin lane, and Delhi is legislating to move traffic off the road entirely, onto electricity. IGL is not broken — it still moves more gas each year and mints cash — but its two defining questions are no longer about growth. They are about a fuel allocation it doesn't control and a fuel transition aimed squarely at it.

01

City gas distribution 101: how a CGD utility earns

No engineering needed — just the one spread, per unit, that drives everything.

A city gas distributor is, financially, a regulated margin business on a buried network. It buys natural gas in bulk, moves it through its own last-mile pipes, and sells it in small quantities to millions of end-users. Almost the entire investment case reduces to two numbers: the margin it earns per unit of gas, and the total volume of gas that flows through its network.

The simple profit equation

Roughly: Gross profit ≈ (retail price − gas cost − network cost) × volume, and the bracketed part is the margin per unit. So a CGD utility makes more money in only two ways — a wider per-unit margin (cheaper gas in, or a higher price it can pass on), or more volume (more vehicles filling up, more households and factories connected). The catch that decides IGL's fate right now: it does not fully control either lever. The price of the cheap gas it buys, and how much of it it is allowed to buy, are set by the government. The volume of CNG it sells depends on how many CNG vehicles exist — which Delhi's transport policy is now actively trying to shrink. A utility that once dialled both levers at will is learning that both are being turned by other hands.

Why CGD is usually a lovely business

Set the current squeeze aside for a moment and the structural appeal is real, and worth understanding before we get to the risks:

  • A local monopoly by design. A CGD operator wins a government authorisation for a defined geographic area and is, for a fixed number of years, the only legal seller of piped/compressed gas there. No price war, no rival pump across the road. (How long that lasts is section 05.)
  • A captive, sticky customer base. Once a household's kitchen is plumbed to PNG or a fleet runs on CNG, it doesn't switch supplier — there is only one. Volumes compound quietly as the network extends street by street.
  • An asset-heavy toll booth. The pipes and stations cost a lot to build, but once laid they run for decades at low incremental cost, throwing off cash. IGL has funded this while staying essentially debt-free, and paid steady dividends.
  • A structural tailwind: clean-air policy. Natural gas burns far cleaner than diesel or petrol. For 25 years, tackling Delhi's notorious pollution meant mandating CNG — the Supreme Court's 1998 order that put Delhi's buses and autos on CNG is what created IGL's market in the first place.
02

CNG vs PNG: the product mix that decides the margin

Same gas, three very different customers — and the mix is the margin story.

IGL sells one molecule to three kinds of buyer, and the mix matters intensely, because each segment earns a different margin and each is protected — or exposed — differently by government policy. Read the mix and you understand both where the profit comes from and where the risk sits.

The three customers, and why the mix is delicate

  • CNG (transport) — the volume engine. Sold by the kilogram at filling stations to autos, taxis, buses, light commercial vehicles and private cars. It is the biggest slice of volume and historically the fattest-margin lane, because it was fed with cheap domestic (APM) gas while the pump price tracked liquid fuels. It is also the lane the government is now rationing on gas and the lane Delhi's EV policy targets.
  • PNG domestic — the sticky base. The gas piped to home kitchens. Small in volume (~8%) but enormously sticky and politically protected — like transport CNG, it sits in the government's top-priority bucket for cheap gas allocation. It is a slow, steady grower: IGL keeps adding household connections street by street (well over 2.5 million homes connected).
  • PNG industrial & commercial — the price-taker. Gas to factories, hotels and restaurants, priced closer to market/imported gas and earning a market-linked margin. This is the segment that is not protected in the cheap-gas allocation — the roughly one-third of CGD demand that took a direct cut when APM gas was reduced. Its volume also swings with industrial activity and competes against alternative fuels (furnace oil, propane).
Why "protected" is a double-edged word

The government sorts CGD demand into priority buckets. Domestic PNG and transport CNG sit in the top bucket — they are promised the full cheap-gas allocation and are shielded first. Industrial and commercial PNG sits lower and gets cut first. That protection sounds like a comfort, and in the short run it is. But it also means IGL's growth lane and its protected lane are the same lane — CNG for vehicles — and that is precisely the lane an EV transition attacks. The company's best-margin, most-protected volume is also its most structurally threatened volume. A CGD peer with a heavier industrial and household mix (say, a Gujarat-focused distributor) faces the APM cut harder today but the EV threat softer tomorrow. IGL is the mirror image: shielded on gas today, exposed on the road tomorrow.

From the gas field to your pump — the value chain

Follow one unit of gas through IGL and the toll-booth model clicks.

IGL's value chain is a physical journey down a shrinking pipe. Gas produced far away (or shipped in as LNG) travels along a national trunk pipeline, arrives at the edge of the city, and then enters IGL's own last-mile network — which fans out to filling stations and household meters. IGL owns and operates only the last mile, but that last mile is where the retail margin lives.

Why "who owns the gas" is the quiet crux

Here is a structural quirk worth pausing on, because it explains much of IGL's recent pain. IGL does not own or produce a single molecule of the gas it sells — and its designated monopoly supplier of cheap domestic gas is GAIL, which is also one of its two 22.5% promoters. So the same state entity that co-owns IGL is also its sole cheap-gas supplier and sits inside the machinery that decides how much of that cheap gas IGL is allocated. When the government trims the APM quota, it is IGL's own shareholder-supplier that hands over the reduced ration. For a "monopoly," IGL is unusually dependent on parties it cannot overrule — the toll booth owns the road, but not the fuel that flows down it.

04

The company and who controls it

A court-ordered market, two state promoters, and a Delhi-government minority.

IGL exists because of a court order and is owned by the state twice over. It was created in 1998 as a joint venture between GAIL and BPCL to build out Delhi's gas network, at the exact moment the Supreme Court was ordering the capital's public transport onto CNG to fight air pollution. That clean-air mandate handed IGL a ready-made, legally-required market.

Ownership & control

  • GAIL — ~22.5%. India's state-owned gas transmission and marketing giant; IGL's co-promoter and its authorised sole supplier of domestic gas. What GAIL supplies and at what allocation shapes IGL's margin directly.
  • BPCL — ~22.5%. Bharat Petroleum, the state oil refiner-marketer; the other co-promoter. Its retail fuel outlets also host IGL CNG dispensing points.
  • Combined promoters — ~45%, with the Government of NCT of Delhi holding ~5% more — so the state, in various guises, controls roughly half the company. The rest is a genuine public float (large FII and mutual-fund holdings), which makes IGL far more liquid and institutionally-owned than a typical thin-float PSU.
  • Two arms of the state, sometimes at cross-purposes. The central government (via GAIL and the petroleum ministry) sets gas allocation and pricing; the Delhi government (a shareholder) sets the EV and transport policy that threatens CNG demand. IGL sits at the intersection of two public policies pointing in different directions.
Why the ownership shapes the stock: IGL is majority-influenced by the state but, unlike many PSUs, has a large free float and strong institutional ownership — so it trades on fundamentals and sentiment, not just on a thin float. The double-edge is that its two biggest value drivers — cheap-gas allocation and CNG-vehicle demand — are both set by government bodies, one of which (Delhi) is itself a shareholder. Minority investors own a real, liquid, well-run utility; they just don't own its two most important dials.
05

The licence-monopoly economics — and its slow erosion

A near-monopoly with a clock on it: exclusivity, PNGRB, and open access.

The core of any CGD investment case is the exclusivity the regulator grants — and understanding it means understanding two separate clocks. IGL's monopoly is real, but it is time-bound, and the market has started to price the day the clocks run out.

How a CGD authorisation works — two kinds of exclusivity
Right grantedDurationWhat it protects
Marketing exclusivity5 years (early GAs, to the 8th bidding round); 8 years for GAs won in the 9th–11th rounds, extendable ~2 moreThe right to be the only seller of gas in the area. Once it lapses, rivals can, in principle, sell gas to customers on IGL's network.
Infrastructure exclusivity25 years from authorisationThe right to be the only network builder. Much longer-lived — nobody else lays a competing pipe grid for a generation.
Common carrier / open accessKicks in after marketing exclusivity ends (PNGRB rules, framed 2020)Obliges IGL to let third parties use its pipes for a regulated tariff — turning a monopoly toll road into a shared one.

For IGL's mature core — Delhi and its immediate NCR — marketing exclusivity lapsed years ago. In theory, a competitor could now use PNGRB's open-access rules to sell gas to customers over IGL's own network, paying IGL a regulated transportation tariff. In practice this has been slow and messy to implement, and IGL still functions as the effective supplier. But the threat is structural: as the framework matures, IGL's Delhi monopoly gradually becomes a regulated-network business — it keeps a tariff for carrying the gas, but loses the fatter retail marketing margin on the molecules a rival might sell.

The moat is the pipe, not the price

The durable part of IGL's advantage is the 25-year infrastructure exclusivity and the sheer physical reality of an installed network — nobody is going to dig up NCR to lay a rival grid. What is not durable is the pricing power: as marketing exclusivity expires and open access matures, IGL's ability to earn a full retail margin on every unit erodes toward a regulated carriage tariff. So the right mental model is a utility whose monopoly is gently converting from "sole seller" to "sole road-owner." Being the road-owner is still a good, cash-generative position — but it is a lower-margin, more-regulated one than being the only shop in town, and the transition is a slow, one-directional headwind on the per-unit spread the whole business is measured on.

The regulator holds the pen: the PNGRB sets network tariffs, compression charges, open-access rules and the terms of every authorisation. IGL cannot simply raise its own carriage economics — a regulator can tighten tariffs to pass benefits to consumers. This is a genuine, permanent overhang layered on top of the gas-cost and EV risks: even a perfectly-run CGD monopoly earns only what the regulator lets it keep.
06

The APM allocation squeeze — the risk that already bit

How a government circular, not a lost customer, cut IGL's profit.

This is the defining, non-obvious risk of the CGD business, and IGL has already felt it. To see it clearly, hold one thing fixed: IGL sold more gas in FY26 than ever before — a record 9.39 mmscmd — and yet earned less profit than in FY24. Volumes grew; profit fell. Nothing about demand caused that. A change in which gas IGL is allowed to buy cheaply caused all of it.

What "APM gas" is, and why it mattered so much

"APM" gas is natural gas produced from India's older domestic fields, sold at an administered (government-set) price far below imported LNG — a ceiling around $6.5/mmbtu versus imported gas at roughly $9–12/mmbtu. For years the government allocated enough cheap APM gas to fully cover CGD companies' CNG and domestic-PNG needs. That cheap input, sold at a pump price benchmarked to expensive diesel and petrol, is what made the CNG lane so profitable. It was, in effect, a hidden subsidy flowing straight into IGL's margin.

What changed

  • October 2024: the government cut APM allocation to the CNG segment by around 20% at short notice — the trigger for a ~20% single-session crash in IGL and peer shares.
  • Repeated cuts followed: further reductions over subsequent months, cumulatively a large structural decline in the cheap-gas share of CGD supply, with limited advance policy communication.
  • The replacement is dearer: every unit of lost APM gas must be topped up with imported LNG (or costlier "new well" domestic gas), lifting IGL's blended input cost by an estimated ₹1–2.5 per scm — on a business whose entire EBITDA margin is only ₹5–8/scm.
  • Priority protection cushions, but doesn't cancel: transport CNG and domestic PNG sit in the top priority bucket and are largely shielded; the roughly one-third of CGD volume that is industrial/commercial took the sharper cut. So the damage was real but partial — and IGL, with its high share of priority volume, was seen as among the more exposed to any further cheap-gas erosion.

Why this is the crux, not a footnote

Most utilities can absorb an input-cost shock by raising the price they charge — and IGL can, and did, hike CNG (Delhi crossed ₹83/kg in 2026). But every hike narrows CNG's price advantage over petrol and, increasingly, over the running cost of an EV — the very advantage that drives customers to CNG in the first place. So IGL is caught in a genuine bind that a generalist should learn from this company specifically: eat the higher gas cost and watch the margin fall, or pass it on and watch the fuel's competitiveness fall. There is no third lane. The APM allocation is therefore not a one-off news event but a structural lever the government can pull again — and each pull tightens the same vice. Watch the direction of the APM share in CGD supply and IGL's EBITDA/scm together: that pair, more than any volume figure, tells you whether the vice is loosening or closing.

07

The EV threat — Delhi electrifying its own IGL

The surprise: a stable utility whose flagship fuel is being legislated out of its flagship city.

Here is the fact that surprises most people about IGL. This is not a sleepy, safe utility that happens to have some long-term energy-transition worry in the background. Its single largest, best-margin product — CNG for vehicles — is being directly targeted for phase-out by the government of the very city that is its heartland. The disruption is not a distant global theme; it is a local statute, dated, with milestones.

What Delhi's EV Policy 2.0 actually says

  • Approved June 2026, effective July 2026, with a headline target of 95% of new vehicle registrations being electric by 2027 — an aggressive, near-term goal.
  • New CNG/ICE auto-rickshaws squeezed out: from 1 January 2027, the policy envisages registering only new electric auto-rickshaws and small commercial (N1) vehicles — striking directly at a fleet that is heavily CNG today.
  • Buses and fleets electrified: a push to convert school buses and public transport to electric over the second half of the decade.
  • The exposure: CNG cars and taxis are ~65% of IGL's CNG volume, and CNG is ~76% of total volume — so the policy aims at the core of the core. Analysts (Nomura among them) flagged the policy as structurally negative for IGL specifically, preferring peers less concentrated in an electrifying metro.
Why CNG was a bridge fuel — and bridges get crossed

CNG's entire policy rationale was clean air. It was always positioned as a cleaner-than-diesel bridge from dirty liquid fuels to zero-tailpipe transport. The awkward truth for IGL is that the bridge is now long enough to cross: EVs offer zero tailpipe emissions, not merely lower ones, so for a city whose only reason to favour CNG was pollution, an electric auto is strictly better on the exact metric that created the CNG mandate in 1998. The same clean-air logic that built IGL's market can dismantle it. This is why the threat is more corrosive than ordinary competition: IGL isn't losing customers to a cheaper rival gas seller — it is losing the policy support that made its fuel compulsory in the first place. The near-term earnings hit is modest (fleets turn over slowly, and the existing CNG vehicle base keeps refuelling for years). The long-term question mark over terminal CNG demand in Delhi is the real overhang, and it is what the de-rated valuation in section 09 is largely about.

The real uncertainty: EV targets in India have a history of slipping — charging infrastructure, grid load, purchase costs and political feasibility all bite, and Delhi has signalled it may revise transition timelines (news of which has moved IGL's stock up on the day). So the 2027 "95%" figure is an aspiration, not a delivered outcome, and CNG will remain a large fuel in Delhi for years regardless. But the direction is unambiguous, and direction is what a long-duration utility's valuation trades on.
08

How IGL is fighting back

If the home turf is squeezed on both fuel and demand, the strategy is: go wider, go deeper, go beyond CNG.

IGL is not standing still. Its response to the twin squeeze has three prongs — expand geographically beyond a saturating Delhi, grow the stickier PNG and industrial base, and diversify into adjacent low-carbon energy. Each helps; none is, on its own, a full replacement for the cash CNG throws off today.

The shape of the pivot

Notice the pattern across every row: each diversification move trades margin quality for margin durability. CNG in Delhi was the highest-margin, most-protected, but now most-threatened lane. Everything IGL is pivoting toward — new geographies, household PNG, EV charging, CBG — is lower-margin but less exposed to the two forces closing the vice. That is the correct strategic response, and management is executing it. But investors should size it honestly: this is a utility deliberately trading a fat, shrinking-optionality core for a thinner, more-durable spread of businesses. The volume can keep growing for years; the question that decides the equity return is whether the blended per-unit margin stabilises around management's ₹7–8/scm target as the mix shifts, or drifts lower as the best lane fades faster than the replacements scale.

09

Financials & valuation

A cash-rich, debt-free utility — re-rated down for what might happen, not what has.

IGL's balance sheet is the opposite of the leveraged, cyclical processor that dominates the rest of the oil-and-gas sector. It is essentially debt-free, cash-generative, and pays steady dividends. The financial debate is not about survival or leverage — it is entirely about the margin per unit and the terminal size of the CNG market, and it is why a former market darling now trades at a utility-grade multiple.

Why the multiple fell — a de-rating, not a collapse

For most of the 2010s, IGL was priced as a structural compounder: a high-RoE, debt-free, dividend-paying utility riding a clean-fuel mandate, and it commanded a premium multiple north of 20× earnings to match. That premium has been marked down to the low-to-mid teens — not because earnings crashed (they slipped ~7%, not 70%), but because the market re-rated the durability of the growth. Two questions did it: can IGL protect its per-unit margin as cheap gas is rationed, and how big is the CNG market in Delhi a decade out if the EV policy sticks? A lower multiple on roughly flat earnings is the de-rating — the market paying less for each rupee of profit because it trusts the future rupees less. The offsetting comfort is genuine: a debt-free balance sheet, ~14% RoE, real dividends (₹4.75/share in FY26) and still-growing volume mean this is a cash machine being repriced for slower, more-regulated growth — not a business in distress.

The industry and the rivals

A regulated club of city-gas monopolies — plus the disruptor that sells no gas at all.

IGL's direct competitors are not rivals in its own streets (its authorisation blocks that) but the other listed CGD monopolies in their cities — comparable businesses investors weigh against it — plus its own supplier-parent GAIL, and, most importantly, a competitor that sells electrons instead of molecules.

The IGL-vs-MGL parlour game — and why it misses the point

Brokers endlessly debate IGL versus MGL as near-identical CGD monopolies, and on today's numbers MGL often screens marginally cheaper or higher-margin. But the more useful distinction is mix. Gujarat Gas, with its industrial tilt, feels the APM cut harder now but the EV wave softer later. Adani Total Gas, with its young, spread-out GAs, has more runway but more execution risk. IGL sits at one extreme: the deepest, most-penetrated, most-cash-generative network, but also the one most concentrated in exactly the product (transport CNG) and city (Delhi) where the EV transition is furthest along. Choosing among the CGD names is therefore less a valuation call than a view on which risk you'd rather own — industrial cyclicality, new-area execution, or Delhi's electrification clock.

The sector's shared truth: every listed CGD name is a regulated, near-monopoly utility earning a per-unit spread, and every one now faces the same two structural questions — the cost and availability of domestic gas, and the pace of vehicle electrification. IGL is the purest, largest, and most directly-in-the-crosshairs expression of both. It is the bellwether for how India's city-gas experiment ages.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cash-rich utility whose two most important dials are held by the government.

IGL is a genuinely good business — debt-free, high-return, cash-generative, with a physical network that no rival will rebuild and a volume base that still grows. The entire investment debate is whether that quality can outrun two policy-driven headwinds it cannot control: a rationed cheap-gas supply and an electrifying home market.

The bull case

  • Debt-free cash machine — ~₹4,000 Cr+ cash, ~14% RoE, real dividends; the balance sheet can fund expansion and absorb margin shocks.
  • Volume still grows — record 9.39 mmscmd in FY26; new GAs, PNG connections and CNG-vehicle stock keep throughput rising for years.
  • The pipe is a real moat — 25-year infrastructure exclusivity and an installed NCR network nobody will duplicate.
  • De-rated already — a ~13–15× P/E on a debt-free utility prices in a lot of the APM and EV worry; margin recovery toward ₹7–8/scm would re-rate it.
  • Protected core — transport CNG and domestic PNG sit in the top priority bucket for cheap gas; the sharpest APM cuts hit others first.
  • EV timelines slip — India's electrification targets routinely run late; the existing CNG fleet refuels for years, cushioning near-term volumes.

The bear case

  • Margin dial held by others — APM allocation and pricing are the government's to cut again; each cut lifts gas cost by ₹1–2.5/scm on a ₹5–8/scm margin.
  • Home-market electrification — Delhi EV Policy 2.0 targets 95% electric new registrations by 2027, aimed at CNG-heavy autos and fleets; ~76% of volume is transport CNG.
  • Pass-through has a ceiling — every CNG price hike erodes its advantage over petrol and EVs, the very edge that drives adoption.
  • Exclusivity expiring — Delhi marketing exclusivity has lapsed; open access can turn the monopoly into a lower-margin regulated carrier over time.
  • Regulator-set economics — PNGRB controls tariffs and open-access terms; the state can pass benefits to consumers, capping IGL's spread.
  • Diversification dilutes margin — new geographies, PNG and EV charging are lower-margin than the CNG core they must replace.
The bottom line

IGL is best understood as a high-quality utility being repriced for a lower-quality future — not because it is failing, but because the two things that made it exceptional (a cheap-gas subsidy and a fuel the city was legally required to buy) are both being withdrawn by the state, slowly and deliberately. The one metric that will settle the argument is not volume, which will keep rising, but EBITDA per scm: if IGL can hold the blended per-unit margin near its ₹7–8/scm target as the mix shifts away from premium Delhi CNG, the de-rated multiple looks like a gift on a debt-free compounder; if the spread keeps drifting toward ₹5 and below as cheap gas is rationed and CNG demand plateaus, the market's caution was earned. Two dated milestones will move the needle more than any quarter's results — the next APM allocation review, and 1 January 2027, when Delhi's rule to register only electric autos is meant to take effect. Watch those two dates, and watch that one number per scm; between them they hold IGL's story.
