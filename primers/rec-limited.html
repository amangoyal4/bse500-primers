00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

REC Limited — the letters once stood for Rural Electrification Corporation — is a non-banking finance company (NBFC) that lends to India's power sector and, increasingly, its wider infrastructure. It funds the companies that build power stations, string transmission lines, run the distribution utilities that reach your home, and build solar and wind farms — and now also roads, metros, ports and airports. It is a Maharatna (the top tier of Indian state-owned enterprises), and it is owned ~52.63% by Power Finance Corporation (PFC) — its larger twin — which is itself ~56% owned by the Government of India. So REC is a government-controlled lender one level removed: the state owns PFC, and PFC owns REC.

Four facts frame everything:

  • It is a spread business built on cheap, quasi-sovereign money. REC borrows at low rates because it carries top domestic credit ratings and implicit government backing, then re-lends to power and infrastructure projects at a higher rate. The gap — the "spread" (2.62% in FY26) — is the profit engine, run across a ₹5.84 lakh crore loan book, an all-time high.
  • It just posted a record year — but the near-term optics are noisy. Full-year FY26 net profit hit a record ₹16,282 crore, yet the March quarter's profit actually fell ~21% year-on-year as one-off provision write-backs faded and hedging costs bit. Reading the full-year and the quarter together is essential (§09).
  • Its old bad-loan mess is essentially cleaned up. Net non-performing loans (net stage-3) are down to 0.12% — nearly zero — after years of resolving stressed thermal-power exposures. What was once the central risk is now largely behind it.
  • It's cheap, pays a fat dividend, and is about to be merged into PFC. The stock trades at roughly 6× earnings, ~1.1× book, with a ~4.9% dividend yield — the classic "PSU discount". And in June 2026 the government approved merging REC into PFC at an 88-for-100 share swap, to create a single power-finance behemoth. That merger is the single biggest thing on REC's horizon.
In plain terms

Think of REC as a wholesale bank for India's electricity system, with roots in a very specific national mission: in 1969 the government created it to lend to state electricity boards so farmers could run irrigation pump-sets during a drought. Six decades on it doesn't take your deposits or run branches; instead it raises enormous sums from bond markets — cheaply, because lenders treat it as almost as safe as the government itself — and channels that money into power plants, grids, solar farms and, now, roads and metros. It earns the difference between what it pays to borrow and what it charges to lend. Two things make REC its own story rather than a copy of parent PFC: it is being absorbed into PFC via a share swap, and it is deliberately diversifying beyond power into broader infrastructure — a growth runway PFC is only just beginning to follow.

01

The defining event: REC is merging into PFC

The one thing every REC shareholder must understand right now — read this first.

For most of its life, REC has been a separately-listed company with its own shares, board and strategy — even though its majority owner, PFC, is a near-identical power financier. That is about to change. In June 2026 the government set in motion a plan to merge REC into PFC, collapsing the two into one entity. If you own REC shares, this is no longer a background fact about your parent company — it is the mechanism that will determine what your shares turn into.

What was decided, and when

  • Feb 2026
    The Union Budget 2026–27 flags a restructuring of PFC and REC "to achieve scale and improve efficiency" among public-sector NBFCs — the policy starting gun.
  • 10 Jun 2026
    The Ministry of Power conveys the President of India's approval for the merger (the government, as promoter, must assent before the boards can act).
  • 28 Jun 2026
    The boards of PFC and REC approve the merger scheme: REC is absorbed into PFC under Sections 230–232 of the Companies Act, at a swap ratio of 88 PFC shares for every 100 REC shares — no cash changes hands.
  • FY27→
    Scheme now moves through regulatory and shareholder/creditor approvals, NCLT and RBI. Completion is expected to run into FY27; the final effective date and any tax treatment remain to be confirmed.

What the 88:100 swap means for a REC shareholder

The swap ratio is the heart of the deal. When the merger completes, a REC holder will stop holding REC shares and instead receive PFC shares — 88 of them for every 100 REC shares owned. That number is not arbitrary: it is meant to reflect the relative per-share value of the two companies as assessed by independent valuers. Because REC trades at a somewhat lower price than PFC, each REC share converts into slightly less than one PFC share.

The swap, in one worked example

Say you hold 1,000 REC shares. After the merger, they become 880 PFC shares. Whether that is a good or bad outcome depends entirely on the relative value of a PFC share versus a REC share at the moment of completion — which is exactly why the swap ratio is scrutinised. If the market judges that 88 PFC shares are worth more than 100 REC shares were, REC holders gain; if less, they lose. In practice the ratio was struck to be broadly fair to both sides, so the bigger prize is strategic: REC holders end up owning a slice of a single ~₹11-lakh-crore power-finance giant rather than a subsidiary that always traded at a discount to its parent.

Why the government wants this

  • Scale & cheaper funding. One combined balance sheet of ~₹11 lakh crore can raise money more efficiently in domestic and global markets — potentially at a lower cost of capital than two separate entities, which directly widens the spread that is the whole business.
  • End the duplication. PFC and REC do broadly the same thing — lend to generation, transmission, distribution, renewables — and often chase the same borrowers. Merging removes overlapping teams, systems and competition between two arms of the same owner.
  • A cleaner structure. Today PFC owns 52.63% of REC and consolidates all of REC's book and profit, then strips out the ~47% "minority interest" belonging to REC's other shareholders. The merger removes that leakage and turns a confusing parent-plus-listed-subsidiary into one straightforward company.
  • A single champion for the energy transition. India's power and renewables build-out needs a colossal amount of long-dated debt; the state would rather back one dominant, well-capitalised lender than split its firepower across two.
Why this is the swing factor for REC: the merger converts REC shareholders into PFC shareholders, so from here their return depends on PFC's valuation and on the swap ratio holding. It also removes REC's independent listing — and with it, the specific "REC discount" and REC's own diversification strategy, which get folded into a larger whole. The upside is a simpler, bigger, cheaper-funded entity that could re-rate; the risk is execution, the relative-value fairness of 88:100, tax treatment, and a completion timeline that stretches into FY27. Everything else in this primer — REC's book, its diversification, its dividend — should be read through the lens that REC is, on current plans, a company on its way into PFC.
02

From irrigation pump-sets to a power-finance giant

REC's origin story is unusually specific — and it explains its identity.

Unlike parent PFC (created in 1986 to fund generation), REC was born of a rural crisis. In 1969, after three years of drought, the government needed a way to get electricity to the countryside so farmers could pump groundwater for irrigation. The Rural Electrification Corporation was set up to lend to state electricity boards for exactly that — energising pump-sets and wiring villages. That heritage still defines REC: it is the financier most associated with getting power to the last mile, and with the distribution utilities that serve ordinary consumers.

Timeline

  • 1969
    REC incorporated during a national drought — its mandate: lend to state electricity boards to energise irrigation pump-sets and electrify villages.
  • 1998
    Registered as a Non-Banking Financial Company (NBFC) with the RBI, formalising its role as a specialist power lender.
  • 2008
    IPO / listing on NSE & BSE (BSE code 532955) — the government part-privatises while keeping majority control.
  • 2010
    Awarded Navratna status, recognising strong operational and financial performance.
  • Apr 2018 → FY22
    India reaches 100% village electrification (Apr 2018) and near-universal household electrification (via DDUGJY & Saubhagya) — schemes REC helped finance, fulfilling its founding mission.
  • Mar 2019
    PFC acquires the government's 52.63% stake in REC for ~₹14,500 Cr — REC becomes a subsidiary of its larger power-finance peer.
  • Sep 2022
    REC granted Maharatna status (India's 12th) — and, crucially, is authorised to lend beyond power into non-power infrastructure & logistics (see §06).
  • FY26
    Record year: net profit ₹16,282 Cr; loan book crosses ₹5.84 lakh Cr; net NPA down to 0.12%; highest-ever dividend of ₹18.55.
  • Jun 2026
    Boards approve — with the President's prior assent — a merger of REC into PFC at an 88:100 swap (see §01).

Ownership & control

  • PFC (promoter): ~52.63%. Power Finance Corporation is REC's controlling parent — it consolidates REC's accounts and, pending the merger, will absorb it entirely.
  • The Government of India controls REC indirectly. The state owns ~56% of PFC, and PFC owns ~53% of REC — so REC is a government-controlled Maharatna one step removed, with the associated backing (cheap funding) and constraints (policy direction, dividend demands).
  • Institutions: ~32%. Domestic institutions hold ~16% and foreign institutional investors ~16% — a heavily institution-owned float, reflecting REC's index weight and dividend appeal. Public/retail holds ~15%.
Why the heritage still matters: REC's rural-electrification DNA is the reason its single biggest exposure today is distribution — the utilities that deliver power to homes and farms — rather than generation. It is also why REC is the government's chosen nodal agency for distribution reform (the RDSS scheme, §05). Where PFC grew up funding power stations, REC grew up funding the wires and the last mile. That difference in origin still shows in the shape of the two books.
03

How REC actually earns money

No finance degree required — just the four ideas that drive an NBFC like this.

REC is a lender, not a bank. A bank funds itself mostly with customer deposits; an NBFC like REC funds itself by borrowing in the market — issuing bonds and taking loans — and re-lending that money to big projects. Strip away the jargon and four numbers explain nearly the whole business.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and REC's FY26 number)
Loan book / AUMThe total money it has lent out and is earning interest on: ~₹5.84 lakh crore at March 2026, an all-time high (up ~₹17,000 Cr on the year).
YieldThe average interest rate it charges borrowers — roughly ~10%. This is its top-line income per rupee lent.
Cost of fundsThe average interest rate it pays to borrow. Because it's a top-rated, government-linked PSU, this is unusually low — the heart of its advantage.
Spread / NIMYield minus cost of funds = the spread (2.62% in FY26). A close cousin, net interest margin (NIM, 3.43%), measures the same profit across the whole balance sheet. This gap, multiplied across ₹5.84 lakh crore, is the money machine.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Net interest income ≈ Loan book × Spread. So REC grows profit in three ways — lend more (grow the book), keep the spread wide (borrow cheap, price loans well), and lose less to bad loans (credit costs). Because its borrowers are largely government-linked utilities and its funding is quasi-sovereign, REC runs an enormous book on a thin spread — the opposite of a small consumer NBFC that earns a fat margin on a small book. Its edge is scale plus cheap funding, not high pricing. That's why the single most important thing to watch is the cost of funds relative to the yield.

Why "infrastructure finance" is a different animal

  • Huge, long-dated loans. A power plant, transmission line or metro is financed over 10–25 years in tickets of hundreds or thousands of crores — very different from a car loan or credit card. REC's balance sheet is built to match long assets with long, cheap borrowings.
  • Concentrated, policy-linked borrowers. A large majority of the book is to government-linked utilities — state discoms and central/state power companies — with a minority to private developers. That concentration is a strength (implicit backing, low ultimate losses) and a risk (fortunes tied to state discom finances and government direction).
  • Leverage is the model. REC operates at high debt-to-equity — normal and healthy for a financier — but it means the spread and asset quality must be managed tightly, because a small book-wide problem is amplified across a giant balance sheet.
04

The moat: quasi-sovereign funding & the nodal role

The single most important reason REC earns — kept concise, since the mechanics mirror any PSU financier.

A financier's only real edge is the cost at which it can raise money. Anyone can lend; the winner is whoever can fund most cheaply and reliably. REC borrows almost as cheaply as the Indian government itself — because it is, in effect, the government in commercial clothing. This "quasi-sovereign" funding advantage, plus its official role in national power policy, is what makes REC hard to dislodge.

Where the cheap money comes from

  • Top domestic credit ratings. REC carries the highest domestic long-term ratings (AAA from Indian agencies; its international ratings sit at India's sovereign ceiling). High rating = low borrowing cost, because lenders view a default by a Maharatna as almost unthinkable.
  • Implicit sovereign backing. With the state controlling REC (through PFC), the market prices REC's bonds as if the government stands behind them — not a formal guarantee, but a powerful belief that shaves the cost of every rupee it borrows.
  • Diversified funding channels. REC raises money across domestic bonds (the bulk), rupee term loans from banks and institutions, and foreign-currency / external commercial borrowings (ECBs, a meaningful ~third of the mix). It also issues 54EC capital-gains bonds and tax-free bonds that retail investors buy for tax reasons — a cheap, sticky funding source unique to a handful of PSUs. Its FY26 borrowing programme was sized at ~₹1.7 lakh crore.
Why a thin spread on cheap money beats a fat spread on dear money

Imagine two lenders. Lender A borrows at 7% and lends at 10% — a 3% spread. Lender B borrows at 12% and lends at 16% — a 4% spread. B looks more profitable per rupee, but B can only lend to riskier borrowers (to justify 16%) and its funding can dry up in a crisis. A — that's REC — borrows cheaply and safely, so it can lend safely to giant, low-risk power and infrastructure projects and still make money on a modest spread. Then it does this across ₹5.84 lakh crore. Cheap, dependable funding is the entire game, and it's the one thing a new private rival cannot replicate — a private NBFC can never borrow at a government-linked PSU's rate.

The nodal-agency role — policy power on top of lending

REC is not just a lender; it is an instrument of national power policy. The government routes flagship reform programmes through REC (and PFC) as designated "nodal agencies" — most importantly the Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme (RDSS), a ~₹3.04 lakh crore programme to fix India's loss-making distribution utilities with smart meters and loss-reduction infrastructure. As RDSS nodal agency, REC oversees implementation across 32 DISCOMs in 19 states and union territories — a direct legacy of its rural-electrification role.

Why nodal-agency status is a quiet moat: being the government's chosen channel for RDSS gives REC a first look at a pipeline of state-backed distribution lending, a policy-anchored reason for utilities to borrow from it, and deep relationships with every state discom. It is business a private lender simply cannot access — and it is why distribution is REC's single largest book (§05).

The loan book, decoded

Who REC lends to — and why distribution, not generation, sits at the centre.

REC's ~₹5.84 lakh crore book is spread across the electricity chain — generation, transmission, distribution and renewables — plus a newer non-power infrastructure slice. Its distinguishing feature versus parent PFC is how distribution-heavy it is: a direct inheritance of its rural-electrification history. Understanding the mix tells you where the growth is and where the risk sits.

The segments in plain English

  • Distribution (~40%) — the discoms: the state utilities that deliver power to homes, farms and factories, and collect the bills. REC's biggest book by far, financing everything from network upgrades to the RDSS smart-metering and loss-reduction push. It is also the sector's structural weak link — discoms are chronically loss-making — which is exactly why RDSS exists.
  • Conventional generation (~27%) — money for building power stations: coal (thermal), hydro, nuclear. Historically the source of REC's old bad-loan problem (stalled private thermal projects); still a big slice, but no longer the growth driver.
  • Renewables (~12%) — solar parks, wind farms and storage. The fastest-growing segment (§07), up ~30% in FY26 to ₹75,347 crore.
  • Infrastructure & logistics (~10%) — the newest bucket: roads, metros, ports, airports, healthcare infra. REC's deliberate expansion beyond power (§06).
  • Transmission (~8%) — the high-voltage grid that moves power between states — set to grow as renewables (built where sun and wind are) need more wires to reach demand centres.
Why the borrower mix is both a comfort and a risk

The overwhelming majority of REC's book — especially in distribution, where ~86% is state utilities — is to government-linked borrowers. That is a double-edged fact. On one hand it means very low ultimate losses: a state discom rarely disappears, and the centre repeatedly bails the sector out (through schemes like UDAY and RDSS), so REC's money tends to come back even when a borrower is stressed. On the other hand it ties REC tightly to the finances of loss-making state utilities and to government policy: if a state's power sector wobbles or reforms stall, REC is exposed. The bet REC (and the government) is making is that distribution reform steadily improves discom health — turning REC's biggest concentration from a worry into a compounding, policy-backed lending engine.

06

Beyond power: the diversification into infrastructure & logistics

REC's most distinctive strategic bet — a growth runway that sets it apart from a pure power lender.

Here is where REC's story genuinely diverges from a plain power financier. In 2022, alongside its Maharatna status, REC was authorised by the government to lend beyond power — into non-power infrastructure and logistics: roads and highways, metros and railways, ports, airports, and even healthcare infrastructure. For a company whose whole life had been electricity, this is a strategic pivot: a way to keep growing the loan book even if power lending slows, by tapping India's much larger infrastructure build-out.

What REC is actually funding

  • Roads & highways — India's flagship road-building programme needs enormous long-dated debt; REC can lend to it exactly as it lends to a transmission line.
  • Metros & railways — urban transit and rail projects, long-gestation and capital-hungry.
  • Ports, airports & logistics — the trade and mobility backbone the government is pushing under its national logistics and infrastructure plans.
  • Healthcare & social infrastructure — a smaller, emerging line within the mandate.
The diversification thesis — and its honest risk

The bull thesis: REC's edge — cheap, quasi-sovereign funding and deep experience lending to big, long-dated, government-linked projects — transfers naturally to roads, metros and ports. So diversification lets REC reuse its moat on a much bigger addressable market: infrastructure lending in India dwarfs power. With a mandate to take infra & logistics up to a third of the book, and a target of ~15% by around FY30 (from ~10% today), this is a genuine, incremental growth engine that a pure power lender lacks. The honest risk: these are new sectors for REC, with different borrowers, different project risks, and less of the policy-backstop comfort that a state discom provides. Roads and ports can and do go bad in ways REC has less history underwriting. So the diversification is a real runway if REC keeps its credit discipline as it moves into less-familiar territory — and a source of fresh credit risk if it chases growth too hard. It is the clearest way REC is trying to be more than "power finance."

Merger footnote: REC's diversification is also a template for the merged entity. Once REC folds into PFC, the combined giant inherits this broader infrastructure mandate — so the "beyond power" strategy doesn't disappear with the merger; it scales up.
07

Green finance & the power-capex wave

The multi-year investment supercycle that gives REC something to lend into.

A lender only grows if there is something to finance. REC's biggest structural tailwind is that India is in the middle of a massive, multi-year power-sector investment cycle — building renewables, rebuilding the grid, and fixing distribution — with a sharp tilt toward green energy. That capex must be funded, and REC, as a government-anchored nodal financier, is a natural channel for a large share of it.

Where the demand for REC's money comes from

  • Renewables build-out. Hitting India's 500 GW non-fossil capacity target by 2030 means hundreds of gigawatts of new solar and wind — capital-intensive projects that need long-dated debt. This is the fastest-growing pool of lending demand and squarely in REC's wheelhouse.
  • Grid & transmission. A greener grid needs far more wires — renewables are built where the sun and wind are, not where demand is, so power must be moved long distances. India's transmission plans imply years of financing.
  • Distribution reform (RDSS). The ~₹3.04 lakh crore RDSS scheme funnels smart-metering and loss-reduction spending through REC as nodal agency — a policy-guaranteed pipeline aimed at the sector's weak link.
  • Non-power infrastructure. On top of all of the above, the roads-metros-ports mandate (§06) widens the runway well beyond power.
The tailwind, and its honest limit

The bull case is simple: India must spend enormous sums on power and infrastructure for the next decade, and REC is positioned to fund a big chunk of it cheaply — so the loan book can keep compounding at low-double-digit rates (management has guided ~11–12% growth). The honest limit is twofold. First, competition from banks: when banks are flush with deposits, they undercut PSU financiers on price for the best projects, squeezing the spread. Second, prepayments: cash-rich borrowers repay REC's loans early (REC saw ~₹49,000 crore of prepayments in FY26), which slows net book growth even when fresh disbursements are strong — which is exactly why the book grew only ~3% in FY26 despite ₹2.11 lakh crore of disbursements. The tailwind is real; converting it into net book growth is not automatic.

08

Asset quality: the great thermal clean-up

The turnaround that quietly transformed REC's risk profile — from stressed to near-spotless.

Not long ago, the biggest worry about REC was its bad loans. During the 2010s thermal-power boom, REC (like PFC) lent heavily to private coal-power projects — many of which stalled over fuel shortages, cancelled power-purchase agreements and cost overruns. A chunk of the book went non-performing, and REC had to set aside huge provisions. The FY26 numbers show how far that clean-up has come — REC has, in effect, reached its long-stated goal of near-zero net NPAs.

Why the clean-up flatters recent profit — and why that fades

When old bad loans get resolved — a stalled project is sold, restructured or recovers — REC can write back provisions it had previously set aside, and those write-backs flow straight to profit. This was a real tailwind in recent years. The tell that it is fading is in the numbers: REC's standalone impairment charge collapsed to just ₹201 crore in FY26 from ₹1,019 crore in FY25 — there is simply far less stress left to provide against, and far less to write back. That's genuinely good news (the risk is largely gone), but it carries a warning for the analyst: part of the recent profit strength was one-off in nature. As the stressed book empties out, the write-back sugar-rush ends, and profit growth has to come from the harder work of net-interest-income growth. This is a big part of why the March-2026 quarter's profit fell ~21% year-on-year even as the full year set a record (§09).

The upshot: what was once REC's central risk is now, largely, behind it. The story has shifted from "will the bad loans sink it?" to "how fast can it grow a clean book — and can it keep its credit discipline as it diversifies into new sectors?"

How to think about the valuation

At around ₹370/share and a ~₹97,300 crore market cap on ~₹16,300 Cr of profit, REC trades around 6× earnings and ~1.1× book value, with a ~4.9% dividend yield and a ~20% return on equity. For a lender, the ratio that matters most is price-to-book against ROE: a financier earning ~20% on equity would, in a "normal" market, trade well above 1× book — a private NBFC with those returns might fetch 2–3×. REC's barely-above-book multiple is the market applying a heavy "PSU discount": it prices in government control, policy/direction risk, the thin spread, and lingering memory of the thermal-NPA era. And now there is a new lens — the merger: REC's price increasingly tracks the 88:100 swap and PFC's own valuation, because that is what a REC share is on its way to becoming.

10

The dividend & the PSU-discount debate

Why REC is a favourite of income investors — and why it stays cheap.

Two things define how the market treats REC: it is a high-dividend-yield stock, and it wears a persistent PSU discount. The two are linked — the low price is exactly what makes the yield so high.

The dividend

  • ₹18.55 per share for FY26 (its highest-ever, paid via interims plus a final dividend), against a share price around ₹370 — a dividend yield of ~4.9%, well above the market average.
  • Why the payouts are generous: the government (through PFC, and PFC in turn owned by the state) relies on dividends from PSUs as a budget resource, so there is a structural incentive for steady, sizeable distributions. For a minority investor, that means a dependable income stream on top of any capital gain.
  • A yield and growth story: unusually, REC combines a bond-like ~5% yield with a business still compounding profit — an income stock that also grows, which is rare.

The re-rating debate in one paragraph

Bulls argue REC is a mispriced compounder: a ~20% ROE, a near-spotless balance sheet, a structural power-and-infrastructure capex tailwind, a fat ~5% dividend, and a merger that could ultimately simplify and enlarge the story — all for ~1.1× book. On that view, even a modest narrowing of the discount, on top of the yield and book-value growth, is a strong total return. Bears counter that the discount has persisted for years for good reasons — government control, mandate and concentration risk, and a thin spread — and that "cheap for a reason" PSUs can stay cheap indefinitely, making REC a value trap that pays you to wait but never re-rates. And now the merger clouds it further: REC holders are being converted into PFC holders at 88:100, so REC's own re-rating is being subsumed into PFC's. The truth usually sits in between and moves with the cycle.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, cleaned-up, sovereign-backed lender — now defined by a merger, diversification and the discount.

REC has done the hard part: it climbed out of a bad-loan crisis to a near-spotless book, record profits and a ~20% ROE, sitting at the centre of a multi-year power-and-infrastructure investment cycle with among the cheapest funding in the sector. The investment debate now is about the merger into PFC, whether the spread holds, whether diversification adds growth without adding risk, and whether the market ever pays a fair multiple.

The bull case

  • Cheap, quasi-sovereign funding — a top-rated, government-linked cost of funds no private rival can match; the durable core moat.
  • Near-spotless book — net NPA 0.12%, ~77% coverage, stage-2 down ~75%; the thermal-NPA risk is essentially resolved.
  • Twin capex tailwinds — power/renewables (500 GW target, RDSS) plus a diversification runway into roads, metros and ports (mandate up to 33% of the book).
  • Cheap + high-yield — ~6× earnings, ~1.1× book on a ~20% ROE, with a ~4.9% dividend — a rare income-plus-growth combination.
  • Merger upside — conversion into a simpler, bigger, cheaper-funded ~₹11-lakh-crore giant that could re-rate.

The bear case

  • Merger execution & relative value — the 88:100 swap, tax treatment and FY27 timeline carry uncertainty; REC's return now hinges on PFC's valuation.
  • Faded one-offs — provision write-backs collapsed (impairment charge ₹201 Cr vs ₹1,019 Cr); Q4 FY26 profit fell ~21% YoY — the easy gains are over.
  • Thin, competed spread + prepayments — bank competition and ~₹49,000 Cr of FY26 prepayments held net book growth to ~3%.
  • Concentration & policy risk — heavy exposure to state discoms and government direction.
  • Diversification risk — new infra/logistics sectors carry credit risks REC has less history underwriting.
The bottom line

REC Limited is a cleaned-up, cheaply-priced, sovereign-backed lender with a proud rural-electrification heritage — now on a path to be absorbed into its parent PFC. The business risk is far lower than a decade ago: the book is near-spotless, capital is abundant (CRAR 23%), funding is cheap, and the capex-plus-diversification runway is real. But three live questions now dominate. First, the merger: the 88:100 swap converts REC holders into PFC holders, so from here REC's fate is bound to PFC's. Second, the quality of growth: with write-backs gone and prepayments high, can REC turn strong disbursements into real net book and spread growth. Third, the discount: will a clean record, a fat dividend and a bigger merged entity finally narrow it. Watch a handful of numbers each period — the spread and cost of funds, net loan-book growth, fresh slippages, the renewable and infra-book mix, and above all the merger's progress and swap-ratio fairness. Get the merger and the spread right, and a ~20% ROE lender at ~1.1× book can re-rate while paying you a healthy dividend to wait; get them wrong, and it stays what the market fears — a high-yield PSU that never quite escapes its discount.
