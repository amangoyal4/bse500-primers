00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

CESC Limited is a private, integrated electricity utility — the flagship of the RP-Sanjiv Goenka (RPSG) Group. "Integrated" means it does the whole chain: it generates power in its own plants and distributes it to homes and businesses through wires and meters it owns. Its heartland is Kolkata, where — remarkably for a private company — it has held the exclusive licence to supply electricity for well over a century, across a 567 sq km area serving ~3.6 million customers. Beyond Kolkata it now runs power distribution in Greater Noida and Chandigarh, plus franchise circuits in Rajasthan and Maharashtra — roughly 4.7 million customers in all.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The core is a regulated, annuity-like monopoly. In its licensed areas CESC is the only electricity supplier, and its tariffs are set by a state regulator on a cost-plus basis — it recovers its costs and earns a fixed, assured return on the capital it has sunk into wires, substations and plants. That makes the Kolkata business steady, inflation-protected and low-risk: closer to a toll-collecting infrastructure asset than a competitive company. FY26 consolidated revenue was ~₹18,570 Cr with net profit of ~₹1,618 Cr.
  • The growth story is distribution privatisation + renewables. India's state-owned power distributors ("discoms") lose staggering amounts of electricity to theft and inefficiency, and the government wants private operators to fix them. CESC is one of the very few listed companies with the licence, the balance sheet and the century of operating know-how to bid for those circuits — it has already taken over Chandigarh and runs franchises in Kota, Bharatpur, Bikaner and Malegaon. On top sits a fast-scaling renewables arm, Purvah Green, targeting ~3,200 MW by FY29 and 10,000 MW by FY32.
  • The market prices it as a cheap, safe utility. CESC trades on a ~14× P/E, near 1× book, with a ~3.5% dividend yield — a discount the market attaches to a business whose return is capped by the regulator, whose generation arm carries coal-cost and merchant-power risk, and which is carrying rising debt to fund the renewable and franchise push. The bet is whether the growth engines re-rate a stock that has long been valued like a bond.
In plain terms

Every time a Kolkata household flips a light switch, CESC has already touched that electron twice — once when it made the power in a plant it owns, and again when it delivered the power down wires it owns — and it earns a regulated margin on both. That double grip on one city, held by a private company for over 120 years, is unusual anywhere in the world. The trade-off is that the same regulator who guarantees the margin also caps it: CESC can't get rich quick on Kolkata, only steadily. So the interesting question isn't the old city monopoly — it's whether CESC can copy that operating skill in other cities the government is now handing to private hands, and build a renewable fleet, fast enough to matter.

01

How an integrated utility actually earns

No engineering degree required — just the two halves of the business and why one is far safer than the other.

Electricity travels a chain from where it is made to where it is used. Most companies own just one link. CESC is unusual because it owns the whole thing in its home market — and the money it earns behaves very differently depending on which link you're looking at. Read the chain once and the whole business clicks.

Owning Links 3 & 4 — the wires and the meter — is the defensive prize: it is a natural monopoly (nobody builds a second set of wires down your street), and the regulator lets the owner earn an assured return on it.

The regulated-return model, decoded

For its licensed distribution business, CESC does not compete on price. Instead, the West Bengal Electricity Regulatory Commission (WBERC) — an independent state body — sets the tariff CESC may charge, using a cost-plus formula. The logic is a bargain between monopoly and public interest: because CESC is the only supplier, it can't be allowed to gouge; but because it must invest billions in a reliable grid, it must be allowed a fair, predictable return. So the regulator does the following:

  • It approves a "regulated asset base" (RAB). This is the value of the capital CESC has prudently sunk into the network — plants, wires, substations, meters — essentially the pile of investment the regulator agrees is legitimately in service.
  • It grants an assured return on that base. CESC is allowed to earn a fixed percentage (a regulated return on equity, historically in the mid-teens) on the RAB, plus recovery of its running costs — fuel, wages, depreciation, interest.
  • It passes fuel costs through. When coal prices rise, the extra fuel cost is largely recovered from customers via a fuel-adjustment charge — so the distribution margin is insulated from commodity swings (with a timing lag).
  • The bigger the approved base, the bigger the rupee profit. Because the return is a percentage of the RAB, CESC grows its regulated earnings mainly by investing more capital into an ever-larger, ever-more-reliable network — RAB growth is the quiet compounding engine of a regulated utility.
Why owning the wire beats owning the turbine

A pure generator — a company that only builds power plants — lives or dies on the price it can sell electricity for and the cost of the coal it burns. If it sells into the open ("merchant") market, its profit swings with prices; if coal spikes, its margin is squeezed. A distribution licensee is different: it has a captive base of customers who must buy from it, a tariff guaranteed to cover costs plus a return, and fuel costs it can pass through. That is why the wires-and-meter business is the defensive heart of CESC, and why analysts value the Kolkata licence like a bond, not like a commodity play. The generation arm, by contrast, is the part that carries real cyclical risk — the subject of section 04.

02

The Kolkata monopoly — a 120-year-old annuity

The crown jewel: a whole city's electricity, held by one private firm since the era of gas lamps.

CESC's origin is genuinely singular. It was born in 1897 as The Calcutta Electric Supply Corporation, and on 17 April 1899 it switched on a 1,000-kilowatt coal dynamo at Emambagh Lane to light Calcutta's streets, offices and tramways — making it, on the company's own telling, India's first fully integrated electricity utility. It has supplied the same city, without interruption, ever since. Today that licensed area covers 567 sq km across Kolkata and Howrah, serving ~3.6 million customers — and within it, CESC is the sole electricity supplier. There is no rival wire down the street.

The number that should surprise you: 6.11%

Here is the fact a smart generalist would not guess. India's power-distribution networks are notorious for losing electricity — to technical leakage and to outright theft. These are called T&D losses (transmission & distribution) or, when unpaid bills are added, AT&C losses (aggregate technical & commercial). The national average is roughly 15–17%; many state discoms lose 20–30%+. Every unit lost is a unit CESC bought or generated but never got paid for — pure leakage from profit.

In FY26, CESC's Kolkata network hit an all-time-low T&D loss of 6.11%. That is not just good for India — it rivals the efficiency of developed-world grids. A century-old private monopoly, often caricatured as a sleepy legacy utility, in fact runs one of the most efficient distribution networks in the country. That operating excellence is the real, transferable asset — and it is exactly the skill CESC is now trying to sell to other cities (section 05).

The annuity, in one sentence

A near-fully-efficient network (6.11% loss), a captive base of 3.6 million customers who cannot switch, tariffs set to guarantee a return, and fuel costs passed through — that combination produces slow, dependable, inflation-linked cash flow that recurs year after year. It won't double overnight. But it is about as close to a private-sector annuity as Indian equities offer, and it underwrites the dividend and funds the riskier growth bets everywhere else in the group.

03

The company and who controls it

A colonial-era utility, a 1989 takeover by an industrialist, and a 2018 cleanup few outsiders remember.

CESC is the flagship of the RP-Sanjiv Goenka Group (RPSG), a Kolkata-headquartered conglomerate spanning power, retail, IT services, FMCG, sports and media. The Goenka family took control of the old Calcutta Electric Supply Corporation in 1989; the group is chaired by Sanjiv Goenka, with son Shashwat Goenka in senior leadership. Promoters hold ~52.1% — a firm controlling stake in a business the family regards as its cornerstone asset.

The surprise hiding in the share register

If you had bought "CESC" a decade ago, you were not buying a pure power company. Until the 2018 restructuring, the same listed entity had absorbed a grab-bag of RPSG ventures — the Spencer's Retail supermarket chain, the Firstsource Solutions IT/BPO business, and other group interests — bolted onto the Kolkata power utility. The 2018 demerger untangled all of it, floating those businesses as their own companies and leaving CESC Ltd as a focused, easier-to-value electricity utility. So the "boring Kolkata power stock" is, in fact, the deliberately cleaned-up remainder of a once-sprawling holding structure — worth knowing when you read older commentary that still lumps CESC in with the group's retail or services arms.

04

The generation fleet — and its cyclical edge

The other half of "integrated": ~2,140 MW of coal plants that make the power the wires carry.

CESC's own power stations supply much of the electricity its distribution business needs, with the balance bought from the grid. The fleet is predominantly coal-fired thermal, ~2,140 MW in total, split between West Bengal (feeding Kolkata) and a merchant-oriented plant in Maharashtra. Understanding this arm matters because it is where CESC's cyclical risk lives — the part not fully wrapped in the regulated-return blanket.

Regulated tied-in generation vs merchant generation

  • Tied-in (regulated) generation. The Budge Budge, Haldia and in-city stations largely feed CESC's own Kolkata distribution under a regulated framework. Their output is effectively sold into a captive, cost-plus system — so these plants share the annuity's stability, and their fuel costs are broadly recoverable through the tariff.
  • Merchant / third-party generation. The Dhariwal plant in Maharashtra is the more exposed asset — its economics depend on securing power-purchase agreements or selling into the merchant market at prevailing prices. When demand and market tariffs are firm, it earns well; when they slacken or coal costs spike faster than it can recover them, it is the weak link.
Why coal is the swing factor: a thermal fleet's biggest variable cost is coal, and India's coal supply and prices are volatile (domestic linkage shortfalls push companies to costly imports or e-auction coal). Within the regulated Kolkata business, fuel-cost pass-through cushions this. Outside it — for merchant volumes — a coal spike lands straight on the margin. So CESC's generation profit is steadier than a pure IPP's but bumpier than its distribution arm's: a middle-risk layer between the annuity and the open market.
05

The distribution-franchise engine

The growth story: exporting Kolkata's operating skill to India's leaky, half-privatised discoms.

India's biggest, most intractable power problem is not making electricity — it is delivering it. State-run discoms lose a fortune to theft, unmetered supply and unpaid bills, and many are chronically loss-making. Successive governments have pushed to hand these networks to competent private operators. CESC — with a licence, a fortress balance sheet and 120 years of running the country's most efficient grid — is one of a tiny handful of companies positioned to answer that call. This is the clearest growth lever it has, and it comes in two flavours.

Two ways CESC operates outside Kolkata

  • The distribution licence (own the area outright). Here CESC — or a subsidiary — is the licensed distributor, like in Kolkata. Examples: Noida Power Company (NPCL) in Greater Noida, and Chandigarh (CPDL), which CESC privatised and took over in February 2025. These earn a full regulated return on their asset base.
  • The distribution franchise (run someone else's area for a margin). Here the state discom still holds the licence, but hands day-to-day operation to CESC under a contract: CESC buys power at an agreed "input rate", runs the network, collects the bills, and keeps the difference. Its profit comes from reducing losses and improving collection — every unit of theft it stamps out becomes a unit it now bills. Examples: Kota, Bharatpur and Bikaner in Rajasthan, and Malegaon in Maharashtra.

The loss-curve arbitrage — CESC's signature trick

Line the operations up by their loss rate and the entire growth thesis appears as a single spectrum. Kolkata at 6.11% is a finished annuity — there is almost nothing left to squeeze, so it simply compounds with the asset base. Chandigarh at 8.3% and Rajasthan at 11.4% are mid-journey — each percentage point of loss recovered drops close to pure profit. Malegaon at 36.3% is raw material — roughly thirty percentage points of theoretical upside, if CESC can repeat on the ground what it perfected on the Hooghly over a century. Same company, same skill, deployed at opposite ends of the same loss curve. The value CESC creates outside Kolkata is, quite literally, the distance between a circuit's starting loss and 6% — and its ability to walk that distance is the one capability that makes the whole expansion strategy investable.

But the catch is real: turning around a high-loss circuit means confronting entrenched theft, weak local enforcement, political sensitivity around cutting off non-payers, and the risk that the input rate you agreed leaves too little margin. Malegaon — still bleeding at 36% after years — is the standing proof that the arbitrage is available but not automatic. Some franchises elsewhere in India (run by peers) have been handed back. Execution, not opportunity, is the binding constraint.
06

The renewable pivot — Purvah Green

A coal-heavy utility building a second, green generation fleet — the decarbonisation growth bet.

CESC's generation today is almost entirely coal. That is a liability in an India racing toward its renewable-energy targets, and an opportunity for a utility that already knows how to sell power. Through its arm Purvah Green Power, CESC is building a solar-and-wind fleet from a standing start — the third leg of the growth story, alongside the regulated core and the distribution-franchise engine.

  • What's being built. Purvah has secured a mix of solar, wind and hybrid projects — including a 300 MW hybrid and a 250 MW wind mandate — while a 300 MW solar project tied to CESC Kolkata's own needs is in the commissioning stage.
  • Why it fits. A utility that already distributes power to 4.7 million customers is a natural home for green generation: it can feed its own regulated demand, sign long-term PPAs, and use renewables to meet the renewable-purchase obligations that discoms are legally required to hit.
  • Why it's risky. Scaling from 300 MW to 3,200 MW in ~2.5 years is enormous capex on a balance sheet already carrying meaningful debt (section 09). Renewable projects also bring their own hazards — land, evacuation/transmission bottlenecks, module prices, and tariff-bid discipline.
The strategic logic in one line

CESC is trying to become a full-stack modern utility: a regulated wires-and-meter monopoly at the core, a distribution-turnaround business for growth, and a green generation fleet to decarbonise its supply and win new long-term contracts. Each leg leans on the same underlying asset — a century of knowing how to build, run and bill an electricity network — but each adds a different risk. The green leg swaps coal-price and merchant risk for execution-and-capex risk on a very steep ramp.

07

The industry and the rivals

Where CESC sits in India's power map — and who else is chasing the distribution-privatisation prize.

India's power sector is split by function. In generation, giants like NTPC dominate; in distribution, most of the country is still served by loss-making state discoms. CESC's rare position is that it does both, privately, with a licensed monopoly at its core. Its truest peer set is the small club of private companies with real integrated generation-plus-distribution footprints and the appetite to take over more discoms.

The theme, and why CESC is a rare pure-play on it

The structural story to grasp is distribution privatisation: India loses so much money in state discoms that reform is a national priority, and the direction of travel is toward handing circuits to private operators — by licence, franchise or smart-metering contract. Very few listed companies are credible bidders. Tata Power is the large-cap way to play it; Torrent is the mid-cap analogue; CESC is the concentrated, distribution-heavy, value-priced expression of the same idea, with the longest single-market track record of them all. If privatisation accelerates, this small club shares a very large opportunity — and CESC's Kolkata scorecard (6.11% loss) is its best sales brochure.

The regulatory backdrop: everything CESC earns on its regulated businesses ultimately depends on state commissions (WBERC in Bengal, and counterparts elsewhere) approving fair tariffs and true-ups on time. Tariff hikes are politically sensitive — West Bengal has seen public protests over CESC increases — so the regulator sits between the company's returns and the electorate's bills. That tension is a permanent feature, not a passing risk.

Why the market prices it like a bond

At a ~14× P/E, roughly 1× book value and a ~3.5% dividend yield, CESC is one of the cheaper names in Indian power — and the reasons are logical, not a mistake. A regulated utility's return is capped by design: WBERC hands CESC a fair return, not a spectacular one, so the core can't compound the way an unregulated growth company can. Layer on the coal/merchant cyclicality of the generation arm, the receivables and turnaround risk in franchises, and rising debt to fund the green build-out, and the market applies a discount for "low growth with real, if manageable, risks." That is the classic utility bargain: you are paid a steady dividend to wait, and the upside is a re-rating if — and only if — the growth engines prove they can lift the trend rate of profit.

09

Debt and the regulated ceiling

The two facts that keep CESC cheap — and the one metric that would change the story.

To understand why a business this stable trades so modestly, hold two facts together. First, the ceiling: the regulated core cannot earn more than the commission allows, so no matter how well CESC runs Kolkata, the licensed return is bounded. Second, the leverage: building plants, buying discoms and scaling renewables all cost capital, and CESC's borrowings have been climbing — total debt in the order of ~₹17,000–18,000 Cr, a net-debt-to-EBITDA around ~4× and a debt-to-equity well above 1×. Rising finance costs are a real drag on consolidated profit, and were flagged by commentators alongside the FY26 results.

  • The ceiling caps the core. A mid-teens regulated return on equity is attractive and dependable — but it is a ceiling. Growth in regulated profit comes mainly from growing the approved asset base (more capex, more RAB), which is slow and itself debt-funded.
  • The leverage funds the growth. The very things that could re-rate the stock — Chandigarh, more privatisations, 3,200 MW of renewables — require heavy upfront investment, so debt and capex rise before the returns show up. That timing gap is what the low multiple is pricing.
  • Receivables and regulatory lag bite. Distribution utilities are chronically exposed to slow-paying customers and to the delay between incurring a cost and being allowed to recover it in tariff. Working capital, not the income statement, is where a discom's stress usually surfaces first.
The one metric that settles the thesis

Strip everything else away and CESC comes down to a single question: how fast can it convert acquired, high-loss circuits into Kolkata-grade, low-loss, profitable ones — and turn that into growing distribution EBITDA — while the renewable megawatts actually come online? Watch the AT&C-loss trajectory of Chandigarh, Malegaon, Rajasthan and any new privatisation win (each point recovered is near-pure margin), alongside the regulated asset base growth in Kolkata/Noida and the Purvah Green MW commissioned toward 3,200 MW by FY29. If those loss curves keep bending toward 6% and the green ramp lands close to schedule, the "bond-like utility" quietly becomes a growth utility, and the discount narrows. If Malegaon-style stalls become the norm and debt outruns delivery, the market is right to keep it cheap. That is the whole case, honestly stated.

  • Group / related-party history. As a family-controlled conglomerate flagship, capital-allocation and related-party governance always warrant scrutiny — though the 2018 demerger meaningfully simplified the structure.
  • Renewable-ramp risk. Land, transmission-evacuation bottlenecks, module prices and bid discipline can all slow the Purvah Green build toward its 3,200 MW / 10,000 MW targets.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A dependable annuity with two growth engines bolted on — valued as if only the annuity is real.

CESC is, at its core, one of the most durable cash machines in Indian equities: a private monopoly over a whole city's electricity, run with world-class efficiency, paying a real dividend. The investment debate is not about whether the core is safe — it is about whether the distribution-franchise and renewable engines can lift a low-growth, capped-return utility into something the market will pay more for.

The bull case

  • An irreplaceable core — a 120-year city monopoly at a 6.11% loss rate, throwing off steady, inflation-linked, regulator-guaranteed cash.
  • A rare pure-play on distribution privatisation — the licence, balance sheet and track record to keep winning circuits (Chandigarh proves it works).
  • The loss-curve arbitrage — franchises like Rajasthan and Malegaon offer points of loss to recover, each near-pure margin.
  • A renewable option — Purvah Green's 3,200 MW (FY29) / 10,000 MW (FY32) ramp adds a growth leg on a decarbonising grid.
  • Cheap with a cushion — ~14× P/E, ~1× book, ~3.5% yield: you're paid to wait for the re-rating.

The bear case

  • A capped core — the regulated return is a ceiling; the annuity can't compound like a growth company.
  • Rising debt — ~4× net debt/EBITDA and climbing finance costs to fund capex-heavy ambitions.
  • Turnarounds are hard — Malegaon still at 36% is proof the franchise arbitrage isn't automatic.
  • Coal & merchant drag — the unregulated generation slice adds cyclicality the wires business doesn't have.
  • Regulatory & political overhang — tariff decisions are contested, and a cheap stock can stay cheap if growth keeps slipping to "next year".
The bottom line

CESC began in 1899 by lighting Calcutta's streets from a single 1,000-kilowatt coal dynamo; in FY26 it is pointing at a 10,000-megawatt green fleet by FY32 — a hundred-thousand-fold arc from the same operating DNA of building, running and billing an electricity network. Between here and there sit two concrete gates that will tell you whether the "bond" is turning into a "grower": the Purvah Green march to 3,200 MW by FY29, and the next state-discom privatisation mandate CESC wins and drags toward 6% loss. Hit those, and a stock valued like an annuity starts to be re-rated for growth; miss them, and CESC remains exactly what it has always been — a slow, safe, dividend-paying utility that quietly powers a city, and asks the market for very little in return.
