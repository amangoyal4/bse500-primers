Axis Bank, the third private bank trying to close the gap

A complete walk through India's third-largest private-sector bank — how a bank actually earns money (NIM, CASA, GNPA, provisioning in plain English), the long, hard asset-quality turnaround behind the "GPS" risk-recalibration, the cheap-deposit gap that holds its margins below HDFC and ICICI, the ₹11,603-crore Citibank India consumer acquisition that bought it premium cards and wealth, and why a clean, professionally-run, no-single-promoter bank still trades at a ~30% discount to its two larger rivals.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Axis Bank is India's third-largest private-sector bank by assets — behind HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank, and ahead of Kotak Mahindra. It does the full sweep of banking: takes deposits from households and companies, makes loans (retail, SME and corporate), runs a big credit-card and payments franchise, and earns fees from cards, transactions, wealth and treasury. It carries roughly ₹13.4 lakh crore of deposits and ₹12.3 lakh crore of advances.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A genuine turnaround, finally clean. A decade ago Axis was the private bank with the scariest hidden bad-loan problem; today its gross NPA is just 1.23% and net NPA 0.37% — the cleanest book in roughly ten years. The risk culture was rebuilt deliberately under the "GPS" programme. The hard part is now broadly done.
  • But it earns less than the leaders — and the market knows it. Axis's net interest margin (~3.6%) and return on equity (~13% in FY26) sit below ICICI and HDFC, mostly because its share of cheap "CASA" deposits is lower and its funding costs higher. That single gap is why the stock trades at roughly a 30% valuation discount to its two big rivals — and why closing it is the whole re-rating debate.
  • FY26 profit actually fell ~7%. Standalone net profit slipped to ₹24,457 Cr (from ₹26,373 Cr), not because the business shrank — income grew — but because the bank front-loaded provisions, including a one-time ₹2,001 Cr buffer, after the RBI flagged that its bad-loan recognition in unsecured retail had been too lenient. Income up, profit down: a "cleaning year".
In plain terms

Think of Axis as the capable, much-improved third sibling in Indian private banking. It has rebuilt its credit discipline so its loan book is now as clean as anyone's, it bought Citibank's Indian card-and-wealth customers to punch up-market, and it has four valuable financial businesses (an NBFC, an asset manager, a broker and an investment bank) hanging off the side. What it still doesn't have is the cheap, sticky deposit base that lets HDFC and ICICI fund loans more cheaply and earn fatter margins. The investment question is simple to state and hard to answer: can Axis keep its book clean and close the funding-cost gap fast enough to deserve a re-rating toward its peers — or is the discount permanent?

01

How a bank earns: the five numbers that matter

No finance degree required — just five terms that explain almost everything.

A bank is, at heart, a spread business. It borrows money cheaply (mostly your deposits) and lends it out at a higher rate; the difference is its core income. Layered on top are fees, and subtracted off are loan losses and running costs. Five terms tell you almost the whole story — and they're the same five you'll use to compare Axis with HDFC or ICICI.

The only banking vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and Axis's number)
NII & NIMNet Interest Income = interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits. Net Interest Margin expresses that as a % of assets — the bank's core "spread". Axis's NIM was ~3.62% in Q4 FY26 — solid, but below ICICI/HDFC.
CASACurrent Account & Savings Account deposits — the cheapest money a bank can get (current accounts pay no interest, savings very little). A high CASA share lowers funding cost and lifts NIM. Axis ran a ~40% CASA ratio at Mar 2026 — its known relative weak spot.
GNPA / NNPAGross / Net Non-Performing Assets — loans the borrower has stopped repaying, as a % of the loan book (net = after setting money aside against them). The single best gauge of lending discipline. Axis: 1.23% gross / 0.37% net at Mar 2026.
Provisioning & credit costProvisions are money set aside for loans that may go bad; credit cost is provisions as a % of loans. High provisions protect the balance sheet but eat into this year's profit. Axis's FY26 net credit cost was ~0.37% — low, though FY26 profit was dented by extra buffer provisions.
ROA / ROEReturn on Assets / Equity — profit as a % of the balance sheet / of shareholders' money. The scoreboard for how well it all comes together. Axis ran around ~1.4% ROA / ~13% ROE in FY26 (depressed by the extra provisions) — a step below ICICI's ~2% / ~18%.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (NII + Fees) − Operating costs − Provisions − Tax. So a bank gets better in four ways — lend more (grow advances), fund cheaper (more CASA → wider NIM), lose less (lower credit cost), and run leaner (lower cost-to-income). The catch unique to banking is the time-bomb quality of the third lever: a bank can look wonderfully profitable for years by lending loosely, right up until those loans default and provisions devour the profit. That's why asset quality is the truest test of a lender — and why Axis's whole story is, at bottom, about credibility on bad loans.

Retail vs corporate — two engines, different risk

  • Retail lending — home loans, personal loans, credit cards, vehicle and small-business loans to individuals. Higher-yielding (so good for NIM), more granular (one default doesn't sink you), but the unsecured slice (personal loans, cards) is where losses spike when households are stretched. This is exactly where Axis got into trouble in FY26.
  • Corporate & SME lending — loans to companies. Lower-yielding and "lumpier" (a few big defaults can hurt badly — this is what wrecked Axis in 2018), but also where a single good relationship brings deposits, salary accounts and fee business.
02

The business model: one balance sheet, four profit engines

How the pieces connect — read this once and the strategy clicks.

Axis isn't just a lender. It is built so that a single customer relationship can be monetised four ways — through the core spread, through fees, through cheap deposits, and through its subsidiaries. Management calls this joined-up model "One Axis": the bank up front, with an NBFC, an asset manager, a broker and an investment bank arranged so the group can serve a customer end-to-end and keep more of the value in-house.

Why the deposit franchise is the foundation, not a detail

Every bank can lend; the scarce, durable edge is cheap, sticky deposits. A current account holder who never moves their salary, GST and vendor payments away from you is funding your loans at near-zero cost — and they're hard for a rival to poach. HDFC and ICICI spent decades building exactly this; it's the bedrock of their fat margins. Axis has a strong deposit base in absolute terms (~₹13.4 lakh crore) but a thinner low-cost share than the leaders, so it pays up more for funding. That's not a cosmetic gap — it flows straight through to a lower NIM, lower ROE, and ultimately a lower valuation. Closing it is the single most valuable thing Axis can do.

03

The company and who controls it

A state-promoted bank that became a professionally-run, no-single-owner franchise.

Axis Bank has an unusual ownership story for a private bank: it was promoted in 1993 by a cluster of state-linked financial institutions — UTI (later SUUTI), LIC and several public-sector insurers — not by a family or an entrepreneur. Over thirty years those founders steadily sold down, leaving a bank with no controlling promoter, run by professional management and owned mostly by institutions. It was called UTI Bank until it rebranded to Axis Bank in 2007.

Ownership & management

  • No single promoter. After SUUTI's exit, LIC (~7.9%) is the only entity still classified as a "promoter", and even that is a legacy label rather than control. The bank is genuinely institution-and-management-led.
  • Ownership is institution-heavy. Roughly ~41% foreign institutional investors and a similar weight of Indian institutions (mutual funds, insurers) — among the most foreign-owned large Indian banks, which makes it sensitive to global EM flows.
  • Professional leadership. Amitabh Chaudhry (MD & CEO since Jan 2019) is the architect of the turnaround; the absence of a dominant promoter means the board and management — not a family — set strategy. A clean governance story, and part of why the franchise is trusted today.
04

The GPS turnaround: how Axis rebuilt its risk culture

The defining chapter — and the thing the whole investment case rests on.

To understand Axis you have to understand 2018. For years it had grown a corporate loan book aggressively; when the cycle turned, a frightening share of those loans went bad. Gross NPAs spiked toward the mid-single digits, profits collapsed under provisioning, and the market lost faith that Axis even knew how bad its book really was. The next seven years are the story of fixing that — and it is the single most important context for every number on this page.

The "GPS" reset

When Amitabh Chaudhry took over in January 2019, he framed the rebuild around three words — Growth, Profitability, Sustainability ("GPS") — but the real work was cultural: changing how the bank decided who to lend to and when to call a loan bad. The concrete moves:

  • Recalibrate underwriting. Tighten corporate credit, shift the book toward better-rated borrowers and granular retail, and stop chasing risky growth.
  • Over-provide, deliberately. Build provision coverage well above the minimum and carry large non-NPA buffer provisions (over ₹11,000 Cr) so a future shock hits a pre-funded cushion, not next year's profit.
  • Recognise pain early. The cultural shift from hiding stress to flagging it fast — the opposite of the pre-2018 instinct.
  • Rebuild deposits & digital. Grow the liability franchise and the digital bank so growth could be funded soundly rather than borrowed expensively.

Why this still matters even though it's "fixed"

Two reasons. First, credibility is the asset: a bank's valuation is partly a bet on whether you can trust its reported numbers, and Axis spent the post-2018 years earning that trust back. Second — and this is the FY26 twist — the journey isn't fully over. In FY26 the RBI flagged that Axis's internal rules for recognising bad loans in unsecured retail (personal loans, cards) had been too lenient. Axis responded the "new culture" way: it raised provisions, took the profit hit, and cleaned up — rather than arguing. That's reassuring on culture, but it's also a reminder that the risk-recalibration is a permanent discipline, not a one-time project. The market watches every quarter to confirm the new Axis is real.

05

Asset quality today — clean book, but a retail wobble

The single most important set of numbers for any lender.

On the headline gauges, Axis's book is now genuinely clean: gross NPA 1.23% and net NPA 0.37% at March 2026 — the best in roughly a decade and comparable to ICICI. But FY26 carried an asterisk: a flare-up in unsecured retail slippages and an RBI nudge on recognition forced extra provisioning, which is exactly why profit fell even as the ratios improved.

Two things make this more than a snapshot. First, the net NPA of 0.37% means almost all recognised bad loans are already covered — there's little hidden damage waiting to surface. Second, the buffer (non-NPA) provisions of over ₹11,000 crore mean a future credit shock would land on a pre-funded cushion rather than on future profit. Both are direct legacies of the GPS over-provisioning discipline.

The FY26 nuance to hold in mind: the clean ratios coexisted with a real retail wobble. Fresh slippages jumped (Q1 FY26 alone saw ~₹8,200 Cr, up ~71% YoY) as the bank tightened recognition on unsecured loans following the RBI's observations, and FY26 provisions rose to ₹13,263 Cr (including a one-time ₹2,001 Cr standard-asset buffer). Management framed it as prudence, not panic — and credit cost still ended low (~0.37%). The thing to track each quarter: are retail slippages normalising back down, or is unsecured stress widening?
"Slippages", in plain English

A slippage is a loan that "slips" from performing into the bad-loan bucket during the quarter — the inflow of new problems, before any recoveries or write-offs. Watching slippages is more forward-looking than watching the GNPA level, because the level can fall (via write-offs) even while fresh problems are still arriving. Axis's FY26 message was a high slippage inflow concentrated in unsecured retail, offset by heavy provisioning and write-offs — so the stock of bad loans fell to a decade low even as the flow of new ones ran hot. Bulls read that as conservative cleaning; bears worry the flow signals genuine household stress.

06

The CASA & deposit-cost gap — Axis's defining weakness

The structural force unique to Axis's investment case — its "China question".

If there is one number that explains why Axis trades below HDFC and ICICI, it is CASA — the share of deposits sitting in low- or no-interest current and savings accounts. Cheap CASA is the raw material of a fat margin: the more of it you have, the less you pay to fund your loans, and the wider your NIM. Axis's CASA share (~40%) and, crucially, its cost of deposits sit a step behind the two leaders — and that gap flows straight through to lower margins, lower returns, and a lower valuation.

Approximate; margins move with the rate cycle and each bank reports on slightly different bases. The structural point: Axis's NIM clusters with HDFC and below ICICI — and a meaningful part of the gap to ICICI is funding cost, i.e. CASA.

Worse, FY26 was a headwind year for everyone's margin: as the RBI cut rates, loan yields repriced down faster than deposit costs, and competition for deposits stayed fierce (Axis even raised FCNR deposit rates to ~6% mid-2026 to attract funds). Axis's NIM compressed from ~3.97% a year earlier to 3.62% — a reminder that, with a thinner cheap-deposit cushion, Axis feels rate and deposit-competition pressure more than the leaders do.

Why this single gap is the whole valuation debate

Run the chain: lower CASA → higher funding cost → lower NIM → lower ROE → lower P/B multiple. Every link is real and well-understood, which is why the market prices Axis at a discount to peers almost mechanically. The bull case is that this is fixable: the Citibank acquisition brought a wealthier, stickier customer base; the digital and salary-account push is deepening; and even a modest lift in CASA share or a fall in deposit cost would widen NIM and lift ROE toward the leaders — at which point the discount should compress. The bear case is that cheap deposits are the hardest thing in banking to build, the leaders aren't standing still, and the gap has proved stubborn for years. Watch CASA ratio and cost-of-deposits each quarter — they are the tell on whether the re-rating thesis is working.

The honest tension: Axis is a clean, well-run bank whose only serious structural shortfall versus HDFC/ICICI is the cost and stickiness of its funding. That makes it a focused bet: you are essentially wagering on whether management can narrow the deposit-franchise gap. Get it, and the valuation discount is a coiled spring; miss it, and a fairly-cheap bank can stay fairly-cheap for a long time.
07

The Citibank India acquisition — buying its way up-market

A once-in-a-generation deal to leapfrog into premium cards and wealth.

In March 2023 Axis completed one of Indian banking's most consequential acquisitions: it bought Citibank India's entire consumer business for ₹11,603 crore in cash. In a single stroke it acquired a base of affluent, urban, premium customers that would have taken a decade and enormous marketing spend to build organically — and crucially, customers who skew exactly toward the high-CASA, high-fee, high-wealth profile Axis most needs.

The integration — the hard, expensive part

Buying the business was the easy half; integrating it — migrating 2.4 million customers onto Axis's technology, retaining premium clients who could defect, and merging Citi's NBFC arm (Citicorp Finance) — was a multi-year, ~₹1,500 Cr undertaking that ran through FY24–FY25. By FY26 the integration was substantially complete, and the deal had become earnings- and ROE-accretive, validating the price.

Why this deal is so well-targeted

Look back at Section 06: Axis's core weakness is a thinner cheap-deposit and affluent franchise than HDFC/ICICI. The Citibank book is almost a precision-engineered fix for that exact gap — it delivered low-cost deposits, premium cards (high fee income), and a large wealth pool (Burgundy) in one move. The risk was always retention and integration: premium customers are fickle, and a botched migration could have bled the very clients Axis paid up for. That it came through largely intact and accretive is one of the genuine successes of the Chaudhry era — and a real, if partial, answer to the CASA question.

What to watch: the deal's lasting value depends on cross-sell — turning ex-Citi cardholders into Axis wealth, deposit and loan customers (and vice-versa). The KPI is whether those acquired relationships deepen over time or merely hold. Early signs (rising Burgundy AUM, card-spend share) are encouraging; the multi-year payoff is the thing to keep tracking.

Premium cards & Burgundy wealth — the affluent franchise

Where Axis competes on brand and service, not just rate.

Two capital-light, high-return businesses sit at the heart of Axis's up-market push: a large credit-card franchise and the Burgundy wealth and private-banking brand. Both throw off fees rather than consuming much capital, both got a major lift from the Citibank deal, and both are central to the "earn more from each affluent customer" strategy that aims to narrow the returns gap to peers.

Why "premium" is strategic, not vanity

Affluent customers are disproportionately valuable to a bank: they keep large low-cost balances (helping CASA), they spend heavily on premium cards (fee income), they buy wealth and investment products (more fees), and they default less. Winning them is how Axis attacks its margin and returns gap from the revenue side rather than just the cost side. The Citibank deal was, in essence, a bulk purchase of exactly this cohort — and Burgundy plus the premium-cards stack is the machine built to keep and grow them. Execute it well, and the same affluent relationship feeds CASA, fees and wealth at once.

09

The subsidiaries — the "One Axis" sum-of-parts

Four financial businesses that add profit the core-bank multiple may under-count.

Beyond the bank itself, Axis owns four meaningful financial-services businesses. Together they earned a combined ~₹2,051 Cr of profit in FY26 (+16% YoY) — real money that the bank's standalone valuation can overlook. Analysts increasingly value Axis on a sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) basis: the core bank, plus the embedded value of these subsidiaries, several of which are franchises that could one day list separately (as ICICI did with its AMC and securities arms).

Why the sum-of-parts matters for valuation

When a bank trades on a price-to-book multiple, the market is mostly valuing the lending business; high-return, capital-light fee businesses like an AMC or an investment bank can be under-valued inside that single multiple. ICICI demonstrated the unlock by listing its AMC and merging in its securities arm to crystallise the value. Axis's subsidiaries are smaller but the same logic applies: as they scale, they add a growing, higher-multiple layer of profit on top of the bank. For a stock the bears call "cheap for a reason", the ~₹2,000 Cr-and-growing subsidiary stack is a quiet, often-overlooked piece of the bull case.

How to think about the ~30% discount

At roughly ₹4.28 lakh crore market cap, Axis trades on a meaningfully lower price-to-earnings and price-to-book multiple than ICICI and HDFC — analysts peg the gap at around 30%. Some discount is deserved: Axis's ROE (~14%) genuinely trails ICICI's (~18%), and lower returns warrant a lower multiple. The bull argument is that the gap has widened beyond what the fundamentals justify — the book is now as clean as ICICI's, the Citibank integration is done and accretive, the subsidiaries add value, and even a modest lift in ROE (toward ~16–17%) as funding costs normalise would mechanically compress the discount. The bear argument is that the discount reflects real, durable disadvantages — a thinner deposit franchise, fresher memories of asset-quality trouble, and lower returns — and that "cheap relative to better banks" can persist for years. Both can be true at different points; the swing variable is ROE, and ROE swings mostly on NIM (CASA) and credit cost.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A clean, capable bank — priced as the value option among the big three.

Axis Bank is, today, a genuinely rehabilitated franchise: a clean book, a premium customer base bought from Citi, four useful subsidiaries, and a fortress capital position. What separates it from HDFC and ICICI is no longer credibility on bad loans — it's the returns gap, rooted in funding costs. The investment debate is almost entirely about whether that gap narrows.

The bull case

  • Cleanest book in a decade — 1.23% GNPA / 0.37% NNPA with ~70% coverage and >₹11,000 Cr buffer provisions; the turnaround is real.
  • The discount is a coiled spring — ~30% cheaper than ICICI/HDFC; even a modest ROE lift toward ~16–17% should compress it.
  • Citibank delivered — premium cards, wealth and low-cost deposits, integrated and accretive; a precision fix for the CASA gap.
  • Capital-light fee engines — top-4 cards, ~₹6.78 L Cr Burgundy wealth, ~₹24,400 Cr fees — high-return, low-capital growth.
  • Subsidiary optionality — ~₹2,050 Cr of growing subsidiary profit the bank multiple under-counts; potential future SOTP unlocks.

The bear case

  • The CASA gap is stubborn — cheap, sticky deposits are the hardest thing in banking to build; the NIM/ROE shortfall to leaders has proved durable.
  • Unsecured retail stress — FY26's slippage spike and the RBI nudge on recognition show the retail book can still wobble.
  • Margin pressure — falling rates and fierce deposit competition compressed NIM to 3.62%; Axis feels this more than the leaders.
  • "Cheap for a reason" — lower ROE genuinely justifies some discount; the gap may simply persist for years.
  • Macro / EM-flow sensitivity — heavy foreign ownership and exposure to the Indian credit cycle and RBI policy add volatility beyond Axis's control.
The bottom line

Axis is the value option among India's big private banks: a clean, well-capitalised, professionally-run franchise that has fixed its asset quality and bought its way into the premium segment, but still earns less than HDFC and ICICI because its funding is dearer. The business risk is much lower than it was a decade ago; the open question is purely about returns convergence. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter — CASA / cost-of-deposits (is the funding gap narrowing?), retail slippages (is the unsecured wobble normalising?), and ROE (the scoreboard that the valuation discount tracks). Narrow the funding gap and keep the book clean, and the discount to peers should close; fail to, and a fairly-cheap bank can stay fairly-cheap for a long time.
