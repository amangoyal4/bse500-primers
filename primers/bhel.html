Bharat Heavy Electricals, the machine behind India's power grid

A complete walk through India's largest power-equipment maker — the state-owned engineering giant that builds the boilers, turbines and generators inside most of the country's coal power stations. This primer explains how a lumpy, thin-margin project company actually earns money; why India is suddenly ordering coal plants again after a seven-year drought, and why BHEL wins ~90% of them; how a record ₹2.4 lakh crore order book is meant to power a swing from near-losses back to profit; the honest story of a decade of stumbles; the railway, defence and nuclear diversification; and why a company earning under 2% net margins trades at a P/E near 90.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Bharat Heavy Electricals — BHEL — is India's largest power-generation equipment maker and one of its oldest heavy-engineering companies. Its core product is the "BTG" package that sits at the heart of a coal power station: the Boiler that burns coal to make steam, the Turbine that the steam spins, and the Generator the turbine drives to make electricity. Most of India's installed coal-fired capacity runs on BHEL equipment. It is a Maharatna — the top tier of India's public-sector companies — with the Government of India its majority owner (~59%). In FY26 (year ended March 2026) it earned ₹33,782 crore of revenue and about ₹1,578 crore of net profit, at a thin ~7% operating margin — while sitting on its highest-ever order book of ~₹2.4 lakh crore.

Four facts frame everything about this company:

  • The order book, not the profit, is the headline. For a company that builds giant power plants over three-to-five years, the single most important number is not this year's earnings — it is the backlog of work already won. At ₹2.4 lakh crore, BHEL's order book is roughly seven times its annual revenue (a "book-to-bill" of ~7×), the highest in its history. That is years of visible work — the raw material for future profit.
  • India is ordering coal power again — and BHEL is the last domestic maker standing. After nearly a decade in which almost no new thermal plants were ordered, India has reversed course, targeting ~80 GW of new coal capacity by 2031-32 to keep the grid stable as demand surges and renewables prove intermittent. BHEL has won ~90% of recent large (800 MW) thermal orders — because its rivals abandoned the business during the drought.
  • The whole bull case is a margin-recovery story. BHEL makes almost nothing on each rupee of sales today (net margin under 2%). The bet is that as the huge order book executes, fixed costs get spread over far more revenue, and margins normalise — turning a barely-profitable company into a properly profitable one. That is operating leverage, and it is the entire investment thesis.
  • It is priced for that recovery to arrive. The stock trades near a P/E of 90 on today's depressed earnings — an eye-watering multiple for a capital-goods company. Bulls value it on the order book and forecast earnings (where it looks reasonable); bears value it on today's earnings (where it looks absurd). Both are looking at the same company through different lenses.
In plain terms

Think of BHEL as India's national power-plant builder — a giant, state-owned engineering shop that, for sixty years, made the industrial machinery that turns coal into electricity. For most of the last decade it had almost no work: the country stopped ordering coal plants, betting on solar and wind instead, and BHEL slowly bled — thin margins, the odd loss, delayed projects, a share price stuck in the mud. Then reality bit. Solar panels only make power when the sun shines; India's electricity demand keeps breaking records; and the grid needs a big, dependable "always-on" source underneath the renewables. That source is coal. So the orders came roaring back — and because every rival had quit the business, they nearly all landed at BHEL's door. The company now has more work booked than it has had in its history. The open question is whether it can execute that mountain of work profitably and on time — because BHEL's long, painful record says building the machines is the easy part; making money doing it is the hard part.

01

How a project company actually earns

No engineering degree required — just the handful of ideas that drive the profit.

BHEL is not a factory that stamps out identical products and sells them off the shelf. It is a long-cycle project business: it wins large, custom contracts to design, manufacture and install power-plant equipment, then executes them over several years. That single fact — that revenue is won years before it is earned — shapes every number in the company, and explains why analysts obsess over the order book instead of the quarterly profit.

The four ideas that explain the whole business
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for BHEL)

Execution / revenue recognitionRevenue is booked as work is done (steel cut, equipment shipped, plant erected), not when the order is signed. Slow execution = order book sits idle and earns nothing.
Operating leverageBHEL's big costs — factories, engineers, overheads — are largely fixed. Push more revenue through the same base and margins jump. This is the recovery mechanism (see §05).
Why the order book beats the profit

A power plant takes three to five years to build. So the profit BHEL reports this year comes from orders it won years ago — often during the lean drought years, at thin prices. The orders it is winning now, in the supercycle, will show up in revenue and profit in FY27, FY28 and beyond. That is why you cannot judge BHEL on its current earnings alone: today's profit reflects yesterday's (weak) order book, while today's (huge) order book is the promise of tomorrow's profit. The gap between "what it earns now" and "what its backlog implies it could earn later" is the investment debate.

Why the margins are so thin — and so volatile

  • Fixed-price contracts, moving costs. Many orders are won at a fixed price years in advance. If steel, copper or labour costs rise during the long build, BHEL eats the difference — squeezing already-thin margins.
  • Competitive, commoditised equipment. BTG equipment is a known technology; when ordering is scarce, players bid aggressively to keep factories busy, driving prices (and margins) down. Much of the current backlog was booked in exactly that environment.
  • Under-utilised factories. During the drought, BHEL's plants ran far below capacity — the fixed cost of idle factories crushed margins. Filling them back up is precisely the operating-leverage opportunity.
  • Lumpy, one-off items. Provisions on stuck old projects, liquidated-damages disputes, and one-time write-backs can swing any single quarter. Treat individual quarters with caution and look at the trend.
Read the trend, not the quarter: BHEL's operating margin moved from ~3% (FY24) to ~5% (FY25) to ~7% (FY26) — and the Q4 FY26 quarter alone printed a ~14% EBITDA margin as execution and mix improved. The direction is the story; single quarters are noisy.
02

The company and who controls it

A sixty-year-old nation-builder, majority-owned by the state.

BHEL was founded in 1964 as part of India's post-independence drive to build heavy industry at home rather than import it. For six decades it has been the backbone of the country's power infrastructure — a large share of India's electricity is generated by equipment BHEL built. It is a Maharatna public-sector enterprise (the elite tier granting the greatest operational and financial autonomy), and the Government of India remains its controlling shareholder.

Timeline

  • 1964
    BHEL incorporated to make India self-reliant in heavy electrical equipment — boilers, turbines, generators, transformers, locomotives.
  • 1970s–2000s
    Becomes the dominant supplier of power-generation equipment for the national grid; builds a large manufacturing footprint (Trichy, Haridwar, Hyderabad, Bhopal and others).
  • ~2012
    Peak years — a bulging order book and record ordering during India's power-capacity boom.
  • 2015–2022
    The thermal drought. New coal-plant ordering all but stops as policy tilts to renewables; BHEL's order book, revenue and margins fall for years, with losses in some periods.
  • FY24–FY25
    The turn. Thermal ordering revives; BHEL wins record thermal orders (9.6 GW in FY24, ~14.6 GW in FY25); operating cash flow turns positive again.
  • FY26
    Record order inflow (₹75,916 Cr) and highest-ever order book (~₹2.4 lakh Cr); revenue +19% to ₹33,782 Cr; PAT up ~200% to ₹1,578 Cr; ~8.9 GW of capacity commissioned; the share hits multi-year highs.

Ownership & control

  • Government of India (~59%) — the promoter and controlling shareholder. Screener showed the Govt holding at ~58.2% as of March 2026, down from ~63% earlier, consistent with a stake sale (offer-for-sale) into the strong share price. Either way, the state controls the company.
  • What state ownership means in practice. BHEL is a national strategic asset and a natural beneficiary of "Make-in-India" and self-reliance policy — a plus when the government wants domestic suppliers for power, defence and railways. But it also means political and social objectives can sit alongside pure profit: PSUs can face pressure on pricing, hiring and dividends, and decision-making can be slower and more bureaucratic than a private peer's.
  • Governed by the Ministry of Heavy Industries, and periodically flagged in disinvestment discussions — government stake sales are a recurring feature and a potential overhang on the share.
The double edge of being a government company

BHEL's owner is also, indirectly, its biggest customer's boss. The government sets India's power policy (how much coal capacity to add), owns the largest power producer (NTPC) that places the orders, and owns BHEL that builds them. When the state decides India needs more thermal power, that policy tailwind flows straight to BHEL — a powerful alignment. The flip side is that BHEL doesn't fully control its own destiny: its fortunes rise and fall with a policy cycle it cannot influence, and as a PSU it carries the historical baggage of slower execution and non-commercial pressures. Being the state's power-equipment champion is both the moat and the millstone.

03

The two segments: Power and Industry

One giant historical core, and a smaller, faster-growing frontier.

BHEL reports through two segments. Power is the historical heart — the boilers, turbines and generators for electricity plants — and dominates both revenue and the order book. Industry is everything else: transportation (railways), transmission, defence, and a range of industrial systems. Roughly ~80% of the order book is Power and ~18–20% is Industry (with a sliver from exports).

Segment 1 — Power (the ~80% core)

  • Thermal (coal) BTG — the dominant piece. The Boiler-Turbine-Generator sets for coal power stations. This is BHEL's crown jewel and where its market dominance is strongest (~90% of recent large-unit orders). The thermal revival (§04) is overwhelmingly a Power-segment story.
  • Emission control / FGD. "Flue-Gas Desulphurisation" kit that strips sulphur-dioxide (SO₂) pollution out of a coal plant's exhaust — required as India tightens emission norms. A big retrofit opportunity across the existing coal fleet (§07).
  • Hydro, nuclear and gas. BHEL makes hydro-power turbines, is involved in all three stages of India's nuclear-power programme (a strategic, hard-to-replicate capability), and builds gas-turbine plants.
  • Solar & clean energy. A smaller presence in solar PV, plus emerging areas like battery storage and EV chargers — real, but not yet material to the numbers.

Segment 2 — Industry (the diversification frontier)

  • Transportation / railways. Electric and diesel locomotives, traction equipment, and — a landmark win — a share of the Vande Bharat semi-high-speed trainset programme (BHEL-led consortium won 80 trainsets in a contract worth >₹23,000 Cr including 35 years of maintenance).
  • Transmission. High-voltage equipment for the grid, including ultra-high-voltage (±800 kV) direct-current links that move bulk power across the country.
  • Defence & aerospace. A 30-year-plus supplier of strategic equipment, including naval Super Rapid Gun Mounts (SRGM) — a small but high-value, "Aatmanirbhar" (self-reliance) growth avenue.
  • Industrial systems. Compressors, valves, electrics and equipment for oil-and-gas, steel, cement and other heavy industries.
Why the split matters

The two segments have very different investment characters. Power is huge, cyclical and lumpy — it swings with a national policy cycle, and today it is in a once-a-decade upswing. Industry is smaller but is BHEL's attempt to reduce its dependence on coal: railways, defence and transmission are structural, government-backed growth areas that don't rise and fall with the thermal cycle. For now, though, Power is so dominant that the whole company's fortunes are still, overwhelmingly, a bet on thermal execution — the diversification is a direction of travel, not yet a counterweight.

04

The thermal-power supercycle — BHEL's defining force

Why India is ordering coal plants again, and why the orders land at BHEL.

This is the single force that has re-made the BHEL story. For most of the 2010s, India ordered almost no new coal capacity — the future was supposed to be solar and wind. Then the arithmetic of the grid caught up with the ambition, and the country reversed course. India is now committed to adding roughly 80 GW of new coal-fired capacity by 2031-32. And because the domestic industry had all but died during the drought, an outsized share of those orders is flowing to BHEL — which wins about 90% of the large (800 MW) thermal units. Understanding why coal is back explains the whole revival.

Why order coal again — despite renewables?

  • Base-load vs. intermittency. Solar makes power only when the sun shines; wind only when it blows. But an economy needs electricity all the time — through the night, on cloudy still days, at the evening demand peak. That steady, dependable, "always-on" supply is called base load, and coal (or nuclear/hydro) provides it in a way renewables alone, without cheap large-scale storage, currently cannot.
  • Demand keeps breaking records. India's peak power demand hit ~231 GW in May 2025 and keeps climbing with economic growth, air-conditioning and industrialisation. More renewables are being added, but total demand is rising faster than storage can firm it up — so firm thermal capacity is needed to keep the lights on and the grid stable.
  • Grid stability. A grid heavy with variable renewables needs a solid, controllable backbone to balance it. Policymakers explicitly concluded India must recreate thermal capacity alongside renewables for grid stability — not instead of them.
In plain terms — the base-load problem

Imagine trying to run a city on solar power alone. At noon on a sunny day you have more electricity than you know what to do with; at 8 p.m., when everyone gets home and switches on the lights, air-conditioners and stoves, your solar panels are producing nothing. Batteries can bridge some of that gap — but storing enough power for a whole country, every night, is still enormously expensive. So India needs a big, steady generator that can run around the clock and be dialled up on command. Coal does that job cheaply today. That's why, even while building the world's largest solar fleets, India decided it also needs ~80 GW of new coal plants — and someone has to build the machines inside them. That someone is, overwhelmingly, BHEL.

Why the orders concentrate at BHEL

  • The rivals quit. During the seven-year drought, private BTG makers (and foreign JVs) scaled back or exited — you cannot keep a heavy-equipment factory alive with no orders. When ordering restarted, BHEL was, in the words of one account, "the only entity available with the needed apparatus." Scarcity of domestic suppliers = pricing power and share.
  • The China factor. Geopolitical concerns effectively rule out Chinese equipment for critical national power infrastructure. That removes the biggest low-cost competitor and channels demand to a trusted domestic maker.
  • Localisation & strategic-supplier status. As a state-owned Maharatna with deep manufacturing and a 60-year track record, BHEL is the natural "Make-in-India" supplier for strategic power equipment. Government-owned NTPC and state utilities placing the orders reinforces the alignment.
  • The scale of the wins. BHEL won 9.6 GW (~₹52,000 Cr) in FY24 and its highest-ever ~14.6 GW in FY25. In mid-2026 a single order — a 3×800 MW plant for MUNPL worth ~₹21,000 Cr — lifted the entire order book by ~10%.
The catch — a capacity ceiling: BHEL's near-monopoly is also a bottleneck. The country's ability to build 80 GW is limited by BHEL's own factory capacity to manufacture boilers and turbines. That protects BHEL's share, but it means execution — not order-winning — is now the binding constraint. Winning the work was the easy part; delivering it on time and at a profit is the test.
05

From order book to margin recovery — the core bull case

The single mechanism the entire investment thesis rests on.

Here is the thesis in one sentence: BHEL has a mountain of work booked, its factories are filling up, and as that work executes, its thin margins are supposed to fatten dramatically. This is operating leverage — and if it plays out, a barely-profitable company becomes a properly profitable one, with earnings growing far faster than revenue. If it doesn't play out, the whole story falls apart. Everything hinges on this.

The bull case, in one mechanism

A heavy-engineering company like BHEL carries huge fixed costs — enormous factories, thousands of engineers, corporate overhead — that it pays whether the plants run at 40% or 90% of capacity. During the drought those factories ran half-empty, so the fixed cost was spread over very little revenue and margins were crushed (even losses). Now flip it: push far more revenue through the same fixed base, and each extra rupee of sales carries very little extra cost — so it drops almost straight to profit. That's why, if revenue climbs steadily as the ₹2.4 lakh crore backlog executes, margins should expand and profit should grow much faster than sales. Analysts betting on this see EPS rising from ~₹1.5 (FY25) toward ~₹12–13 by FY28 — an ~8× jump in three years. That forecast, not today's earnings, is what the share price is really pricing.

The honest risk in the same mechanism

Operating leverage cuts both ways, and BHEL's history (§06) is a warning. Three things can break the thesis:

  • Execution delays. A power plant is a vast, multi-year project with hundreds of moving parts — land, clearances, fuel supply, the customer's readiness. If projects slip, the order book sits idle: no revenue, no leverage. A gas-supply shortage alone was estimated to cost BHEL ~₹2,500–3,000 Cr of revenue in a single recent quarter — showing how fragile the pace of execution is.
  • Thin cushion for error. On a net margin under 2%, a small cost overrun or a fixed-price order signed cheaply can wipe out the profit on that project entirely. There is very little room for mistakes.
  • Working-capital drag. Big projects tie up enormous cash in inventory and unpaid dues from customers (see §08). If cash gets stuck, the "profit" on paper doesn't translate into real returns.
The order book answers "is there enough work?" — emphatically yes. Execution answers "can BHEL turn that work into profit and cash?" — and that is the only question that now matters.
06

The volatile history — the honest part

Why a company with a record order book still has to prove it can profit from it.

It would be dishonest to present BHEL purely as a recovery story without the context that makes the recovery uncertain. This is a company that spent the better part of a decade struggling — and its record is the strongest argument the bears have. The past does not have to repeat, but it explains why seasoned investors demand proof, not promises.

What went wrong — the lost decade

  • The ordering cliff. After the ~2012 peak, India's thermal ordering collapsed as policy pivoted to renewables and the power sector was saddled with stressed, over-built capacity. BHEL's core market simply dried up for years.
  • Falling revenue and losses. With the order book shrinking, revenue fell, factories ran idle, and BHEL slid into losses in some years — a jarring outcome for a Maharatna. It reported the odd widening quarterly loss even into the early part of the recent recovery.
  • Execution and cost problems. Project delays, cost overruns and disputes (liquidated damages, stuck receivables) repeatedly ate into results. Even as revenue recovered, translating it into sustainable margin proved hard.
  • A strategic miss. Being ~70%+ dependent on thermal, BHEL was badly exposed when the industry bet against coal — and was slow to diversify meaningfully into the areas that were growing.
The bear's trump card: BHEL has had a big order book before — in 2012 — and still ended up with a lost decade when the cycle turned and execution disappointed. The bear argument is simple: this is a cyclical, thin-margin, government-run project company whose fortunes depend on a lumpy policy cycle it cannot control, and whose history of turning backlog into profit is patchy. Buying it at ~90× earnings, the bear says, is paying a premium price for a company that has repeatedly failed to earn a premium return.

What's genuinely different this time

  • Scale & durability of the order book. A ~7× book-to-bill and an 80 GW national commitment give years of visibility — a structurally different backdrop from a one-off order spike.
  • Cash flow turned positive. Operating cash flow swung to positive (~₹2,191 Cr in FY25) after years of outflows — a sign of better working-capital and execution discipline.
  • Margins actually rising. The move from ~3% to ~7% operating margin (and a ~14% exit quarter) is real, early evidence the leverage is starting to work — not just a forecast.
  • Less competition than ever. With rivals gone, BHEL keeps pricing power on new orders, which should lift the margin profile of the newer backlog versus the cheap drought-era orders.
The balanced verdict

The honest read is that BHEL is a genuine cyclical turnaround with a genuinely uncertain outcome. The demand side of the story is as strong as it has ever been — the orders are real, huge, and durable. The question is entirely on the supply/execution side: can a historically slow, thin-margin PSU deliver a decade of work on time and at expanding margins, when it has stumbled at exactly this task before? Bulls point to the improving margins and cash flow as proof the company has changed; bears point to sixty years of evidence that heavy-engineering PSUs disappoint on execution. Both deserve a hearing — which is exactly why the stock is a debate, not a consensus.

07

Diversification beyond thermal — and its limits

The effort to reduce coal dependence — how far it goes, and how far it doesn't.

BHEL knows that being a coal-plant maker is a risky place to stand for the long run — coal is politically contested, cyclical, and ultimately a shrinking share of the world's energy future. So it is diversifying: into emission control, railways, defence, transmission, nuclear, hydro and clean energy. The push is real and strategically sensible. But it is important to be clear-eyed about how small these are relative to the thermal core today.

The pieces, decoded

  • FGD (emission control) — the closest adjacency. As India tightens sulphur-dioxide emission norms for coal plants, the entire existing fleet needs "scrubbers" (Flue-Gas Desulphurisation) retrofitted. BHEL supplies wet-limestone and seawater FGD systems (up to ~99.9% SO₂ removal). This is a large, coal-linked opportunity — but its timing depends on how strictly the norms are enforced, which has repeatedly been pushed back.
  • Railways & Vande Bharat — the flagship diversification. A six-decade rail supplier (locos up to 9,000 HP), now with a landmark share of the Vande Bharat trainset programme — 80 trainsets worth >₹23,000 Cr including 35 years of maintenance (an annuity-like tail). This is the most tangible non-power growth engine.
  • Defence & transmission — strategic, high-value. Naval Super Rapid Gun Mounts and other defence equipment (a 30-year-plus relationship), plus grid transmission gear including ±800 kV UHVDC links. Small in revenue, but sticky and aligned with self-reliance policy.
  • Nuclear & hydro — hard-to-replicate capability. Involvement across all three stages of India's nuclear programme and hydro turbines — long-dated, strategic work that few can do, but slow to move the needle.
  • Solar, storage, EV chargers — optionality. A foot in the clean-energy door for the long term; not yet financially material.
The honest limit: Diversification is the right strategy and it de-risks the very-long-term picture — but investors should not overstate it today. With Power at ~80% of the backlog, BHEL remains, for the foreseeable future, primarily a bet on thermal execution. The Industry segment is a promising second leg, not yet a replacement for the first.
08

The balance sheet — a working-capital business

Why a company with a huge order book still has to watch its cash.

Long-cycle project companies are working-capital heavy by nature, and BHEL is no exception. Understanding this is essential, because it is where "profit on paper" can fail to become "cash in the bank" — the classic trap for engineering PSUs.

Why the cash gets tied up

  • Inventory & work-in-progress. Building a boiler or turbine means buying steel and components and holding half-finished equipment for months or years before it is delivered and paid for. That is cash locked in warehouses and factory floors.
  • Receivables & retention money. Customers (often government utilities) pay in stages, hold back "retention" until a plant is fully commissioned, and sometimes pay slowly. BHEL can book revenue while the cash is still owed — inflating profit relative to cash generated.
  • The scale of it. Working-capital days ran around ~79 days in FY26 — meaningful, though improved from the worst of the drought years when receivables ballooned and cash flow was negative.

Why this matters for the thesis

Here is the trap in one line: an order book only creates value if it converts to cash. BHEL can win ₹2.4 lakh crore of orders and even book rising profit — but if that profit stays trapped in inventory and unpaid customer bills, the returns to shareholders never materialise. The healthy sign is that operating cash flow turned positive in FY25 for the first time in years, and working-capital days have improved. The thing to watch every year is whether cash flow keeps pace with the reported profit as execution scales up. If it does, the recovery is real; if profit rises but cash doesn't follow, be sceptical.

10

The valuation debate — two lenses, one stock

Expensive on earnings, arguably cheap on the order book. Both can be true.

No discussion of BHEL is complete without confronting its valuation, because it looks either terrifying or reasonable depending entirely on which number you divide the price by. At a ~₹1.44 lakh crore market cap on ~₹1,578 crore of FY26 profit, BHEL trades near a P/E of ~90 — extraordinary for a heavy-engineering company. Yet many analysts still rate it a buy. The reconciliation is the key insight.

In plain terms — what the P/E is really pricing

A P/E of 90 doesn't mean investors think BHEL is worth 90 years of today's profit. It means they are betting today's profit is artificially depressed and about to multiply. When you build power plants that take years, this year's earnings reflect old, cheap, drought-era orders — while the huge new backlog will show up in profit only in FY27–FY28. So the bulls quietly ignore the trailing P/E and look at forward earnings: if EPS really goes from ~₹1.5 to ~₹12–13 in three years (an ~8× jump), then at today's price the stock is on ~30× future earnings — pricey but not crazy for a company with seven years of work booked and no real competition. The entire bet, therefore, is a bet on that earnings forecast coming true. Believe the ₹12 EPS and it's cheap; fear the earnings land at ₹6–7 instead (because execution disappoints), and a ~90× stock has a very long way to fall.

The trap in the forward view: forward P/E is only as good as the forecast behind it. BHEL's own history is the reason to be cautious — this is a company that has disappointed execution expectations before. The valuation offers little cushion: it already assumes the recovery works. If it does, the price is justified; if it stumbles, there is no margin of safety in the multiple.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine turnaround with a genuinely uncertain outcome.

BHEL is one of the clearest examples in the Indian market of a cyclical turnaround priced for success. The demand backdrop is the strongest it has been in over a decade; the record order book is real; the early margin recovery is real. What is not yet proven is execution — and BHEL's own history is the reason that word carries so much weight. The debate is almost entirely about whether the company can convert an unprecedented backlog into profit and cash without repeating past mistakes.

The bull case

  • Once-a-decade thermal supercycle — ~80 GW national coal pipeline, and BHEL wins ~90% of large orders with almost no competition.
  • Record, durable order book — ~₹2.4 lakh Cr, ~7× book-to-bill: years of visible revenue.
  • Operating leverage kicking in — margins already doubled (3%→7%), a ~14% exit quarter; big earnings upside as fixed costs get spread.
  • Diversification optionality — Vande Bharat, defence, transmission, nuclear and FGD add non-thermal legs.
  • Strategic, state-backed champion — the natural "Make-in-India" supplier for power, defence and railways; light on debt.

The bear case

  • Execution risk — multi-year projects slip; a single gas shortage cost ~₹2,500–3,000 Cr of quarterly revenue. The backlog is worthless if it doesn't execute.
  • Wafer-thin margins — net margin under 2% leaves no room for cost overruns on fixed-price orders.
  • Extreme valuation — ~90× trailing earnings assumes the recovery works; no margin of safety if it doesn't.
  • A patchy record — a lost decade, losses, and a history of disappointing on exactly this "execute the backlog" task.
  • Structural & policy risk — tied to a lumpy policy cycle it can't control; coal's long-term decline; PSU overhangs (govt stake sales, non-commercial pressures); working-capital drag.
The bottom line

BHEL is a high-conviction demand story wrapped around a high-uncertainty execution story. Almost nobody disputes that India needs the power plants, that BHEL will win the orders, or that the backlog is real — the argument is entirely about whether a historically slow, thin-margin PSU can deliver that work on time and at rising margins, and thereby justify a ~90× multiple. The two numbers to watch every quarter are execution pace (is revenue rising as the backlog converts?) and margin trajectory (is operating leverage actually showing up?). Get both right and today's rich valuation looks forward-looking and fair; stumble on either, and the gap between BHEL's share price and its earnings closes the painful way. It is, in the truest sense, a bet on a turnaround the company has promised before — and now, finally, has the order book to attempt.
