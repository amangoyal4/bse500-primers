Skipper Ltd, towers, pipes, and a hungry grid

A complete walk through India's largest maker of power transmission & distribution towers — how a Kolkata steel-fabricator rolls its own angles into 100-metre lattice towers, why the same company also sells PVC plumbing pipes to hardware shops, and how a decade-long grid-building boom at home plus a scramble to rewire ageing grids abroad is filling its order book faster than it has ever been filled.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Skipper Ltd makes the steel lattice towers that carry high-voltage power lines across the country — those tall, criss-crossed steel skeletons you see marching over fields with cables slung between them. It is India's largest manufacturer of transmission & distribution (T&D) towers and structures, and among the largest in the world by capacity. But it is not only a tower company. Skipper is really three businesses stitched onto one balance sheet: an Engineering Products arm (the towers, monopoles, poles and angles — the core and by far the largest), a Polymer Products arm that sells branded PVC and CPVC plumbing and agri pipes under the "Skipper Pipes" name, and an Infrastructure / EPC arm that builds entire transmission lines turnkey.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • It rides India's biggest infrastructure spend that nobody talks about. The country is pouring lakhs of crores into its grid — the wires and towers that move electricity from where it is generated to where it is used. Renewable power built in sunny Rajasthan or windy Gujarat is worthless unless a transmission line carries it to a city, and India is short of exactly those lines. Skipper sells the steel that those lines stand on. FY26 revenue reached ~₹5,553 Cr, up ~20%, and the order book hit an all-time high of ~₹8,502 Cr — well over a year of forward work.
  • It makes its own steel, which is the whole margin story. Unlike a pure line-building contractor, Skipper is backward-integrated — it rolls raw steel into angles in its own mills, then fabricates and galvanises those angles into finished towers. It captures margin at every step from steel section to erected tower, which is why its engineering business earns a structurally different margin from a firm that merely buys towers and bolts them together.
  • It quietly runs a consumer building-products brand on the side. The Polymer arm — PVC, CPVC, uPVC and SWR pipes sold through plumbers and dealers — crossed ₹500 Cr of revenue for the first time in FY26. It is small (~9% of sales) but it is a completely different animal: a branded, distribution-led, working-capital-heavy consumer play competing with Supreme, Astral and Finolex — and the market values that kind of business very differently from a lattice-steel fabricator.
In plain terms

Picture two very different factories owned by the same Kolkata family. In one, red-hot steel billets are rolled into angle sections, punched, galvanised in a bath of molten zinc, and shipped out as the giant towers that hold up power lines — sold by the tonne, to state utilities and Power Grid, and increasingly exported. In the other, a shinier plant extrudes plastic pipes sold by the metre through hardware shops to plumbers and farmers. Bolted between them is a project crew that erects entire transmission lines kilometre by kilometre. Same promoter, same share, three units of measure — and a single order book now fuller than at any point in the company's history because India (and half the world) urgently needs more grid.

01

Power T&D 101: what a tower actually does

No engineering degree needed — just the handful of terms that drive the business.

Electricity is generated in big plants — a coal station, a solar park, a hydro dam — that sit far from the cities and factories that use the power. Getting it from there to here is the job of the grid, and the grid is physical: steel towers holding aloft aluminium conductors (the cables), stepping the voltage up for the long haul and down again for delivery. Skipper makes the towers and, increasingly, builds the whole line. To follow the business you need five ideas.

The only vocabulary you need

Transmission vs distributionTransmission is the long-distance, high-voltage backbone (400 kV, 765 kV lines on tall lattice towers); distribution is the lower-voltage network that reaches homes (on poles). Skipper makes structures for both — the tall towers and the poles.
kV (kilovolt)The line's voltage — bigger number, longer haul, bigger tower. India's newest backbone runs at 765 kV; the towers for it are massive, higher-value structures. Winning 765 kV work is a mark of capability.
Lattice tower vs monopoleA lattice tower is the classic criss-cross steel skeleton; a monopole is a single tubular steel column, used where land is tight (cities, roads). Skipper makes both, plus poles for distribution.

EPC"Engineering, Procurement & Construction" — taking a transmission line from drawing to energised: surveying, foundations, erecting towers, stringing conductors, commissioning. Skipper does this turnkey through its Infrastructure arm.
The economics that decide everything: tonnes of steel, at a spread

A transmission tower is, at heart, a precise arrangement of galvanised steel sold by weight. Skipper buys steel, converts it into angles and structures, and sells the finished tower for more than the steel inside it — the difference (the "conversion spread") pays for the fabrication, the galvanising, the freight and the profit. Two things therefore move Skipper's engineering margin more than anything else: how full its plants are running (a fabrication plant has heavy fixed costs, so every extra tonne through it is highly profitable), and how well it manages the steel price between order and delivery. Understand those two levers and you understand why this looks like a low-margin business that can nonetheless throw off rising profits when volume climbs.

Who buys transmission towers

  • Power Grid Corporation (PGCIL) — the giant central transmission utility, the single most important customer in Indian T&D. It builds the inter-state backbone and tenders enormous line packages; a Skipper 765 kV win is almost always a PGCIL or state-utility award.
  • State transmission & distribution companies — every state has its own utility building and upgrading local lines and substations; collectively a huge, steady buyer of towers and poles.
  • Private developers & EPC firms — renewable-energy developers needing evacuation lines from their solar/wind parks, and other EPC contractors who buy Skipper's towers as a component.
  • Overseas utilities & contractors — 65+ countries across Africa, the Middle East, Latin America, Europe and now North America, buying towers, poles and monopoles as global grids are rebuilt.
02

What Skipper actually sells: three arms under one roof

Read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Skipper reports three business segments, and they are genuinely different businesses — different customers, different sales cycles, different working capital, different margins. The mistake is to treat Skipper as one thing. It is a steel-tower manufacturer, a line-building contractor, and a consumer-pipes brand, and the three sit in very different competitive worlds even though they share a factory campus, a promoter and a share price.

The three segments, decoded

  • Engineering Products (~₹4,359 Cr, +24% in FY26) — the core. Transmission & distribution towers, tubular poles, monopoles, telecom structures, railway electrification masts, and the angles it rolls in-house. This is what makes Skipper the largest T&D-structures maker in India. It sells to Power Grid, state utilities and, increasingly, to export markets. Total structural steel capacity is around 375,000 tonnes a year.
  • Infrastructure / EPC (~₹687 Cr, roughly flat in FY26) — turnkey transmission-line construction. Instead of just supplying towers, Skipper builds the entire line: surveying, foundations, erection, conductor stringing and commissioning. This deepens the relationship with utilities and captures more of the project, but it is a lumpier, more execution-sensitive, lower-margin, working-capital-hungry business. Recent wins include 765 kV line packages from PGCIL.
  • Polymer Products (~₹507 Cr, +17% in FY26) — "Skipper Pipes": PVC, CPVC, uPVC and SWR pipes and fittings for plumbing, agriculture and infrastructure. This is a branded, dealer-distributed consumer/agri product — a genuine diversification away from steel and government tenders, into a market of plumbers, farmers and builders, competing against the likes of Supreme and Astral.
Why one company, three units of measure

The tower business is priced and sold by the tonne of galvanised steel; the pipe business by the metre (or kilogram) of extruded plastic; the EPC business by the circuit-kilometre of line delivered. Those are not just different products — they are different demand engines. Towers rise and fall with government grid capex and export cycles. Pipes rise and fall with construction, monsoon and farm incomes, and depend on brand and distribution, not tenders. EPC depends on execution discipline and the health of the balance sheet. When you value Skipper you are really valuing a weighted blend of three businesses that rarely have a bad year at the same time — and rarely a great one together either.

03

The company, the Bansals, and who controls it

A Kolkata family steel house, four decades in the making.

Skipper is a Bansal-family company, run out of Kolkata, with roots in the S. K. Bansal group's steel-trading and fabrication business going back to the 1960s. It is a first-generation-into-second industrial house — founder-led, promoter-heavy, and still tightly controlled by the family that built it.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Bansal family — patriarch Sajan Kumar Bansal and his sons — hold the controlling majority stake (broadly ~70%, approx.). This is a closely-held, founder-run company; the family is deeply operationally involved, which is both a source of long-term commitment and a concentration to keep an eye on. Track promoter pledge levels directly in the shareholding filings.
  • Head office: Kolkata (Loudon Street), with manufacturing spread across multiple states — the flagship engineering complexes plus polymer plants at sites including Ahmedabad, Guwahati and Sikandrabad (UP).
  • Capital: the company has periodically raised capital to fund growth and reduce debt (including a rights issue). A T&D-plus-pipes business is capital-intensive at both ends — steel fabrication needs plants and working capital; the pipe brand needs inventory and dealer credit — so the balance sheet is a live part of the story (§08).
Why the structure matters: Skipper is not a diversified conglomerate run by professional managers across unrelated divisions — it is a family steel house that grew a pipe brand and a construction arm alongside its core. That concentration is a strength (patient, aligned, decades-deep in T&D relationships) and a watch-item (key-man dependence, related-party discipline, capital-allocation calls made by owners). The three-segment sprawl is deliberate diversification by the same hands, not a portfolio assembled by acquisition.
04

The grid capex supercycle: why the order book is bursting

The single biggest tailwind — and it is unusually visible.

For years, India's power story was about generation — building coal plants, then solar parks. The next decade is about transmission: the wires and towers to move all that power around. This is where Skipper lives, and the spending pipeline behind it is one of the most concrete, policy-backed capex cycles in the Indian market.

The insight that makes this cycle unusually legible

Most industrial demand you have to forecast. Grid capex you can almost read off a map. A solar park of a given size, in a given location, requires a broadly calculable number of towers and kilometres of line to evacuate its power — and those projects are announced, tendered and tracked years in advance by Power Grid and the states. So Skipper's demand is not a guess about consumer sentiment; it is the physical, engineered consequence of a renewable build-out that the government has committed to and budgeted for. That is why the order book (§08) can run over a year of revenue with real visibility — and why a T&D tower maker is, counter-intuitively, one of the cleaner ways to own the energy transition.

None of this makes the cycle risk-free. Tendering can be lumpy, awards can slip a quarter, and the whole industry competes hard on price for the same PGCIL and state packages. But the direction is unusually secure: you cannot decarbonise the power system without physically rebuilding the grid, and that rebuild is made of exactly the galvanised steel Skipper produces.

05

The angle-to-tower edge: why Skipper's margin is different

The most important operational idea — and the reason it isn't just another contractor.

Two companies can both "make transmission towers" and have completely different economics. The difference is where they start in the value chain. Skipper starts at raw steel: it operates its own angle-rolling mills, turning steel into the angle sections that are the raw material of a lattice tower, then fabricates and galvanises those angles into finished structures. A firm that buys angles or finished towers from outside starts much later — and captures much less of the value.

This integration does three things. First, margin — every step done in-house is a step whose profit stays with Skipper. Second, control — it owns quality and delivery timing on the critical galvanising and fabrication steps rather than waiting on suppliers, which matters when a utility penalises late delivery. Third, cost competitiveness on exports — a fully integrated Indian maker can land a galvanised tower in Africa or the Americas at a price that wins against local fabricators, because it isn't stacking three suppliers' margins into the quote.

The device that only fits Skipper: the same steel, three times the work

Here is the sentence that survives no find-and-replace to another tower company: a rival that buys finished towers is a bolting business, but Skipper is a steel-conversion business that happens to sell towers. It takes one input — steel — and adds rolling, then fabrication, then galvanising, keeping the spread at each stage; the tower it ships has been "made" three times over inside its own walls. That is why, when steel prices are stable and plants run full, Skipper's engineering margin can widen even as the headline "tower price per tonne" looks like a commodity. The lever isn't the tower price; it's the spread across four in-house steps times the tonnes pushed through fixed-cost plants.

A genuine surprise: most people assume a "transmission EPC" company and a "tower manufacturer" are the same thing sitting in the same bucket. They are not, and the gap shows up in the accounts — a heavy-EPC contractor earns thin, project-cycle margins on other people's steel, while Skipper's manufacturing-led mix earns a conversion margin on its own steel. The engineering arm, not the flashier EPC order wins, is where the real, repeatable profit is made.
06

The export tower story: a Kolkata firm rewiring the world

The tailwind most domestic investors underrate.

India is not the only country that needs new grid. Ageing networks in the United States and Europe, fast-growing demand in Africa and the Middle East, and grid build-outs across Latin America have created a global scramble for transmission structures — and India's integrated, low-cost tower makers are well placed to supply it. Skipper exports towers, poles and monopoles to 65+ countries, and the export order book is a growing slice of the whole.

The export order book sits around ₹900 Cr. Small as a share, but it grows faster than the domestic book, tends to carry better margins, and diversifies away from Indian tender timing.

Two developments make the export story more than a footnote. First, Skipper landed its first major pole-supply order from the USA in FY25 — a beachhead in the world's most demanding, highest-value grid market, where utilities are spending heavily to harden ageing networks against storms and to connect new renewables. Second, the export mix skews toward higher-value structures (monopoles for tight urban corridors, specialised poles), where Skipper competes on engineering and reliability rather than only price.

Why exports change the quality of the earnings

A domestic-only tower maker is hostage to the rhythm of Indian tenders — a slow ordering quarter from Power Grid hits everyone at once. Exports break that correlation: a US utility's storm-hardening programme and a Rajasthan green corridor do not run on the same calendar. So each rupee of export order does double duty — it usually earns a better margin than a fiercely-bid domestic package, and it smooths the cycle. The risk is the mirror image: exports carry currency, freight, trade-policy and customer-concentration risks that a domestic supply does not. The prize is worth the friction — global grid capex is arguably a bigger, longer wave than India's alone — but it has to be executed order by order, country by country.

07

Skipper Pipes: the plastic business bolted to a steel company

The diversification that makes Skipper hard to categorise — and interesting.

Alongside the towers, Skipper runs a branded PVC pipes and fittings business — "Skipper Pipes" — making CPVC, uPVC, PVC and SWR pipes for plumbing, agriculture and infrastructure. In FY26 the Polymer segment crossed ₹500 Cr of revenue for the first time, up ~17%. It is small next to the tower business, but it is strategically the most different thing the company does — and potentially the most valuable per rupee of sales.

Why so different? A transmission tower is sold to a handful of sophisticated buyers (utilities, EPC firms) via competitive tender, once, in bulk. A plastic pipe is sold to thousands of plumbers, farmers and small builders through a network of dealers and distributors, on the strength of a brand and shelf availability. That means the Polymer business needs the things a steel-tender business doesn't: consumer marketing, a wide dealer network, channel financing, and inventory sitting in warehouses across the country. It is a building-materials consumer play grafted onto an industrial company.

The surprise hiding in the segment mix

Here is the counter-intuitive bit. The stock market values a branded pipes company — think Astral or Supreme — on a rich multiple, because it looks like a sticky, growing consumer franchise. It values a lattice-steel / T&D EPC company far more cheaply, because that looks cyclical and tender-driven. Skipper is one share straddling both worlds — and the pipes arm, at under a tenth of sales, carries an outsized share of the imagination in any bull case, because it is the piece that could one day be re-rated like a consumer company rather than a steel fabricator. The bear will point out the flip side: pipes is where the working capital and dealer-credit risk lives, and Skipper is a challenger brand fighting entrenched incumbents with far bigger ad budgets and deeper distribution.

For now, Polymer is best understood as a real, growing option rather than the main event. If Skipper can keep compounding pipe volumes and build genuine brand pull, it earns a mix-shift toward higher-quality, less-cyclical earnings. If it can't, Polymer is a modest, competent third leg that adds ballast but not magic. Either way, it is what stops Skipper from being a pure play on government grid tenders.

08

The order book, execution, and the working-capital tightrope

Where a project-plus-manufacturing hybrid can trip.

Skipper exited FY26 with a record ~₹8,502 Cr order book — comfortably more than a year of revenue booked in advance. That visibility is the good news. The hard part, as with any business that both manufactures and builds projects, is the working capital: Skipper buys steel and fabricates towers months before it is paid, and its EPC and export contracts stretch payment terms further still.

The mechanism to watch is the cash-conversion cycle — the time between paying for steel and collecting from customers. A manufacturing-plus-EPC business runs a lot of inventory (steel, half-finished towers) and receivables (utilities and export clients that pay on milestones). Grow revenue fast and, without discipline, working capital balloons and eats the very cash the profit is generating. Skipper's whole history of tighter and looser years maps closely onto how well it has managed exactly this. Net debt, receivable days and inventory days are therefore not footnotes here — they are the health check.

Why a fat order book is necessary but not sufficient

An order book is a promise to do work; profit only appears when a tower is delivered, a line is commissioned, and — crucially — the customer pays. For a firm selling to government utilities and overseas clients, that last step can be slow. So the same growth that thrills the market can strain the balance sheet if collections lag despatches. The reassuring version of Skipper is a company growing revenue ~15–20% while holding its working-capital cycle steady and creeping its EBITDA margin toward the ~12% target. The worrying version is one where revenue races ahead but receivables and inventory race faster, and reported profit stops turning into cash. The order book tells you the demand is there; the working-capital discipline tells you whether the demand becomes wealth.

Skipper fights different companies in each of its arms.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

In towers, Skipper's edge is not size — KEC and Kalpataru dwarf it — but focus and integration: it is a specialist that makes its own steel structures rather than a giant contractor that also builds railways and pipelines. That makes it a purer, more manufacturing-margined way to own the T&D theme, while the majors offer diversification Skipper lacks. In pipes, the honest read is that Skipper is the underdog: Supreme and Astral have decades of brand and distribution, and dislodging them is slow, expensive work. Skipper's advantage across the whole company is the one thing rivals can't easily copy — a single roof that turns raw steel into an exported galvanised tower and a branded pipe, spreading its bets across the grid, the export wave and the building-materials aisle.

Financials & valuation

A real growth ramp and a clear margin lever — priced for the ramp to continue.

FY26 was the best year in Skipper's listed life: revenue at a record, profit up sharply, the order book at an all-time high, and management guiding margins higher. The market has noticed — the stock trades at a premium to a plain tower fabricator. The debate is whether utilisation and mix can lift margins the way management expects while working capital stays under control.

The one number that settles the thesis: engineering-plant utilisation

At a high-20s P/E, Skipper is not priced as a cheap cyclical — it is priced as a company whose margins are about to rise. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is capacity utilisation of the ~375,000-tonne engineering plant, because that single lever is what pulls the EBITDA margin from ~10.3% toward the ~12% goal. Read it alongside three companions: working-capital days (is the growth turning into cash, §08?), export mix (the higher-margin, cycle-smoothing slice, §06), and book-to-bill (is the record order book still refilling?). If utilisation climbs and the cash cycle holds, the margin walk is credible and the premium defensible. If the plants run full but working capital swells, or margins stall at 10-point-something, the multiple has nothing to stand on. Utilisation is the scoreboard; everything else is commentary on it.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A focused grid-infrastructure compounder — asked to prove its margins and its cash.

Skipper enters FY27 with the strongest hand it has held: a record order book, a structural grid-capex tailwind at home, a real export wave abroad, a growing pipe brand, and a margin target management is confident about. The debate is not whether demand exists — the order book settles that — but whether Skipper can turn record revenue into rising margins and, above all, into cash.

The bull case

  • Grid supercycle — renewable evacuation and the national transmission plan give multi-year, policy-backed visibility on tower demand; grid capex is a cleaner way to own the energy transition than generation.
  • Integration margin — owning angle rolling, fabrication and galvanising means a conversion spread rivals who buy-and-erect can't match; utilisation gains flow to the bottom line.
  • Export wave — 65+ countries and a new US beachhead add higher-margin, cycle-diversifying revenue as global grids are rebuilt.
  • Pipe optionality — a branded polymer business past ₹500 Cr that could re-rate the mix toward consumer-quality earnings if it keeps compounding.
  • Record order book — ~₹8,502 Cr, well over a year of revenue, still refilling on fresh PGCIL and export wins.

The bear case

  • Working-capital risk — a manufacturing-plus-EPC business can grow revenue while cash goes backwards if receivables and inventory outrun collections; this is the historic soft spot.
  • Thin, competitive margins — even at target ~12% EBITDA, T&D structures is a fiercely-bid business; steel-price swings and aggressive tender pricing can erode the spread.
  • Order-timing lumpiness — reliance on PGCIL and state tenders means a slow ordering patch can hit revenue regardless of the map's long-term promise.
  • Pipe challenger risk — Skipper is the underdog against Supreme/Astral/Finolex; brand-building is slow and expensive, and the segment carries its own dealer-credit risk.
  • Valuation & concentration — a high-20s P/E already prices margin expansion; a closely-held, founder-run structure concentrates key-man and capital-allocation risk.
The bottom line

Skipper has spent four decades turning steel into the towers that hold up the grid, and it has arrived at a moment when India and much of the world urgently need far more of exactly that. Demand is not the question — the record order book answers it. The question is whether a family steel house can run its plants full, keep its cash cycle tight, and nudge margins toward 12% while a fledgling pipe brand and a booming export book do their part. The next several quarters answer it in a single, checkable figure — engineering-plant utilisation, and the EBITDA margin it drags with it — read against whether reported profit is still turning into collected cash. Watch the margin walk and the working-capital days print together each quarter; between them they tell you whether the record order book is building wealth or just building inventory.
