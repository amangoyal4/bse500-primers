Industry & Company Primer NSE: AEQUS · BSE: 544634 Built for a first-time reader

Aequs, one factory town betting India can out-China China — for jet parts and for toys

A complete walk through India's most unusual contract manufacturer — a Belagavi company that machines precision aircraft components for Airbus, Boeing and Safran and, in the same corporate body, moulds toys, cookware and appliances for global consumer brands. Two utterly different businesses, one integrated-cluster strategy, and a single wager: that India can be the "China+1" factory for both. It IPO'd in December 2025 while still losing money at the group level — which is exactly what makes it interesting.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Aequs is a contract manufacturer — it doesn't sell products under its own brand; it makes things for other companies to sell under theirs. What makes it strange, and worth a primer, is what it makes. Under one roof (or rather, one set of industrial clusters in northern Karnataka), Aequs runs two completely unrelated factories-of-factories: a precision aerospace business that machines, forges and assembles flight-critical aircraft parts for the world's aviation giants, and a consumer business that manufactures toys, cookware, small appliances and electronics components for global consumer brands. Aircraft landing-gear parts and children's toys, in the same annual report. That is deliberate, and it is the whole story.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is two businesses stapled together by one idea. The idea is vertical integration inside a cluster — Aequs co-locates many manufacturing steps (forging, machining, surface treatment, assembly) in a single ecosystem so it can offer a global customer an end-to-end, cost-competitive alternative to Chinese supply. It built India's first precision-engineering SEZ in Belagavi for aerospace, and a dedicated toy-making cluster in Koppal for consumer. Same playbook, two industries.
  • The aerospace half is the profitable, moated engine; the consumer half is the young, hungry one. In FY26 aerospace did ~₹1,046 Cr of revenue (+27%) on a USD 889 M order book, and it makes money. Consumer did only ~₹184 Cr but grew ~84% — and it is where the losses live. The group as a whole posted a ~₹113 Cr consolidated net loss in FY26 even though the standalone parent turned a ~₹50 Cr profit. That split is the single most important number in the company.
  • It went public mid-ramp. Aequs listed on 10 December 2025 at ₹140 (a modest premium over the ₹124 issue price) after an IPO that was heavily oversubscribed. Investors bought a company still in the red, on the promise that both engines scale into profit as India's "China+1" moment arrives. Mid-2026 market cap is around ₹12,800 Cr — a valuation that only works if the two-engine bet pays off.
In plain terms

Aequs is best understood as a landlord-operator of two industrial ecosystems, not a maker of any one product. Its bet is that a global brand — an aircraft OEM or a toy company — trying to move sourcing out of China will find it easier to hand the whole job to one integrated Indian cluster than to stitch together a dozen small suppliers. Aerospace proves the model works and pays the bills today; consumer is the land-grab, spending now to win volume tomorrow. You are not buying a jet-parts company or a toy company. You are buying the wager that one firm can be India's answer to Chinese manufacturing in both — and the near-term price of that wager is a company that still loses money at the group level.

01

Contract manufacturing 101: making things for other people's brands

No industry background needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

Most companies you know sell a product with their name on it. A contract manufacturer does the opposite: it is the invisible factory behind the brands. Apple designs the iPhone; contract manufacturers build it. A toy company designs the doll and owns the character; someone else moulds the plastic and packs the box. Aequs is one of those someone-elses — for aircraft parts and for consumer goods. To follow it, you need five ideas.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Contract manufacturingMaking a product to another company's design and specification, for them to sell under their own brand. Aequs never owns the end brand — it owns the capability to build. Revenue comes from purchase orders, not shelf sales.
OEM"Original Equipment Manufacturer" — the brand owner who ultimately sells the product. In aerospace, the OEMs are Airbus and Boeing (and engine-maker Safran); their approved suppliers are the tier-1s. Aequs supplies OEMs and tier-1s.
Vertical integrationDoing many production steps yourself instead of buying them in. Aequs forges, machines, treats and assembles — rather than machining a part someone else forged. More steps in-house = more control over cost, quality and schedule, and more value captured per part.
China+1The global strategy of not depending on China alone — brands adding a second country to their supply chain to cut risk (tariffs, geopolitics, COVID-era shocks). India and Vietnam are the main candidates. Aequs's entire consumer pitch rides on this shift landing in India, and specifically on Aequs.
QualificationThe long, expensive process of getting certified to make a specific aerospace part. It can take years and locks a supplier into a programme for its life. This is the aerospace half's moat — and the reason its revenue is slow to win but hard to lose.
The economics that decide everything: who captures the value

A contract manufacturer's profit lives in a simple gap — the price a brand will pay for a finished, on-spec part minus the cost of every step needed to make it. There are two ways to widen that gap. The first is to do more steps in-house, so you keep the margin that would otherwise go to sub-suppliers (that is vertical integration). The second is to reach scale, so fixed costs — the forge, the machine shop, the moulding lines — spread across more units. Aequs is trying to do both at once, in two industries. In aerospace, integration plus qualification makes each part sticky and profitable. In consumer, the whole game is reaching scale fast enough to make low per-unit margins add up — which is precisely why the consumer arm loses money until the volume arrives.

Why "for other brands" changes how you read the numbers

  • Revenue follows customers' programmes, not consumer demand. Aequs grows when Airbus raises its build rate or a toy brand shifts a product line to India — not when shoppers buy more. Its fortunes are tied to a handful of large customers' decisions.
  • Winning is slow; losing is slow too. Especially in aerospace, it takes years to get onto a programme — but once you're on it, you're usually on it for the life of the aircraft. That cuts both ways: hard to grow overnight, hard to be displaced.
  • Capital comes before revenue. You build the forge, the SEZ, the toy cluster first, then wait for the parts and the orders. Both of Aequs's engines are capital-hungry, which is why debt and capex matter as much as margins.
02

The two-engine company: aerospace and consumer under one roof

Read this once and the whole, odd shape of Aequs snaps into focus.

Almost every manufacturer you can name does one kind of thing. Aequs runs two engines that share nothing but a strategy. One machines metal for aircraft to tolerances measured in microns; the other moulds plastic and metal for toys and kitchens at consumer volumes and consumer prices. They have different customers, different cycles, different margins and different risks. What unites them is a single conviction: that an integrated Indian cluster can under-price and out-reliable Chinese supply — and that the same operating muscle works whether the end product flies or gets unwrapped on a birthday.

Why anyone would run both at once

The obvious question is: why not just be an aerospace company? The answer Aequs is betting on is that its real asset isn't jet-part expertise — it's the ability to stand up a fully-integrated manufacturing cluster in India and fill it with global-brand work. Aerospace was the proving ground: if you can satisfy Airbus's and Boeing's brutal quality bar, you have industrial discipline most consumer suppliers can only dream of. Consumer is the attempt to point that same muscle at a far larger market — toys and appliances are enormous, fast-moving, and right at the centre of the China+1 shift. The risk, of course, is the mirror image: that running two capital-heavy businesses at once means neither gets enough capital to win, and the discipline that suits aerospace is wasted on a price-driven consumer game.

03

The integrated cluster: Aequs's actual moat

The one mechanism that explains why Aequs thinks it can beat China in both verticals.

Strip away the two-industry oddity and Aequs has exactly one big idea, repeated twice: build a self-contained manufacturing ecosystem in a single location, where most of the steps that usually sit in a dozen separate companies happen inside one integrated cluster. This is how a factory town in northern Karnataka positions itself as a credible alternative to Shenzhen or Dongguan. It is worth slowing down on, because it is the source of whatever durable advantage the company has.

What "integrated cluster" means in practice

Making a precision aerospace part normally passes through several independent firms: one forges the raw metal, another machines it, a third does surface treatment and special processes, a fourth assembles, a fifth handles logistics. Every hand-off adds cost, time, freight and a chance for something to go wrong. Aequs's Belagavi cluster tries to put all of those steps behind one gate — forging, machining, heat/surface treatment, assembly and testing co-located inside an aerospace-focused special economic zone. The part never leaves the ecosystem until it's finished. That compresses cost and lead-time in a way a string of scattered suppliers cannot match, and it is the reason a global OEM might trust a single Indian address with a whole component rather than just a machining step.

The device that only fits Aequs: two clusters, one playbook

Here is the construct that no other primer in this sector could borrow. Aequs's value isn't a product — it's a repeatable cluster template. In Belagavi it aimed that template at aerospace and built India's first precision-engineering SEZ; in Koppal it aimed the identical template at toys and built an integrated toy ecosystem. The bet is that this template is the asset — that having proved it can gather forging-to-assembly under one roof for Airbus, it can gather moulding-to-packing under one roof for a toy giant. If that's true, Aequs isn't a jet-parts firm dabbling in toys; it's a cluster-builder that happens to have started in aerospace. If it's false — if the aerospace discipline doesn't transfer and each cluster is really its own hard, separate problem — then the company is two undercapitalised bets wearing one ticker.

A genuine surprise: the plant that machines parts for the world's most demanding aerospace customers, and the plant that moulds toys for global consumer brands, belong to the same Belagavi-headquartered company and spring from the same strategic idea. Most investors meet Aequs expecting a precision-engineering pure-play like Sansera or MTAR — and are startled to learn a big chunk of the growth story is, quite literally, toys.
04

Engine one: the aerospace business

The profitable, qualification-moated core that pays the bills today.

Aequs's aerospace arm is a classic — and genuinely good — precision contract-manufacturing business. It makes flight-critical components and sub-assemblies: machined structural and engine parts, forgings, sheet-metal fabrications and assembled modules that go into commercial aircraft and their engines. Its customers are the apex of the industry — global OEMs like Airbus and Boeing, engine-maker Safran, and the network of tier-1 suppliers that feed them. In FY26 this engine generated roughly ₹1,046 Cr of revenue, up ~27%, and it carries an order book of USD 889 M (≈ ₹7,400 Cr) — years of booked work.

Why aerospace is a quietly excellent business — once you're in

  • Qualification is a moat that works in your favour. Getting approved to make a specific flight-critical part takes years of audits, first-article inspections and test builds. It is a painful cost to win a programme — but once qualified, a supplier typically holds that part for the life of the aircraft programme, often decades. Slow to win, hard to lose.
  • Integration lets Aequs capture more of each part. Because Belagavi does forging, machining, treatment and assembly in one place, Aequs can bid to supply a finished sub-assembly rather than just a machining operation — more value, more margin, and more indispensability per part.
  • The aviation up-cycle is a real tailwind. Airbus and Boeing are trying to lift build rates against multi-year order backlogs, and both are keen to diversify their supply chains beyond traditional geographies. A qualified, low-cost, integrated Indian supplier is exactly what they want more of.
  • It throws off the credibility the whole company runs on. Satisfying aerospace quality regimes is a badge that Aequs can build to world standards — the reputational capital it now spends trying to win consumer customers.
The catch inside the good news

Aerospace's virtues are also its constraints. Revenue is hostage to customers' build rates — if Airbus slows a programme (as the industry's supply-chain snarls have repeatedly forced), Aequs's deliveries slow with it, order book or no order book. Concentration is real: a handful of OEMs and tier-1s drive the book, so losing or being de-prioritised on one big programme hurts. And the business is long-cycle and capital-heavy — the forge and machine shops are expensive, qualification ties up cash before revenue, and it exports, so a swinging rupee moves the maths. This is a good engine; it is not a fast or a forgiving one.

05

Engine two: the consumer & toys business

The young, fast-growing, loss-making engine — and the reason this stock is a bet, not a bond.

If aerospace is the proven core, consumer is the ambition. Aequs contract-manufactures toys, cookware, small appliances and electronics components for global brands looking to source from India instead of China. In FY26 this engine did only about ₹184 Cr of revenue — a fraction of aerospace — but it grew roughly 84%, and by the fourth quarter it already contributed around 17% of group revenue. This is a business in the steep part of its ramp: programmes moving from pilot to full production, new customers being onboarded, capacity being filled.

The flagship of this engine is the toy cluster at Koppal, Karnataka — an attempt to build in India the kind of integrated toy-making ecosystem that historically only China possessed. Making a toy for a global brand isn't just injection-moulding; it's tooling, decoration, electronics, assembly, safety testing and packaging, usually spread across many small Chinese factories. Aequs's pitch is to gather that whole chain into one cluster, so a Western toy company can move a product line to India as a single decision rather than a supply-chain reconstruction project.

Why the consumer engine loses money — for now

A ramping contract-manufacturing line is a cost sponge before it is a profit source. You install moulding and assembly capacity for volumes you don't yet have, you fund tooling and qualification for each new customer programme, and you run the plant below its efficient scale while orders build. Every one of those is a cost today against revenue tomorrow. That is why consumer can grow 84% and still drag group profit — and why its losses are best read not as failure but as the price of the land-grab. The judgement call for an investor is whether the volume arrives fast enough to flip the arithmetic before the cash runs thin. It is genuinely unproven, and it is the crux of the whole company.

The dependency to watch: the consumer engine's success is not fully in Aequs's hands. It needs the China+1 shift to actually move to India — and specifically to Aequs rather than to a rival Indian or Vietnamese contract maker. A brand can admire the Koppal cluster and still place its orders in Vietnam. So consumer revenue is a function of two things Aequs only partly controls: how fast global brands diversify out of China, and how much of that flow it can capture against determined competition.
06

The China+1 bet: why now, and why India

The structural tailwind both engines are riding — and its limits.

Everything in the Aequs story ultimately leans on one macro shift: global brands no longer want to depend on China alone. A decade of tariff wars, a pandemic that froze Chinese supply, and hardening geopolitics have convinced Western OEMs and consumer brands to add a second manufacturing base — the "China+1" strategy. India and Vietnam are the two serious candidates. Aequs is built, in both engines, to be India's answer.

The insight that separates the pitch from the reality

China+1 is real, but it is not a gift — it is a contest, and India is not obviously winning it. In consumer manufacturing especially, Vietnam has been the default first choice for many brands leaving China, and other Indian contract makers are chasing the same slots. So the tailwind explains why the opportunity exists; it does not guarantee that Aequs captures it. The company's real claim is narrower and more interesting than "India is rising": it is that a vertically-integrated cluster — where a brand can source a finished part or product from one ecosystem rather than assembling a supply chain from scratch — is the specific thing that tips a China+1 decision toward India, and toward Aequs. That claim is testable, quarter by quarter, in the consumer revenue line.

None of this makes the bet safe. Tariffs can move again; a strong rupee can erode India's cost edge against Vietnam; and a brand's sourcing decision can take years and still land elsewhere. But the direction of the tailwind is not seriously in doubt — the debate is only about speed, and about how much of the flow Aequs's clusters can actually catch.

07

The company, the founder, and who controls it

A founder-built, Belagavi-headquartered company that only just went public.

Aequs is very much a founder's company. It was built by Aravind Melligeri, its Executive Chairman and CEO, who has spent more than two decades in the aerospace sector and has led the group since its beginnings around 2000. From a base in Belagavi (Belgaum), Karnataka, he set about an unusually ambitious idea for an Indian engineering entrepreneur — not just to machine parts, but to build entire integrated manufacturing clusters, starting with India's first precision-engineering SEZ.

The profit problem: two engines, one bottom line

The single most important idea in the entire investment case — and it is specific to this company.

Here is the tension that defines Aequs, in one comparison. In FY26 the standalone parent (essentially the mature aerospace core) turned a profit of roughly ₹50 Cr — a clean reversal from a prior-year loss. Yet the consolidated group — parent plus subsidiaries, which is where the consumer ramp lives — posted a net loss of about ₹113 Cr. Same company, same year, and the sign of the bottom line flips depending on whether you include the second engine. That single divergence is the whole investment debate compressed into two numbers.

The group makes positive EBITDA (~₹155 Cr, ~13% margin) but a negative bottom line — the gap is eaten by the consumer ramp's costs plus depreciation and interest on two capital-heavy ecosystems. The path to consolidated profit runs through consumer reaching scale. Figures approx.

Read the two engines as a machine with two flywheels. Aerospace is already spinning under its own power — profitable, order-backed, self-justifying. Consumer is being spun up by hand, and right now the effort of turning it costs more than the energy it returns. The entire equity is a wager that the consumer flywheel reaches self-sustaining scale — enough volume that its per-unit margins finally cover its fixed costs — before the drain on the group becomes a problem. Get there, and Aequs becomes a two-engine compounder with a rare integrated position in two China+1 markets. Fall short, and it is a profitable aerospace company carrying a permanent consumer anchor, valued as though both engines already worked.

The metric that settles the thesis — say it precisely

Don't watch revenue growth alone; a loss-making ramp can grow revenue impressively for years. The number that actually settles Aequs is a blend of three, read together every quarter: (1) consumer revenue still compounding at a steep rate (proof the China+1 flow is landing at Aequs, not Vietnam); (2) group EBITDA margin climbing back toward the high-teens as consumer scales and operating leverage kicks in; and (3) the consolidated bottom line crossing into profit — the moment the standalone-vs-consolidated gap closes. That third print is the scoreboard. Aerospace's USD 889 M book tells you the profitable engine keeps humming; consumer's growth tells you the second engine is filling; but only consolidated PAT turning and staying positive proves the two-engine bet actually paid. Everything else is a leading indicator of that one event.

How to think about the valuation — without a P/E to lean on

You cannot value Aequs on earnings, because at the group level there aren't any yet. So the market is valuing it on what the two engines could earn at scale — an aerospace core compounding off a USD 889 M book at improving margins, plus a consumer arm that reaches profitability as volume fills the Koppal cluster. At ~₹12,800 Cr, roughly ten times a single year's revenue for a business that lost money last year, the price already assumes a lot of that future arrives. The honest way to hold it: this is a venture-style bet inside a listed manufacturer — the upside is a genuinely rare two-market China+1 platform; the downside is paying a growth multiple for a capital-heavy firm whose second engine may never earn its cost of capital. The industry and the rivals — two competitive worlds

Aequs competes in two entirely separate arenas at once, against two different sets of players.

Most companies have one competitive set. Aequs has two, because its two engines fight in different markets. In aerospace it is measured against India's precision-engineering names and the global tier-1s. In consumer it is measured against other India- and Vietnam-based contract makers chasing the same China+1 slots. It is rarely the biggest player in either arena — its distinctiveness is being in both.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, in aerospace Aequs is a credible but not dominant player among a strong Indian cohort (Sansera, Azad, MTAR) — the sector tailwind can lift several of them, and Aequs's specific claim is its integrated forge-to-assembly cluster rather than sheer scale. Second, the consumer engine faces the tougher, more open fight: China+1 in toys and durables is a genuine scramble, Vietnam has a head start with many brands, and being early to build an integrated Indian toy cluster is Aequs's answer — but it has to be validated by actual brand wins, not architecture. In neither arena does Aequs win by being biggest; the bet is that being integrated, in both, is a position no single-vertical rival occupies.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A rare two-market position, an unproven bottom line, and a valuation that assumes it all works.

Aequs enters its life as a listed company with something genuinely unusual: a profitable, order-rich aerospace engine and a fast-scaling consumer engine, both aimed at the same China+1 wave, both built on the same integrated-cluster idea. What it does not yet have is proof that running two capital-heavy manufacturing businesses at once produces a profit rather than a stalemate. The debate is not whether the opportunity is real — it plainly is — but whether this particular company can convert both ramps into group-level earnings at the pace its price demands.

The bull case

  • A rare two-market China+1 platform — an integrated position in both precision aerospace and consumer/toys that no single-vertical rival holds.
  • Profitable, order-backed aerospace core — ~₹1,046 Cr revenue on a USD 889 M book, with a qualification moat that makes the business sticky.
  • Consumer ramping fast — ~84% growth as toy, cookware and appliance programmes move into full production; operating leverage still ahead.
  • The integrated-cluster template — a repeatable way to gather a whole supply chain into one Indian ecosystem, the thing that tips a China+1 decision toward Aequs.
  • Founder-led, unencumbered promoter stake — Melligeri controls ~59% with no pledge, aligned to the long build.

The bear case

  • Group still loses money — a ~₹113 Cr consolidated FY26 loss; scaling two capital-heavy engines to profit at once is unproven and the defining risk.
  • Customer concentration, twice over — a few aerospace OEMs and a few consumer brands; losing or being de-prioritised on one big customer in either engine hurts.
  • Aerospace is cycle- and build-rate-dependent — an order book only earns as fast as Airbus/Boeing ramp; qualification and delivery are slow.
  • Consumer depends on a shift it doesn't control — China+1 must actually move to India, and to Aequs rather than Vietnam or another Indian rival.
  • Capital, working capital & FX — two capital-intensive ecosystems, export exposure, and a rich recent-IPO valuation priced for both engines succeeding.
The bottom line

Aequs is the most unusual manufacturer in this part of the market: a single Belagavi company that machines flight-critical parts for Airbus and Boeing and, with the same strategic muscle, moulds toys and cookware for global consumer brands — two engines, one integrated-cluster bet on India as the world's China+1 factory. The aerospace engine is already good; the consumer engine is the whole question. The next few years answer it in one checkable event, not a slogan: the quarter the consolidated bottom line crosses into the black and stays there — the moment the standalone profit and the consolidated result stop disagreeing. Until that print arrives, Aequs is a bet on a template; after it, it is a business. Watch the consumer revenue curve and the group margin every quarter, because they are the countdown to that single number.
