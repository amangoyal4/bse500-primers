Finolex Cables, the cautious, cash-heavy grandfather of Indian wires

A full walk through one of India's oldest wires-and-cables makers — the Pune company that once wired the nation's telephone network, still sells house wire through 5,000-plus dealers, is trying (late) to build a consumer-electricals brand, and quietly sits on a treasury and a stake in a listed pipes company worth a large slice of its own stock-market value. Why it earns money differently from Polycab or R R Kabel, why its "profit" leans on investment income and a family feud runs beneath it, and what has to change for the cheapest big cable stock to stop being cheap.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Finolex Cables makes the electrical and communication cables that run through India's buildings and networks — house wire behind the walls, power and industrial cables, and the telephone, LAN and optical-fibre cables that were once its claim to fame — plus a small and growing range of consumer electrical goods (switches, fans, water heaters, lighting, conduit pipes). It is a Pune-based, Chhabria-family business, one of the oldest brands in the sector, and it is defined less by how fast it grows than by how conservatively it is run: nearly debt-free, sitting on a large pile of investments, and — for years — content to grow slower than flashier rivals.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • Electrical cables are the engine; the rest is small. Electrical cables — house wire, power and industrial cable — are roughly 88% of revenue and grew 22% in FY26. Communication cables (telephone, LAN, optical fibre), the company's historic heart, are down to about 8% and barely growing. The consumer-electricals (FMEG) push everyone talks about is still tiny — the "Others" segment was only ₹261 Cr, about 4% of sales.
  • A big chunk of the company isn't the cable business at all. Finolex carries a ~₹4,195 Cr book of investments, almost no debt, and owns 32.9% of Finolex Industries — a separate listed company that makes PVC pipes. In FY26 the pipes stake alone added about ₹91 Cr to consolidated profit, and investment/other income supplied a large share of pre-tax earnings. The cable factory is only part of what you own.
  • It is the cheap one — and the slow one. Finolex trades near 22–24× earnings, well below Polycab, KEI and Havells, which sit at much richer multiples. The debate is whether that discount is deserved (a slow-grower with a family feud and lazy cash) or an opportunity (a cheap, debt-free brand that could finally accelerate).
In plain terms

Finolex Cables is the tortoise of the Indian cable industry. While Polycab and R R Kabel sprinted — building brands, expanding capacity, pushing into fans and switches — Finolex advanced steadily, kept its balance sheet pristine, and let cash accumulate rather than betting it aggressively. The result is a company that is safe, cheap and under-marketed all at once: strong in house wire, quietly earning interest and dividends on a large treasury, and only now waking up to the consumer-electricals opportunity its peers grabbed a decade ago. The whole investment question is whether the tortoise decides to run.

01

Wires & cables 101: how a cable maker earns

No engineering needed — just how metal, plastic and a name turn into a margin.

A wire is a metal conductor — copper or aluminium — wrapped in insulation (PVC or cross-linked plastic) that stops it shorting, shocking or catching fire, with armour and outer sheathing added for tougher cables. The economics are unusual because the metal, not the manufacturing, dominates the bill: a cable maker earns a thin, steady spread on top of a large, swinging commodity cost. Four levers move the whole business.

The four levers that move a cable maker's profit
LeverWhat it means — and why it matters
Metal costCopper and aluminium are roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of a cable's cost. So revenue and margins ride the metal cycle — a cable company is, financially, a copper business with a manufacturing markup bolted on.
Pass-throughBecause metal dominates cost, makers try to pass copper-price moves on to buyers. Rising copper lifts revenue and can hand inventory gains; a sudden fall can leave a maker holding expensive stock. The margin is the spread, not the metal.
Volume & mixPlants carry fixed costs, so running them fuller drops more to profit. Growth means more tonnes sold — and, crucially, a richer mix (premium wire, special cable, branded consumer goods) that earns more per kilo than plain house wire.
Brand & channelThe one thing that lifts a cable maker above a commodity trader: a trusted name and a deep dealer-and-electrician network that lets it charge a small premium and collect its money reliably. This is the real moat — and the whole "unorganised versus branded" contest.
Why a "safety premium" is the whole game

Two coils of 1.5 mm house wire from two makers are near-identical strands of copper — so in theory this should be a cut-throat commodity. What lets a branded maker like Finolex earn a real margin is trust in a product you cannot inspect after it's plastered into a wall. A wiring fault can burn a house down, so the electrician recommends a name he knows won't fail, the dealer stocks a brand that sells and pays on time, and the homeowner accepts a few percent premium for a warranty and a familiar logo. That premium is tiny per metre but enormous across millions of kilometres — and it is defended by reputation and cost, not patents, which is exactly why deep-pocketed newcomers think they can muscle in.

Finolex's twist: it makes its own copper rod

Most branded cable makers buy copper rod — the finger-thick wire stock that gets drawn down into conductors. Finolex runs its own continuous copper-rod plant, converting cathode into rod in-house. In FY26 this internal copper-rod activity was booked at about ₹2,142 Cr (most of it consumed within the company rather than sold outside). That backward integration gives Finolex tighter control of quality and cost on its single biggest input — a genuinely different starting point from a pure converter, and part of why the company has always described itself as a manufacturer first and a marketer second.

03

The company and the Chhabria family

From post-Partition traders in Pune, to the nation's phone-cable supplier, to a family at war.

Finolex is the creation of two brothers, Pralhad (P. P.) and Kishan (K. P.) Chhabria, who built a Pune business from the late 1950s selling electrical wire and, later, PVC insulation. The group became a national name in the 1980s as the largest maker of jelly-filled telephone cables during India's landline build-out. Two listed companies eventually emerged from it — Finolex Cables (wires and cables) and Finolex Industries (PVC pipes) — tied together by cross-shareholdings. After the founders' generation passed, control became the subject of a long and bitter family dispute.

Timeline

  • 1967

  • 1981

  • 1990s–2000s
    Landline demand fades with mobile telephony; Finolex pivots hard into electrical wires and cables, builds a dealer network, adds optical-fibre and LAN cable, and integrates backward into copper rod.
  • 2011
    A memorandum between Finolex Cables and Finolex Industries on how each would vote its cross-holding in the other — later a flashpoint in the family feud.
  • 2016
    Founder Pralhad Chhabria dies; a succession fight erupts between his son Prakash and nephew Deepak over who controls the group's holding vehicle, Orbit Electricals.
  • 2019–2023
    The dispute runs through the NCLT, NCLAT and the Supreme Court — an unusually public corporate-governance battle for a company this staid.
  • Aug 2024
    The NCLAT upholds the shareholder vote that removed Deepak Chhabria from the board (72% voted against his reappointment), consolidating Prakash's faction in control — though litigation over the Orbit shares continues.
  • FY26
    Revenue ₹6,321 Cr (+19%); standalone PAT ₹623 Cr; nearly debt-free with a ~₹4,195 Cr investment book and a 32.9% stake in Finolex Industries. Dividend raised to ₹9/share.

Ownership & the two-company knot

  • Promoters: the Chhabria family holds roughly 36%, most of it through Orbit Electricals (~30.7%). Prakash Chhabria's faction controls Orbit; the settled outcome of the feud is that Prakash's side runs both listed companies.
  • The cross-holding: here is the knot to hold in your head. Finolex Cables owns 32.9% of Finolex Industries (the pipes maker), and Finolex Industries owns 14.5% of Finolex Cables. Each company owns a big chunk of the other — a structure the family built and the feud tried to unpick.
  • Two separate listed companies — do not confuse them. Finolex Cables (NSE: FINCABLES) is this primer: wires and cables. Finolex Industries (NSE: FINPIPE) is a different listed company that makes PVC pipes and fittings for agriculture and plumbing. They share a name, a family and cross-shareholdings, but they are distinct businesses with distinct shares. When you read "Finolex," check which one.
  • Institutions: domestic funds hold ~16% and foreign investors ~10% — a normal float for a mid-cap, but note Finolex Industries' 14.5% is effectively a friendly, permanent block inside the register.
Why the feud matters to a shareholder: a controlling family that spent years suing itself is a governance flag, not a footnote. The dispute distracted management, delayed decisions, and left open questions about how the group's cross-holdings and cash would ultimately be used. With Prakash's faction now in control the acute phase has passed — but the episode is part of why Finolex trades at a discount: the market has learned to price in family risk here.
04

Electrical cables — the engine

Nearly nine-tenths of revenue, and the part of Finolex that actually compounds.

Everything healthy about Finolex's operations lives here. The electrical-cables segment — house wire plus power, industrial, flexible and special cables — was about ₹5,490 Cr in FY26 and grew 22%, comfortably the fastest-moving part of the company and the reason revenue crossed ₹6,300 Cr. It sells largely through a retail dealer network for construction, renovation and industry, with the Finolex brand carrying a genuine premium in house wire.

House wire is the branded, high-margin, retail-facing heart. The wider cable range extends Finolex into infrastructure, industry and niche applications where technical spec matters more than shelf appeal.

Where Finolex's moat actually sits

  • A decades-old brand in house wire. Finolex has sold wire to Indian households and electricians for two generations. In the one category where trust is worth most — the wire you cannot see once the wall is closed — that heritage translates into a real, defensible price premium.
  • The dealer-and-electrician network. House wire is usually chosen by the electrician or contractor, not the homeowner. Finolex's network of thousands of dealers and the loyalty of the tradespeople who specify its wire is a slow, expensive asset to replicate — the main barrier that keeps unbranded local makers at bay.
  • Cost control from backward integration. Owning the copper-rod step and running lean, debt-free plants gives Finolex a low-cost position. In a commodity, the low-cost producer endures — and Finolex has always leaned on manufacturing discipline rather than marketing flash.
The unorganised → branded shift (the sector's quiet tailwind)

A large share of India's wire-and-cable demand has always been met by small, unbranded local makers. Three forces keep nudging that spend toward branded players: GST and formalisation erode the informal sector's tax-and-price edge; rising safety awareness means fewer people want cheap wire behind a new home's walls; and tighter standards enforcement favours makers who can certify their product. Every point of share that moves from unorganised to branded is growth for Finolex on top of the market's own expansion — a tailwind that can run for years regardless of the economic cycle, and one Finolex's trusted-house-wire position is well placed to capture.

The working-capital reality: cables are a working-capital-hungry business. Finolex carries large copper inventories (a commodity bet in itself) and extends credit to dealers, so growth ties up cash in stock and receivables even when the P&L looks fine — and a sharp copper move can push inventory gains or losses straight through margins. The saving grace, unusually, is that Finolex has so much surplus cash that funding its own working capital has never been a strain.
05

Communication cables — the fading heritage

The business that made Finolex famous, now a tenth of what it was in importance.

There is a real poignancy in Finolex's communication-cable segment. In the 1980s and 1990s, jelly-filled telephone cables — copper pairs packed in a water-blocking jelly, strung across the country for the landline network — made Finolex a household name and the largest supplier of its kind in India. Then mobile phones arrived, the landline stopped growing, and the category that built the company faded to about ₹500 Cr, roughly 8% of FY26 revenue, and shrinking slightly.

What's inside communication cables today

  • Jelly-filled telephone cable (JFTC) — the legacy product, now a small, declining line as copper landlines give way to wireless and fibre.
  • Optical-fibre cable (OFC) — the potential revival story: the glass-fibre cable that carries broadband, 5G backhaul and data-centre links. Finolex's OFC volumes have grown (up ~30% in a recent year), but fibre prices have fallen so sharply that revenue slipped even as volume rose — a vivid example of a growing product that isn't yet a growing business.
  • LAN & coaxial cable — networking and data cable for buildings and campuses; steady, competitive, unglamorous.
The one paradox worth remembering here

Finolex shipped more optical fibre and earned less for it. Global fibre over-supply and a price collapse meant a ~30% jump in OFC volume still translated into flat-to-lower segment revenue. That is the trap of a commoditising technology product: the demand is real and rising (India is laying fibre everywhere), but without pricing power the volume growth leaks away before it reaches the P&L. For the heritage segment to become a growth engine again, fibre prices have to stabilise — and Finolex has to win a bigger share of India's fibre roll-out. Until then, communication cables are a legacy to be managed, not a lever to be pulled.

The company the public still thinks of as "the telephone-cable maker" now earns nineteen rupees in electrical cable for every rupee it earns in communication cable.
06

FMEG — the catch-up bet

The small "Others" segment that carries most of the company's future-growth hopes.

FMEG — Fast-Moving Electrical Goods — is the branded consumer-electricals business every cable maker now chases: switches, fans, LED lighting, water heaters, MCBs and conduit pipe, sold through the same dealers that carry the wire. For Finolex it is the answer to a nagging question — how does a cable company earn a richer, brand-led margin and a higher valuation? The trouble is that Finolex is years behind its peers, and the segment is still tiny: the "Others" line was about ₹261 Cr in FY26, roughly 4% of revenue, and grew only 2%.

Switches & switchgearModular switches, MCBs, protection devices — higher-margin, "specified" products that ride the same electrician who fits the wire.
Fans & lightingCeiling fans and LED bulbs, battens and fixtures — large, brand-driven, competitive categories where Finolex is a small challenger.

Why Finolex is late — and what "success" would look like

Building a consumer-electrical brand takes years of advertising, a widening product range and distribution reach before scale covers the cost — the journey Havells and Polycab funded aggressively a decade or more ago. Finolex, true to character, moved slowly and spent lightly, so it sits far behind with a segment still measured in low hundreds of crores against rivals doing thousands. "Success" is not complicated to describe: the "Others" line needs to compound at a high rate off its small base, cross into the hundreds and then the thousands of crores, and do it profitably — turning FMEG from a rounding error into a real second engine. Whether Finolex has the marketing appetite to make that happen, after years of not trying, is the single biggest open question about its growth.

07

The treasury and the pipes stake — the part that isn't a cable company

The defining Finolex quirk: a large chunk of what you buy is cash, investments and a share of a pipe maker.

This is the section that separates Finolex from every other name in the sector. Strip a normal cable company down and you find factories, inventory and receivables. Strip Finolex down and you also find a ~₹4,195 Cr book of investments, almost no debt (borrowings of ~₹19 Cr against reserves of ~₹6,055 Cr), and a 32.9% stake in Finolex Industries, a separately listed PVC-pipes company. A meaningful slice of Finolex's market value is not the cable operation at all — it is a treasury and a shareholding.

The "how much am I paying for the factory?" test

Here is a device that works for almost no other cable stock. Finolex's book value is about ₹398 per share — and roughly ₹274 of that per share is financial investments, not plant and inventory. Put differently, close to seven-tenths of Finolex's net worth sits in mutual funds, bonds and a pipe-company stake rather than in the cable business that gives the company its name. When you buy the share near ₹1,050, a large portion of the price is a claim on a securities portfolio and a slice of a pipes maker; only the remainder is the wire-and-cable operation that actually earns ~₹620 Cr of operating profit. That is why Finolex screens as "cheap" on a simple P/E — a chunk of the value is essentially a low-return investment fund wearing a cable company's clothes.

The genuine surprise: the pipes company that props up the profit line

In FY26 Finolex Cables earned ₹623 Cr of standalone profit — but its consolidated profit was ₹714 Cr. The extra ~₹91 Cr came not from any cable it made, but from its 32.9% share of Finolex Industries' profit — a pipes business it doesn't operate. And here is the twist within the twist: that pipes contribution fell from about ₹157 Cr the year before, because PVC pipes had a weak year. So Finolex's consolidated profit was nearly flat year-on-year (~₹701 Cr → ~₹714 Cr) even though its own cable business grew profit 14% — the drag was a pipe company on the other side of a cross-holding. A shareholder who watches only the headline consolidated number can badly misread how the actual cable business is doing.

Cash that mostly sits still: the flip side of a fortress balance sheet is idle capital. Finolex's treasury throws off steady interest and dividend income — which flatters reported earnings (more on that in the financials section) — but earns a low return compared with what re-investing in FMEG, capacity or acquisitions could yield. Conservative shareholders like the safety; impatient ones see a pile of money working far below its potential and want it deployed or returned. The rising dividend (₹9/share in FY26) is a first, modest answer to that pressure.
08

Why Finolex grew slower — the cost of caution

The same conservatism that keeps it safe is why it fell behind the pack.

For much of the last fifteen years, Finolex had every advantage — an old brand, a clean balance sheet, a pile of cash — and still watched Polycab, KEI and R R Kabel grow faster and win a richer valuation. Understanding why is understanding the whole Finolex character, because the traits that held it back are the same ones that make it safe.

The question that decides the next decade

Finolex's FY26 — electrical cables up 22%, revenue up 19%, a dividend hike, the family feud settled — hints that the tortoise may finally be picking up its pace. The bet a buyer is really making is behavioural: that a newly-unified, cash-rich company chooses to spend on brand, capacity and FMEG rather than let the money accumulate as before. There is no structural reason Finolex cannot grow like its peers — it has the balance sheet to out-invest all of them. The only thing that has been missing is the will. Every quarter now is a test of whether that will has actually changed.

09

The rivals — and the new-entrant shock

A comfortable branded oligopoly that two industrial giants just decided to crash.

India's organised wires-and-cables market has been a pleasant, growing club of branded players. Finolex sits inside it as a respected but mid-sized name, out-grown in recent years by Polycab (the clear leader), KEI Industries and R R Kabel, and out-marketed on the consumer side by Havells. Then, in early 2025, the club's comfort evaporated: two of India's biggest industrial houses announced they were entering the sector.

The shock of early 2025

Within weeks of each other, two giants declared entry into wires and cables:

  • UltraTech Cement (Aditya Birla group) announced a large capex to enter the cables-and-wires segment, extending its construction-materials play into the wiring that goes into the same buildings its cement builds.
  • The Adani group, via Kutch Copper, formed a cables-and-wires joint venture (Praneetha Ecocables), backed by its copper-smelting ambitions and vast distribution muscle.

The market reaction was sharp: on the announcements, shares across the sector — Polycab, KEI, R R Kabel, Havells and Finolex — fell heavily in a day. The fear is simple: well-capitalised newcomers with their own copper and a cement-dealer network could compete on price, squeeze industry margins, and trigger a broad de-rating.

How the threat lands specifically on Finolex

The new-entrant risk hits the whole sector, but it lands on Finolex with a peculiar mix. On the defensive side, Finolex is the best-insulated of the group: no debt, a huge cash cushion, a low-cost integrated plant, and the lowest valuation — there is simply less premium to lose. On the offensive side, it is the worst-placed to respond, because the classic answer to a price war — out-invest, out-market, out-innovate on the consumer side — is exactly the muscle Finolex spent years not building. A price war rewards the cheap, cash-rich survivor; a battle for the consumer's brand loyalty rewards the aggressive marketer. Finolex is emphatically the former and emphatically not the latter.

The point that isn't debatable: the entry announcements have permanently raised the perceived risk on the sector's margins and multiples, whatever the eventual volume impact. For Finolex specifically, deep pockets are a real defence against a price war — but they are no defence at all against being left behind in the consumer-electricals race the newcomers and incumbents are both running.

Financials & valuation

Solid growth, thin-ish operating margins flattered by investment income, and a distinctly cheap price.

Finolex's financials tell a consistent story: a well-run, debt-free cable maker with roughly a 10% operating margin, decent returns, and a reported profit that leans noticeably on other/investment income from its treasury and the pipes stake. The result is a company that looks cheaper than its peers on a headline multiple — partly because a real portion of its value is low-returning cash.

The one metric that settles the thesis

Ignore the headline P/E for a moment; it is muddied by the cash. The number that actually decides whether Finolex deserves to re-rate is the growth and profitability of its operating cable-and-FMEG business, measured net of investment income. Concretely: can electrical-cable revenue keep growing at peer-like rates and can the "Others"/FMEG line finally scale from ₹261 Cr into the high hundreds — profitably — so that operating profit growth stops depending on the treasury and the pipes dividend? If the answer becomes yes, the discount to Polycab and KEI has no reason to persist. If operating profit keeps growing only in line with other income, the market is right to treat Finolex as a cheap, cash-heavy slow-grower. The tell is in the segment lines, not the consolidated bottom line.

How to read the discount: Finolex is cheap for reasons, not by accident — slower historical growth, a sub-scale FMEG, a lingering governance shadow, and a big block of low-returning cash that drags return ratios. The bull case is that several of those reasons are fixable (the feud is settled, growth has picked up, cash can be deployed or returned). The bear case is that a company that spent fifteen years being cautious rarely turns aggressive because a lawyer's letter finally stopped arriving.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A safe, cheap, well-made business — waiting to prove it wants to grow.

Finolex Cables is a genuinely good operator sitting on a genuinely strong balance sheet, riding the same electrification-and-construction tailwind as the rest of the sector — but carrying a self-imposed handicap of caution that has cost it a decade of relative growth. The investment debate is almost entirely about whether the newly-unified company finally uses its advantages, not whether the core wire business works.

The bull case

  • Fortress balance sheet — debt-free with a ~₹4,195 Cr investment book; the best-insulated major against a price war and downturns.
  • Cheap — trades at ~22–24× vs much richer peers, and a chunk of value is cash and a pipes stake that could be surfaced.
  • Trusted house-wire brand — decades of goodwill in the one category where trust commands a premium.
  • Feud settled — with Prakash's faction in control, the governance distraction has eased; FY26 growth (+19%) hints at a quicker pace.
  • Optionality — a real FMEG runway from a tiny base, plus cash that could fund brand, capacity or an acquisition if management chooses.

The bear case

  • Chronic slow grower — out-grown by Polycab, KEI and R R Kabel for years; caution is cultural, not circumstantial.
  • Sub-scale FMEG — a decade behind peers at ~4% of revenue; the "catch-up" is a hope, not a plan with a track record.
  • Profit leans on other income — a large share of pre-tax profit is treasury and pipes-dividend income, not cable-making.
  • Idle capital — a big cash pile earning low returns drags ROE; deployment has been talked about far longer than done.
  • Governance & new entrants — a family that litigated for years, plus Adani/UltraTech arriving to test the whole sector's margins.
The bottom line

Finolex is the value corner of a growth sector: cheaper, safer and slower than its rivals, with an old brand, a spotless balance sheet, and a large treasury that the market only half counts because it earns so little. The whole case rests on one behavioural shift — that a company which spent fifteen years hoarding cash and under-marketing decides, now that the family war is over, to actually spend. Watch two very concrete things: the "Others"/FMEG line climbing out of its ₹261 Cr rut into the high hundreds of crores, and the first sign that the ₹4,195 Cr sitting in mutual funds gets put to work — a capacity announcement, a brand push, a buy-out, a much bigger buyback. The day Finolex treats its cash as fuel rather than a cushion is the day the discount to Polycab starts to close.
