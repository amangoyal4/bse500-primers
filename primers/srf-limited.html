SRF Limited, the fluorine-and-molecules compounder

A complete walk through India's premier fluorine-chemistry house — how a 1970s tyre-cord textile mill became a maker of complex, patented molecules for the world's biggest agrochemical and pharma innovators, why refrigerant gases run in a boom-and-bust "F-gas cycle", and how a commoditised films-and-textiles tail keeps dragging on the crown-jewel chemicals engine that the market really pays for.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

SRF is a diversified chemicals multinational built around a rare industrial skill: fluorine chemistry. It makes four broad things — (1) chemicals (the crown jewel, ~half of revenue: complex custom-made molecules for global agro/pharma innovators, plus refrigerant gases and fluoropolymers); (2) packaging films (the plastic films that wrap your food and snacks); (3) technical textiles (industrial fabrics — tyre cord, belting, yarns); and (4) a small "other" of coated fabrics. Controlled by the Bharat Ram family (~50% promoter stake), it is one of the marquee names in the Indian speciality-chemicals story.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Chemicals is the profit engine; everything else is ballast. In FY26 the Chemicals Business alone did ₹7,779 Cr of revenue (~49% of the total) and grew 16% — and it earns far fatter margins than films or textiles. When analysts value SRF as a "speciality chemicals" company, this is the segment they mean; the rest is a cyclical, lower-margin tail that dilutes the blended picture.
  • FY26 was a two-speed year inside chemicals. Fluorochemicals (refrigerant gases) had a record year on firm HFC prices and strong volumes, while specialty chemicals (the custom-synthesis business) was subdued — hit by aggressive Chinese pricing and global agrochemical destocking. The two halves of the crown jewel moved in opposite directions.
  • It's spending big to grow. SRF is deep in a multi-year capex cycle — a raised ₹2,300 Cr refrigerants complex in Odisha, a fluoropolymer (PTFE) plant with Chemours, and new pharma-intermediate capacity — funded partly by debt. Net debt was ~₹3,850 Cr at FY26-end. Consolidated PAT still jumped 47% to ₹1,835 Cr, but the stock trades on a premium ~43× earnings that assumes the capex and the specialty-chem recovery pay off.
In plain terms

Think of SRF as two very different companies wearing one badge. One is a high-skill "molecule factory" — it takes a patented agro or pharma compound designed by a global giant like Bayer or Syngenta and figures out how to make it at industrial scale, safely and cheaply, using tricky fluorine chemistry that few rivals can copy. That business is sticky, high-margin, and the reason SRF trades at a rich multiple. The other is a cyclical commodity maker — refrigerant gases, plastic films and industrial fabrics — whose profits swing wildly with global supply, Chinese competition and the auto cycle. SRF's whole story is: can the clever molecule business grow fast enough to outrun the boring commodity businesses dragging on it — while it borrows heavily to build the next wave of capacity?

01

What SRF actually is: fluorine, films & fabrics

One company, four businesses, one common thread — chemistry skill.

SRF is not easy to categorise in one line, and that's the point: it is a diversified industrial group that has deliberately spread across four businesses so no single cycle can sink it. But the businesses are not equal. The connective tissue — and the source of nearly all the profit growth — is chemistry, and specifically fluorine chemistry: the ability to handle fluorine, one of the most reactive and dangerous elements in the periodic table, and bolt it onto molecules to make them more potent, more durable or more effective.

Why fluorine is the secret ingredient

Fluorine turns up in a startling share of modern products. Roughly a fifth to a third of all new agrochemicals and pharmaceuticals now contain at least one fluorine atom, because adding fluorine to a molecule often makes a crop-protection chemical work at lower doses, or a drug survive longer in the body. But fluorine is hard — it's corrosive, hazardous and needs specialised reactors and deep safety know-how. Companies that master it (a short global list) enjoy a genuine moat. SRF's entire chemicals franchise sits on this foundation: the same fluorine skill feeds refrigerant gases, custom molecules, and now fluoropolymers.

The one distinction to hold onto

Inside "Chemicals" there are two very different sub-businesses, and confusing them is the single most common mistake with SRF. Specialty chemicals = making patented, complex molecules to order for a named customer (a service business — think a boutique tailor). Fluorochemicals = making standardised commodity gases (chiefly refrigerants) sold on price into a global market (a volume business — think a mill). The first is high-margin, sticky and secular; the second is high-barrier but cyclical, with pricing that booms and busts. Both use fluorine skill; they behave nothing alike. Sections 04 and 05 take each in turn.

02

The company and who controls it

From a Shri Ram-family tyre-cord mill to a global chemistry house.

SRF's roots run back to 1970, when it was founded as Shri Ram Fibres Limited — a subsidiary of the old DCM (Delhi Cloth Mills) group — to make nylon tyre cord fabric, the reinforcement inside truck and car tyres. It is still controlled by that lineage: the Bharat Ram family, descendants of the industrialist Sir Shri Ram. The company only became a chemicals business in the late 1980s, and only became the speciality-chemicals star it's known as today in the 2000s.

  • 2010s

  • 2020s
    Rides the "China+1" speciality-chemicals re-rating; grows the specialty-chem book at a rapid clip; scales refrigerants for the HFC quota era.
  • FY26
    Record fluorochemicals year on firm HFC prices; specialty chemicals subdued on Chinese pricing. Revenue ₹15,787 Cr, PAT ₹1,835 Cr (+47%). Raises the Odisha refrigerants capex to ₹2,300 Cr; defers the Indore BOPP project; advances the Chemours fluoropolymer JV.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters — the Bharat Ram family (~50.3%). Arun Bharat Ram is Chairman Emeritus; his sons Ashish Bharat Ram (Chairman & Managing Director) and Kartik Bharat Ram (Joint Managing Director) run the company. A long-tenured family promoter with roughly half the equity means patient, owner-operator decision-making — helpful for a business that plants multi-year capex bets.
  • Global footprint. ~11 manufacturing plants across India, Thailand, South Africa and Hungary, exporting to 90+ countries — a genuine multinational, not just an Indian exporter. The Dahej (Gujarat) complex is the chemicals nerve-centre.
  • Balance sheet. Not debt-free — SRF is in an invest-for-growth phase. Net debt was ~₹3,850 Cr at FY26-end (gross borrowings ~₹5,083 Cr), funding a heavy capex programme. This is the key difference from an asset-light compounder: SRF is levering up to build capacity (Section 08).

The clinical distinction: revenue mix vs profit mix

In FY26 the Chemicals Business ran an operating margin in the low-30s per cent range (its Q4 operating profit of ₹783 Cr on ₹2,448 Cr of revenue implies ~32%), whereas Packaging Films earned single-digit-to-low-teens margins (Q4: ₹154 Cr on ₹1,596 Cr ≈ 10%) and Technical Textiles the low-teens (Q4: ₹65 Cr on ₹483 Cr ≈ 13%). Stack those up and chemicals contributes a clear majority of group operating profit despite being under half of revenue.

Why "diversified" is a double-edged sword

The four-business spread is genuine de-risking: when specialty chemicals stumbled in FY26, a record fluorochemicals year and a films recovery cushioned the group, and PAT still rose 47%. That's the bull case for diversification — the cycles don't all trough together. But it's also margin dilution: bolting two commodity businesses (films, textiles) onto a high-margin speciality business drags the blended margin down and, arguably, keeps the whole company on a lower multiple than a pure-play speciality-chem name would command. The market's persistent question: would SRF be worth more if it spun the commodity tail off and became a clean fluorine-chemistry pure-play? Management's answer, so far, is that the cash from the cyclical businesses funds the chemistry growth — so they stay.

04

Specialty chemicals: the custom-synthesis crown jewel

The single most important thing to understand about SRF — its re-rating engine.

If you learn one thing about SRF, learn this business. Specialty chemicals — also called custom synthesis / CSM (custom synthesis & manufacturing) or, in pharma, CDMO — is where SRF makes complex, patented molecules to order for the world's largest agrochemical and pharmaceutical innovators. It is high-margin, sticky, chemistry-led, and a prime "China+1" beneficiary. It is the reason SRF trades like a speciality-chemicals company rather than a commodity chemicals maker.

What "custom synthesis" actually means

A global innovator — say a Bayer, Syngenta, Corteva or a big pharma company — invents and patents a new crop-protection molecule or a new drug. Inventing it is one thing; manufacturing it at industrial scale, safely, consistently and cheaply is a completely different skill, often involving 8–12 hazardous chemical steps. Rather than build that capability in-house, the innovator outsources the manufacturing to a specialist like SRF. SRF's chemists work out the process, build a dedicated plant, and supply the innovator — often the intermediate stages, sometimes the finished active ingredient (AI) — under a long-term relationship.

Why this business earns fat, durable margins

Three things make custom synthesis special. (1) High switching costs. Getting a supplier "qualified" to make a patented molecule takes years of audits, trials and regulatory sign-off; once SRF is in, the innovator rarely re-tenders — ripping out a proven supplier mid-patent-life is risky and slow. (2) Chemistry as a moat. These are hazardous, multi-step, fluorine-heavy processes; only a handful of firms worldwide can run them safely at scale, so it's not a price auction. (3) It's a service, not a commodity. Pricing reflects capability and reliability, not a spot market — which is why margins are far above the commodity fluorochemicals business. The result: a business that has historically grown fast (SRF's specialty-chem book compounded at a very high rate through the late 2010s and early 2020s) at margins the commodity tail can only dream of.

Why it's the re-rating driver — and the "China+1" angle

For two decades, China dominated the world's supply of chemical intermediates. But rising Chinese costs, environmental crackdowns, and — after COVID and geopolitics — a global desire to de-risk supply chains away from China ("China+1"), pushed innovators to seek reliable non-Chinese partners. India, with its chemistry talent and lower costs, became the obvious alternative, and SRF one of its flagship beneficiaries. Every new molecule SRF wins, every new plant it dedicates to an innovator, deepens this moat and lengthens the growth runway. This is the story that lifted SRF's multiple from a commodity-chemicals rating to a speciality-chemicals premium.

The FY26 reality check: the specialty-chem story hit a rough patch in FY26. A global agrochemical destocking cycle (customers had over-ordered post-COVID and were running down inventory) plus aggressive Chinese pricing and deferred customer offtake left the specialty-chemicals sub-business "subdued" — Chairman Ashish Bharat Ram called the current China pricing "unsustainable". Management insists the long-term foundation is being strengthened: an improved product mix, cost initiatives, and a strong pipeline (in H1 FY26 it launched 1 new active ingredient, 3 agro products and 1 pharma product, with more AIs and pharma intermediates in development, plus a new ₹180 Cr Dahej pharma-intermediates plant). Watch this segment's volume-and-pricing recovery — it is the swing factor for the whole SRF thesis.
05

Fluorochemicals & the refrigerant-gas cycle

The other half of the chemicals engine — high-barrier, but cyclical.

The second sub-engine inside Chemicals is fluorochemicals: chiefly refrigerant gases (the "F-gases" that make your air-conditioner and fridge cold — R-32, R-125 and their blends), plus industrial fluorochemicals and, increasingly, fluoropolymers. Unlike specialty chemicals, this is a commodity — standardised gases sold on price into a global market — but a commodity protected by a very high barrier to entry (fluorine handling) and shaped by an unusual regulatory force: the global phase-down of refrigerants under the Montreal Protocol's Kigali Amendment.

The refrigerant supercycle — how F-gases boom and bust

Refrigerant demand rises steadily with air-conditioning penetration in a hot, growing economy like India. But pricing is cyclical and can be violent. When global supply is tight — because rivals under-invest, or a Chinese plant shuts, or quota rules bite — refrigerant prices spike and margins balloon; SRF makes a fortune. When everyone adds capacity into the boom, prices crash and margins collapse. This boom-bust is the "F-gas cycle", and it's why fluorochemicals profits swing far more than specialty-chem profits from year to year.

The Kigali / HFC phase-down — why regulation creates the opportunity

Old refrigerants (CFCs, then HCFCs) damaged the ozone layer and were banned. Their replacements — HFCs like R-32 and R-125 — don't harm ozone but are potent greenhouse gases, so the world agreed (under the 2016 Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol) to phase them down too. Crucially, governments do this with quotas: each country's HFC production/consumption is capped and then ratcheted down over time. India is a "developing" (Article-5) country: its baseline is set on 2024–2026 output, with a freeze in 2028 and cuts thereafter. This is why SRF is racing to build refrigerant capacity right now — the more it produces during the baseline years, the larger its permanent quota (its "grandfathered" right to make and sell HFCs) once the cap locks in. Quota = future franchise. It's a land-grab with a regulatory clock.

The demand backdrop is favourable: management pegs domestic R-32 demand at ~17,000–18,000 tonnes and rising with India's AC boom, and expects to redirect exports toward the growing home market as the demand-supply balance stays firm. FY26 was, on this front, a record year for fluorochemicals in both realisations and volumes — the offset that carried the group while specialty chemicals lagged.

The strategic logic in one line: fluorine handling is a scarce skill, refrigerant demand grows with Indian ACs, and the Kigali quota system rewards whoever builds capacity before the cap. The risk: refrigerants are still a commodity, and if too much global capacity arrives at once, pricing — and SRF's fluorochemicals margins — can turn hard.
06

The fluoropolymer (PTFE) bet

The newest fluorine play — a China+1, import-substitution opportunity.

Beyond refrigerants, SRF is pushing into fluoropolymers — the high-performance plastics made from fluorine, the most famous being PTFE (you know it as Teflon). Fluoropolymers are prized for being chemically inert, heat-resistant and non-stick, and they're used in everything from non-stick coatings and gaskets to chemical-plant linings, wiring, and increasingly in electronics, EVs and green-hydrogen equipment. It's a natural extension of SRF's fluorine skill — and a classic import-substitution / China+1 opportunity, because India imports most of its fluoropolymers today.

Why fluoropolymers fit SRF

  • Same core skill. Fluoropolymers are built from the same fluorine building blocks SRF already makes for refrigerants — so the company is climbing up the value chain from a gas to a higher-value engineered material, using capability it already owns.
  • High barriers, few makers. Like refrigerants and specialty chemicals, fluoropolymer manufacturing is technically demanding and dominated by a handful of global players — a structure that favours margins for whoever can crack it.
  • Import substitution + China+1. India buys most of its fluoropolymers from abroad (much from China); a credible domestic maker can capture that demand and win export share as customers de-risk away from China.
  • A marquee partner. SRF is building fluoropolymer/fluoroelastomer capacity in collaboration with The Chemours Company (a global fluoroproducts leader), with the capex revised to ~₹745 Cr and the final phase targeted around end-2026 — a validation of SRF's fluorine credentials by a global incumbent.
In plain terms

Refrigerants are the low rung of the fluorine ladder — a bulk gas. Fluoropolymers are a higher rung — an engineered plastic that sells for far more per kilo and serves faster-growing, higher-tech end markets (electronics, EVs, green hydrogen). By pushing into PTFE and fluoroelastomers, SRF is trying to convert its fluorine expertise into steadily higher-value products — the same "move up the value chain" logic that took it from tyre cord to custom molecules. It's early, capital-hungry, and unproven at scale, but if it works it broadens the fluorine franchise well beyond the refrigerant cycle.

07

Packaging films & technical textiles: the cyclical drag

The commoditised half of SRF — where blended margins go to get diluted.

The two non-chemical segments — packaging films and technical textiles — together make up roughly half of SRF's revenue but a much smaller slice of its profit. They are the cyclical, commoditised businesses whose margin swings pull the group's blended profitability around, and they are the reason the market discounts SRF's overall multiple relative to a pure speciality-chemicals name.

Packaging films — BOPET, BOPP & the capacity cycle

SRF is a major maker of BOPET and BOPP films — the thin, strong plastic films used to wrap food, snacks, and consumer goods (plus aluminium foil). This is a global commodity governed by a brutal capacity cycle: when film prices are high, everyone builds new plants; the new supply floods the market a couple of years later; prices and margins crash; investment stops; supply tightens again; and the cycle repeats. SRF's films margins have swung sharply through these cycles. FY26 was a recovery year — Q4 films revenue rose 13% and operating profit jumped ~47% as BOPET/BOPP volumes and margins improved off a weak base.

Technical textiles — the auto-linked legacy

This is where SRF began: tyre cord fabric (the reinforcement inside tyres), belting fabric (for industrial conveyor belts) and industrial yarns. It's a mature, low-growth, cyclical business tied to the auto and industrial cycle — and, lately, squeezed by aggressive Chinese competition and softer US conveyor-belt exports (Q3 FY26 textiles revenue fell 11%). It throws off steady cash but adds little growth; it's ballast, not a growth engine. SRF keeps it partly for cash generation and partly for the diversification cushion.

The portfolio tension in a sentence: the films-and-textiles half genuinely de-risks SRF (it cushioned the group when specialty chemicals slipped in FY26) — but it also dilutes margins and holds down the multiple. Bulls see a self-funding conglomerate whose cyclical cash pays for chemistry growth; bears see two commodity anchors that would be worth more spun off. The deferral of the ₹490 Cr Indore BOPP plant (management cited "excess global BOPP capacity depressing margins") is a telling signal — even SRF is wary of adding commodity-film capacity into a glut.
08

The heavy-capex growth cycle & the balance sheet

SRF is spending big to build the next decade — on borrowed money.

The defining financial feature of SRF right now is that it is in a heavy multi-year capex phase. Unlike an asset-light compounder, SRF must build expensive chemical plants to grow — and it's doing several at once, across refrigerants, fluoropolymers and pharma intermediates. That spending is funded partly by debt, which is why net debt has climbed even as profits have risen. This is the "invest for growth" bet, and it's central to the risk/reward.

Why the balance sheet is the thing to watch

Heavy capex is a bet with a lag. SRF spends the money now (debt goes up, free cash goes down) and the returns arrive years later when the new plants fill and the F-gas/specialty cycles cooperate. If the timing works — refrigerant demand tight, specialty chemicals recovers, fluoropolymers scale — the new capacity drops high-margin revenue onto a fixed cost base and returns on capital climb. If it doesn't — a soft specialty-chem cycle drags on, or refrigerant pricing turns while the plants are still ramping — SRF is left carrying more debt and heavier depreciation against under-utilised assets, which squeezes returns (already reflected in a mid-teens ROCE/ROE, ~14–15%, modest for a "premium" chemicals name). Net debt, capacity utilisation and the specialty-chem recovery are the numbers to track each quarter.

09

Financials & valuation

A strong FY26 profit rebound — at a premium price the recovery must justify.

FY26 was a strong headline year: revenue up 7%, but profit up a punchy 47%, driven by the record fluorochemicals year, operating leverage and a films recovery — even as specialty chemicals lagged. The debate is not the direction of travel; it's whether a mid-teens-return, capex-heavy conglomerate deserves the ~43× multiple the market is paying.

How to think about the ~43× multiple

At ~₹82,000 Cr market cap on ~₹1,835 Cr of FY26 profit, SRF trades around 43× trailing earnings — a rich multiple for a company earning mid-teens returns on capital and carrying rising debt. That premium is a bet on the specialty-chemicals engine re-accelerating: bulls argue the FY26 specialty-chem weakness is a cyclical trough (agrochemical destocking + a China price war both look "unsustainable" and self-correcting), and that as the destock ends and SRF's heavy capex fills, the high-margin chemistry franchise resumes fast growth — justifying the multiple. Bears argue that a mid-teens-ROCE conglomerate with a commodity tail and heavy leverage shouldn't trade like a pure-play compounder, and that at 43× there's little room for the specialty-chem recovery to disappoint or the F-gas cycle to roll over. Analyst 12-month targets are scattered widely (roughly ₹2,400 to ₹4,500), which tells you the same thing: the valuation is a live argument, not a settled one.

10

The industry and the rivals

Where SRF sits in the Indian chemicals landscape.

SRF competes in several arenas at once, and its peer set differs by segment. In specialty chemicals / custom synthesis it sits alongside India's cohort of CSM/CDMO players; in fluorochemicals it competes with a small club of global and Indian fluorine houses; in films and textiles it's a commodity player among many. Its edge is the combination — few peers span the whole fluorine value chain from refrigerant gas to custom molecule to fluoropolymer.

The competitive edge to hold onto

SRF's genuine differentiation is fluorine breadth. Most peers are strong in one arena — a pharma CDMO here, a film maker there. SRF is one of very few that runs the whole fluorine value chain: refrigerant gases → fluorinated intermediates → custom molecules → fluoropolymers, all off a common chemistry base and a captive supply of fluorine building blocks. That vertical integration lets it capture more of the value and cross-leverage capability (the fluorine skill that makes R-32 also feeds a custom molecule and a fluoropolymer). Its nearest structural analogue is Gujarat Fluorochemicals; against the broader chemicals field, the fluorine franchise plus the CSM book is what earns it the premium tag.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine fluorine franchise — priced for the growth to keep coming.

SRF is one of India's best fluorine-chemistry franchises: a scaled, vertically integrated group riding two structural tailwinds (China+1 in specialty chemicals; the Kigali quota land-grab in refrigerants), spending heavily to build the next decade. The investment debate is about cycle timing, execution and price — will the specialty-chem recovery and the capex pay off before the market's patience (and the ~43× multiple) runs out?

The bull case

  • Fluorine moat — a scarce, high-barrier skill spanning refrigerants, custom molecules and fluoropolymers, with captive building blocks.
  • Specialty-chem re-acceleration — FY26 weakness looks like a cyclical destock + "unsustainable" China pricing; a recovery restores the high-margin growth engine.
  • Kigali quota land-grab — heavy refrigerant capex now banks permanent HFC quota ahead of the 2028 freeze; a structural franchise.
  • Value-chain climb — fluoropolymers (with Chemours) and pharma intermediates move SRF up into higher-value, faster-growing markets.
  • Aligned owner-operators — the Bharat Ram family (~50%) taking patient, multi-year capex bets.

The bear case

  • Premium valuation — ~43× on mid-teens ROCE leaves little margin for error; vulnerable to de-rating if the specialty-chem recovery slips.
  • Specialty-chem cycle risk — agrochemical destocking and aggressive Chinese pricing could linger longer than bulls hope.
  • F-gas cyclicality — refrigerant pricing can turn hard if global capacity floods in; FY26's record could prove a peak.
  • Heavy capex & rising debt — ~₹3,850 Cr net debt and years of spending before returns; execution and utilisation risk.
  • Commodity drag — films & textiles dilute margins, add cyclicality, and cap the multiple vs a pure-play speciality name.
The bottom line

SRF is a high-quality fluorine-chemistry franchise wrapped in a diversified, capex-heavy conglomerate. The business quality of the chemicals engine is real; the questions are cyclical and financial. Two numbers will write the next chapter each quarter: the specialty-chemicals recovery (is volume-and-pricing turning back up as the destock ends?) and refrigerant/fluorochemicals pricing (is the F-gas cycle still firm, or rolling over?) — with net debt and capacity utilisation as the financial backstop. Get those right and the heavy capex compounds into the premium the market is paying for; get them wrong and a 43× stock built on a commodity-diluted, leveraged base has a long way to fall.
