00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Trident Limited is a vertically integrated textile-and-paper manufacturer headquartered in the small Punjab town of Barnala. Its biggest business, by a wide margin, is home textiles — Trident is one of the world's largest makers of terry towels (the thick, looped bathroom towels) and a large producer of bed sheets and bed linen, most of it exported to US and global retailers under their own private-label brands. Feeding that mill is a large cotton-spinning operation that turns raw cotton into yarn — some sold on the open market, much of it consumed in-house. And running alongside, on an entirely separate cycle, sits the company's most distinctive asset: a leading writing-and-printing paper business that makes paper not from wood pulp but from wheat straw — the crop residue Punjab farmers would otherwise set alight.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a cotton-textile exporter with a domestic paper cushion. Roughly 82–85% of revenue is textiles (yarn + towels + sheets) and ~15–18% is paper & chemicals. The textile book swings with the US retail cycle, the cotton price and the rupee; the paper book is steadier, domestic and cost-advantaged. FY26 revenue was ~₹6,775 Cr, down ~4% on a soft year for exports and cotton — but net profit still edged up ~2% to ~₹377 Cr, and paper held its ground while textiles wobbled. That divergence is the whole point of owning both.
  • Vertical integration is the moat. Trident does not buy yarn to weave towels; it spins its own cotton yarn, weaves it, dyes and finishes it, and powers the process with captive coal and solar generation. Owning the chain from raw cotton to a finished, packed towel means it captures the full cotton-to-product spread and controls quality, cost and delivery in a way a pure weaver cannot. Peers who buy yarn on the market do not have this cushion.
  • The balance sheet is in reasonable shape and the stock is not cheap for a textile. Net debt was ~₹975 Cr at FY26 close — about 1.0× EBITDA and just 0.22× equity. The market cap sits near ₹12,900 Cr on a trailing ~34–35× earnings, a full multiple for a low-teens-margin cyclical. The bet the price embeds is that a soft FY26 was the trough, that a ₹2,000 Cr capacity expansion and a domestic-branding push lift the mix, and that the China+1 shift keeps handing Trident global towel share.
In plain terms

Trident is really two businesses stapled together by a balance sheet. One weaves cotton into towels and sheets for shoppers in Ohio and Manchester — a high-volume, thin-margin export business that lives and dies on the cotton price, the dollar and how confident Western retailers feel. The other presses paper out of wheat stubble for offices and schools in India — a quieter, steadier, cash-generative domestic business almost nobody associates with a towel maker. When cotton spikes or American shoppers pull back, the towel business hurts and the paper business keeps humming; the two rarely have a bad year at the same time. That built-in offset is why a Trident earnings line is smoother than a pure towel exporter's — and why the company survived cycles that broke single-product rivals.

01

Textiles & paper 101: the handful of ideas that drive it

No industry background needed — just the terms and the economics that decide the whole story.

Trident sits in two commodity-ish manufacturing industries at once, so it helps to hold a few concepts before the numbers. Both businesses share one trait: they turn a cheap agricultural raw material into a finished consumer product, and the profit is the spread between the two, minus the cost of conversion. In textiles the raw material is cotton; in paper it is wheat straw. Master the spread and you understand the company.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Terry towelA woven cotton towel with raised loops ("terry" pile) that make it absorbent. Trident is among the world's largest producers — the towels stacked in a US big-box store's home aisle. Sold mostly as retailer private label, not under Trident's own brand.
Yarn / spindleRaw cotton is spun into yarn (thread) on machines counted in spindles. Trident runs ~7.8 lakh spindles — a very large spinning base. Yarn is both a product it sells and the input its own looms consume.
Vertical integrationOwning consecutive stages of the chain — here cotton → yarn → fabric → finished towel — rather than buying an intermediate from someone else. It captures more of the total spread and controls the schedule. Trident is more integrated than most home-textile peers.
Cotton-to-product spreadThe gap between what a finished towel sells for and what the cotton in it cost. This spread — not the cotton price by itself — is what actually drives textile margins. A spinner-weaver that owns both ends is exposed to the spread, not just to cotton going up.
Wheat-straw pulpPaper is usually made from wood pulp. Trident instead pulps wheat straw — the leftover stalks after grain harvest — into writing-and-printing paper. Cheaper feedstock, no trees felled, and it uses a residue that is otherwise burned.
Captive powerThe mill runs its own power plants (coal + solar) rather than buying all its electricity from the grid. Spinning, weaving and paper-making are energy-hungry, so cheap in-house power is a real cost edge.
The economics that decide everything: it's the spread, not the input price

A common mistake is to assume a cotton company suffers whenever cotton rises. For a vertically integrated player it is subtler. When cotton climbs, the towel price to retailers usually follows — with a lag. The pain (or gain) is in the timing and the spread: a fast cotton spike that Trident can't pass on immediately squeezes margin; a stable or falling cotton price with firm retail prices widens the spread and fattens margin. Because Trident owns the yarn stage too, it also earns (or loses) on the spinning margin. So the number that governs textile profitability is not "the cotton price" on its own — it is the cotton-to-finished-product spread across the whole chain it controls. Paper works the same way, with wheat straw as the input; the difference is that wheat straw is local, abundant and far less volatile than globally traded cotton.

Who buys what Trident makes

  • Global retailers & importers — big US and international chains (the kind that stock own-label home goods) that buy towels and sheets in bulk for their shelves. Trident exports to 75+ countries and counts large Western retail names among its customers. This is the bulk of the textile book.
  • Domestic textile buyers — Indian yarn traders, weavers and a growing home-grown retail/e-commerce channel where Trident is pushing its own branded towels and sheets rather than only private label.
  • Paper buyers — Indian publishers, notebook and copier-paper converters, stationery brands, printers and, indirectly, every school and office that uses writing-and-printing paper. This is a largely domestic customer base, insulated from the export cycle.

The product map — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Trident reports as textiles and paper, but under the hood there are three distinct product engines with very different economics: home textiles (towels + sheets), yarn (cotton spinning), and paper & chemicals. The three engines, decoded

  • Home textiles (towels + bed linen) — the flagship. Terry towels are the crown: Trident operates a very large towel capacity (~90,000 tonnes a year across hundreds of looms) and a big bed-sheet operation (~500 looms, tens of millions of metres). Most of it is exported as retailer private label. Margins are thin per unit but the volumes are enormous, and this is the segment most exposed to US retail demand, cotton and the rupee.
  • Yarn (cotton spinning) — the base of the chain. With roughly 7.8 lakh spindles and thousands of rotors, Trident is one of India's larger cotton spinners, producing ~1.8 lakh tonnes of yarn a year. A chunk is captive — woven into its own towels and sheets — and the rest is sold to the open yarn market. Selling yarn externally makes the spinning margin visible; consuming it internally is what makes the towel business integrated.
  • Paper & chemicals — the distinctive leg. A ~1.75 lakh-tonne writing-and-printing paper mill fed by wheat straw, with a captive sulphuric-acid and chemical loop that supports the pulping. It sells largely into the domestic market, generates steadier cash than textiles, and is one of the world's largest agri-residue (non-wood) paper operations. Small in revenue share, but disproportionately important to the earnings stability.
Why "one integrated mill" is worth explaining

Almost every rival does one slice of this. A spinner just sells yarn. A weaver buys yarn and sells towels. A paper company makes paper and nothing else. Trident does the spinning and the weaving and the finishing and the power generation and, separately, the paper — on adjacent sites in the same district, sharing land, management, utilities and a treasury. The upside is cost: it keeps the spinning margin, the weaving margin and the finishing margin instead of paying them to three suppliers, and it can guarantee a Western retailer both quality and on-time shipping from a single vendor. The catch is that it also carries every stage's capital and every stage's cyclicality on one balance sheet — a lot of fixed assets, a lot of working capital in cotton and finished-goods inventory, and exposure to two commodity inputs rather than one.

03

The company, the group, and who controls it

A first-generation Punjab industrial house — Rajinder Gupta's Trident Group.

Trident is the flagship of the Trident Group, built from scratch by Rajinder Gupta, a first-generation entrepreneur from Punjab who started with a single yarn unit in 1990 and grew it into a roughly billion-dollar industrial group. The story is unusual for Indian large-caps: no inherited conglomerate, no old trading house — a spinning mill in Barnala that kept integrating forward into towels and sideways into wheat-straw paper.

Timeline

  • 1990
    Rajinder Gupta incorporates the company (originally Abhishek Industries) around a cotton-yarn spinning unit in Barnala, Punjab — the seed of everything.
  • 1990s–2000s
    Forward integration into terry towels and, distinctively, into wheat-straw paper — two very different diversifications off the same Punjab agricultural base.
  • 2011
    Renamed Trident Limited, consolidating the towel, yarn and paper businesses under one recognisable brand.
  • 2010s
    Massive capacity build-out in towels and bed sheets to become one of the world's largest terry-towel makers, anchored by big US retail relationships and a second campus at Budhni, Madhya Pradesh.
  • 2020–2023
    Post-Covid home-textile boom lifts US demand and Trident's utilisation; the company leans into the China+1 shift as Western buyers diversify sourcing away from China.
  • FY26
    Softer year — revenue ~₹6,775 Cr (down ~4%) on weaker exports and cotton, but net profit up ~2% to ~₹377 Cr; net debt held ~₹975 Cr (~1.0× EBITDA); a ~₹2,000 Cr capacity expansion and a domestic-branding push underway.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: Rajinder Gupta and the Trident Group promoter entities hold roughly 73.7% — a very high, committed founder stake. Promoter shares are reported as unencumbered (no pledge) in recent FY26 disclosures, which is a healthy governance signal for a family-controlled cyclical.
  • Sites: two big manufacturing campuses — the original Barnala (Punjab) base (spinning, towels, paper, chemicals, captive power) and Budhni (Madhya Pradesh) (spinning, bed linen, towels). Roughly 68 MW of captive thermal power plus ~52 MW of solar keep the energy-hungry mill cost-competitive.
  • What Trident is not: despite the name, it is unrelated to the various other "Trident"-branded businesses (e.g. Trident Techlabs, an unconnected engineering-services listco). This primer is strictly the Punjab textile-and-paper maker, NSE: TRIDENT / BSE: 521064.
Why the founder-owner structure matters here: a low-margin, capital-heavy, two-commodity cyclical needs patient capital and a long time horizon — exactly what a 73.7% founder stake tends to supply. Gupta built the wheat-straw paper leg and the captive-power loop precisely because they made the whole system cheaper and steadier, not because a quarter's analyst wanted them. The flip side of a dominant promoter is the usual one: minority holders are along for the ride on capital-allocation calls (the ₹2,000 Cr expansion, the branding spend) they don't get to vote down.
04

Home textiles: the towel engine that feeds American bathrooms

The flagship — huge volumes, thin margins, and a China+1 tailwind.

If you have dried off with a towel bought in a large US retail chain, there is a real chance a Trident loom in Barnala or Budhni wove it. Home textiles — terry towels plus bed sheets and linen — is the company's largest and most visible business, and it is overwhelmingly an export story: Trident sells to global retailers who put their own label on the product. It is a business of enormous scale and modest per-unit margin, where winning is about being the lowest-cost, most reliable, highest-quality vendor a Western buyer can find.

Two structural forces make the towel business attractive despite thin margins. The first is scale as a moat: at Trident's size, it can serve a global retailer's entire towel program from one vendor, with the consistency and compliance those buyers demand — a barrier a small weaver can't clear. The second is China+1: as Western retailers deliberately shift sourcing out of China, India is the obvious alternative in home textiles, and Trident (alongside Welspun, Himatsingka and Indo Count) is a direct beneficiary of that share migration. Every towel program that moves from a Chinese vendor to an Indian one is addressable volume.

Why private label is a double-edged sword

Selling under a retailer's own brand is how Trident moves the volume — the buyer handles the shelf, the marketing and the customer, and Trident just has to make the towel superbly and cheaply. That keeps the factories full. But it also means Trident owns little pricing power: it is competing on cost against other approved vendors, the retailer can re-tender the program, and there is no consumer brand loyalty accruing to Trident itself. This is precisely why the company is spending to build its own branded towel-and-sheet business in India and online — to earn a slice of margin that private label structurally denies it. Whether that branding push works is one of the real open questions in the story.

Yarn & vertical integration: cotton to finished towel, in one company

The cost moat — and the reason Trident's towel economics differ from a pure weaver's.

Most home-textile companies start with yarn they buy. Trident starts with cotton it spins itself. That single difference — owning the spinning stage — is the heart of its competitive position. It runs one of India's larger cotton-spinning operations (~7.8 lakh spindles, ~1.8 lakh tonnes of yarn a year), and that yarn does double duty: sold to the market as a product in its own right, and fed inward as the raw material for its own looms.

A pure weaver pays away the spinning margin (to a yarn supplier) and often buys grid power. Trident keeps the spinning margin, the weaving margin, the finishing margin and generates much of its own electricity — so more of the cotton-to-towel spread lands on its P&L. That is the entire logic of integration.

The strategic value of the yarn arm is not just the margin it keeps — it is optionality. When export towel demand is soft, Trident can tilt more yarn into the open market; when towel demand is hot, it can pull yarn inward to feed its own looms. Owning the input means it is never held hostage to a yarn supplier's price or delivery, and it can flex the mix toward wherever the spread is best. Add captive power — around 68 MW of thermal plus ~52 MW of solar — and Trident controls two of the three biggest cost lines in textiles (yarn and energy), leaving only cotton itself as a genuinely external variable.

Where integration helps, and where it doesn't

Integration is a cost and control advantage in normal and good times: Trident keeps margin others pay away, and it can promise a retailer end-to-end reliability. But it is not a free lunch. Owning spinning means owning a large, fixed, capital-heavy asset that must run near-full to earn its keep — when demand falls, the spinning plant's fixed costs still have to be covered, so integration can amplify a downturn as easily as it cushions cost. And it does nothing to hedge the one input Trident can't make itself: raw cotton. So the moat is real but conditional — it rewards scale and high utilisation, and it punishes idle capacity.

06

The cotton cycle: the spread that decides the textile half

The single biggest swing factor in Trident's largest business.

Cotton is to Trident's textile arm what crude is to a refiner: the raw material whose price moves faster than the finished product's, and whose swings therefore decide whether a year is good or painful. But — as with a refiner and the crack spread — the number that actually matters is not the cotton price in isolation. It is the cotton-to-product spread: how far the price of a finished towel sits above the cotton (and yarn) inside it, after the lag with which retail prices adjust.

How the cotton cycle plays out for a vertically integrated maker
ScenarioWhat happens to the spreadEffect on Trident
Cotton stable / softening, retail prices firmSpread widens — input falls while towel prices hold.The sweet spot: fat textile margins, strong cash. Integration lets Trident keep the full widened spread.
Cotton spikes sharply, fastSpread compresses — retail prices lag the input jump.Margin squeeze until Trident can re-price; high-cost cotton inventory can turn a loss on goods already committed.

FY26 was a taste of the harder scenarios: soft global demand and an unfavourable cotton-and-currency backdrop pulled textile revenue and towel/sheet volumes down, which is why consolidated revenue slipped ~4%. Crucially, though, the damage was contained — net profit still rose slightly — because the paper leg held up and because Trident's own-cotton, own-power cost structure absorbs a spread squeeze better than a buy-the-yarn weaver can. That is the cushion integration buys.

The refiner parallel, made precise

A refiner earns the crack spread — the gap between crude in and product out — and a $6/bbl swing in that spread can swamp everything else on the P&L. Trident's textile arm earns a cotton-to-towel spread that behaves the same way: two similar volume years can post very different textile margins purely because the spread was wide in one and pinched in the other. This is why tracking "the cotton price" alone will mislead you. What you want is the textile-segment EBITDA margin — the observable read-out of the spread after Trident's own spinning and power costs — quarter by quarter. When that margin holds through a cotton spike, the integration thesis is working; when it collapses, it isn't, regardless of where the towel volumes landed.

07

Wheat-straw paper: the leg nobody expects

The distinctive engine — steadier, domestic, cost-advantaged, and quietly remarkable.

Here is the part of Trident that surprises almost everyone. The same company that weaves towels for American bathrooms also runs one of the world's largest non-wood paper mills — and it makes its writing-and-printing paper not from felled trees but from wheat straw, the leftover stalks after Punjab's grain harvest. It is the agricultural residue that farmers across north India otherwise burn in their fields every autumn, the stubble-burning that blankets Delhi in winter smog. Trident buys it, pulps it, and turns it into copier reams and notebook paper.

The same Barnala company makes the towel drying an American family and the paper in an Indian schoolchild's notebook — one from cotton, the other from the wheat stubble that would otherwise be set alight.

The economics of the paper leg are genuinely different from — and complementary to — the textile arm:

  • Cheap, local, abundant feedstock. Punjab is India's wheat bowl, so wheat straw is available in enormous quantity right next to the mill, at a fraction of the cost (and price volatility) of imported wood pulp. The raw material is a waste product with a negative externality attached — using it is cheaper and greener.
  • Domestic demand, different cycle. Paper sells largely into India — publishers, notebook and copier converters, printers, stationery. That demand tracks Indian education, offices and government printing, not US retail sentiment or the dollar. So the paper cycle and the towel cycle are driven by almost entirely separate forces.
  • A captive chemical & energy loop. Pulping needs chemicals; Trident runs a captive sulphuric-acid and chemical operation and captive power alongside the paper mill, tightening cost control the same way it does in textiles.
  • Environmental edge with commercial teeth. Using wheat straw is estimated to save on the order of thousands of trees a day versus wood-based paper, and it consumes a residue that would otherwise be burned — a real ESG story that also happens to lower the input bill. Green and cheap, at once.
The genuine surprise, and why it matters financially

The counter-intuitive fact is not just that a towel maker also makes paper — it is what the paper is made from. Trident's paper feedstock is the same crop residue behind north India's most notorious pollution problem; the company has turned a burning liability into a manufacturing input. Beyond the good optics, this is why the paper leg is Trident's earnings ballast: its cost base (local straw + captive power) is low and stable, its demand (Indian offices and schools) is steady, and it generates cash reliably even when towels have a bad year. In FY26, paper & chemicals revenue actually rose (to ~₹1,040 Cr) while textiles fell — the clearest possible illustration of why the two legs belong on the same balance sheet.

The honest caveat on paper: it is still a commodity. Domestic writing-and-printing paper has its own cycle — periods of industry overcapacity, imports and pulp-price swings can compress paper margins too, and wheat-straw availability can tighten in a poor harvest year. Paper cushions the textile cycle; it does not abolish cyclicality. Its job in the portfolio is diversification, not a guaranteed floor.
08

The two-cycle machine: one company, two crops, two continents

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it fits no other primer.

Strip Trident down and it is a bet on a structural quirk almost unique to it: it runs two commodity-conversion businesses whose cycles have nearly nothing in common. One converts cotton into towels for American retail shoppers — cyclical, dollar-linked, demand-sensitive. The other converts wheat straw into paper for Indian offices and schools — steadier, rupee-based, domestically driven. Two different crops, two different customers, two different continents, two different cycles, under one roof in Barnala.

US retail confidence and a US slowdown, a global cotton spike, a rupee move — the things that hurt the towel book — do essentially nothing to Indian copier-paper demand or Punjab wheat-straw supply. Because the two legs answer to different masters, a bad year for one is rarely a bad year for both. FY26 was the proof of concept: textiles down, paper up, blended profit still positive.

This is the device to hold onto, because it survives no find-and-replace with any peer. Welspun Living is a home-textile pure-play; Himatsingka and Indo Count are home-textile pure-plays; JK Paper and West Coast are paper pure-plays. Only Trident runs both a US-consumer cotton-textile export book and a Punjab-wheat-straw domestic paper book at material scale. That is not a slogan — it is a measurable smoothing effect: the blended EBITDA line moves less than a pure towel exporter's would, because the paper leg keeps paying while textiles wait out a soft patch. An investor is buying a cyclical whose amplitude is dampened by a second, uncorrelated cash engine.

The construct that only fits Trident: two crops, two continents, one P&L

Try to restate the Trident thesis for any competitor and it breaks. You cannot say of Welspun that "a weak US towel year is offset by strong Indian paper demand," because Welspun has no paper. You cannot say of JK Paper that "a stubble-straw cost edge cushions a cotton-export downturn," because JK Paper has no cotton exports. The diversification here is not conglomerate sprawl for its own sake — it is two conversion businesses that happen to draw on two different Punjab-adjacent agricultural inputs and sell to two different worlds, so their booms and busts land at different times. That structural fact is the reason a Trident earnings series is smoother than the sector it screens in, and it is the one thing a rival cannot copy without becoming Trident.

09

The industry and the rivals

Two competitive sets, because Trident straddles two industries.

Because Trident lives in two industries, it faces two entirely separate peer groups. In home textiles it competes with India's other big China+1 exporters; in paper it competes with India's writing-and-printing majors. It is genuinely comparable to each group on one leg — and directly comparable to no single company across both, which is the whole point of the two-cycle model.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

On towels and sheets, Trident is one of a small handful of scaled Indian exporters riding the same China+1 tide — a rising-tide dynamic more than a knife-fight, at least while Western buyers keep shifting volume out of China. Trident's specific edge in that group is integration: owning yarn and power means its cost floor is lower, which matters most in a low-margin, private-label business where the cheapest reliable vendor wins. On paper, Trident is a large non-wood player in a field of wood-pulp majors; its straw feedstock is a structural cost-and-ESG advantage but also ties it to Punjab's harvest. No competitor sits in both tables — which is exactly why Trident's blended earnings behave differently from anyone on either list.

Financials & valuation

A soft-but-resilient FY26, a clean balance sheet — and a full multiple for a low-teens-margin cyclical.

FY26 was, on the surface, a down year — revenue fell ~4% — yet the shape of it is the point: profit held, the balance sheet stayed sound, and the paper leg offset a soft textile market. The debate for an investor is whether FY26 was the trough of the cotton/export cycle (with the expansion and branding push setting up a rebound) or whether a ~34–35× multiple is simply too rich for a business that earns low-teens EBITDA margins and single-digit returns on capital.

The financial character of Trident is clear once you line it up: low-teens EBITDA margins, single-digit returns on capital (ROCE around the high-single digits, ROE around 8%), a solid balance sheet (~1.0× net-debt/EBITDA, unpledged promoter stake), a generous dividend (payout well above half of earnings; yield around 2%), and a rich-for-the-sector valuation (~34–35× trailing earnings, ~2.7× book). The market is not paying a distressed-cyclical price; it is paying a premium that only makes sense if margins and returns are set to improve from here.

The one number that settles the thesis: the blended EBITDA margin through the cotton cycle

At ~34× earnings on ~8% returns on capital, Trident is priced for a recovery in profitability, not for more flat years. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the blended (consolidated) EBITDA margin — and, underneath it, the two components that build it: the textile-segment margin (the read-out of the cotton-to-product spread after Trident's own yarn and power costs) and the paper-segment margin and utilisation (the cushion). Read those two alongside net-debt/EBITDA holding near 1×. If the textile margin re-expands as the cotton cycle turns and paper keeps generating, the blended margin climbs off ~14% and the multiple can be justified; if the blended margin stays stuck in the low teens while capital keeps getting spent, an ~8%-ROCE cyclical does not support a growth multiple. The order book of towels and the tonnage of paper are the volume; the blended margin is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A resilient, integrated cyclical asked to prove that FY26 was the floor.

Trident enters FY27 with the ingredients of a recovery — a clean balance sheet, a China+1 tailwind in towels, a steady paper engine, a ~₹2,000 Cr capacity expansion in flight and a genuine push to earn branded margin at home. What it has to prove is that the soft FY26 was the trough of the cotton-and-export cycle rather than a new normal for a mature, low-return manufacturer. The two-cycle design keeps the downside shallow; the question is where the upside comes from.

The bull case

  • China+1 share gains — Western retailers keep shifting towel and sheet volume from China to India; Trident's scale and integration make it a default beneficiary.
  • Two-cycle stability — the wheat-straw paper leg keeps generating cash through soft textile years, dampening the earnings swing a pure exporter suffers.
  • Cost moat — own yarn, own power and cheap local straw give Trident one of the lowest cost bases in its peer set, which wins low-margin private-label business.
  • Self-help upside — the domestic branding push and value-added mix could lift margins above the private-label ceiling; the ₹2,000 Cr expansion adds volume for a recovery.
  • Sound finances — ~1.0× net-debt/EBITDA, unpledged 73.7% promoter stake, a ~2% dividend — a conservatively run cyclical.

The bear case

  • US-retail & customer concentration — a US slowdown, tariff shifts or the loss of a large private-label program hits the biggest revenue leg directly.
  • Cotton & FX volatility — a fast cotton spike or an adverse rupee move compresses the textile spread before prices can reset.
  • Thin, price-taking margins — private-label towels carry little pricing power; low-teens EBITDA and ~8% ROCE leave scant room for error on cost.
  • Paper isn't a guaranteed floor — domestic paper overcapacity, imports and a weak wheat harvest can pinch the very leg meant to cushion textiles.
  • Rich multiple, modest returns — ~34× earnings on ~8% ROCE needs a real margin recovery to justify; capital-and-working-capital intensity keeps returns capped.
The bottom line

Trident is the rare textile stock you can partly de-risk by reading a paper-industry report: a world-scale towel exporter whose worst years are softened by a wheat-straw paper mill running on the crop residue its neighbours would otherwise burn. That two-cycle design has kept it steady — FY26 proved it, with textiles down and profit still up. But steadiness is not the same as growth, and at ~34× earnings on high-single-digit returns the market is paying for a profitability recovery that FY26 did not yet deliver. The concrete things to watch are specific and checkable: the textile-segment EBITDA margin re-expanding as the cotton cycle turns, the paper mill holding its utilisation and margin, the ₹2,000 Cr expansion commissioning on budget, and the domestic branded book actually earning a premium over private label. Get those four moving together and the blended margin climbs off ~14% into territory that supports the multiple. Until they do, Trident remains what it has long been — an exceptionally well-integrated, conservatively financed maker of towels and paper, priced as if the next up-cycle is already underway.
