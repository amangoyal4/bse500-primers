Sky Gold, the workshop behind the showcase

A complete walk through one of India's fastest-growing B2B gold-jewellery manufacturers — the Mumbai company that designs and casts the rings, chains and mangalsutras sold under the names of Malabar, Kalyan and Joyalukkas, not its own. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Sky Gold and Diamonds Limited is a Mumbai-based manufacturer of gold jewellery that sells almost none of it to the public. It designs, casts and finishes rings, chains, mangalsutras and studded pieces in its own factories, then ships them wholesale to India's big jewellery retail chains — Malabar, Kalyan, Joyalukkas, GRT, Senco and a few dozen others — who put them in their own showcases under their own names. When a shopper admires a lightweight gold chain in a branded showroom, there is a fair chance a company they have never heard of made it. Sky Gold is a business-to-business (B2B) contract manufacturer and wholesaler, not a consumer brand.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Almost all its "revenue" is just gold moving through. Of the ~₹6,295 Cr Sky Gold invoiced in FY26, the overwhelming majority is the cost of the physical gold and other metal, which it buys and passes straight through. What Sky Gold actually earns is the making charge — a fee for design, casting and finishing — which shows up as a gross margin of only about 8.5% and an EBITDA margin of ~6.9%. The gold is the passenger; the making charge is the fare.
  • It is scaling at a startling pace. Revenue has gone from ~₹786 Cr in FY22 to ~₹6,295 Cr in FY26 — roughly 8× in four years — and FY26 alone grew ~77%, with net profit up ~112% to ~₹282 Cr. The operating margin has climbed from ~2.6% to ~6.9% over that stretch as the mix improved. The stock re-rated hard alongside, from an obscure SME-board listing to a ~₹10,500 Cr company.
  • Its cleverest move is to hold as little gold as possible. Increasingly, its retail customers supply the gold themselves, and Sky Gold books only the making charge — a model that removes gold-price risk and shrinks the working capital a gold business normally drowns in. That "advance-gold" share was ~20% of volume by the end of FY26 and is targeted at ~30%.
In plain terms

Sky Gold's job is to be the unseen factory floor for other people's jewellery brands. It takes gold — increasingly the retailer's own gold — turns it into finished, design-led pieces at scale and speed, and charges a fee for the craft. Because the metal itself is worth so much and the fee is so thin, the top line looks enormous while the real business is a sliver: a few paise of making charge on every rupee of gold that passes through. The company grows by pushing more tonnes of jewellery through its Navi Mumbai floor for more of India's big chains, and by keeping each tonne from sitting still. Everything in this primer is really about widening that sliver and speeding up that flow.

01

The invisible workshop: how jewellery manufacturing works

No trade knowledge needed — just who makes the jewellery, who sells it, and where the money separates.

India buys more gold jewellery than almost anywhere on Earth, and the industry that supplies it splits into two very different jobs. There are the retailers — the chains with the marble showrooms and the television advertisements, who sell finished jewellery to you and me. And there are the manufacturers — the workshops that actually make the pieces. Sky Gold sits squarely in the second camp. To understand it you have to separate the two economics, because they could not be more different.

The only jewellery-trade terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Making chargeThe fee a jeweller charges for turning raw gold into a finished piece — design, casting, setting, polishing. It is separate from the gold's value. For a manufacturer like Sky Gold, the making charge is essentially the entire business; the gold is pass-through.
B2B vs B2CB2C ("business-to-consumer") is selling to the public — the retail chains. B2B ("business-to-business") is selling to other businesses. Sky Gold is B2B: its customers are the retailers, not the shoppers.
Lightweight / cast jewelleryMachine-cast, hollow or thin gold designs that use less metal per piece — fashionable, affordable, and a large and growing slice of Indian demand. Sky Gold's core specialty.
Studded jewelleryPieces set with stones — diamonds or cubic zirconia (CZ). More design-intensive, so they carry a richer making charge than plain gold. A deliberate growth area.
Gold Metal Loan (GML)A way of borrowing physical gold from a bank instead of buying it with cash — you repay in gold, paying only a small lease rate. It lets a jeweller run on far less working capital and sidestep price risk. Central to Sky Gold's plan (section 06).
The split that defines the sector: the gold is the same, the margins are not

Here is the crucial thing. The gold in a piece is a global commodity — its price is set in London and Mumbai bullion markets, and every jeweller, big or small, pays roughly the same for it. So no one makes money on the gold itself; it just passes through the bill. Money is made on two other things: the making charge (the craft) and, for retailers, the brand markup and store experience. A manufacturer like Sky Gold earns only the first of those — a wholesale making charge — which is why its percentage margins look tiny next to a consumer chain's. But it also carries none of the cost of running showrooms and advertising. It is a volume-and-design game played on a razor-thin slice of a very large number.

Why retailers outsource to a Sky Gold at all

A big chain could, in theory, make its own jewellery. Most choose not to, for reasons that are Sky Gold's business case:

  • Design breadth without the overhead. A retailer needs thousands of fresh designs a year to keep showcases current across regions and festivals. Running an in-house design-and-casting operation at that variety is expensive and distracting. Outsourcing to a specialist with a vast design library is simpler.
  • Capital freed for stores. Manufacturing ties up capital in machinery, inventory and artisans. A retailer would rather spend its capital on the thing customers see — locations, gold display stock, advertising — and let a partner carry the factory.
  • Speed and flexibility. Fashions and festival demand move fast. A manufacturer geared for quick turnaround can refill a chain's fastest-selling lines far quicker than a slow in-house shop.

That is the demand Sky Gold feeds: it is the outsourced design-and-production arm for retailers who would rather sell than manufacture. The trade-off, which runs through the whole investment case, is that this leaves it dependent on a handful of very large customers who could, in principle, choose to make more in-house — the concentration risk we return to in section 03.

02

The making charge: 8.5 paise on every gold rupee

The single most important idea in the case — and it is specific to a gold contract-manufacturer.

Look at Sky Gold's revenue and you will misread the business. A ~₹6,295 Cr top line sounds like a large industrial company. It is not, in the way that number implies — because most of that ₹6,295 Cr never belonged to Sky Gold in any economic sense. It is the value of gold that flowed through the company on its way from a bullion dealer to a retailer's vault. The number that actually describes the business is far smaller: the making charge it keeps for the craft of turning that gold into jewellery.

This single fact reorganises how you read everything else. When gold prices rise — as they did sharply through 2025 and into 2026 — Sky Gold's revenue can balloon even if it made no more jewellery, simply because the pass-through metal is worth more per gram. Conversely, revenue can dip when gold softens while the underlying business is perfectly healthy. So the top line is a poor gauge. The honest yardsticks are the making-charge margin (is Sky Gold earning more per gram of craft?) and the tonnes of gold it processes (is it doing more craft?). Multiply those two and you have the real engine; the gold price is mostly noise layered on top.

The device that only fits a gold contract-manufacturer: margin × tonnes × turns

Value at Sky Gold is not the gold and it is not the revenue. It is the making-charge margin (~8.5 paise per rupee) × the tonnes of gold pushed through the factory × how fast that gold turns from raw metal into a shipped, invoiced piece. Each term is a separate lever. Widen the margin by making more studded and value-added designs. Grow the tonnes by winning more chains and adding capacity. Speed the turns by holding gold for days rather than weeks — ideally holding the customer's gold, not your own. A jeweller that lets metal sit idle is financing a commodity for free; a jeweller that keeps it moving earns its fee again and again on the same working capital. Sky Gold's entire strategy — richer mix, more capacity, advance-gold, faster receivables — is an attack on those three terms at once. Nothing about that sentence would survive a find-and-replace with another company's name, because almost no other kind of business earns a fixed fee on a commodity it is trying never to own for long.

Notice how much of the story is a mix story. Plain, machine-cast gold is close to a commodity conversion job — thin, competitive, fast. Studded and diamond pieces, Turkish designs and other value-added lines carry a fatter making charge because the craft is harder and the buyer will pay for it. Every percentage point Sky Gold adds to its blended making-charge margin — from 7.1% gross in FY25 to 8.5% in FY26 — drops with unusual force to the bottom line, precisely because the base it is layered on (the gold) is so large. That leverage is why a two-percentage-point margin move matters more here than a big revenue swing.

03

What Sky Gold makes — and the handful of chains it makes it for

A wide product library sold to a narrow, powerful customer list.

Sky Gold's catalogue is broad — reportedly more than 500,000 designs across some 14 categories, from plain gold chains and rings to mangalsutras, Turkish jewellery and diamond-studded pieces — supported by an in-house design team turning out thousands of new patterns a year. That breadth is the point: it is what lets a single supplier serve very different regional tastes for many chains at once. But the customer side is the opposite of broad. It is a short list of very large, very demanding buyers.

The product mix

  • Plain / lightweight cast gold — the core: fashionable, lower-metal designs (chains, rings, everyday pieces) made at high volume. This is where Sky Gold built its reputation and most of its throughput. Thin making charge, but fast-moving.
  • Studded & diamond jewellery — the mix-improvement lever: pieces set with diamonds or cubic zirconia carry a richer making charge. Diamond-studded is still small (~1.5% of sales) but a stated growth priority, alongside 18-carat and 9-carat lines.
  • Mangalsutras & chains — bolstered by the 2024 acquisitions (Starmangalsutra for mangalsutras, Sparkling Chains for machine-made chains), adding categories that together make up a meaningful share of a typical store's gold sales.
  • Exports — a rising slice, roughly 11–12% of FY26 revenue (up from ~6%), which the company wants to push toward ~20%. Selling design-led Indian jewellery to overseas wholesalers extends the same B2B model beyond India.

The concentration paradox to hold in mind

Selling to a few giant chains cuts both ways, and a first-timer should sit with both edges. On one side, these relationships are sticky and scalable: once a Malabar or a Kalyan trusts your quality, turnaround and design, it channels growing volumes your way, and it is a nuisance for them to re-qualify a new supplier. Some pay strikingly fast — Malabar reportedly within days of delivery — which is gold for a working-capital-heavy business. On the other side, losing even one such customer, or having it pull manufacturing in-house, would leave a crater no long tail of small buyers could quickly fill. The same names that make Sky Gold's growth credible also make it fragile. Watching whether it can broaden the customer base while keeping the big chains is one of the real tests of the story.

04

The family, the float, and the climb from the SME board

A promoter-family jeweller that grew up in public — from a tiny SME listing to a mid-cap in a few years.

Sky Gold is a promoter-family business, run by the Chauhans, that took an unusual path to the mainstream market: it listed young and small on the SME exchange, then grew into a full board listing. Understanding that arc explains both the pace of its scale-up and the dilution that funded it.

Timeline

  • 2005–2008
    The Chauhan family builds a gold-jewellery manufacturing business in Mumbai, supplying wholesale to jewellers — the foundation of the present company.
  • Oct 2018
    SME IPO on the BSE SME platform at ~₹180/share, raising a modest ~₹25.6 Cr — a tiny, little-noticed listing of a niche jewellery maker.
  • Jan 2023
    Migrates to the NSE & BSE main boards from the SME exchange — the step-change in visibility and liquidity that opened the door to institutional money and a re-rating.
  • FY24–FY25
    Explosive growth: revenue roughly doubles to ~₹3,548 Cr in FY25. The company raises capital (QIP / warrants) and acquires Starmangalsutra and Sparkling Chains (June 2024), adding mangalsutra and chain capacity; a bonus issue follows in Dec 2024.
  • FY26
    Record year: revenue ~₹6,295 Cr (+77%), EBITDA ~₹434 Cr, PAT ~₹282 Cr (+112%); working-capital days cut; advance-gold and export shares rising. Market cap approaches ~₹10,500 Cr.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Chauhan family — Mangesh Chauhan (Managing Director), with Mahendra and Darshan Chauhan — remain in control, holding roughly 52% of the company as of early 2026. Investors should confirm the exact figure in the latest shareholding filing.
  • Dilution funded the growth: promoter holding has fallen materially over three years — the price of raising equity (QIP, warrants) and issuing shares for acquisitions to bankroll a very fast scale-up. That is normal for a company growing this quickly, but it is worth tracking that each fund-raise expands the share count.
  • A young public company still maturing: Sky Gold went from an SME micro-cap to a mid-cap in a compressed window. Governance, disclosure quality and institutional ownership are still building out — a reason to read its filings and related-party dealings with care rather than assume large-cap polish.
Why the SME-to-mainboard arc matters: a large part of Sky Gold's stock re-rating is simply the market discovering a business that was, until January 2023, invisible on a low-liquidity SME board. Fast growth met a widening pool of buyers, and the multiple expanded as much as the earnings did. That is a real phenomenon — but it also means some of the past return came from re-rating rather than pure business performance, and re-ratings do not repeat indefinitely. From here, the earnings have to do more of the work.
05

The advance-gold trick: getting paid to hold no gold

The counter-intuitive model that turns a working-capital hog into something lighter — the surprise most first-timers miss.

Here is the fact that surprises almost everyone who looks closely at Sky Gold: its smartest strategic move is to own as little gold as possible, and, increasingly, to hold its customers' gold rather than its own. A gold-jewellery maker is normally a working-capital monster — it has to buy expensive metal, hold it as inventory while it is worked, and wait to be paid. Sky Gold is systematically dismantling that burden through what it calls the advance-gold (or client-supplied-gold) model.

The mechanism is elegant. Instead of Sky Gold buying the gold, financing it, and selling a finished piece at full value, the retail customer supplies the gold up front. Sky Gold simply converts it into jewellery and invoices only the making charge. In that arrangement, the company never pays for the metal, never carries its price risk, and books a much smaller — but far higher-quality — revenue line that is essentially pure fee. Its return on the capital it does deploy shoots up, because the biggest chunk of capital (the gold) has left its balance sheet entirely.

This is why Sky Gold can post triple-digit profit growth while its net working-capital days actually fell — from around 71 to 59 across FY26 — rather than swelling as they usually would for a fast-growing gold business. Debtor days sit in the low-30s (helped by fast-paying chains like Malabar), inventory around 50 days, and the whole cash-conversion cycle is being squeezed deliberately. Borrowings of ~₹869 Cr at March 2026 exist mainly to fund the gold the company still buys itself — and management has set targets to cut debt sharply in FY27 and aim for net-debt-free by FY30 as advance-gold and gold-leasing take over more of the metal financing.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with

Most manufacturers grow by owning more — more inventory, more raw material, more capital tied up as they scale. Sky Gold is trying to grow by owning less of the expensive part. The more of its jewellery that is made from someone else's gold, the closer it moves to a pure design-and-conversion fee business: it earns the making charge without renting the balance-sheet space that gold usually demands. That is a genuinely unusual shape for a company whose products are made of one of the world's most valuable commodities. The catch is that it depends on customers being willing to supply their own gold — which only the more sophisticated, organised buyers do — so the model's reach is tied to how fast that kind of customer grows. But directionally, a gold maker deliberately trying to touch as little gold as possible is the quiet heart of this investment case.

06

A business made of gold: price risk, leases and the hedge

When your product is a volatile commodity, how you finance and hedge the metal is half the skill.

Every piece Sky Gold makes is mostly gold, and gold is one of the most price-volatile things a company can hold. In 2025–26, with gold near record highs, that risk is acute: hold a tonne of unhedged gold through a sharp price fall and the loss can dwarf a whole year's making charges. So the second craft in this business — after making jewellery — is not losing money on the metal while you make it. There are three tools, and Sky Gold is leaning into all of them.

How a jeweller neutralises gold-price risk
ToolHow it worksWhat it does for Sky Gold
Advance / client-supplied goldThe customer supplies the metal; Sky Gold never buys or owns it.The cleanest hedge of all — no gold on the books means no price risk and no financing cost. The rising share (section 05) is the biggest structural fix.
Gold Metal Loan (GML)Borrow physical gold from a bank, work it, sell the piece, and repay in gold — paying only a small lease rate rather than buying the metal with cash.Cuts financing cost (a lease rate of a few percent vs ~9%+ working-capital interest) and naturally hedges price, since the liability and the asset are both gold.
Hedging / back-to-back buyingFixing the gold price at the moment an order is taken, or hedging on the exchange, so cost and selling price move together.Locks the making charge as the real margin and takes the price gamble out of ordinary purchased-gold orders.

The GML piece is worth a caveat, because it shows how theory meets reality. Sky Gold has arranged gold-metal-loan lines with several banks at attractive lease rates — on paper a powerful way to slash both financing cost and price risk. But as of the FY26 results, those lines were sanctioned yet largely unutilised: at prevailing conditions the leased gold worked out costlier or harder to source than management wanted, and the rollout was delayed. That is a useful reminder that a good idea on a slide is not the same as a benefit in the numbers. The GML upside is real but still ahead of the company, not banked.

Why the financing model is as important as the factory

A jeweller earning ~8.5% on the gold it works simply cannot afford to lose that and more on the gold's price. So the real art is arranging things so the metal is either not yours (advance-gold), borrowed as metal and repaid as metal (GML), or price-locked at order (hedging) — leaving the making charge exposed and the commodity neutralised. Get that plumbing right and Sky Gold is a clean fee business layered over a hedged commodity flow. Get it wrong — carry a big slug of unhedged gold into a price drop — and a strong operating year can be swallowed by a mark-to-market loss the making charge was never big enough to absorb. This is why the treasury discipline behind the scenes matters at least as much as the designs on the shop floor.

07

Sky Gold 3.0: acquisitions, tonnes and the scale-up

The plan to multiply throughput — how the company intends to grow the "tonnes" term of the value equation.

If the making charge is the margin and the metal is hedged away, growth comes down to one thing: pushing more tonnes of jewellery through the factory for more chains. Sky Gold's scale-up plan — branded internally as "Sky Gold 3.0" — is an explicit attack on that throughput term, combining capacity expansion, acquisitions and geographic reach.

The pieces of the scale-up

  • Capacity: the core Navi Mumbai facility (~130,000 sq ft) has been scaled from a few hundred kilograms a month to roughly 1.2 tonnes of gold processed per month. Crucially, in Q4 FY26 the plant was running at only ~55% utilisation (~650 kg/month), which means a long runway of volume growth without major new capex — the company has said its current footprint can support growth into around 2028.
  • The 3.0 ambition: management's stated vision is to lift capacity toward ~4 tonnes per month over the coming years — more than triple today's run-rate — the physical backbone for its multi-year revenue targets.
  • Acquisitions: the June-2024 purchases of Starmangalsutra (mangalsutras) and Sparkling Chains (machine-made chains) for a combined ~₹50 Cr added both product categories and roughly 300 kg/month of capacity at attractive valuations (reportedly under ~7× EBITDA) — a bolt-on way to widen the catalogue and the factory at once.
  • Exports & value-added mix: pushing exports from ~11–12% toward ~20% of revenue, and lifting the share of studded and other higher-making-charge lines, so that each incremental tonne is worth a little more.

The ambition is sized accordingly. Management has guided to roughly ₹8,100 Cr of revenue in FY27 (up ~30% on FY26), and sketched a longer vision toward ~₹18,000–19,000 Cr by FY30 at a higher ~5%-plus net margin and a net-debt-free balance sheet — underpinned by the utilisation runway, the 3.0 capacity build and the mix shift. These are company targets, not results: treat them as the yardstick to measure delivery against, not as facts, and watch each year's revenue-and-margin combination against them rather than the revenue alone.

Why spare capacity is the quiet asset here

The most under-appreciated number in the scale-up is the ~55% utilisation. It means the next stretch of growth can come largely by filling an existing, already-paid-for factory rather than by pouring capital into new plants — which is exactly the kind of growth that lifts return on capital instead of diluting it. If Sky Gold can win the orders to fill that floor while nudging the mix richer and shifting more volume to advance-gold, it grows revenue, margins and capital efficiency together. The risk is symmetric: spare capacity only helps if the demand shows up to fill it. The 3.0 targets assume a lot of new order flow from a customer base that is currently concentrated — so the scale-up is a bet on winning share, not just on building capacity.

08

Why this is not Titan: B2B maker vs consumer brand

The most common misread — treating Sky Gold like a jewellery brand — and why the economics are opposite.

Because it is a jewellery company that is growing fast and re-rating, Sky Gold is easy to lump in with the household names of Indian jewellery — Titan's Tanishq, Kalyan, Senco. That comparison is misleading. Those are consumer brands that sell to the public; Sky Gold is their supplier. The two make money in almost opposite ways, and confusing them leads to wrong expectations about margins, growth and risk.

The reframe that prevents the wrong comparison

Sky Gold should be read as a specialised contract manufacturer that happens to work in gold, not as a baby Tanishq. Its ~7% EBITDA margin is not a sign of a weak business; it is the correct margin for a high-throughput, low-overhead wholesale maker, and it comes with asset turns and return-on-capital a store-heavy retailer can only envy. The flip side is that it will never enjoy a brand's pricing power or its captive consumer demand — its fate is tied to a handful of buyers rather than a nation of shoppers. Judge it, then, by manufacturer's yardsticks: making-charge margin, tonnes processed, capital efficiency and customer breadth — not by the metrics you would apply to a consumer jewellery brand.

09

The industry and the rivals

A fragmented, largely unorganised craft that a few listed names are professionalising.

Gold-jewellery manufacturing in India is still overwhelmingly a fragmented, family-workshop cottage industry — thousands of small karigars and job-workers scattered across trading hubs. That is precisely the opportunity: as organised retail chains grow and demand consistent quality, design breadth and reliable turnaround at scale, they increasingly want organised, listed suppliers they can trust and grow with. Sky Gold is one of the few players professionalising that supply side — and the listed comparison set is small and varied.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two forces define this contest. First, the tailwind: the formalisation of Indian gold — hallmarking, GST, the rise of organised chains — steadily pushes retailers toward organised suppliers and away from opaque small workshops, and there are few listed players positioned to catch that shift at scale. That is Sky Gold's structural opportunity. Second, the discipline: because everyone pays the same for gold and competes on the making charge, this is fundamentally a cost, design and turnaround contest. The winner is the maker who can offer the widest design library, the fastest refill and the lowest conversion cost per gram — and who can do it while barely touching the metal. Sky Gold's design breadth, spare capacity and advance-gold push are all aimed at exactly that competitive test.

Explosive growth and rising margins on a rich multiple — the question is durability, not whether it grew.

The financial story is, on its face, spectacular: revenue up ~8× in four years, margins climbing every step, profit more than doubling in FY26. Unlike a cheap cyclical, Sky Gold is not lowly rated — it trades on a rich earnings multiple that already assumes a lot of the growth continues. So the valuation debate is not whether the business is good; it is whether the pace and the margin expansion can hold long enough to justify the price.

The one number that settles the thesis: making-charge margin, read against customer concentration and working-capital days

Because the price already assumes strong growth, the case turns on whether the making-charge (EBITDA) margin keeps rising toward the guided 7–7.5% as volumes scale — and it must be read together with two companions that could undo it. The first is customer concentration: is Sky Gold broadening its base, or is a rising margin masking growing dependence on two or three chains? The second is working-capital intensity: are net working-capital days and debt actually falling as advance-gold and gold-leasing take hold, proving the light-balance-sheet model is real and not just a slide? If margin rises while concentration eases and working capital tightens, the rich multiple is buying a genuinely improving fee business and can hold. If margin stalls, or climbs only because the company is leaning ever harder on a few customers while debt stays sticky, then FY26 was the peak of a re-rating rather than the base of a compounder. Making charge per tonne, the customer list, and the cash cycle — read those three together and you have read the stock.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A fast-scaling, lightening-balance-sheet fee business on a demanding multiple — asked whether it can keep it all going at once.

Sky Gold enters FY27 with real momentum: a record year behind it, a factory running at barely half capacity, an advance-gold model that lightens its balance sheet as it grows, and a formalising industry pushing organised retailers toward organised suppliers. What a manufacturer cannot promise is that a thin making-charge, a concentrated customer list and a balance sheet built of gold will all stay favourable together while it triples throughput. That tension frames both cases.

The bull case

  • Rare organised play on a formalising market — few listed, at-scale B2B jewellery makers exist to catch retailers' shift away from the unorganised trade.
  • Margin ladder still climbing — EBITDA margin more than doubled in three years; richer studded/export mix and advance-gold point higher still, with outsized profit leverage on the gold base.
  • Capacity runway without capex — ~55% utilisation means years of volume growth by filling an existing plant, lifting returns on capital rather than diluting them.
  • Lightening balance sheet — advance-gold and gold-leasing are cutting working-capital days and gold-price risk; net-debt-free is a stated FY30 goal.
  • Sticky marquee customers — trusted, fast-paying relationships with Malabar, Kalyan, Joyalukkas and others anchor the order book and are a nuisance to displace.

The bear case

  • Customer concentration — a large share of revenue rides on a few chains; losing one, or having it manufacture in-house, would leave a hole the long tail can't quickly fill.
  • Thin margin on a huge gold base — a ~7% EBITDA margin leaves little cushion; a mis-hedged gold-price move or a mix slip can swing profit hard.
  • Rich valuation — ~38× trailing earnings already discounts years of flawless execution; any stumble in growth or margin re-rates the stock quickly.
  • Execution & governance of a fast scale-up — tripling capacity, integrating acquisitions and delivering the GML/advance-gold plan in a young public company that grew from the SME board is a lot to get right at once.
  • Gold-demand & price cyclicality — very high gold prices can dent jewellery volumes; the tonnes that fill the factory are not guaranteed.
The bottom line

Sky Gold is the unseen craftsman behind India's jewellery counters — a design-led factory earning a few paise of making charge on rivers of gold it is increasingly clever enough not to own. The self-help is genuine: a richer product mix, an under-filled plant to grow into, and an advance-gold model that turns a working-capital hog into something lighter and higher-returning. But the price already assumes most of that keeps working, and three things must hold together for it to: the making-charge margin, the loyalty of a short list of giant customers, and the discipline that keeps a balance sheet made of gold from betraying it. The next tests are concrete and datable: whether FY27 revenue lands near the guided ~₹8,100 Cr with EBITDA margin holding at 7%-plus, whether advance-gold volume clears ~30% and the sanctioned gold-metal-loan lines finally get used, and whether the customer base broadens as the factory fills toward its 4-tonne-a-month ambition. Deliver those, and the rich multiple is buying a real compounder in a formalising trade. Miss them, and FY26 will look like the year the discovery-driven re-rating ran ahead of the workshop.
