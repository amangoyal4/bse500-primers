00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Bank of Baroda ("BoB") is one of India's largest public-sector banks — a 117-year-old, government-controlled lender that takes deposits from savers across the country and lends to households, farmers, small businesses and corporates. After the 2019 amalgamation that folded Vijaya Bank and Dena Bank into it, BoB became the country's second- or third-largest state-owned bank, with a ~₹31 lakh crore total business (deposits + advances) and a genuinely global footprint across ~17 countries.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A textbook turnaround. A decade ago BoB was at the centre of India's bad-loan crisis — gross NPAs peaked near ~12% in FY16 and the bank posted the largest quarterly loss in Indian banking history at the time. FY26 is a different animal: net profit of ₹20,021 Cr, gross NPAs of just 1.89%, net NPAs of 0.45%, a ~94% provision coverage and a ~1.06% return on assets. Cleaning the book is what turned losses into ~₹20,000 Cr of annual profit.
  • Strong loan growth, squeezed margins. In FY26 global advances grew a healthy 16.2% — ahead of deposits (+12%) — but the net interest margin slipped to 2.89% in Q4 (from ~3.1% a year earlier) as rate cuts and a fierce deposit war pushed up funding costs. That tension — good volume, thinner spread — is the story of the year.
  • It is ~64% owned by the Government of India. That is the double-edge the whole valuation turns on: sovereign backing makes its deposits ultra-safe and cheap, but state ownership, social-lending mandates and a history of NPAs keep the stock at a P/E of ~7× and below book value (~0.85× P/B) — a steep discount to private peers like HDFC and ICICI.
In plain terms

Think of Bank of Baroda as a ₹16.5-lakh-crore deposit machine wired to a ₹14.3-lakh-crore loan book, run by the government. It pays savers a little, lends that money out for more, and pockets the gap. For years the machine was broken — too many loans went bad, wiping out profits. It has since been repaired: bad loans are back near 2%, and the bank now earns a steady ~₹20,000 crore a year. The open questions are whether it can keep margins from eroding in a tough rate-and-deposit environment, and whether the market ever stops treating "government-owned" as a permanent 60%-off sticker on the share price.

01

Bank economics 101: how a bank actually earns

No finance degree required — just the numbers that drive everything.

A bank is, at its core, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors) and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap between what it earns on loans and pays on deposits — scaled to its assets — is the engine. A handful of terms tell you almost the whole story, and they matter doubly for a bank like BoB whose entire investment case is about a margin under pressure and a bad-loan book being cleaned up.

The only banking units you need
TermWhat it means (and Bank of Baroda's number)
NIINet Interest Income — interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings. The core revenue line. BoB: ~₹47,682 Cr in FY26 (+2.5%).
NIMNet Interest Margin — NII as a % of interest-earning assets. The "spread", standardised. BoB: ~2.89% in Q4 FY26, down from ~3.1% — the pressure point of the year.
CASACurrent + Savings Account deposits — money in everyday accounts that pays little or no interest. The cheaper your funding, the fatter your margin. BoB: ~38.9% domestic CASA.
GNPA / NNPAGross / Net Non-Performing Assets — the % of loans that have gone bad. The single best gauge of lending discipline. BoB: 1.89% / 0.45% — a world away from ~12% in 2016.
PCRProvision Coverage Ratio — how much of the bad loans the bank has already set money aside against. BoB: ~93.9%, meaning almost all recognised bad loans are pre-funded.
CAR / CET-1Capital Adequacy — the equity cushion versus loans, the buffer against losses. BoB: CRAR 15.82%, CET-1 13.16% — comfortably above the regulatory floor.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. So a bank gets richer four ways — lend more (grow advances), fund cheaper (more CASA, lower deposit cost), lose less (low NPAs and low credit cost), and earn fees (cards, insurance, forex, distribution). BoB's turnaround was almost entirely about the third lever: for years, "loan losses" were so enormous they swamped everything else and pushed the bank into the red. Fix the bad loans and the same interest spread suddenly drops through to profit — which is exactly what happened.

Why "loan losses" are the swing factor for a PSU bank

  • Provisioning is the hidden lever. When a loan turns bad, the bank must set aside money to cover the expected loss — a provision, charged straight against profit. In the crisis years BoB was provisioning tens of thousands of crores; as the book cleaned up, that charge collapsed and profit surged. This is why credit cost (provisions as a % of loans — now just ~0.76%) is the number that made the turnaround.
  • Slippages are the leading indicator. A "slippage" is a loan freshly turning bad. BoB's slippage ratio of ~0.89% in FY26 is low and stable — the early-warning gauge that the book is healthy, not just historically cleaned.
  • The rate cycle sets the margin. Because most of BoB's loans reprice quickly when the RBI cuts rates, but deposits reprice more slowly, a rate-cut cycle squeezes NIM first — loan yields fall before funding costs do. That is precisely the pinch showing up in FY26.
02

How the money flows: deposits → loans → margin

Read this once and the whole bank clicks into place.

A bank is a conveyor belt for money. Savers' deposits come in one end; loans go out the other; the spread between the two is harvested as net interest income. Two things govern how much profit drips out: how cheap the deposits are (the CASA mix) and how much more the loans yield than the deposits cost (the margin). For BoB right now, the belt is running fast — loans are growing 16% — but the spread being harvested is getting thinner.

When the RBI cuts rates, Stage-3 loan yields fall almost immediately while Stage-1/2 deposit costs lag — so the spread narrows first. Rebuilding CASA and holding down deposit costs is the only way to defend the margin.

Why the CASA mix is the whole game

A current account pays 0% and a savings account ~3%; a fixed deposit pays 6.5–7.5%. The more of your funding that sits in low-cost CASA, the cheaper your money — and the wider your margin on the same loan. BoB's domestic CASA of ~38.9% is decent for a PSU bank, but it has drifted down from ~40% a year earlier as savers chase higher fixed-deposit and mutual-fund returns. Every point of CASA lost has to be replaced with pricier term deposits, and that is a direct headwind to margin.

Volume up, spread down — why both can be true at once

It sounds contradictory that a bank can grow loans 16% and still see profit growth slow to ~2%. Here's why: net interest income = volume × spread. In FY26 BoB pushed volume up hard (advances +16%), but the spread (NIM) shrank because loan yields fell with RBI rate cuts while deposit costs stayed sticky and CASA slipped. The two roughly cancelled, so NII grew only ~2.5% and profit only ~2.2%. The whole near-term investment question is which force wins from here — does volume keep out-running the margin squeeze, or does the squeeze catch up?

03

The company and who controls it

A 117-year-old institution, majority-owned by the state.

Bank of Baroda was founded in 1908 by the Maharaja of Baroda, Sayajirao Gaekwad III, and nationalised in 1969 along with India's other large banks. Today it is a professionally-run public-sector bank whose controlling shareholder is the Government of India (~63.97%). It is headquartered in Vadodara, Gujarat, with its corporate office in Mumbai, and operates ~8,000+ domestic branches plus a large overseas network.

Timeline

  • 1953

  • 2016–2019
    Aggressive clean-up and recapitalisation — heavy provisioning, tighter underwriting, government capital injections — as the book is scrubbed.
  • 1 Apr 2019
    Three-way amalgamation: Vijaya Bank and Dena Bank merge into BoB — India's first tripartite bank merger — creating a top-tier PSU bank with ~₹15 lakh crore of business.
  • 2020
    Branch and technology integration completed; ~5 crore accounts migrated. The unified bob World digital platform is launched.
  • FY26
    Net profit ₹20,021 Cr (+2.2%); advances +16.2%; GNPA 1.89% / NNPA 0.45%; PCR ~94%; ROA 1.06%; final dividend ₹8.50/share. The turnaround is complete; the margin defence is the new task.

Ownership & management

  • Government of India ~63.97%. The state is the promoter and controlling shareholder. This anchors the stock's identity as a "PSU bank" and drives both its funding advantage and its valuation discount.
  • Public & institutional float ~36%. The balance is held by domestic institutions (LIC, mutual funds), foreign investors and retail. The government's stake is high enough that periodic dilution risk — the state selling shares (OFS) or the bank raising equity — hangs over minority holders.
  • Board-driven, government-appointed leadership. Like all PSU banks, top management (MD & CEO, executive directors) is appointed via the government's Banks Board Bureau process, with fixed tenures — a governance model that differs sharply from the founder- or professional-CEO continuity of private banks.
  • A financial-services group. Alongside the bank sit subsidiaries and JVs in cards (BOBCARD), investment banking (BOB Capital Markets), asset management (Baroda BNP Paribas MF), life insurance (IndiaFirst Life) and a majority stake in Nainital Bank — covered in §09.
04

The 2019 three-way merger

The consolidation that created today's Bank of Baroda.

On 1 April 2019, in the first three-way amalgamation in Indian banking, the government folded two weaker public-sector banks — Vijaya Bank (a relatively healthy south-India lender) and Dena Bank (a troubled, NPA-heavy bank under RBI's corrective-action framework) — into Bank of Baroda. Overnight, BoB jumped to ~₹15 lakh crore of total business and became one of India's largest banks, with deposit and advance market shares of roughly 7%.

Why the government did it

  • PSU consolidation. India had too many small, sub-scale state banks. Merging them into a few strong anchors was meant to create globally competitive banks, cut duplicated costs, and give weak banks (Dena) the balance sheet of a strong one (BoB) to absorb their bad loans.
  • Rescue by absorption. Dena Bank was in such poor shape it had been barred from fresh lending. Merging it into BoB — cushioned by Vijaya Bank's healthier book — was a way to resolve a failing bank without a disorderly collapse.
  • Scale & reach. The combined entity gained a far larger branch network (especially Vijaya's strong southern presence, complementing BoB's western/northern base) and a bigger, more diversified deposit franchise.
The merger's legacy in today's numbers

Unlike a private-bank merger, this was a government-engineered consolidation, and its digestion is largely behind BoB — branches and systems were integrated by 2020, and the extra bad loans Dena brought in have long since been provisioned. The lasting effect is scale and geographic balance: today's BoB is a genuinely national bank with a coast-to-coast footprint it did not have a decade ago. When you see "Bank of Baroda" today, you are really looking at three banks in one — which is why its FY19-onward figures step up sharply versus its history, and why comparisons to pre-2019 numbers can mislead.

05

The turnaround and the re-rating story

The single most important thing to understand about this bank — how a lender goes from losses to ~₹20,000 Cr of profit.

Rewind to FY16. Bank of Baroda's gross NPAs were spiking toward ~12%, provisions had exploded almost five-fold, and the bank posted the largest quarterly loss in Indian banking history at the time. It was one of the poster children for the great Indian PSU bad-loan crisis — years of aggressive, poorly-underwritten corporate lending (infrastructure, steel, power) that turned sour all at once. Understanding how a bank climbs out of that hole is the key to the entire BoB investment case, because a bank's earnings are governed less by how much it lends than by how little of what it lent goes bad.

The mechanism: why cleaning bad loans creates profit

A bank's profit is roughly net interest income + fees − operating costs − loan-loss provisions − tax. In the crisis years, the provisions line was so vast — the bank was forced to set aside money against tens of thousands of crores of bad loans — that it wiped out the entire spread the bank earned, pushing it into losses. Why this drives the "re-rating"

A "re-rating" means the market pays a higher multiple for the same earnings because it trusts them more. For years, investors valued BoB cheaply because its profits were fragile — a bad credit cycle could vaporise them, as FY16 showed. Now that GNPA is under 2%, net NPA is a negligible 0.45%, provision coverage is ~94% and credit cost is a low ~0.76%, those earnings look durable. The bull thesis is simple: a bank earning ~1% on assets with a clean book and a ~15% ROE should trade closer to book value or above — yet BoB trades at ~0.85× book. Closing even part of that gap is the re-rating prize. The bear counter is that PSU banks always look cheap for structural reasons (§08) that never fully go away.

Why this is the section that matters: almost every good number in BoB's FY26 results — ~₹20,000 Cr profit, ~1% ROA, a ~15% ROE — exists because the credit cost fell from crisis-era levels to ~0.76%. The franchise didn't suddenly get better at lending; it stopped losing money on old loans. Watch slippages and credit cost every quarter: they are the vital signs of whether the clean book stays clean.
06

Deposits, NIM and the margin squeeze

The live pressure point — how a bank earns, and why the spread is under strain right now.

With the bad-loan problem solved, the swing factor for BoB's earnings has shifted to the margin. And the margin is being squeezed from both sides: the RBI's rate-cut cycle drags loan yields down quickly, while a deposit war — banks competing hard for a shrinking pool of household savings — keeps funding costs stubbornly high. FY26's NIM slid to 2.89% in Q4 from ~3.1%, and that ~0.2-point compression is why profit growth stalled at ~2% despite 16% loan growth.

Why margins are hard to defend right now

  • Loans reprice faster than deposits. A large share of BoB's loans are linked to external benchmarks (the RBI repo rate). When the RBI cuts, those loan rates drop almost immediately — but the bank's existing fixed deposits keep paying their old, higher rates until they mature. So in a rate-cut cycle, income falls before costs do, and NIM compresses first.
  • The deposit war. Indian households are increasingly moving savings into mutual funds, equities and higher-yield products. That shrinks the pool of cheap bank deposits, so every bank — public and private — must offer higher fixed-deposit rates to attract funds. Higher deposit costs eat directly into the spread.
  • CASA is slipping. BoB's domestic CASA fell from ~40% to ~38.9% year-on-year. Because CASA is the cheapest funding, every point lost must be replaced with pricier term deposits — a slow, structural drag on margin.

Why the deposit franchise is the ultimate moat

Anyone can lend money; the hard, valuable part of banking is gathering deposits cheaply and reliably. A bank with a sticky, low-cost deposit base can lend profitably through any cycle; one that has to "buy" deposits at high rates is always squeezed. BoB's advantage here is its sovereign backing and 117-year brand: as a government-majority bank, its deposits are perceived as ultra-safe, so nervous savers park money with it even at slightly lower rates. That is a genuine funding edge — but it is being tested by a savings market that increasingly prizes returns over safety. Watch the cost of deposits and CASA ratio: if they stabilise while the rate-cut cycle ends, NIM can recover; if CASA keeps bleeding, the margin stays under pressure.

07

The retail shift and the bob World engine

Moving the loan book toward higher-yield, granular lending — and distributing it digitally.

The other half of BoB's post-crisis strategy is a deliberate shift in the loan mix. The bad-loan crisis was a corporate-lending disaster — a handful of large infrastructure and industrial borrowers defaulted and blew a hole in the book. The lesson learned across every PSU bank was the same: grow the granular, secured retail and agri book, where losses are small and diversified, and be far more selective with big corporate loans. That mix shift is both safer and higher-yielding — retail loans typically earn more than wholesale corporate loans.

bob World — the digital distribution layer

bob World is Bank of Baroda's unified mobile-banking and digital platform, launched after the 2019 merger integration. It matters for three reasons:

  • Cheaper distribution. Onboarding customers, opening accounts and disbursing small retail loans through an app costs a fraction of doing it through a branch. For a bank with ~8,000 branches and a huge rural base, digital is the way to grow the customer count without proportionally growing cost.
  • Deposit and cross-sell reach. A good app helps gather low-cost CASA deposits and cross-sell cards, insurance and investments — feeding both the funding side (cheaper deposits) and the fee side (non-interest income).
  • Data-driven, safer lending. Digital footprints and account data let the bank underwrite small retail and MSME loans faster and more accurately — supporting the granular-book strategy that keeps NPAs low.
Why the retail shift is a quiet margin defence

Here's the elegant part: the same strategy that made BoB safer also makes it more profitable per rupee lent. Retail and MSME loans (home, auto, personal, small-business) typically yield more than large corporate loans, which are priced razor-thin because big companies can shop around. So by tilting the book toward retail/agri/MSME — which grew 16–21% in FY26 versus 11% for corporate — BoB is quietly lifting its average loan yield, which partly offsets the deposit-cost pressure squeezing its margin. Digital (bob World) makes that shift cheaper to execute at scale. The risk to watch is unsecured personal lending, where a downturn can bite — but so far BoB has grown this segment more cautiously than some peers.

08

Government ownership and the PSU-discount debate

Why a clean, profitable bank still trades below book value — and whether that ever changes.

Here is the puzzle at the heart of the BoB stock: it earns ~1% on assets, ~15% on equity, has a fortress ~94% provision coverage and single-digit-below-book valuation — yet the market prices it at a P/E of ~7× and ~0.85× book, while private peers HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank trade at ~18–20× earnings and 2–3× book. That gap — the "PSU discount" — is not an accident. It reflects real structural concerns about government-owned banks, and whether it narrows is the single biggest driver of the stock's return.

Indicative multiples. PSU banks trade at a fraction of private-bank valuations — BoB at roughly a third of HDFC/ICICI's P/E. The re-rating thesis is that this gap narrows as PSU asset quality and returns prove durable; the value-trap fear is that it never fully does.

Why PSU banks trade at a discount

  • Governance & non-commercial mandates. The government is both owner and policymaker. PSU banks can be asked to support social schemes, farm-loan waivers, priority-sector lending and national-priority projects — lending that may not maximise shareholder returns. Management is government-appointed on fixed tenures, so continuity and incentives differ from a founder-led private bank.
  • The NPA scar tissue. Institutional investors remember the FY16–FY18 crisis, when PSU banks' hidden bad loans destroyed years of profit. Even with clean books today, that credibility gap lingers in the multiple.
  • Dilution risk. With the government at ~64%, there is persistent overhang from potential equity raises or government share sales (OFS) that could dilute minority holders — and PSU banks have historically diluted at low valuations to shore up capital.
  • Slower structural growth. Private banks have taken market share from PSU banks for two decades on service, technology and underwriting. The market extrapolates that trend.
The re-rating case vs the value-trap case

The bull ("closing the gap"): BoB's fundamentals now rival a private bank — ~1% ROA, ~15% ROE, sub-2% GNPA, strong capital. If it sustains this for a few years, the market should stop pricing it like a fragile, crisis-prone lender and re-rate it from ~0.85× book toward, or above, book value — a big move even without earnings growth. Foreign investors gradually re-entering PSU banks, and the government's own interest in higher PSU-bank valuations (it makes divestment easier), both support this.

The bear ("value trap"): PSU banks have looked "cheap" for a decade and the discount has never fully closed, because the structural reasons — government control, mandate risk, dilution, slower growth — never go away. A clean book is necessary but not sufficient; without a change in ownership structure or governance, the discount is permanent, and the low multiple is the market correctly pricing lower-quality, government-directed earnings.

How to hold both ideas: BoB can be a genuinely good, cheap bank and a stock whose discount only partly closes. The realistic middle case is that a proven-durable clean book earns a modest re-rating (toward ~1× book) plus a healthy dividend yield (~3%+) — a decent return — rather than a full convergence to private-bank multiples. The swing factor is credibility: every quarter of clean, stable results chips away at the discount; any fresh asset-quality wobble or dilutive capital raise widens it again.
09

The international arm and the subsidiaries

"India's International Bank" — plus a spread of financial-services businesses.

Two features distinguish BoB from a typical domestic PSU bank: an unusually large overseas footprint, and a set of subsidiaries and joint ventures across cards, asset management, life insurance and investment banking. Neither is huge relative to the core bank, but together they add diversification and a sliver of hidden value.

International operations

  • ~17 countries. BoB has branded itself "India's International Bank" for decades. It operates through overseas subsidiaries in the UK, Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Botswana, Guyana and New Zealand, a joint venture in Malaysia, and branches in financial hubs (Singapore, the Gulf, the US) plus GIFT City in India.
  • What it adds. Overseas operations serve the Indian diaspora and trade-finance flows, provide dollar funding and forex fee income, and diversify the loan book geographically. It is a modest but genuine differentiator — most PSU peers are almost entirely domestic.
  • The caveat. International lending has historically carried its own risks (some of BoB's past bad loans were overseas), and the segment is lower-margin and more cyclical than the domestic book. It is optionality, not the core engine.

Don't overstate them — BoB is ~95%+ a plain lending bank, and the core bank drives the numbers. For now, treat them as a modest positive and a source of optionality, not a reason to own the stock.

Financials & valuation

Clean book, decent returns, at a striking discount — the debate is durability and dilution.

BoB's financials read like a healed PSU bank: strong loan growth, a clean balance sheet, ~1% return on assets and ~15% return on equity — but with margin pressure capping earnings growth, and a valuation that prices in heavy scepticism. The debate is less "is it a healthy bank" (it plainly is now) and more "how durable are the returns, and will the discount ever close".

How to think about the valuation

BoB trades around a ~₹1.40 lakh crore market cap (~₹270/share) on FY26 earnings — roughly 7× earnings, ~0.85× book value, with a ~3% dividend yield. For a bank, the multiple that matters most is price-to-book against ROE: a bank earning a ~15% ROE would, in a "normal" world, trade around or above 1× book. BoB's sub-book valuation is the market applying a large "PSU discount" — pricing in government control, mandate/dilution risk and lingering NPA scepticism (§08). The bull case is that a durable clean book earns a re-rating toward 1×+ book (a substantial move) plus a healthy dividend; the bear case is that the discount is structural and the low multiple correctly prices lower-quality, government-directed earnings. Unlike a private bank, the debate here is more about the multiple than the earnings.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A healed, cheap PSU bank — the debate is durability, margins and the discount.

Bank of Baroda has done the hard part: it has climbed out of a near-existential bad-loan crisis to a clean book, steady ~₹20,000 Cr profits and ~1% returns on assets. The investment debate now is almost entirely about whether those returns are durable (can it defend margins in a tough rate-and-deposit cycle?) and whether the market ever pays up for a government-owned bank.

The bull case

  • Clean, de-risked book — GNPA 1.89%, NNPA 0.45%, PCR ~94%; the crisis-era risk is provided-for and behind it.
  • Strong, granular loan growth — advances +16.2%, led by higher-yielding retail (+18%), agri (+21%) and MSME (+16%).
  • Cheap valuation — ~7× P/E, ~0.85× book for a ~15% ROE bank, plus a ~3% dividend yield; a modest re-rating is a big return.
  • Sovereign-backed deposit franchise — ultra-safe, low-cost funding and a 117-year national + international network.
  • Optionality — subsidiaries (cards, AMC, IndiaFirst Life) and an overseas arm add fee income and latent value.

The bear case

  • Margin squeeze — NIM slipped to 2.89%; rate cuts and the deposit war keep spreads under pressure, capping profit growth (~2% in FY26).
  • The PSU discount may be permanent — government control, social-lending mandates and dilution risk have kept the multiple low for a decade.
  • Dilution overhang — with the state at ~64%, equity raises or OFS could dilute minority holders, often at low valuations.
  • CASA erosion — the cheap-funding advantage is slowly bleeding as savers chase higher returns.
  • Cyclical & governance risk — asset quality can turn in a downturn; government-appointed management and mandate risk cap the quality premium.
The bottom line

Bank of Baroda is a genuinely healed public-sector bank trading at a fraction of private-bank valuations. The business risk is far lower than it was a decade ago — the book is clean, capital is strong, and returns are respectable. The two live questions are: can it defend the margin through the current rate-and-deposit cycle so that its strong loan growth translates into profit growth again; and will a durable clean record finally narrow the PSU discount. The story over the next few years will be written in a handful of numbers each quarter — NIM and cost of deposits, slippages and credit cost, and CASA. Hold those steady and a cheap, clean bank can re-rate and pay you a healthy dividend while you wait; let margins or asset quality slip, and it stays exactly what the market fears — a permanent value trap.
