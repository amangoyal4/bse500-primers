00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Equitas Small Finance Bank is a Chennai-headquartered small finance bank — a special class of bank licensed to serve small borrowers and the financially under-served. What makes it unusual within that class is its book: unlike the microfinance-heavy SFBs it is often lumped with, Equitas has spent years turning itself into a mostly-secured lender, led by small-business loans against property and used-commercial-vehicle finance, with a deliberately shrunken microfinance tail. About 86% of its ₹36,209-crore loan book is secured.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • Secured by design, not by accident. Small-business loans (SBL) are the largest slice at roughly 43% of advances, used-vehicle finance about 25%, and microfinance — once a quarter of the book — is down to about 12%. The rest is affordable housing, micro/SME loans and other retail. This is a bank that chose collateral over yield years ago.
  • FY25 was a genuinely bad year — because of the smallest book. Net profit fell from ₹799 Cr in FY24 to just ₹147 Cr in FY25, and return on equity dropped from ~13.5% to ~2.4%. Almost the entire hit came from the microfinance tail as the industry-wide 2024–25 stress in unsecured rural lending broke over the sector. The secured 86% kept performing; the unsecured 12% did the damage.
  • The deposit franchise kept growing through it. Deposits rose 19.3% to ₹43,107 Cr — faster than the loan book — with current-account balances up ~36%. An SFB lives or dies by whether it can gather cheap, sticky deposits; Equitas is still winning that race even in a bad credit year.
In plain terms

Picture Equitas as a ₹43,000-crore deposit machine feeding a loan book that is four-fifths collateral-backed. It borrows from savers, lends mostly to shopkeepers (against their property) and truck-owners (against their vehicles), and pockets the gap. It kept a small microfinance business on the side — tiny unsecured loans to rural women's groups — and that small business is exactly what blew a hole in FY25 profit when rural India's borrowers overstretched. The secured core barely flinched. The whole investment question is how fast that microfinance wound heals, and whether the secured engine plus a growing deposit base can carry returns back to normal.

01

What a small finance bank actually is

A licence category invented for financial inclusion — and the five numbers that run any bank.

A small finance bank (SFB) is a full-service bank with a specific mandate. The Reserve Bank of India created the category in 2015 to push formal banking to people the big banks overlooked — small businesses, marginal farmers, micro-enterprises, the informal sector. In return for a licence, an SFB accepts rules a universal bank doesn't: at least 75% of its loans must go to "priority sectors", and at least 50% of its loan book must be small-ticket loans (up to ₹25 lakh). It can take deposits like any bank — that is the whole point.

So an SFB is not a microfinance company with a fancier name. It is a bank, subject to the same core arithmetic as HDFC or SBI, just pointed at a different, smaller-ticket customer. And a bank's arithmetic comes down to five terms.

The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. For a universal bank the spread is thin (~3%) and losses are tiny, so scale and cheap funding win. For an SFB the spread is wide (~6–8%) because the borrowers are riskier — which means the loan-loss line is where fortunes are made or lost. A great SFB year and a terrible one can share the same revenue; the difference is entirely in credit cost. That is precisely what separated Equitas's FY24 (₹799 Cr profit) from its FY25 (₹147 Cr): the top line barely changed, but credit cost tripled.

Why an SFB is really two businesses stitched together

  • A lending business — reaching small, informal borrowers at high yields, which requires feet-on-street collection and hyper-local underwriting. This is where an SFB's roots (usually microfinance) lie.
  • A deposit business — persuading savers to trust a young, small-brand bank with their money, cheaply. This is the harder, newer muscle every SFB must build, because without low-cost deposits the wide lending spread gets eaten by expensive funding.
  • The bridge is trust and cost. An SFB that only lends well but funds expensively has no durable edge. The winners are those that convert a lending franchise into a genuine bank — with sticky CASA, salary accounts and current accounts. Equitas's deposit growth says it is building that bridge.
02

How the money flows: deposits → secured loans → margin

Read this once and the whole bank — and why FY25 broke — clicks into place.

A bank is a conveyor belt for money: savers' deposits come in one end, loans go out the other, and the spread between the two is harvested as net interest income. For Equitas the belt has a distinctive shape. The money going out is mostly secured — loans backed by a shop, a house or a truck — so even when borrowers stumble, there is collateral to recover against. The exception is the microfinance slice, which is unsecured, and that is exactly the segment that jams the belt when rural incomes wobble.

Why "secured" changes the whole risk picture

When a secured borrower defaults, the bank can seize and sell the collateral — a used lorry, a small commercial property — recovering much of the loan. When an unsecured microfinance borrower defaults, there is nothing to seize; the loss is close to total. That single difference is why Equitas's secured pivot matters so much: it doesn't stop borrowers from defaulting, but it caps how much each default costs. A secured book has a floor under its losses that an unsecured book does not.

In a year when the lending side was under stress, the funding side quietly got stronger — which is exactly the foundation a recovery is built on.

03

The company and who controls it

From a Chennai microfinance NGO to a listed bank that absorbed its own holding company.

Equitas traces back to 2007, when it was founded in Chennai as a microfinance lender with a strong financial-inclusion ethos. Over the next decade it broadened into vehicle finance and small-business lending, won a small finance bank licence, and began operating as Equitas Small Finance Bank in September 2016. Its defining structural quirk is that it later absorbed its own parent: the listed holding company, Equitas Holdings, was reverse-merged into the bank, collapsing a two-layer structure into one listed entity.

  • 2011–2015

  • FY25
    The MFI stress year — microfinance credit cost spikes, group credit cost hits 2.32%, and net profit collapses to ₹147 Cr even as deposits (+19%) and advances (+17%) keep growing.
  • 2025–26

Ownership & management

  • No promoter, widely held. After the reverse merger of Equitas Holdings, the bank has no identifiable promoter — it is a professionally-managed, widely-held institution owned by public shareholders, institutions and funds. That removes promoter-overhang risk but also means no anchor shareholder.
  • Institutional register. Domestic and foreign institutions, mutual funds and retail investors hold the stock; there is no controlling block. Governance rests on the board and professional management rather than a founding family.
  • Management continuity. The leadership has carried the secured-diversification strategy for years — the microfinance de-risking was a chosen, multi-year plan, not a panic reaction to FY25. That consistency is the franchise's soft asset.
  • South-India roots, national reach. Equitas is strongest in Tamil Nadu and the South but operates a nationwide branch and asset-finance network, with the small-business and vehicle books giving it geographic and product spread that a pure microlender lacks.
04

The secured pivot — why Equitas doesn't look like its peers

The single decision that defines the bank: collateral first, yield second.

Most small finance banks were born from microfinance and still lean on it — lots of small, unsecured group loans at very high yields. Equitas made a different choice years ago: it deliberately built a secured book around two engines — small-business loans (SBL) secured against property and used-commercial-vehicle finance secured against the vehicle — and consciously let microfinance shrink as a share of the whole. Today about 86% of the loan book is secured, and microfinance is a minority tail. This is the fact that makes Equitas distinct in its cohort, and it is the lens through which every other number should be read.

The two secured engines, explained

  • Small-business loans (SBL) — the anchor. These are loans to small shopkeepers, traders and self-employed people, secured against property (often the borrower's shop or home). Ticket sizes are modest, yields are healthy, and — crucially — there is collateral. Because these borrowers are informal and hard for big banks to underwrite, a specialist SFB earns a wide spread while still holding security. This is Equitas's largest and most defining business.
  • Used-commercial-vehicle finance — the cyclical engine. Equitas lends to buyers of used trucks and commercial vehicles — small transporters and first-time owners the mainstream lenders avoid. The vehicle is the collateral. This book is more cyclical (it moves with freight demand, fuel prices and the broader economy) but it is secured and high-yielding, and it gives Equitas a genuine niche few banks compete in seriously.
  • Housing and micro-mortgages — the long-duration ballast. Affordable-housing and property-backed micro-loans add lower-risk, longer-tenure secured assets that steady the book and support the priority-sector requirement.
The mental model: a bank that swapped yield for collateral

The trade Equitas made is simple to state and hard to execute. A pure microfinance book yields more — unsecured group loans carry the highest rates in retail lending — but every downturn threatens to wipe out a chunk of it, because there is nothing to recover. By tilting toward SBL and used-vehicle finance, Equitas accepted somewhat lower headline yields in exchange for collateral under most of its loans. The pay-off is not visible in good years, when even unsecured books look fine. It shows up precisely in bad ones — like FY25 — when the secured 86% keeps paying and recovering while the unsecured tail takes the pain. The secured pivot is an insurance policy you only see cash in during a storm.

Why this is the section that matters: keep Equitas mentally separate from the more microfinance-heavy SFBs it is grouped with. Its diversified, secured mix is the reason it entered the 2024–25 stress cycle with only about an eighth of its book in the unsecured firing line — a structurally cushioned position, not an exposed one. Every judgement about its risk and its recovery should start here.
05

The microfinance de-risking — and the 2024–25 storm

The book Equitas spent years shrinking is the one that still hurt it — and the tale of the cycle.

Microfinance — tiny, unsecured loans to groups of rural women — is where Equitas began, and for years it was the highest-yielding, fastest-growing part of Indian small-ticket lending. But it is also the most fragile: unsecured, concentrated in the rural informal economy, and prone to over-lending cycles where too many lenders pile credit onto the same borrowers until repayment breaks down. Equitas saw the fragility early and spent years running microfinance down as a share of its book — from about a quarter of loans in 2019 to roughly an eighth by 2025.

The share was cut roughly in half over six years — a chosen, gradual de-risking, not a fire-sale. It meant Equitas met the 2024–25 stress with a much smaller unsecured exposure than a microfinance-led SFB.

What went wrong across the industry in 2024–25

Through 2024, India's microfinance sector ran into a classic over-leverage cycle. After a post-pandemic lending boom, many rural borrowers were carrying loans from multiple lenders at once; when incomes tightened, repayment discipline broke down in pockets, delinquencies rose across the whole industry, and lenders had to write off unsecured loans en masse. Every microfinance-exposed lender — banks, SFBs and NBFC-MFIs alike — took a hit. Equitas was cushioned by its secured mix, but not immune: its ~12% microfinance tail deteriorated along with the industry, and that is where FY25's damage was concentrated.

The genuine surprise: an eighth of the book, most of the damage

Here is the counter-intuitive heart of the Equitas story. This is an 86%-secured bank — and yet its smallest, ~12% unsecured microfinance book drove profit down by roughly 82%, from ₹799 Cr to ₹147 Cr in a single year. Revenue barely moved; the entire swing was loan losses on the microfinance tail. That is the mathematics of unsecured lending: a small book with a high loss rate can overwhelm a large book with a low one. The secured pivot did its job — non-MFI credit cost stayed modest, and the collateralised 86% kept paying — but it cushioned the blow rather than preventing it. Anyone who assumed "mostly secured" meant "insulated from the MFI cycle" learned the difference in FY25.

The read-through: because the wound is concentrated in a small, shrinking, unsecured book, the recovery mechanism is clear and relatively fast — as the MFI cycle turns and that tail is provided-for and worked down, credit cost normalises back toward the ~1.4% through-cycle average, and profit snaps back off a depressed base. The thing to watch each quarter is the microfinance credit cost specifically, not the group headline.
06

The deposit & CASA franchise — the real moat

Anyone can lend; the hard part is funding it cheaply. This is where an SFB becomes a bank.

Lending, for all its drama, is the easier half of banking. The genuinely hard, slow, valuable work is gathering deposits — persuading savers to hand a young, mid-sized bank their money at a low rate, and to keep it there. For an SFB with a wide lending spread, cheap funding is what turns a high gross yield into a durable net margin. Equitas's deposit franchise — ₹43,107 Cr and growing ~19% a year, with current accounts up ~36% — is arguably the most under-appreciated part of the story.

A ~29% CASA ratio is respectable for a young SFB but leaves the book leaning on costlier term deposits. Lifting CASA is the single biggest lever on funding cost — and therefore on net margin — over the next few years.

Why CASA is the number to compound

  • Current accounts pay ~0%, savings ~3–4%, fixed deposits 7–8%. The more of the funding that sits in CASA, the cheaper the whole book, and the wider the spread on the same loan. The ~36% jump in current-account balances is a direct attack on funding cost.
  • An SFB competes on rate, then earns loyalty. Young banks often win savers first with higher deposit rates than incumbents offer, then work to convert that base into stickier, lower-cost CASA and salary relationships. Equitas is mid-way through exactly that transition.
  • Deposits are getting scarcer industry-wide. Indian households are shifting savings toward mutual funds and equities, so every bank is fighting for the same pool. An SFB that keeps growing deposits ~19% through a bad credit year — as Equitas did — is demonstrating the funding franchise actually works.
Why the funding side is the quiet recovery engine

Two things can drive Equitas's net margin back up as the MFI cycle heals. One is obvious — lower credit cost. The other is quieter — cheaper funding. Every basis point that CASA rises, and every rupee of expensive borrowing replaced by a retail deposit, widens the spread independently of what happens to loan losses. So even before the microfinance wound fully closes, an improving deposit mix can lift profitability. The deposit franchise isn't glamorous, but it is the part of Equitas that kept getting stronger in its worst year — and that is exactly the base a recovery compounds from.

07

Asset quality and the FY25 air-pocket

Elevated but concentrated, well-provided-for, and — because it is mostly secured — recoverable.

Asset quality is where an SFB's whole story is settled. Equitas ended FY25 with gross NPAs around 2.9% and net NPAs under ~1% — elevated versus its own benign FY24, but the detail matters more than the headline. The stress is concentrated in the microfinance tail; the secured SBL and vehicle books held up far better, with non-MFI credit cost running near ~0.9% even in the worst quarter. And because most of the book is secured, even the loans that go bad carry collateral to recover against.

The scoreboard reads brutally at first glance — return on equity fell from ~13.5% in FY24 to ~2.4% in FY25, and return on assets to just ~0.32%. But that is a trough driven almost entirely by the credit-cost spike, not by any collapse in the underlying franchise. It is the depressed base a recovery lifts off, not evidence of a broken bank.

What "provisioning" means and why the secured mix helps

When a loan turns doubtful, the bank sets aside money to cover the expected loss — a provision, charged against profit. FY25's profit collapse was, in large part, a wave of provisions against the microfinance tail. Two things make this less alarming than a bare ₹147 Cr profit suggests. First, provisions are forward-looking — Equitas took the pain up front, so the net NPA left on the book is small and well-covered. Second, for the secured loans, a provision is not the end of the story: the bank can still recover value by selling the collateral, so realised losses on SBL and vehicle loans run far below the microfinance loss rate. The unsecured tail is where provisions convert to permanent losses; the secured core is where much of the money comes back.

Why the damage is recoverable, not structural

  • Concentrated, not broad-based. The stress sits in ~12% of the book. The other ~88% — secured SBL, vehicle, housing — performed through the cycle, so this is a segment problem, not a franchise problem.
  • Collateral under most defaults. An 86%-secured book means most bad loans can be worked out against security, capping ultimate losses in a way a pure microlender cannot.
  • Provisioned early. By taking heavy provisions in FY25, Equitas cleared much of the bad news off the books, leaving a low net NPA and setting up a cleaner FY26 as the MFI cycle turns.
The honest caveat: a trough year is only reassuring if the cycle actually turns — if rural stress persists or the vehicle-finance book weakens in a slowdown, credit cost could stay elevated longer than bulls expect. The evidence (secured mix, early provisioning, non-MFI credit cost near ~0.9%) points to a concentrated, recoverable shock rather than a structural break — but the proof is a falling microfinance credit cost over the coming quarters, not a single reassuring slide.

The rivals and the universal-bank prize

A crowded SFB cohort — and the licence upgrade that could reset the whole category.

Equitas sits in a competitive small-finance-bank cohort, but its secured, diversified profile sets it apart from the more microfinance-led names. The comparison that matters most is with Ujjivan SFB (a larger, more microfinance-heavy peer) and AU Small Finance Bank (the scale leader, also secured-and-vehicle-led and the furthest along toward becoming a full bank). Above all of them hovers the same tantalising prize: the universal banking licence that would let an SFB shed its restrictive mandate and compete as an ordinary bank.

The universal-bank prize — and why Equitas is in no hurry

A universal banking licence would let an SFB drop the restrictive quotas (75% priority-sector, 50% small-ticket) and operate as an ordinary bank — lower regulatory drag, a wider product set, and typically a valuation re-rating. Equitas meets the eligibility gate on paper: the RBI's thresholds require gross NPAs under ~3% and net NPAs under ~1%, and at December 2025 Equitas stood around 2.75% / 0.92%. Yet management has deliberately paused a formal application, choosing to study the rejected applications of peers (Jana, Ujjivan) and strengthen its model first rather than risk a public knock-back. It is a telling piece of discipline: eligible, but unwilling to spend the option prematurely.

The regulatory backdrop: SFBs live under the RBI's SFB framework — priority-sector and small-ticket quotas, capital rules, and tight provisioning norms — plus, for microfinance, the RBI's borrower-level indebtedness and pricing rules that were themselves tightened after the 2024–25 stress. The path to a universal licence, the RBI's stance on MFI lending, and deposit-competition dynamics all directly shape Equitas's room to manoeuvre. Regulatory comfort with its asset quality is itself part of the investment case.

Financials & valuation

A trough year in earnings against a growing balance sheet — priced as a recovery bet, not a growth darling.

Equitas's FY25 financials read like a good bank caught in a bad segment cycle: a balance sheet that kept scaling, a wide net margin, and a profit line temporarily wrecked by microfinance provisions. The debate is not whether the franchise works — the secured book and deposit growth say it does — but how fast earnings normalise off a depressed base as the MFI cycle turns.

How to think about the valuation

With a market capitalisation around ~₹6,500 Cr (mid-2026, approximate) against a fast-growing ₹43,000-crore deposit base and ₹36,000-crore loan book, Equitas trades at a fraction of book value — a discount that reflects the FY25 earnings trough and lingering MFI-cycle uncertainty rather than any doubt about the deposit or secured-lending franchise. For a bank, the multiple that matters is price-to-book measured against return on equity: at a trough ~2.4% RoE the low multiple is arithmetic, not a bargain. The re-rating case rests on one thing — RoE climbing back toward the low-to-mid teens as microfinance credit cost normalises and the secured book plus cheaper funding reassert themselves. The metric that settles the thesis is therefore RoA (and RoE) recovery, gated by the microfinance credit cost: when that credit cost drops back under ~1% and RoA crosses back above ~1.5%, the earnings base — and the multiple — should follow.

10

The growth runway

A cyclical recovery first, then a structural climb toward a fuller bank.

Equitas's path from here has two layers: a near-term cyclical recovery as the microfinance shock passes, and a longer structural climb — deeper secured lending, a richer deposit mix, and eventually the optionality of a universal licence. The engines don't need a new strategy to fire; they need the credit cycle to turn and the deposit franchise to keep compounding.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cushioned franchise in a bad-year trough — the debate is recovery speed, not survival.

Equitas is a fundamentally sound small finance bank caught mid-way through an industry-wide microfinance shock it was structurally well-positioned for — but not immune to. The secured book, the growing deposit franchise and the early, heavy provisioning all point to a concentrated, recoverable setback. The investment debate is about how quickly returns climb out of the FY25 trough, and whether the credit cycle cooperates.

The bull case

  • Secured by design — ~86% collateral-backed, led by SBL and used-vehicle finance, with the unsecured MFI tail down to ~12%. The stress is concentrated and recoverable.
  • Depressed earnings base — FY25 RoE of ~2.4% is a trough; as microfinance credit cost normalises toward ~1.4%, profit snaps back off a low base.
  • Deposit franchise compounding — deposits +19%, current accounts +36%, CASA ~29% — cheaper funding widens margin independently of loan losses.
  • Wide, durable spread — ~6.6% NIM, far above a universal bank's, from serving borrowers the big banks can't underwrite economically.
  • Universal-licence optionality — eligibility already met (GNPA/NNPA within thresholds); a future application is a potential re-rating catalyst.

The bear case

  • The MFI cycle may not be done — if rural stress lingers, credit cost stays elevated and the recovery keeps slipping.
  • Vehicle-finance cyclicality — the used-CV book moves with freight demand, fuel and the economy; a slowdown would pressure a second engine.
  • Deposit competition & cost — savers chasing mutual funds make cheap deposits scarce; funding cost could stay high and cap the margin recovery.
  • NIM drift — de-risking toward secured lending structurally lowers yields; the spread may not return to FY24 levels.
  • No promoter, execution-dependent — a widely-held bank with no anchor owner leans entirely on management execution and the RBI's evolving rules.
The bottom line

Equitas is the small finance bank that de-risked to a secured book before the storm arrived — and then discovered that a well-run insurance policy cushions a blow without cancelling it. FY25 proved both halves of that sentence: an eighth of the book, the microfinance tail, drove an ~82% profit fall, while the secured 86% and a ₹43,000-crore deposit franchise kept compounding right through the worst of it. The business risk is contained; the open question is pace — how fast the microfinance credit cost falls back under ~1% and returns climb out of the trough. The next milestone that matters isn't the universal-bank paperwork the market keeps asking about; it is the first clean quarter where microfinance provisions fade and RoA crosses back above ~1.5%. That, more than any licence, is what re-rates this bank.
