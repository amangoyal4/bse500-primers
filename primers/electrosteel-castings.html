Electrosteel Castings, the pipe under India's taps

A full walk through one of India's oldest and largest makers of ductile-iron water pipes — the buried metal spine that carries drinking water and sewage through a city. It explains why a seemingly dull pipe business swings so violently with a single line in the Union Budget, why owning its own iron ore and coke is the real profit engine, and how a record FY24 turned into a bruising FY26 the moment the government slowed its water spending — just as a ₹8.69 lakh crore mission promises to turn the tap back on.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Electrosteel Castings makes the pipes that carry water. Specifically, it is one of India's oldest and largest producers of ductile-iron (DI) pipes — the thick, coated, cast-iron pipes buried under roads that move drinking water from a treatment plant to a city's homes, and sewage away from them. Founded by the Kejriwal family in 1955 and headquartered in Kolkata, the company also makes DI fittings, flanged pipes, cast-iron pipes and pig iron, and it exports pipes to Europe, the Middle East, Africa and the Americas. Its customers are overwhelmingly governments: state water boards, municipal corporations and the contractors who build India's water networks under national schemes.

Three facts frame everything about this company:

  • It is a leveraged bet on government water spending. DI pipes are almost entirely bought for public water-supply and sanitation projects. So Electrosteel's fortunes track a handful of Central schemes — above all the Jal Jeevan Mission (Har Ghar Jal), which set out to put a tap in every rural home, plus AMRUT for urban water and the Namami Gange river-cleaning programme. When Delhi writes cheques, pipes fly out of the door; when it pauses, they pile up.
  • FY26 was the pause. After a record FY24 (net profit ~₹740 Cr) and a strong FY25 (~₹710 Cr), the water-scheme tap slowed — the original Jal Jeevan Mission wound down toward its deadline and fresh ordering thinned. Volumes fell from ~7.81 lakh tonnes in FY25 to ~5.84 lakh tonnes in FY26, revenue dropped ~18% to ~₹5,918 Cr, and net profit collapsed ~77% to ~₹161 Cr. The factories didn't change; the government's spending did.
  • The next tap is already turning. In March 2026 the Union Cabinet approved Jal Jeevan Mission 2.0, extending the programme to December 2028 with a total outlay reported around ₹8.69 lakh crore. DI-pipe stocks jumped on the news — Electrosteel hit its 20% upper circuit. The whole investment debate is whether that fresh outlay converts into pipe orders fast enough to lift volumes back toward — and past — the FY24/FY25 peak.
In plain terms

Electrosteel Castings is a heavy metals manufacturer whose demand is set in a government budget meeting, not a boardroom. It digs iron ore, smelts it, and casts it into long pipes that a municipality buries to carry your tap water — a genuinely essential product with a near-monopoly-grade domestic industry structure. The catch is that it sells to one dominant customer type: the state. That makes revenue lumpy and tender-driven, ties working capital up in slow-paying government contracts, and means the company can do everything right in its factories and still watch profit fall by three-quarters because an election year slowed the flow of approvals. The bet is that a decade-long national push to give every Indian home piped water is far from finished — and that the next wave of spending has just been sanctioned.

01

The ductile-iron pipe, explained

No metallurgy degree needed — just why a city pays extra for this specific pipe.

Start with the product, because it is the whole business. A ductile-iron pipe is a pipe cast from a special grade of iron in which the carbon forms tiny spheres rather than flakes. That one change in microstructure makes the metal strong but slightly flexible — it can take pressure surges and ground movement without cracking, unlike the brittle grey cast-iron pipes of a century ago. Coat it inside with cement mortar and outside with zinc and bitumen, and you get a pipe that can sit buried, carrying pressurised water, for 50 to 100 years. For a city planner laying a water network meant to last generations, that longevity is the entire sales pitch.

Why a city pays more for iron than plastic

A municipality choosing a water main weighs cost against lifespan. Cheaper plastic (HDPE, PVC) pipes exist and are used for smaller distribution lines, but for the large, high-pressure trunk mains that form the backbone of a city's supply, ductile iron dominates — it withstands higher pressures, resists damage during digging and road traffic above it, and lasts far longer before it has to be dug up and replaced. Digging up a road to relay a pipe is enormously disruptive and expensive, so the buyer's real calculation is cost over the pipe's whole life, not the sticker price. On that basis, iron wins the backbone of almost every serious municipal water project — which is exactly why a government committed to universal piped water becomes a committed buyer of DI pipe.

Who actually buys the pipe

  • State water boards & municipal corporations — the direct end-users, laying supply and sewerage networks. The bulk of demand ultimately traces back here.
  • EPC & project contractors — the firms that win government water-project tenders and buy pipe as a bought-out input. Electrosteel often sells to and through them.
  • Export customers — utilities and contractors in Europe, the Middle East, Africa, South-East Asia and the Americas, served via Electrosteel's overseas subsidiaries. A meaningful and steadier slice of the order book.
02

What Electrosteel actually makes: one furnace, a ladder of products

Pipes are the story, but the plant produces a whole family of iron products.

At its heart Electrosteel runs an integrated iron-making operation that ends in a pipe. The same blast furnaces and casting lines that produce DI pipes also throw off a ladder of related products — some sold, some used internally — which is why the company describes itself as a castings maker, not merely a pipe seller. Understanding that ladder explains both the margins and the cyclicality.

Because Electrosteel owns Stages 1–3, its cost per tonne is set inside the company, not bought at market — the crux of the margin story in §05.

The product ladder, decoded

  • DI pipes — the core, the large majority of revenue. Sizes from small distribution pipe to very large trunk mains. This is where scale, quality certification and delivery reliability win orders.
  • DI fittings (DIF) — the bends, tees, tapers and joints that connect pipes into a real network. Small in tonnage but higher-value and stickier, since a network needs matched fittings for every pipe run.
  • DI flanged pipes & cast-iron (CI) pipes — specialised pipe for pumping stations and treatment plants, plus legacy grey cast-iron pipe. A supporting range that rounds out what a project needs.
  • Pig iron & by-products — surplus molten iron sold to foundries, plus ferro-alloys, coke, slag-based cement and captive power. These monetise the integrated plant and cushion the pipe cycle a little.
The counter-intuitive shape of the business

It is tempting to picture Electrosteel as a pipe factory that buys metal and shapes it. It is closer to the opposite: a captive iron-and-coke smelter that happens to pour its metal into a pipe mould at the end. Most of the value — and most of the cost risk — sits upstream, in ore, coke and furnaces, not in the casting line. That is why the company sells surplus pig iron, makes cement from its slag, and generates its own power: these are the natural outputs of running blast furnaces at scale. The pipe is the headline product, but the profit is really made or lost in how cheaply the furnaces turn ore into iron.

03

The Kejriwals, the plants, and the Srikalahasthi merger

A seven-decade Kolkata industrial house that consolidated its way to scale.

Electrosteel Castings is controlled by the Kejriwal family, a Kolkata industrial house that has run the company since its founding in 1955 and pioneered ductile-iron pipe manufacturing in India. The promoters hold roughly 50% of the company. The single most important corporate event of the last decade was the 2021 merger of Srikalahasthi Pipes — a sister DI-pipe company in Andhra Pradesh — into Electrosteel, which reshaped its scale, geography and cost structure.

  • FY24
    Peak year — record net profit of ~₹740 Cr and ROCE around 16%, riding the Jal Jeevan Mission pipe boom; capacity expansion pushed toward ~1 million tonnes of DI pipe.
  • FY25
    Still strong — revenue ~₹7,320 Cr, net profit ~₹710 Cr, pipe volume ~7.81 lakh tonnes — but the water-scheme cycle is quietly rolling over.
  • FY26
    The air-pocket — revenue ~₹5,918 Cr (−18%), net profit ~₹161 Cr (−77%), volume ~5.84 lakh tonnes, on a sharp slowdown in government water spending. Management targets doubling revenue over four years as JJM 2.0 (approved March 2026) revives demand.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Kejriwal family and associated entities hold roughly 50% of the equity (around 50.1% as of mid-2026), leaving a large public float. Investors should track any promoter-pledge levels directly in the shareholding disclosures.
  • Manufacturing footprint: plants at Khardah, Haldia and Bansberia (West Bengal), Elavur (Tamil Nadu) and Srikalahasthi (Andhra Pradesh) — a two-region base that serves the water-hungry east and south and keeps freight to project sites manageable (pipe is heavy and expensive to haul far).
  • The number to respect: Electrosteel Castings ended FY26 with a book value of around ₹96 per share against a market price near ₹83 — the stock trades a touch below its own net worth. That is unusual for a market leader in a structurally growing industry, and it tells you the market is pricing the FY26 shock, not the FY24 peak.
Do not confuse this with "Electrosteel Steels": the Kejriwal group once promoted a separate company, Electrosteel Steels Ltd — a greenfield integrated steel plant that ran into heavy debt, entered India's insolvency process, and was acquired by Vedanta in 2018 (now Vedanta ESL / ESL Steel). That was a distinct entity and a painful chapter; Electrosteel Castings Ltd (NSE: ELECTCAST) — the DI water-pipe leader in this primer — is a different company that kept its focus on pipes and continues under Kejriwal control. Same family name at the root, very different fate.
04

The water-infrastructure decade

The structural tailwind — a multi-year national push to pipe water to every home.

To understand Electrosteel you have to understand the demand that sits above it. India is in the middle of the largest expansion of water infrastructure in its history, and DI pipe is the physical medium that expansion runs through. Three Central programmes do most of the work, and together they turned a sleepy pipe industry into a growth sector.

The runway is long because the job is not finished. Rural tap coverage went from a small minority of households at the mission's 2019 launch to the large majority by FY25 — a genuine transformation — but the last stretch to universal coverage, plus decades of urban upgrades and, eventually, the replacement of ageing pipe, is the multi-year demand base sitting under Electrosteel. A pipe laid today is a pipe that must be dug up and re-laid a lifetime from now; a country that has only recently started piping water everywhere has barely begun its first replacement cycle.

The mechanism that makes this durable — and cyclical at once

Piping water to a home is not a one-time event that ends when coverage hits 100%. Networks have to be extended to new households, upgraded in cities, connected to sewage treatment, and — over the coming decades — replaced as old pipe ages out. That is the durable, structural case for DI pipe. But the demand arrives in waves of government tenders, and those waves rise and fall with budget cycles, election timing and how fast states can award and pay for projects. So the same company can enjoy a multi-year secular tailwind and suffer a brutal single-year slump — which is precisely the two-sided story FY24 and FY26 tell back-to-back.

05

The backward-integration edge

Why owning the ore, coke and power is the difference between a good margin and a thin one.

Here is the part of Electrosteel that a casual reader misses. A DI pipe is mostly iron, and iron is made from iron ore smelted with coke (baked coking coal), using a lot of power. A pure pipe caster who buys all three on the open market is at the mercy of commodity prices — when ore and coking coal spike, its margin gets crushed. Electrosteel is different: it has spent decades building backward integration, so it controls a large share of its own inputs rather than buying them at the market's mercy.

Electrosteel makes its own coke (at Haldia and Srikalahasthi) and pig iron, and generates a chunk of its own power; iron ore is largely sourced from Odisha and Jharkhand with some captive access. The more of this stack it owns, the more its cost per tonne is set internally rather than by volatile commodity markets.

The payoff of integration shows up in two places. In good times it means a wider, steadier margin than a non-integrated pipe maker, because Electrosteel captures the value of the iron-making itself rather than paying someone else for pig iron. And it partially hedges the commodity cycle: when coking coal prices surge, a competitor buying coke on the market suffers more than a company baking its own. The Srikalahasthi merger deepened exactly this advantage in the south — adding pig-iron and coke-making capacity alongside the pipe lines.

The other side of integration

Integration is not a free lunch. Owning blast furnaces, coke ovens and power plants means a large fixed asset base that has to run near full to earn its keep. When pipe demand falls — as in FY26 — those furnaces can't simply be switched off; they carry fixed costs whether volumes are high or low, which is one reason the FY26 profit drop (−77%) was so much steeper than the volume drop (−25%). The same integrated plant that fattens margins in a boom amplifies the pain in a bust. So the edge is real, but it makes the company more operationally geared to volume, not less — a nuance that matters enormously when reading a weak year.

A genuine surprise: Electrosteel does not just make pipe and pig iron. Its integrated plants also turn blast-furnace slag into cement and generate captive power, and it produces ferro-alloys and coke for sale. A company most investors file under "water pipes" is, on the factory floor, a small diversified metals-and-materials complex — a reminder that the profit lever sits upstream in the furnaces, not only at the pipe-casting line.
06

The FY26 air-pocket: what a spending pause does

The single most important thing to understand about owning this stock — and it is specific to Electrosteel.

Most manufacturers you can read through their factories: capacity, utilisation, product mix. Electrosteel has a second set of controls it does not own — the pace at which the government awards and pays for water projects — and in FY26 those controls were pulled back sharply. The result is the cleanest illustration you will find of how a single customer type can swing an entire P&L. Watch the two numbers that tell the whole story.

Because the plant carries heavy fixed costs, each tonne of pipe above break-even is highly profitable and each tonne lost hurts disproportionately — so the same operating gearing that made FY26 ugly is what could make a volume recovery so powerful on the way back up.

The device that only fits Electrosteel: same plant, opposite year

Hold FY25 and FY26 side by side. The same furnaces, the same casting lines, the same roughly 7,000-strong workforce, the same product — and yet net profit went from ~₹710 Cr to ~₹161 Cr in twelve months. Nearly two lakh tonnes of pipe and about ₹550 Cr of profit vanished, and not one rupee of it was because the company did something wrong. It was because the government's water-spending cheque got slower. No cost-cutting, no new competitor, no product failure explains it — just the demand faucet turning down. That is the single most important thing to internalise before owning this stock: its best and worst years can look identical on the shop floor, because the swing factor is written in New Delhi, not in Kolkata.

The recovery case rests on a simple sequence: JJM 2.0 outlay → state tenders awarded → contractors order pipe → Electrosteel's volumes climb back toward and past the ~7.8 lakh-tonne run-rate → fixed costs get re-absorbed → profit rebounds far faster than volume. Each link in that chain has to actually fire, and the lag between a scheme being approved and pipe being ordered can run several quarters. But the direction of travel — from a spending trough back toward a spending wave — is what the whole FY27 investment case turns on.

07

The export engine: the quieter, steadier half

The part of the order book that doesn't depend on an Indian budget line.

Not all of Electrosteel's demand is hostage to Indian government spending. The company has built a genuine export franchise over decades, selling DI pipe and fittings through overseas subsidiaries into Europe, the Middle East, Africa, South-East Asia and the Americas. In a business where the domestic customer is one lumpy, tender-driven buyer, exports are the diversifier — a second demand pool that runs on different cycles and different currencies.

The geographic spread is real: Europe is the certification-heavy core, the Middle East a large and long-standing market, Africa a growing one, and the Americas and South-East Asia more selective — all served through Electrosteel's network of overseas subsidiaries. Exports typically carry stricter product approvals and can command steadier pricing than a competitive domestic tender, giving the company a base of demand that does not rise and fall with the Indian budget calendar.

Exports matter for three reasons beyond simple diversification. They smooth the domestic cycle — when Indian ordering pauses, an established export book keeps the furnaces running closer to full. They validate quality — European and Middle-Eastern water utilities demand tight certification, and holding those approvals is a competitive moat that newer domestic entrants can't quickly replicate. And they provide a natural currency spread, since export realisations are in hard currency while much of the cost base is in rupees. In a year like FY26, that overseas order flow is part of what kept the company profitable at all rather than loss-making.

Why the export book is worth more than its size suggests

Exports are usually a modest share of total tonnage, so it is easy to wave them off. That undersells them. Their value is not in the volume but in the certification barrier and the counter-cyclical timing: getting a large European or Gulf water utility to approve your pipe takes years of testing and track record, and once approved you are a repeat supplier insulated from India's budget swings. For a company whose domestic demand can drop a quarter in a single year, a hard-won, steadier export franchise is disproportionately valuable ballast — the piece of the business least visible to a casual reader and least easy for a rival to copy.

08

The pipe pack: a concentrated industry with real leaders

A handful of scaled players share a market that is very hard to enter.

DI-pipe making is not a fragmented cottage industry — it is a capital- and certification-heavy business dominated by a few large names, because you need blast furnaces, casting lines, quality approvals and a track record before a state water board will trust you with a city's water main. India also supplies the great majority of South Asia's DI pipe. The listed conversation is really Electrosteel Castings versus Jindal SAW, with Tata Metaliks and a cluster of eastern-India players behind them.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two forces define the arena. First, a rising tide: with a multi-year national water build-out, the DI-pipe market is growing fast enough (analysts peg double-digit annual growth) that the established players can each expand without a share-stealing price war — for now. Second, a real threat to that comfort: capacity is being added, notably by Tata Metaliks and eastern-India integrated players, which raises the risk that when a demand wave crests, too much pipe capacity chases the same tenders and realisations compress. Electrosteel's defences are its scale, its backward integration (a cost edge in a price fight) and its export certifications. Its exposure is that it is the purest water-pipe play in the pack, so it feels the domestic cycle most directly — both up and down.

09

Financials & valuation

A cyclical trough year, a repaired balance sheet, and a stock priced near its own net worth.

Electrosteel's financials read as a textbook cyclical: two strong years, one sharp trough, and a balance sheet quietly improving through all of it. FY26 was the trough — margins compressed hard as fixed costs met falling volumes. The valuation reflects the shock rather than the recovery, which is what makes the setup interesting and the risk real at the same time.

The one number that settles the thesis: pipe volume per year (and the margin per tonne behind it)

Forget, for a moment, the P/E. For a fixed-cost integrated pipe maker, the metric that proves or breaks the case is lakh tonnes of pipe shipped per year, and the EBITDA earned on each tonne. FY26 troughed at ~5.84 lakh tonnes on a ~6% margin; the FY24/FY25 peak was ~7.8 lakh tonnes on a low-double-digit margin. The entire recovery thesis is that JJM 2.0 tenders push volume back toward and beyond the ~7.8 lakh-tonne run-rate while integration protects margin per tonne — and because the plant is fixed-cost-heavy, profit would rebuild far faster than volume, just as it fell far faster on the way down. So track two things each quarter: tonnes shipped and EBITDA per tonne. If both climb, the below-book valuation looks like a cyclical bargain; if government ordering stays slow, the stock is cheap for a reason and the trough simply lasts longer. Volume is the scoreboard; everything else is commentary on it.

10

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A market leader in a growing industry, caught in the trough of a government-spending cycle.

Electrosteel Castings enters FY27 as a scaled, integrated leader in a structurally growing industry — trading below its own book value because the market is still looking at a bruising FY26. The genuine debate is not whether India needs more water pipe; it plainly does. It is about timing and margin: how quickly the freshly sanctioned JJM 2.0 money turns into pipe orders, and whether a wave of new industry capacity lets the leaders keep their pricing when it does.

The bull case

  • Structural water build-out — universal piped water is a multi-year national priority; JJM 2.0 (~₹8.69 L Cr, to Dec 2028) refills the order pipeline, with urban upgrades and eventual replacement demand behind it.
  • Operating gearing in reverse — a fixed-cost integrated plant means profit rebuilds far faster than volume once tonnes recover, exactly as it fell faster on the way down.
  • Cost edge from integration — own coke, pig iron and captive power give a margin advantage and a partial hedge against ore/coal spikes that a non-integrated rival lacks.
  • Cheap on assets — trades slightly below book value with debt down ~a third from FY24; a leader priced for the trough, not the recovery.
  • Export ballast — hard-won overseas certifications give a steadier, counter-cyclical demand pool insulated from India's budget swings.

The bear case

  • One customer, one budget — demand is dominated by government water spending; a slow tender cycle or fiscal squeeze can flatten volumes with nothing the company can do, as FY26 showed.
  • Severe operating gearing — the same fixed-cost integration that fattens boom margins made FY26 profit fall three-times faster than volume; a prolonged trough is punishing.
  • Working-capital drag — selling to state boards and contractors ties cash up in slow-paying receivables, straining the balance sheet in weak years.
  • New capacity, pricing risk — Tata Metaliks and eastern-India players are adding pipe capacity; if it arrives faster than demand, realisations and margins compress.
  • Commodity exposure — iron ore and coking-coal swings still move the cost base; integration softens but does not remove it.
The bottom line

Electrosteel Castings spent seven decades becoming a market leader in an essential, hard-to-enter business, then watched a single year of slower government cheques cut its profit by more than three-quarters without a thing going wrong in its factories. That is the dual nature of the stock: a structurally advantaged leader riding a real, multi-decade water build-out, wrapped around a demand engine it does not control and an operating model that amplifies every swing. The recovery is not a matter of faith in the business — it is a matter of a sanctioned ₹8.69 lakh crore mission converting into awarded tenders and shipped tonnes. The checkable milestone is concrete and near: pipe volume climbing back through the ~6 and toward the ~7.8 lakh-tonne mark, quarter by quarter, as JJM 2.0 orders flow. Watch the tonnes shipped and the margin per tonne; the tap in New Delhi has been turned back on, and the only question left is how fast the water reaches the pipe.
