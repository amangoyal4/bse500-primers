H.G. Infra Engineering, a roads builder learning new trades

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

H.G. Infra Engineering builds roads and highways — the physical business of turning a strip of land into a four- or six-lane expressway: earthwork, embankments, bridges, flyovers, the asphalt itself. It is a roads-and-highways EPC contractor (Engineering, Procurement & Construction) run out of Jaipur by the Girdhari Lal Mundra family, and for most of its listed life it has been one of the sector's quiet outperformers: high return on capital, low debt, and a clean record of finishing projects on time. It builds mainly for the National Highways Authority of India (NHAI) and state governments, and it also develops a portfolio of road assets under the hybrid-annuity (HAM) model, which it later sells to recycle its capital.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a good builder that hit an order-intake wall. H.G. Infra's execution has rarely been the problem — it delivers. But in FY26 the thing that feeds a construction company dried up: NHAI's road awarding slowed to a trickle, and the company booked only a token amount of fresh work all year. Revenue held near ₹5,667 Cr, but with margins and profit sliding, the market is asking whether the growth engine has simply run out of fuel.
  • It is deliberately diversifying off the highway. The order book at FY26 exit was roughly ₹10,150 Cr — and, tellingly, close to half of it is now non-road: railways and metro civil work, battery-storage (BESS), a little solar, and power transmission. Management wants to grow the share of orders coming from outside roads to reduce a decades-old dependence on NHAI's tendering cycle. That pivot is the re-rating thesis.
  • The balance sheet is a genuine strength. Unlike the leveraged EPC names that blew up in the last cycle, H.G. Infra has kept its construction business low-debt and high-return, and in FY26 it sold five HAM road assets for ~₹3,584 Cr to pull cash back out of its own projects. That capital-recycling discipline is what separates it from peers who drowned in their own BOT/HAM debt.
In plain terms

H.G. Infra is a builder-for-hire that also, on some jobs, part-owns the road it builds for a while and sells it later. The building business is excellent — fast, profitable, lightly geared — but it is only as busy as the government's tender pipeline lets it be, and in FY26 that pipeline nearly shut. So the company is doing two things at once: waiting for road awarding to normalise, and hustling to win rail, water and solar work so that the next time the highway tap slows, it has other taps running. The whole investment question is whether a firm this good at building roads can become equally good at building things that are not roads — fast enough to matter.

01

Roads & highways 101: EPC, HAM and BOT

No jargon left standing — the handful of terms that drive the whole sector.

Building a highway in India means winning a contract from a government agency (usually NHAI, sometimes a state PWD or a ministry) and being paid to construct it. But how the contractor gets paid — and how much risk it carries — depends entirely on the contract model. There are three, and the difference between them is the single most important thing to understand about a roads company.

The three road-contract models
ModelWho funds it, who owns itWhat it means for the contractor
EPCThe government funds the whole project and owns the road. The contractor is simply paid to build it, against milestones.The cleanest, lowest-risk model. The builder puts in almost no long-term capital — it builds, gets paid, moves on. H.G. Infra's core, and the highest-quality part of its revenue.
HAM"Hybrid Annuity Model." The government pays ~40% during construction; the contractor funds the other ~60% (part equity, part debt) and is repaid over ~15 years as annuities plus interest.The builder becomes a part-owner for a while. It earns construction margin and a return on its invested equity — but it ties up capital and takes on project debt. This is where discipline separates winners from casualties.
BOT (toll)"Build-Operate-Transfer." The contractor funds the whole road and is repaid by collecting tolls for 15–30 years, then hands it back.The riskiest: revenue depends on how much traffic actually shows up. The model that bankrupted a generation of over-leveraged road developers a decade ago. H.G. Infra uses it sparingly.
Why the mix between these three decides the company's fate

A contractor that is all EPC is safe but grows only as fast as the government tenders. A contractor that piles into BOT toll roads can grow fast but drowns in debt if traffic disappoints — exactly what happened to several road builders in the 2012–2016 bust. HAM sits in between: it lets a good builder earn two streams — a construction margin and an owner's return — while the government guarantees the annuity so traffic risk is off the table. H.G. Infra's whole reputation rests on running a heavy EPC base, using HAM selectively to boost returns, and then selling those HAM assets once built so the capital comes back. Get that recycling right and you compound at high returns with little permanent debt; get it wrong and you become the levered EPC name everyone avoids.

Who awards the work

  • NHAI — the National Highways Authority of India, the single biggest buyer of highway construction and the source of most EPC and HAM road tenders. When NHAI awards a lot, roads contractors feast; when it slows, they starve. This dependence is the sector's central risk — and the whole reason H.G. Infra is diversifying.
  • MoRTH & state PWDs — the roads ministry and state public-works departments award additional highway and state-road work, a useful supplement when NHAI is quiet.
  • New agencies — as H.G. Infra moves beyond roads, its client list widens to railway and metro authorities, water/Jal Jeevan agencies, renewable developers and power-transmission utilities, each with its own tendering rhythm.
02

What H.G. Infra actually sells: two engines under one roof

A builder and a part-time owner — read this once and the company clicks into place.

H.G. Infra runs two linked businesses. The first, and by far the larger, is construction — being paid to build roads (and now rail, water and solar projects) for whoever tenders them. The second is being a road-asset developer — funding and part-owning a set of HAM highways for a while, earning an owner's return, and then selling them. The genius of the model, when it works, is that the second business feeds the first: the company builds its own HAM roads, books the construction margin itself, and later sells the finished asset to fund the next round.

The revenue, decoded

  • EPC construction — the overwhelming majority of revenue. H.G. Infra is paid against construction milestones to build roads, bridges and, increasingly, non-road infrastructure. This is the high-return, capital-light engine, and its quality rests on one thing: doing the job fast, because a road finished ahead of schedule earns its margin sooner and frees crews and equipment for the next site.
  • HAM asset development — on selected projects the company also invests equity to become the road's developer, earning annuities and a return on that equity through construction and the operating period. It is capital that leaves the balance sheet for a few years, then — ideally — comes back on sale.
  • New verticals — railways and metro civil work, water pipeline (Jal Jeevan), solar and BESS EPC, and power transmission. Today these are mostly construction contracts like the road EPC business, but in a different, less NHAI-dependent client universe. This is the growth that is meant to make the company less of a one-agency bet.
Why "disciplined" is the whole reputation

Every roads company can win a tender. The difference is what happens next. H.G. Infra's edge is a track record of building quickly and profitably while keeping its construction arm nearly debt-free, using HAM only where the return justifies the tied-up capital, and — crucially — selling those HAM assets rather than hoarding a growing pile of project debt. That capital-recycling habit is why it has historically posted returns on capital well above the sector while carrying a fraction of the leverage. The trap it has always avoided is the one that ruined its flashier rivals: chasing top-line growth by piling on toll roads and HAM equity faster than the cash could ever come back. The reputation is not "biggest" — it is "cleanest."

03

The company, the family, and who controls it

A Jaipur contracting family that scaled a roads business the hard way.

H.G. Infra Engineering is a promoter-run, family-controlled company built around the Girdhari Lal Mundra family of Jaipur, Rajasthan. It is not a group offshoot or a private-equity roll-up — it grew out of a family contracting business, scaled steadily through the highway-building boom, listed in 2018, and has stayed tightly held and conservatively run ever since. The founder-family's fingerprints are all over the culture: low debt, careful bidding, and an operational, on-site mindset rather than a financial-engineering one.

Ownership & character

  • Promoters: the Mundra family holds the controlling majority stake (broadly ~74% (approx.)), typical of a founder-run Indian infrastructure company. This alignment cuts both ways: strong owner-operator incentives, but also key-person and family-succession considerations investors should track in the filings.
  • What kind of company it is: an execution-led contractor, not a financial holding structure. Its identity is in-house equipment fleets, project managers and a bidding discipline that walks away from work priced too thin — closer in temperament to KNR Constructions and PNC Infratech than to the aggressive, debt-fuelled builders of the last cycle.
  • Balance-sheet posture: the construction business is run lightly geared; most of the group's reported debt sits in HAM special-purpose vehicles (SPVs) and is non-recourse project debt that leaves the balance sheet as assets are sold. Distinguishing construction debt from HAM SPV debt is essential to reading this company correctly.
The family character is the moat and the risk in one: H.G. Infra's low-debt, high-return discipline is a direct product of a conservative founding family that refuses to over-bid or over-leverage. That same tight control means the company's fortunes are closely tied to the promoters' judgement and continuity — a strength when they get capital allocation right, and a concentration to watch as the business diversifies into unfamiliar verticals.

The execution machine: why the returns were so good

The operational discipline that made H.G. Infra a sector outperformer.

For most of its listed life, H.G. Infra's numbers looked like those of a much better business than "a roads contractor" implies: revenue compounding fast, EBITDA margins in the mid-teens, and returns on capital that peers could not match. None of that was luck. It came from a specific way of building — in-house, fast, and capital-light — that is worth understanding because it is exactly what the company is now trying to carry into new verticals.

Why owning the machines matters more than it sounds

A roads company that subcontracts or rents its equipment is at the mercy of others' schedules and prices; every delay is someone else's fault but still its problem. By owning a deep fleet of excavators, pavers, crushers and crews, H.G. Infra controls its own timeline — it can throw resources at a site to finish early, and it captures the margin a subcontractor would otherwise take. That is the mechanical reason its returns beat the sector. It is also the reason an order-intake drought hurts so much: an owned fleet is a fixed cost that must be kept busy, so a year with too little new work means expensive machines standing idle — the operating leverage that lifted margins in good years works in reverse in a lean one.

The order book and the award freeze

The single most important idea in the case — and it inverts the usual EPC risk.

Most people assume the danger for a construction company is execution — that it will win work and then fail to build it on time or on budget. H.G. Infra's FY26 tells the opposite story. Its execution held up. What broke was the supply of new work: NHAI, the buyer that feeds most of the roads industry, sharply slowed its awarding, and H.G. Infra booked almost no fresh orders for an entire year. For a company whose whole model is "win, build, repeat," a year where the winning stops is the real emergency.

The device that only fits H.G. Infra: a builder idled by its buyer, not its own hands

Track most contractors on the question "can they execute?" H.G. Infra in FY26 must be tracked on a different one entirely: "will anyone give it work to execute?" Its plants, fleet and crews were ready; its balance sheet could fund the build; its record said it would finish on time. 06

Beyond roads: the rail, water and solar pivot

The re-rating thesis — turning a one-agency roads firm into a broader infrastructure builder.

If FY26 proved anything, it is that being brilliant at roads is not enough when the road-awarder goes quiet. H.G. Infra's answer is to become a multi-vertical infrastructure contractor — carrying its execution discipline into railways, metro, water, solar, battery storage and power transmission, so the business no longer rises and falls with a single agency's tender calendar. The pivot is not a slide-deck aspiration; as the order-book split shows, it is already close to half done in mix terms.

Management's stated ambition is to lift the non-road share of new orders toward 40% and beyond over the next two to three years, and to keep the overall order book growing even in a weak roads year by leaning on these segments. There is also a longer-dated ambition to develop solar generation assets of its own — a renewable IPP overlay that would sit alongside the HAM road-asset model, using the same "build it, own it, recycle it" logic in a different sector.

The honest tension inside the diversification

Diversifying is the right strategy, but it is not free. New verticals mean bidding against specialists — dedicated rail, water and transmission EPC players who know those clients and cost structures better than a roads firm does. The margin discipline that made H.G. Infra special in roads has to survive contact with markets where it is the newcomer, and where early contracts are often won at thinner margins to build a reference list. So the pivot carries a genuine trade-off: it reduces the roads-only risk, but it can dilute the very returns that made the company worth a premium. The bet worth making is that a firm with this much execution DNA can earn acceptable returns in adjacent civil work — but the first few non-road projects need to be watched for exactly this margin slippage.

A genuine surprise: the diversification is further along than the "roads company" label suggests — non-road work is already close to half the order book, and railways alone is a bigger block than most casual observers realise. The market still prices H.G. Infra largely as a pure roads contractor hostage to NHAI. If the rail/water/solar mix keeps compounding while roads awarding is soft, the company the market thinks it owns and the company it actually owns are quietly diverging.
07

The HAM asset engine and capital recycling

How the company earns an owner's return without becoming a debt-laden road owner.

Alongside the pure building business, H.G. Infra develops a portfolio of HAM road assets — highways it part-funds, builds, and operates for a stretch, earning government-backed annuities and a return on its invested equity. Done carelessly, this is how road builders drown: equity and debt pile into asset after asset, and the cash never comes back. Done with discipline — which is H.G. Infra's whole claim — it is a way to squeeze extra return out of the same road and then sell the finished asset to fund the next round.

The mechanism is a cycle. The company wins a HAM road, invests equity (and arranges non-recourse SPV debt), builds it — booking its own construction margin along the way — and operates it. Once the asset is built and de-risked, it is worth more to a long-term infrastructure owner (an InvIT, a fund, a strategic buyer) than to a contractor who would rather redeploy the capital. So H.G. Infra sells it, pulls its equity back out with a gain, and recycles that cash into new construction — where its returns are highest.

Why the sale is the point, not the ownership

Some road companies fall in love with owning assets — a toll road throwing off cash feels like a crown jewel. But for a contractor, a built road is trapped capital earning a regulated annuity return, when the same rupee redeployed into new construction earns far more. H.G. Infra's discipline is to treat HAM ownership as a phase, not a destination: build the asset, capture the construction margin, hold it long enough to de-risk, then sell and move on. The ~₹3,584 Cr HAM monetisation is that philosophy in action — it deleverages the group, frees the balance sheet, and keeps the company a high-return builder rather than sliding into a low-return owner. This is the exact discipline the leveraged EPC casualties of the last cycle lacked, and it is why H.G. Infra can distinguish itself from both an over-geared contractor and a pure road-asset InvIT: it is neither — it is a builder that visits ownership and leaves.

The nuance to hold: capital recycling only works if there are willing buyers at good prices. In a soft market for infrastructure assets, or a period of high interest rates, HAM asset sales can slow or fetch less — trapping capital that the model assumes will flow back. The recycling engine is a strength precisely because H.G. Infra has proven it can execute the sales; watch that it keeps doing so, because the whole low-debt story depends on that cash coming home.
08

The balance sheet and working capital

Why "how much debt" is the wrong question until you split construction from HAM.

Read H.G. Infra's headline debt figure cold and it looks heavy — several thousand crore. Read it correctly and the picture flips. The bulk of that debt sits inside HAM SPVs — ring-fenced, non-recourse project vehicles backed by government annuities — not in the construction company itself. The building business, the part that earns the high returns, is run lightly geared. Getting this distinction right is the difference between seeing a levered road developer and seeing a disciplined, capital-light contractor.

Concretely, the great majority of the group's borrowing is project debt sitting inside the HAM vehicles — repaid by government annuities and extinguished when an asset is sold — while the core construction arm carries only modest leverage. That is a fundamentally different animal from debt weighing on the builder itself, and it is the whole reason H.G. Infra's returns held up where over-geared peers' did not.

The other half of the balance-sheet story is working capital — the lifeblood constraint of any contractor. Building roads means spending on materials, fuel, equipment and crews before the client pays, and payments from government agencies can run slow. A weak-order year like FY26 makes this worse in a subtle way: idle crews and under-utilised equipment still cost money, and delayed appointed dates on new projects push out the point at which cash starts flowing. This is why the HAM monetisation matters beyond deleveraging — it injects a large slug of cash exactly when the operating cash cycle is under strain.

Why the low-debt builder is the real asset

Strip out the HAM SPVs and what remains is a construction company that funds its own work order-to-order without heavy borrowing — the thing that let it earn sector-beating returns and survive the roads bust that felled its rivals. That is the durable value here, and it is why the FY26 profit dip, while real, is a demand problem rather than a solvency one: a lightly geared builder with a full order book and a fresh injection of monetisation cash is not in distress; it is a good business waiting for its buyer to start tendering again. The risk is not that the balance sheet breaks — it is that a prolonged intake drought slowly shrinks the order book faster than new verticals can refill it.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded EPC field, sorted by one thing: capital discipline.

Roads EPC in India is a competitive, mid-cap-heavy field, and on paper many of the listed names look alike — all bid for the same NHAI and state tenders, all talk about HAM and diversification. The real way to sort them is by balance-sheet discipline: which contractors grew profitably with low debt and recycled their asset capital, and which chased top-line growth into a debt trap. On that axis, H.G. Infra sits firmly with the disciplined camp.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two forces define the field. Infra's near-half non-road order book is a strong opening hand in that race; the test is whether it can hold its return discipline while playing it.

10

Financials & valuation

A quality builder marked down for a demand air-pocket — cheap if the taps reopen.

FY26 was the hard year: revenue flat, margins compressed, profit down as an idle fleet met a frozen order tap, and the stock de-rated well below its former multiple. That is a very different valuation setup from a richly-priced growth story — H.G. Infra trades like a beaten-down cyclical, not a market darling. The question is whether the market is pricing a pause or a permanent step-down.

Management guides FY27 revenue to roughly ₹6,500–7,000 Cr with the EBITDA margin recovering toward ~14% — a return to growth that leans on the existing order book converting and on fresh intake reviving. That is a plausible bridge back to the old trajectory, but it is guidance, not a result, and it rests on the one thing FY26 proved the company cannot conjure alone: new work to build.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality builder, cheaply priced, waiting on a tender cycle it cannot control.

H.G. Infra enters FY27 as it has always been: a disciplined, low-debt, high-return roads contractor — but now one that has been forced to prove it can be more than that. The debate is not about whether it can build; its record answers that. It is about two things outside its own control room: whether NHAI starts awarding again, and whether the new verticals can grow fast enough, at good enough returns, to make the roads cycle matter less.

The bull case

  • Quality at a cyclical-trough price — a historically sector-beating ROCE and clean balance sheet, trading at a single-digit multiple on a temporary demand gap.
  • Diversification already half-done — non-road work is ~47% of the order book; railways, BESS and water reduce the NHAI-only dependence in real time.
  • Capital-recycling discipline — the ~₹3,584 Cr HAM monetisation deleverages and funds growth; the model that avoids the debt trap.
  • Low-debt builder — the construction arm is lightly geared; this is a demand problem, not a solvency one.
  • Award recovery optionality — if NHAI tendering normalises, a proven builder with ready capacity captures the rebound quickly.

The bear case

  • Order-intake dependence — the whole model needs a steady tender pipeline; FY26 showed how fast a frozen NHAI can idle even a great builder.
  • Diversification margin risk — winning rail/water/transmission work as a newcomer can mean thinner early margins, diluting the returns that justified a premium.
  • Draining order book — if intake stays weak, the ~₹10,150 Cr book shrinks faster than new verticals refill it, and revenue follows down.
  • Working-capital & payment lag — slow government payments and delayed appointed dates strain cash exactly when execution slows.
  • Family concentration & asset-sale reliance — tightly promoter-held, and the low-debt story depends on HAM buyers staying willing at good prices.
The bottom line

H.G. Infra spent years as one of the cleanest builders in a sector littered with cautionary tales — fast, profitable, lightly geared, disciplined with its asset capital. FY26 did not break any of that; it exposed the one dependency the model never solved: a company that lives on winning tenders is at the mercy of whoever tenders them, and India's road-awarder went quiet. The response — pushing hard into railways, water, solar and transmission so no single agency can idle the whole firm again — is already visibly underway in the order book, further than the market's "roads company" label admits. What remains unproven is speed and returns: can the new verticals grow fast enough, and profitably enough, to matter before the roads drought does real damage? The next few quarters answer it in one printed figure each results season — the order inflow, and how much of it is not roads. Watch that number climb back above what the company can build in a year; until it does, this is a very good builder priced for a very quiet road ahead.
