Intellect Design Arena, India's rare software-product house

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Intellect Design Arena builds software products for banks and insurers and licenses them worldwide. Not consulting. Not staffing. Not "we'll send you forty engineers." It writes the actual systems that move a corporation's cash across borders, open and run retail bank accounts, price a treasury trade, or underwrite an insurance policy — and then sells the right to use those systems to financial institutions in the Middle East, Europe, the Americas, Asia and India. Headquartered in Chennai, founded and still led by Arun Jain, it was carved out of Polaris in a 2014 demerger and today is one of a very short list of Indian companies with a genuinely global software product franchise.

Four facts frame everything that follows:

  • It owns intellectual property, not timesheets. The Indian IT giants — TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCLTech — mostly sell capacity: skilled people, billed by the hour or by the project. Their revenue scales roughly with headcount. Intellect sells the same product to customer number two at a fraction of the cost of building it for customer number one. That gives it product economics: high gross margins and real operating leverage — paid for by heavy, permanent, upfront R&D and sales cycles measured in years.
  • FY26 was a strong year on the top line. Total income rose 23% to ₹3,161 Cr, EBITDA rose to ₹703 Cr from ₹608 Cr, profit before tax reached ₹493 Cr, and collections hit ₹3,043 Cr. The company won 59 new customers during the year and had 91 live digital transformations running at global financial institutions.
  • The revenue is deliberately changing shape. "Platform" revenue — Intellect's word for cloud/subscription — more than doubled to ₹580 Cr from ₹241 Cr. Traditional upfront licence revenue barely moved (₹517 Cr vs ₹503 Cr). Together with ₹570 Cr of annual maintenance contracts, "licence-linked" revenue reached ₹1,667 Cr — 53% of total income. That mix shift is the whole strategy, and it is also why headline growth understates what is happening.
  • The profit line has gone nowhere for four years. Net profit in FY26 was about ₹343 Cr — against ₹350 Cr in FY22, on revenue that grew ~62% over the same stretch. Four years of top-line expansion have been consumed by R&D, platform build-out and a deliberate revenue-model transition. Whether that reverses is, in one sentence, the investment question.
In plain terms

Two software companies can earn the same rupee very differently. One rents you people; when the project ends, the revenue ends. The other writes a system once, sells it to sixty banks, and charges each of them again every year to keep using it. Intellect is the second kind — rare for an Indian listed company, and much harder to build. The cost of being the second kind is that you must fund a large engineering organisation before anyone buys, and then wait through 12-to-24-month bank procurement cycles for the cheque. The reward, if it works, is that the sixty-first bank costs almost nothing to serve.

01

Products vs services: the distinction that defines this company

If you take one idea away from this primer, make it this one.

India's technology industry is world-famous for something Intellect does not do. The giants of Indian IT built their fortunes on services: a bank in New Jersey or a retailer in Frankfurt needs software written, maintained, migrated or run, and an Indian firm supplies a team to do it — highly skilled, well managed, and far cheaper than hiring locally. Revenue is essentially rate × people × utilisation. Grow revenue by 20% and, broadly, you need something close to 20% more engineers.

A software product company works on entirely different arithmetic. You spend years and a great deal of money building a system nobody has paid for yet. Then you sell the right to use it — over and over. The tenth customer does not require you to rewrite the product; they require configuration, integration, training and support. The gross margin on that tenth sale is dramatically higher than on the first, and the engineering cost sits mostly in a fixed pool rather than scaling one-for-one with revenue.

Why India produced so few product companies

The services model was so successful, and so much safer, that it absorbed the country's best engineering talent for three decades. A services contract pays from month one; a product burns cash for years and may never find a buyer. Building banking software adds a further barrier: you are asking a regulated institution to replace the system that holds its customers' money — the highest-consequence software purchase in commerce. References matter more than features, and you cannot get references until someone takes the first leap. That combination is why the Indian banking-product list is short: Infosys Finacle, TCS BaNCS, Nucleus, and Intellect. It is also why the position, once won, is unusually hard for a newcomer to attack.

Two consequences follow for anyone reading Intellect's numbers. First, headcount growth is not the growth metric — the useful measures are deal wins, licence-linked revenue and margin. Second, a soft quarter is not automatically a soft business. A services firm with falling revenue is losing work. A product firm with falling revenue may simply have had two large deals sign in April rather than March, or may be choosing to sell those deals as subscriptions that recognise slowly instead of as licences that land all at once. Section 05 takes that apart in detail.

What Intellect actually sells: four suites and one architecture

The product map — read this once and the results releases become readable.

Intellect organises itself around four product families, each aimed at a different part of a financial institution, all now built on a common technology architecture called eMACH.ai. A large bank might buy just one; a transforming bank might buy several.

All four sit on eMACH.ai — roughly 700 microservices, 3,061 APIs and 942 events — so a bank can buy one component rather than a monolith, and add others later. That composability is the modern sales pitch and the reason a "small" first deal can grow.

iGTB — the crown jewel most people have never heard of

Transaction banking is the least glamorous and most profitable corner of a bank. It is the plumbing a corporate treasurer uses: collecting receipts across dozens of accounts and currencies, sweeping cash into one pool overnight, issuing letters of credit for a shipment, paying ten thousand suppliers on a Friday, and seeing all of it on one screen. Banks fight hard for this business because it produces fee income and sticky corporate deposits without consuming much capital.

Somebody has to build the software that does all that. Historically the choices were a handful of Western vendors or an in-house build. iGTB is Intellect's answer, and it is genuinely world-class — the company was named Euromoney's World's Best Transaction Banking Software Provider for 2025, one of 18 industry awards it collected in the year, with recognition from 56 analyst firms. For an Indian company selling a product rather than people, that is an unusual position to hold.

iGCB — core banking, the highest-stakes purchase in banking

The core banking system is the ledger: it holds every account, posts every transaction, calculates every interest accrual, and must never be wrong. Replacing one is the corporate-software equivalent of changing an aircraft engine mid-flight, which is why banks run 25-year-old cores and why "Mainframe to Cloud" (M2C) — moving those ancient systems onto modern cloud architecture — is one of Arun Jain's stated strategic priorities. iGCB covers core banking, deposits, lending, cards and the digital front-end that sits on top.

Capital markets, treasury and wealth

Risk, treasury and markets platforms serve the institutional side — trading and treasury operations, risk and liquidity management — while the wealth and advisory products serve private banks and distributors. Smaller than the two banking suites, but strategically useful: it lets Intellect stay relevant to a client's whole technology estate rather than one department.

Insurance and IntellectAI

The insurance suite handles underwriting and policy administration for commercial insurers — the work of reading a submission, pricing the risk and issuing the policy. This is where Purple Fabric, Intellect's AI platform, has landed hardest: the company reports 550+ domain-aware AI agents, claimed accuracy above 95% in regulated workflows, and deployment across 45+ clients. Underwriting is a natural fit for AI because the raw input is a pile of unstructured documents that a human currently reads line by line.

What "composable" means, without the jargon

Older banking software arrived as one enormous block: buy it, and you took the whole thing, configured over two or three years. Composable software breaks that block into hundreds of independent pieces — a payments piece, a limits piece, a trade-finance piece — each reachable through a standard interface. A bank can buy the one piece it urgently needs, run it alongside the systems it already owns, and add more later. Commercially this changes the sales motion completely: the first contract is smaller and easier to approve, but the relationship can compound for a decade. It is a lower door into a bigger house.

03

Arun Jain, the Polaris demerger, and who owns it

A founder-led company with a services past it deliberately separated from.

Intellect exists because of a decision that most Indian IT founders never made: to split a profitable services business away from an unproven products business, and back the products. Arun Jain built Polaris into a well-regarded banking-technology services firm from the early 1990s. Inside it, over years, he also built banking products. In 2014 the two were separated — the products business was demerged and listed as Intellect Design Arena, and the services business (Polaris Consulting) was subsequently sold to Virtusa. Jain went with the products.

Ownership

  • Foreign institutions ~23.5% — a high FII weight for a mid-cap, reflecting that global investors can benchmark Intellect against Temenos and nCino rather than against Indian IT. A structural quirk worth holding on to: a 29.6% promoter stake and a 23.5% foreign-institutional stake means Intellect's register looks more like a global software company's than an Indian mid-cap's. That cuts both ways. It brings shareholders who understand what a subscription transition costs and are willing to wait. It also means the stock reprices on global software sentiment and on foreign flows — sharply, and with little domestic ballast to cushion it.
Why the demerger still matters

Services and products cannot comfortably share a roof. A services business measures success by how many people are billing today; a product business must spend money on engineers who bill nobody for two years. Put them together and the services logic always wins the budget argument, because it shows results this quarter. Separating them in 2014 was the decision that made everything in this primer possible — and it is why Intellect's cost base looks strange next to an Indian IT firm's: a permanently large R&D and product organisation supported by a revenue base a fraction the size of its rivals'.

Why implementation revenue is not "bad" revenue

Roughly half of Intellect's income is the unglamorous work of making the product actually run inside a specific bank — mapping it to that bank's data, connecting it to twenty other systems, testing, training staff, and going live without breaking anything. Margins on this are ordinary, and a services purist would call it dilution. It is better read as the toll gate: no bank pays an AMC or a subscription on software that never went live. Every rupee of annuity in the table above stands on a completed implementation. The line to watch is not whether services shrinks, but whether the annuity that follows it grows faster.

Where the customers are

Intellect sells across five broad markets — India, the Middle East and Africa, Europe (including the UK), the Americas, and Asia-Pacific. The Middle East has historically been an unusually strong region for iGTB, with Europe and the Americas the largest addressable markets and the toughest to win. The company does not publish a clean geographic split in its results release, so any precise percentage should be treated as approx. and taken from the annual report rather than from summaries. What is safe to say: the majority of revenue is earned outside India, in foreign currency, which makes the rupee's level a live input to reported margins.

05

The licence-to-subscription switch: why winning better business looks like slowing down

The single most important mechanism in this company — and the easiest one to misread.

Here is the mechanism that governs how Intellect's results look, and it has nothing to do with how well the business is performing. It is an accounting consequence of how the same deal is sold.

Suppose a bank agrees to use one of Intellect's platforms, and suppose the economic value of that relationship is broadly similar under either commercial model. Sold the old way, the bank buys a perpetual licence — a large fee paid upfront for the right to run the software forever, with a maintenance contract alongside. Sold the new way, the bank pays a subscription: a smaller annual fee for as long as it uses the software, hosted in the cloud, with updates included.

Illustrative only. The point is the shape: the subscription route earns roughly a third as much in year one and keeps earning after year five, while the licence route front-loads everything into a single quarter and then decays to maintenance.

Now put a manager in charge of that choice. If they need this quarter to look good, they push licences. If they are building a business that compounds, they push subscriptions — and accept that the year they succeed hardest will be the year reported revenue growth looks weakest, because they have deliberately deferred cash they were previously allowed to book at once.

That is precisely what Intellect's FY26 disclosure shows. Platform revenue ₹580 Cr against ₹241 Cr — up 141%. Licence revenue essentially flat at ₹517 Cr against ₹503 Cr. If those two lines had been swapped — if the same customer demand had been converted into upfront licences instead of subscriptions — reported revenue growth would have been higher and the year would have looked more exciting. It would also have left the company with less recurring revenue entering FY27.

The counter-intuitive part: the quarter in which Intellect converts the most demand into recurring revenue is the quarter its reported growth looks least impressive. Reported revenue and business quality can move in opposite directions for years.
The reader's rule for this company

Do not judge Intellect on headline revenue growth alone. Judge it on three things the company discloses: new customer wins (59 in FY26, 15 in Q4), the licence-linked revenue figure (₹1,667 Cr, 53% of income) and specifically the platform sub-line inside it, and the EBITDA margin. A year with modest revenue growth but a jump in platform revenue and steady deal wins is a good year, however it reads in a headline. A year with strong revenue growth driven by one enormous licence and few new logos is a worse one, however it reads.

There is a further wrinkle worth flagging: the transition also changes cash timing, not only revenue. Under a licence, cash arrives near the front. Under a subscription, cash arrives annually. FY26 collections of ₹3,043 Cr against total income of ₹3,161 Cr suggest the cash side is broadly keeping pace with the reported line — a useful sanity check that the growth is real, not a receivable.

Name the number that settles the case: the platform (subscription) revenue line — ₹580 Cr in FY26 — and its share of licence-linked revenue. It rose from 19% of licence-linked revenue in FY25 to 35% in FY26. If that share keeps climbing while total licence-linked revenue grows and EBITDA margin holds in the low-to-mid twenties, the transition is working. If platform growth stalls in the ₹600–700 Cr region while licence revenue stays flat, the company is neither an annuity business nor a licence business, and the multiple has nothing to stand on.
06

Lumpiness: the quarter that made ₹27 Cr and the quarter that made ₹120 Cr

The most under-appreciated fact about owning a product company — shown in this company's own FY26 numbers.

Selling software to a bank is not a smooth process. A deal involves a procurement committee, a technology committee, a risk assessment, a regulator's comfort, a proof of concept, a competitive bake-off against two or three global vendors, and finally a board sign-off. It can take eighteen months. It can slip a quarter for reasons as mundane as a change of CIO. And when it lands, it lands all at once.

Intellect's own FY26 makes the point better than any explanation. Take the four quarters of a single year — same company, same products, same engineers, same eMACH.ai architecture, same customers in the pipeline:

Read the PAT column again. Roughly ₹102 Cr, then roughly ₹27 Cr, then ₹120 Cr — in three consecutive quarters, at the same company. Nothing structural changed between October and March. No product was lost, no competitor arrived, no market closed. What changed was when contracts were signed and how revenue was recognised against a largely fixed cost base. Intellect's engineering, sales and R&D costs run more or less continuously; when a licence signature moves across a quarter boundary, the cost stays and the revenue does not, and the profit line — the thin residue between two large numbers — swings violently.

What this means for how you read the stock

A quarterly result from Intellect carries far less information than a quarterly result from a services company. A single bad quarter is normal and frequently reverses within ninety days; a single spectacular quarter is equally suspect. Anyone drawing a trend line through Intellect's quarterly profit is drawing a trend line through signature dates. The meaningful unit of observation here is a full year, and even then it is worth looking at the licence-linked line rather than the bottom line. The market does not always agree — which is exactly why the 52-week range on this stock runs from ₹595 to ₹1,245, a high more than double the low, within one year.

This lumpiness is not a flaw to be engineered away; it is the natural consequence of selling a small number of very large, very consequential things. It is, incidentally, one more reason the subscription transition matters: a subscription book smooths the very volatility described above. Every rupee moved from licence to platform revenue makes the next Q3 a little less alarming.

07

eMACH.ai and Purple Fabric: the architecture bet

The current strategic push, in language a non-engineer can hold.

Every decade or so, enterprise software changes shape, and every incumbent must decide whether to rebuild. The current change has two parts: software delivered from the cloud in small independent pieces rather than as one installed block, and artificial intelligence embedded in the workflow rather than bolted on. eMACH.ai is Intellect's answer to the first; Purple Fabric is its answer to the second.

Unpacking eMACH.ai

The name is an acronym of the design principles — events, Microservices, API-first, Cloud-native, Headless — with AI layered across. In practice:

  • Microservices — the software is split into hundreds of small, independent programs (roughly 700 of them) each doing one job. One can be changed or replaced without touching the rest.
  • API-first — every capability is reachable through a standard published interface (about 3,061 APIs), so a bank's other systems can call it directly rather than needing custom integration each time.
  • Event-driven (~942 events) — the system reacts to things happening ("a payment cleared", "a limit was breached") rather than waiting for a nightly batch. This is what makes real-time banking possible.
  • Cloud-native and headless — designed to run on cloud infrastructure, with the business logic separated from the screens, so a bank can put its own front-end on top.
Why this architecture is a commercial weapon, not just an engineering choice

An incumbent vendor with a twenty-year-old monolithic product has a painful problem: it can sell only the whole thing, to a bank willing to undertake a three-year replacement. A vendor whose product is already 700 independent services can sell one service into a bank that is not ready to replace anything — sitting alongside the old core, solving one urgent problem, proving itself, and expanding from inside. That is how a challenger takes territory from larger rivals without ever winning a head-to-head core-replacement bake-off. It is also why Intellect can report 59 new customers in a year in an industry where full core replacements number in the low hundreds globally.

Purple Fabric — AI where the documents are

Purple Fabric is Intellect's AI platform, and the company reports 550+ domain-aware AI agents live across 45+ clients, with claimed accuracy above 95% in regulated environments. The most credible use case is insurance underwriting and commercial banking documentation: a submission arrives as a heap of PDFs, spreadsheets, loss runs and emails, and a human currently reads all of it to extract a few dozen facts. That is expensive, slow and — importantly — auditable work, which is why generic chatbots have not solved it. An AI agent that knows the domain, cites its source document and hands a human the final decision fits the regulatory reality far better.

Management describes the strategy as "AI-First", alongside Mainframe-to-Cloud migration, the wholesale banking ecosystem, payments and financial advisory as the priorities for the next cycle. The intellectual-property position behind it is real: 125 patents filed, of which 9 are granted and 111 in progress.

Who buys this software, and how the sale actually happens

The customer, the cycle, and the reason a pipeline number is not an order book.

Intellect's customers are banks, insurers and financial institutions — from very large global banks buying a single transaction-banking module, to mid-sized regional banks replacing an entire core, to insurers automating underwriting. The buying process is unlike almost any other software purchase, and understanding it explains most of what happens to the reported numbers.

Pipeline is not backlog

Management has referenced a pipeline of the order of ₹12,000 Cr (as reported in results coverage — treat the exact figure as approx. and check the presentation). It is essential not to read that as an order book. A pipeline is the value of opportunities being pursued; an order book is signed contracts. In a business where a bake-off can be lost to Temenos on a Tuesday, the conversion rate from pipeline to signature is the number that matters, and it is not published. The more reliable disclosures are the hard counts: 59 new customers in FY26, 15 in Q4, and 91 live digital transformations during the year (25 in Q4).

The go-live is where the value is created

A signed contract is a promise. A live system is a relationship. Once a bank's corporate customers are logging into a platform every morning, the switching cost becomes enormous — retraining, re-integration, migration risk, regulatory re-approval — and the vendor collects maintenance or subscription revenue for a decade or more. This is why "91 live digital transformations" is arguably a better forward indicator than any revenue figure: each one is a future annuity that has just cleared its riskiest hurdle. It is also why a delayed implementation is expensive twice over — the annuity starts later, and the reference story that wins the next bank arrives later too.

Geography and currency

The customer base spans India, the Middle East and Africa, Europe, the Americas and Asia-Pacific, with most revenue earned outside India. Three practical consequences: reported revenue moves with the rupee even when the underlying contracts do not; bank IT budgets are cyclical and tighten when the sector is under stress; and regional concentration matters — a strong Middle East franchise is a genuine asset, but it also means a slowdown in one region's bank spending is visible in the annual numbers.

The competitive set: who Intellect actually fights

Not Infosys and TCS the services firms — Temenos, Finastra and a bank's own developers.

Because Intellect is listed in India and classified under Information Technology, it gets compared to Indian IT. That comparison is close to useless. Its competitors are the global banking-software vendors, plus one rival that never appears in a market-share table: the bank's decision to build it in-house.

What it can do is arrive with a modern, composable product at a moment when incumbents are carrying twenty-year-old code, and win the piece of the estate the bank is most desperate to fix.

Why "smaller" is not automatically "weaker" here

In enterprise banking software, scale wins procurement and references — but architecture wins the technical evaluation. A large incumbent with a monolithic legacy product faces a genuine dilemma: rebuilding it cannibalises a profitable maintenance base and takes years, while not rebuilding it slowly loses every modern bake-off. Intellect has no legacy revenue worth protecting on that scale, which makes the rebuild it has already done cheaper for it than for the incumbent. The countervailing truth is equally real: a Chief Risk Officer choosing the system that will hold the bank's ledger has never been fired for picking the biggest vendor.

How Intellect compares to the Indian IT giants — the useful version

  • Scale: Intellect's ₹3,161 Cr of FY26 income is a rounding error against TCS or Infosys. It is not competing for the same contracts, and its growth is not a read-across for Indian IT demand.
  • Margins: at ~22% EBITDA on total income, Intellect sits below the best Indian IT operating margins — despite product economics — because it carries R&D that services firms do not, and a services-heavy implementation line inside its own revenue.
  • Volatility: Indian IT reports gentle, predictable quarters. Intellect reported quarterly profits of roughly ₹94 Cr, ₹102 Cr, ₹27 Cr and ₹120 Cr inside one year. Same sector classification; a different instrument entirely.
  • The upside asymmetry: a services firm's revenue is capped by how many people it can hire and bill. A product firm's is capped by how many institutions will buy the product. That ceiling is far higher — and far less certain.

The metric that settles this, stated once and precisely

Watch platform (subscription) revenue and its share of licence-linked revenue, together with the EBITDA margin. FY26 gives the baseline: platform ₹580 Cr, 35% of the ₹1,667 Cr licence-linked total, EBITDA ₹703 Cr on ₹3,161 Cr of income. If FY27 shows platform revenue clearly above ₹800 Cr, licence-linked revenue past 55% of income, and EBITDA margin held at or above 22%, the transition thesis is proved and profit growth follows mechanically from there — the cost base does not need to grow at the same rate. If platform revenue lands near ₹650 Cr and margin drifts under 20%, the company has swapped high-margin upfront licences for subscriptions that are not compounding fast enough, and four flat profit years become six.

11

Future, risks, and the two sides of the argument

Where this can go, what breaks it, and the specific thing to watch next.

Management's stated ambition for the next cycle rests on five planks: AI-First, Mainframe-to-Cloud migration, the wholesale banking ecosystem, payments, and financial advisory. The guidance framework accompanying FY26 results points to 15–20% revenue growth with a 20–25% EBITDA margin, supported by a large pipeline. That combination — mid-to-high-teens growth at low-twenties margins on a net-cash balance sheet — is what the current valuation is underwriting.

The risks, set out squarely

  • Deal risk is the defining operational risk. Large bank software deals take 12–24 months, involve committees and regulators, and slip. A dry patch of two or three quarters without a marquee win does real damage — not because the business has changed, but because a largely fixed cost base has nothing to absorb it. FY26's roughly ₹27 Cr third quarter is what that looks like in practice.
  • Transition risk. Moving from licences to subscriptions makes reported revenue optically worse before it makes the business better. If subscription revenue does not compound fast enough, the company ends up with neither the fat upfront licences it gave up nor the annuity it was buying. This is the risk that would take the longest to become visible.
  • Competition from far larger balance sheets. Temenos, Finastra, Oracle Financial Services, FIS and nCino all have more sales coverage, more references and, in several cases, more money to lose in a price fight. TCS BaNCS and Infosys Finacle bring the added weight of parents with global bank relationships Intellect cannot match.
  • R&D that may not convert. A product company must spend heavily before demand exists. Some of that spend is capitalised as an intangible asset rather than expensed — an accounting judgement that raises reported profit today and creates amortisation tomorrow. If a product line does not sell, the write-down arrives years after the money was spent.
  • Customer and sector concentration. A meaningful share of revenue comes from a limited number of large institutions and from banking IT budgets that move in cycles. When banks tighten, discretionary transformation spending is among the first things cut.
  • Currency. Most revenue is earned abroad while a large part of the cost base is Indian. The rupee's level flows straight into reported margin, in both directions.
  • Valuation and flows. A ~29× multiple on earnings that have swung by 4× between consecutive quarters is a nervous combination. With foreign institutions holding ~23.5% and having reduced from ~27.9%, the register itself can amplify a de-rating — the fall from ₹1,245 to ₹595 within a year needed no change in the products.

The case for

  • A real product franchise, not a services firm in disguise. Euromoney's world's-best transaction-banking software award for 2025, coverage by 56 analyst firms, 125 patents filed — evidence the IP is competitive on merit, not price.
  • The mix is improving fast. Platform revenue up 141% to ₹580 Cr; licence-linked revenue up 34% to ₹1,667 Cr and now 53% of income. Recurring revenue of this kind is worth more per rupee than project revenue.
  • Operating leverage is available. R&D and product engineering are largely fixed. If revenue grows 15–20% without a proportionate rise in that base, incremental margin flows disproportionately to profit.
  • Composability lowers the entry barrier. 700 microservices and 3,061 APIs let Intellect enter a bank through one small deal and expand — a route around the incumbents' procurement advantage. 59 new customers in FY26 is the evidence.
  • Net cash, dividend-paying, founder-run. ₹1,257 Cr of cash against ~₹159 Cr of debt, a ₹7 per share payout including a special dividend, and a founder who has run this business through two full technology cycles.

The case against

  • Four years without profit growth. ₹350 Cr in FY22; ~₹343 Cr in FY26 — on 62% more revenue. At some point "investment phase" must produce a result.
  • Margins are drifting the wrong way. OPM of 25% in FY22 against 19% in FY26 on the narrower revenue base. Guidance of 20–25% EBITDA margin requires reversing that drift, not merely holding it.
  • Quarterly earnings are close to unreadable. Roughly ₹102 Cr, ₹27 Cr and ₹120 Cr of profit in three consecutive quarters makes position sizing and forecasting genuinely hard, and invites violent re-ratings in both directions.
  • It is a mid-cap fighting giants. A ~US$1.2bn company competing against Oracle, FIS, Temenos and Finastra for the most conservative buyers in commerce. One lost flagship bake-off costs more than a year of small wins.
  • Foreign holders have been leaving. FII stake down from ~27.9% to ~23.5% since March 2024, and a share price at roughly half its 52-week high — the marginal buyer has not been convinced.

The bottom line

Intellect Design Arena is doing something structurally rare and structurally difficult: selling Indian-built software products to the world's most risk-averse buyers, while simultaneously rebuilding how those products are sold. FY26 showed the first outcome working — 23% income growth, 59 new customers, EBITDA past ₹700 Cr — and the second outcome consuming the benefit, with net profit still shy of where it stood in FY22. Both things are true of the same year, and neither cancels the other.

The next checkpoint is specific and dated. The ₹7 dividend (₹4 final + ₹3 special) goes ex around 24 July 2026, and the Q1 FY27 results land in the same window. In that print, one line deserves to be read before any other: platform revenue. FY26 exited with the quarterly platform run-rate at ₹162 Cr. A Q1 that holds above ₹170 Cr while EBITDA margin stays north of 22% says the subscription engine has taken over from the licence engine. A Q1 that slips back toward ₹130 Cr says FY26's platform surge borrowed from a handful of large cloud contracts rather than establishing a new base — and the four flat profit years get a fifth.

One last thing to carry away

This is a company whose annual report tells a materially different story from its quarterly ones, and whose quality-of-revenue disclosure tells a different story again from its revenue-growth headline. Almost every wrong conclusion about Intellect comes from reading the fast number instead of the slow one. The slow one — licence-linked revenue, and the platform line inside it — is where this business is actually being built.
