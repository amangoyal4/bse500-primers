00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Swiggy runs two big businesses on one app: food delivery (it brings restaurant meals to your door) and Instamart (quick commerce — groceries and daily essentials delivered in ~10–15 minutes from small neighbourhood warehouses called "dark stores"). It is India's #2 food-delivery player behind Zomato/Eternal, and a fierce #2–#3 challenger in quick commerce against Blinkit and Zepto. It listed on the stock market only in November 2024.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a high-growth, still-loss-making platform — so forget P/E. FY26 (year ended March 2026) revenue was ₹23,053 Cr, up 45%, but the company posted a net loss of ₹4,154 Cr (a loss that widened ~33% from ₹3,117 Cr the year before). With no profit, a price-to-earnings ratio is meaningless; you value it on price-to-sales (~2.7×) and by valuing each segment separately.
  • Two engines pulling in opposite directions. Food delivery is now genuinely profitable and self-funding — it crossed ₹1,000 Cr of adjusted EBITDA in FY26 and delivered a ₹1,041 Cr segment profit. Instamart is the opposite: it is burning cash hard in a market-share war, posting a FY26 segment loss of ₹3,063 Cr (up from ₹1,896 Cr). The whole group loss is essentially the food-delivery profit minus the quick-commerce burn, minus corporate costs.
  • A well-funded landgrab. In late 2025 Swiggy raised ₹10,000 Cr via a QIP, taking its cash pile to roughly ₹17,000 Cr — a war chest specifically to fund Instamart's dark-store expansion against deep-pocketed rivals. The entire investment debate is: can food delivery's profit and this cash carry Instamart to break-even before the burn does real damage?
In plain terms

Think of Swiggy as a company running two shops under one roof. The first shop — bringing you dinner from a restaurant — has finally started making money after years of losses. The second shop — a swarm of tiny warehouses racing to drop a packet of milk at your door in ten minutes — is spending furiously to grab territory before Blinkit and Zepto lock it up. Swiggy is deliberately using the first shop's profits (plus a big pile of freshly-raised cash) to fund the second shop's land grab. You can't judge it like a normal profitable company; you judge it on whether it's growing fast, whether the losses are shrinking as a share of sales, and whether it has enough cash to reach the finish line.

01

Why you read a loss-making platform differently

The analytical frame is not "how much does it earn?" but "when will it earn, and can it survive until then?"

A profitable company like a bank or an FMCG major is judged on earnings — profit, return on capital, dividends, a P/E multiple. Swiggy has no profit, so that entire toolkit is useless here. A loss-making, high-growth "new-economy" platform is judged on a completely different set of questions, and understanding them is the key to the whole primer.

The four words you must learn

GOV (Gross Order Value) is the total rupee value of everything ordered through the platform — the ₹500 dinner or ₹800 grocery basket. Take rate is the slice of GOV Swiggy keeps as its own revenue (via commissions, delivery fees, ads). Contribution margin is what's left from that revenue after the direct costs of fulfilling an order (delivery-partner payout, packaging, discounts) — it tells you if an order makes money before head-office costs. Adjusted EBITDA then subtracts the fixed costs (salaries, tech, marketing) to show if the whole business unit is viable. Profit (PAT) comes last, after depreciation, ESOP costs, interest and tax. Swiggy is positive on food-delivery adjusted EBITDA but still negative on group PAT — because Instamart's losses sit in between.

So throughout this primer, watch the ladder: GOV → take-rate revenue → contribution margin → adjusted EBITDA → net profit. Each rung strips out more cost. A young platform climbs this ladder rung by rung — first getting each order to contribute, then getting a segment to break even, then finally getting the whole company into profit. Swiggy's food delivery is near the top of the ladder; Instamart is still on the lower rungs.

02

Food delivery, explained from scratch

A three-sided marketplace — and the business that finally earns.

Swiggy's original business is a three-sided marketplace. It connects hungry customers, restaurants, and a fleet of gig delivery partners on scooters. Swiggy owns none of the food and employs none of the riders as staff — it runs the software, the logistics brain, and the brand that makes all three sides trust each other.

Swiggy's revenue is the sum of what it charges restaurants (commission + advertising) and what it charges customers (delivery + platform fees). Its biggest cost is paying the rider. The gap between the two, across millions of orders, is the profit.

Where the money comes from

  • Restaurant commission — Swiggy takes a cut (roughly 18–25%) of each order's value from the restaurant. This is the single largest revenue line.
  • Delivery & platform fees — charged to the customer, these have risen steadily and now cover much of the rider cost, which was historically the profit-killer.
  • Advertising — restaurants pay to appear higher in search and on the home screen. This is pure-margin revenue (no rider cost attached) and is the quiet profit engine of every food-delivery platform.
  • Swiggy One — a paid membership (~₹899/year, 5.7M+ members in 2025) that gives free delivery and discounts, locking in the most frequent, most valuable customers.
Why food delivery is a "good" business once it scales

Food delivery has strong network effects and a natural duopoly structure. More restaurants attract more customers, who attract more restaurants; more orders in a neighbourhood mean riders spend less time idle and can "batch" deliveries, cutting cost per order. Once you dominate a city, a new entrant would have to burn enormous cash to build the same density — so India's food-delivery market has settled into essentially two players (Zomato/Eternal and Swiggy) rather than a crowd. That structure is exactly why food delivery can be rationally priced (both players raised fees and cut discounting together) and why Swiggy's food-delivery arm finally turned profitable. The catch for Swiggy: it is the smaller of the two, so it earns less of the market's profit pool.

03

Quick commerce & the dark-store machine

A completely different model from food delivery — and a far more capital-hungry one.

"Quick commerce" (q-commerce) means delivering groceries and everyday items — milk, snacks, shampoo, a phone charger — in roughly 10 to 15 minutes. Swiggy's brand for this is Instamart. Crucially, it is not a marketplace like food delivery. To deliver that fast, Swiggy must hold the inventory itself in a dense grid of small local warehouses called dark stores — retail-sized outlets with no shop-front, staffed only for picking and packing.

The magic is proximity: because a dark store sits inside your neighbourhood, the delivery leg is tiny. The cost is that Swiggy must rent, staff, and stock hundreds of these stores before enough customers order to make them pay — that upfront spend is the cash burn.

Why q-commerce is so much harder to make profitable

  • You own the inventory. Unlike food delivery (where the restaurant owns the food), Swiggy buys the groceries. That ties up working capital and adds spoilage and wastage risk.
  • Dark stores are fixed costs. Rent, electricity, and staff for 1,143 stores run whether orders come or not. A new store opens near-empty and loses money until enough local customers adopt it — a "J-curve" per store, just like a new hospital or a new retail outlet.
  • Basket sizes are small and margins thin. Groceries are low-margin; a ₹300 basket of essentials leaves little room to cover picking, packing, and a ten-minute delivery. Profitability depends on getting enough orders per store per day and nudging basket sizes up.
  • The competition is discount-fuelled. Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart have been subsidising deliveries and prices to grab share — which delays everyone's profitability.
The core tension in one line: food delivery is asset-light (no inventory, no warehouses) and now profitable; quick commerce is asset-heavy (inventory + hundreds of stores) and deeply loss-making. Swiggy is deliberately pouring the first's profits and its QIP cash into the second, betting that q-commerce becomes a far bigger market than food delivery ever was.
04

The company and who controls it

A founder-led, professionally-managed platform with no traditional "promoter".

Swiggy grew out of Bundl Technologies, a failed courier-aggregation idea that its founders pivoted into food delivery in 2014. Unlike most Indian listed companies, it has no "promoter" in the legal sense — no single family or founder controls it. Ownership is spread across global investors and the founding team, making it a genuinely professionally-managed, institution-backed platform in the mould of a Silicon Valley company.

Ownership & management

  • No promoter. Under SEBI rules Swiggy has no identified promoter — it is professionally managed. Sriharsha Majety is Group CEO and the largest founder-holder (~6.2%); co-founder Nandan Reddy remains involved; Rahul Jaimini exited operationally years ago but retains a stake. Anand Kripalu chairs the board.
  • Big institutional holders. Prosus/Naspers is the largest single shareholder (~31%), followed by SoftBank (~7.8%) and Accel (~6%). These are long-standing venture backers; watch for gradual selling as lock-ups expire — a recurring overhang for the share price.
  • Balance sheet. Post-QIP the company sits on roughly ₹17,000 Cr of cash and is essentially net-cash — the fuel for the expansion, not a source of leverage risk.
The "no promoter" nuance: for Indian investors used to a controlling family, a promoter-less structure cuts both ways — no promoter-pledging or related-party risk, but also no single anchor with skin in the game steering long-term. Governance rests on the board and professional management, and on the discipline of large institutional holders.

Reading the two margins together

Notice the two Instamart numbers: contribution margin (−1.8%) and adjusted EBITDA margin (−10.9%). The gap between them is fixed store and support cost spread over GOV. As Instamart pours money into new stores that are still filling up, this fixed-cost drag stays heavy — even as the underlying order economics (contribution margin) improve. The path to profitability

Why the company is still loss-making — and the mechanism by which that changes.

Swiggy's group loss is not a mystery: it is arithmetic. Food delivery makes a profit; Instamart makes a bigger loss; corporate costs, ESOPs, depreciation and finance costs sit on top. Add them up and you get a group net loss. The route to group profitability runs almost entirely through one number: the Instamart loss.

Read it plainly: the profitable businesses can't yet offset the Instamart burn plus central overheads. Shrink the Instamart loss toward zero and the group loss collapses. That is the entire equation.
The bull case in one mechanism

Because Instamart's contribution margin is nearly break-even and its GOV is growing ~69% a year, a plausible path exists: as each existing dark store matures and does more orders, its fixed costs get spread over far more GOV, so the adjusted-EBITDA loss shrinks as a percentage — and eventually flips positive. Management guides that Instamart is tracking toward adjusted-EBITDA break-even as maturing stores outweigh the drag from newly-opened ones. If that happens while food delivery keeps compounding its ₹1,000 Cr+ profit, the group loss narrows sharply and then turns. That is the sequence the equity is priced for.

The bear case is the same mechanism running the other way: if competition forces Swiggy to keep subsidising and opening stores faster than they mature, the fixed-cost drag never lifts, contribution-margin gains get competed away, and break-even keeps sliding further out — burning cash the whole time. This is why the quarter-by-quarter Instamart margin trajectory (Section 05's chart) is the single most-watched line in every results release.

A subtlety on the reported loss: the FY26 group loss actually widened to ₹4,154 Cr (from ₹3,117 Cr) even as food delivery improved — because Instamart's absolute burn grew as Swiggy opened stores and defended share. A widening loss in rupees alongside an improving loss as a % of GOV is exactly what a deliberate landgrab looks like. Whether that's disciplined investment or value destruction is the crux of the debate.
07

The Instamart landgrab — Swiggy's defining bet

The force an analyst leads with: a capital-fuelled race to own the fastest-growing channel in Indian retail.

Quick commerce is the most important — and most contested — thing about Swiggy today. India's q-commerce market is growing at a blistering pace (GMV across the industry roughly doubling year-on-year, reaching around ₹11,000 Cr in a single month in early 2026), and the three leaders are spending enormous sums to lock in customers, dark-store locations and delivery density before the market consolidates. Whoever owns the neighbourhood grid owns a channel that is eating into supermarkets, kirana stores and even e-commerce.

Note the shift: after a frantic store-opening phase, net store additions slowed (just 7 net stores in Q4) even as GOV surged ~69% — a sign management is pivoting from "add stores" to "fill the stores we have". That pivot is exactly what drives the operating-leverage story toward break-even.

The three drivers of the burn — and when it turns

  • Store expansion. Every new dark store opens loss-making and drags the blended margin down until it matures (typically several quarters). A slower opening pace lets the existing base mature — the reason the burn can improve even as GOV races ahead.
  • Discounting & marketing. To win and keep customers against Blinkit and Zepto, all three subsidise prices and delivery. Rational competition (less discounting) would accelerate everyone's profitability; irrational competition delays it.
  • Basket & frequency. Bigger baskets and more orders per customer spread fixed costs and lift contribution margin. Instamart is expanding assortment (electronics, larger packs, "maxxsaver" bulk buys) to push basket sizes up.
Why the market is so big it justifies the burn (the bull view)

Bulls argue quick commerce is not a niche — it is a structural re-platforming of Indian grocery and daily retail, a market many times larger than food delivery. If Instamart secures a durable #2 or #3 position in a market that reaches hundreds of thousands of crores of GOV, the eventual profit pool dwarfs today's losses. On this view, the ₹3,000 Cr annual burn is customer-acquisition and land cost for a decades-long franchise — and pulling back now to save money would be strategically suicidal, ceding the market to Blinkit. The ₹10,000 Cr QIP was raised precisely so Swiggy never has to make that retreat.

Why the market is nervous (the bear view): Swiggy is the smaller q-commerce player — roughly a ~24% share versus Blinkit's ~46% (see Section 08). In a business with heavy fixed costs and network density benefits, the leader can reach profitability first and then out-spend followers. The bear fear is that Swiggy burns billions to stay in a race it structurally can't win outright, and that "reaching break-even" keeps receding by a quarter every quarter. The stock's fall well below its ₹390 IPO price reflects exactly this doubt.

How Swiggy differs from Zomato/Eternal — and why it matters

Swiggy and Eternal (Zomato) look like mirror images — both run food delivery + quick commerce — but they are not the same investment. Eternal is the leader on both fronts (bigger food-delivery share, and Blinkit is the q-commerce #1), and reached group profitability earlier; it trades at a premium price-to-sales. Swiggy is the challenger on both fronts — smaller share, later to profit, still loss-making — and trades at a lower multiple. So the two stocks price a different bet: Eternal is "buy the winner at a full price"; Swiggy is "buy the #2 at a discount, on the wager that it closes the gap." Anyone comparing them should hold that framing front of mind — Swiggy is emphatically the higher-risk, higher-variance of the pair.

How analysts actually value Swiggy — sum-of-the-parts

Because a single P/E is impossible, the standard approach is sum-of-the-parts (SOTP): value each business on its own and add them up. (1) Food delivery — profitable and steady, so it can be valued on an EBITDA multiple or on a multiple of its GOV, like a mature platform. (2) Instamart — loss-making, so it's valued on a multiple of GOV or revenue (EV/GOV), benchmarked against Blinkit and Zepto, with the multiple reflecting how close it is to break-even. (3) Other bets + net cash — add Dineout, add the ~₹17,000 Cr cash pile. Sum the three, and you have an enterprise value that doesn't need a profit figure at all. The debate then becomes: what GOV multiple does a #2 q-commerce player deserve, and how fast will it turn? That single assumption swings the valuation enormously — which is why Swiggy is a high-variance stock.

The IPO reality check: Swiggy listed at ₹390 in Nov 2024 and traded around ₹255 by mid-2026 — well below issue price. The de-rating reflects deepening Instamart losses and impatience with the burn, even as revenue and food-delivery profit improved. A recovering share price would need visible progress on the one thing that matters: Instamart marching to break-even.

Why the runway matters more than the loss

Against a group operating burn running at a few thousand crore a year, a ~₹17,000 Cr cash pile buys Swiggy several years of runway — critically, several years in which food delivery keeps generating profit and Instamart marches toward break-even. The QIP was explicitly ring-fenced: roughly ₹4,475 Cr for expanding the q-commerce fulfilment network, ~₹2,340 Cr for brand marketing, ~₹985 Cr for technology. In other words, the market handed Swiggy fresh capital specifically to keep fighting the Instamart war. This is the crucial de-risking: a cash-rich challenger can afford to wait out an irrational competitor; a cash-poor one gets squeezed out. The QIP moved Swiggy firmly into the former camp.

The dilution cost: a QIP is not free — it issues new shares, diluting existing holders. Combined with the overhang of early venture investors (Prosus, SoftBank) gradually selling as lock-ups lapse, share supply is a real headwind for the price even if the business executes well. The trade-off — dilution now for survival and firepower — is one most q-commerce bulls accept; bears see it as a sign the model can't self-fund.

The honest framing: cash removes the solvency risk but not the value risk. A well-funded company can still destroy value by burning that cash on a race it never wins. The runway buys time; whether that time converts into a profitable Instamart is the open question the next several quarters will answer.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A profitable core, a cash-hungry challenger arm, and a share price that reflects the doubt.

Swiggy is a genuinely different animal from a profitable compounder. It pairs a maturing, profitable food-delivery duopoly with a loss-making, fast-growing quick-commerce landgrab, funded by a large cash pile. The investment debate is almost entirely about quick commerce — will Instamart turn, and when?

The bull case

  • Food delivery is now a real profit engine — ₹1,000 Cr+ adjusted EBITDA and rising margins, in a rational duopoly.
  • Instamart economics are improving — contribution margin marching from deeply negative toward break-even; GOV up ~69%.
  • Huge cash runway — ~₹17,000 Cr post-QIP funds years of the landgrab without a solvency worry.
  • Enormous TAM — quick commerce is re-platforming Indian grocery; a durable #2 in a vast market is hugely valuable.
  • Cheaper than Eternal — a lower price-to-sales for the challenger; if it closes the gap, the re-rating is large.

The bear case

  • Still deeply loss-making — FY26 loss widened to ₹4,154 Cr; no P/E, and profitability keeps being pushed out.
  • The smaller q-commerce player — ~24% vs Blinkit's ~46%; the leader can reach profit first and out-spend followers.
  • The burn may never turn — irrational discounting could keep contribution gains from ever covering fixed store costs.
  • Dilution & overhang — the QIP diluted holders; venture backers (Prosus, SoftBank) selling weighs on the price.
  • Below IPO price — the stock trades well under ₹390, and re-rating needs a clear Instamart break-even in sight.
The bottom line

Swiggy is not a value-on-earnings stock — it is a bet on a transition. The safe part of the business (food delivery) works and makes money. The risky part (Instamart) is a well-funded gamble on owning a slice of the biggest change in Indian retail in a generation — and it is currently the smaller challenger, burning cash against a larger, profitable leader. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter: Instamart contribution margin (is it crossing zero?), Instamart adjusted-EBITDA loss as a % of GOV (is the fixed-cost drag lifting?), and the group net loss (is it finally narrowing?). Get those turning, and a challenger bought at a discount re-rates hard. Stay stuck in the burn, and a loss-making #2 has plenty of room to disappoint. That two-sided, high-variance outcome — not a steady compounding — is precisely what you are buying.
