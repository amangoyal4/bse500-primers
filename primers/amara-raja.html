Amara Raja Energy & Mobility, the cash cow building the plant that could replace it

A complete walk through India's number-two lead-acid battery maker — the company behind the AMARON brand under your car bonnet — and its attempt to turn a steady, high-cash battery business into a foothold in the electric age. How a 12-volt lead-acid battery is a recurring annuity that dies on a clock, why the industrial and telecom batteries most people forget are half the moat, and how the cash from all of it is now funding a ₹9,500 Cr lithium gigafactory that may be the future — or a capital sink.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Amara Raja Energy & Mobility (until 2023, Amara Raja Batteries) is India's number-two lead-acid battery maker, behind Exide Industries and roughly its equal — together the two of them dominate the organised market. Its consumer face is the AMARON brand — the maintenance-free battery under the bonnet of millions of Indian cars, bikes, trucks and inverters. But the automotive battery is only half the story: Amara Raja is also the leader in industrial batteries that keep telecom towers, data centres, UPS systems and railways running when the grid drops. It is a steady, cash-generating, duopoly-ish business — and it has just renamed itself and staked a large bet on the electric future.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a cash machine with a recurring core. A lead-acid battery is not a one-time sale — every one ever made dies in roughly 3–5 years and must be replaced. That turns Amara Raja's installed base into an aftermarket annuity: replacement demand that recurs on a clock, is far larger and higher-margin than fresh sales to carmakers, and travels with India's enormous, growing fleet of vehicles. FY26 revenue was ~₹13,549 Cr with an ~11% EBITDA margin, and the balance sheet carries ~₹2,200 Cr of net cash.
  • It renamed itself to signal a pivot. Dropping "Batteries" for "Energy & Mobility" was deliberate. The company is pouring capital into new energy — a giga-scale lithium-ion cell and pack factory in Telangana (the "Giga Corridor," up to 16 GWh, ~₹9,500 Cr planned to 2031), plus EV chargers and energy-storage systems — to avoid being disrupted as vehicles electrify. New-energy revenue is still small, but it grew ~60% in FY26.
  • The whole debate is one of tension. The lead-acid cash cow is mature, high-return and defensive; the lithium bet is capital-hungry, unproven and up against global cell giants and every other Indian entrant. The cash cow must fund the pivot — and the pivot, if it works, must eventually replace the cash cow. At ~21× earnings and a ~₹16,600 Cr market cap, the stock is not expensive by Indian standards; the argument is about whether you are buying a durable annuity with a free lithium option, or a legacy business spending heavily to outrun its own obsolescence.
In plain terms

Amara Raja earns its living from a humble, unglamorous object: the 12-volt lead-acid battery that starts your car and the big industrial batteries that keep a phone tower alive in a power cut. It is a good business precisely because it is boring — the batteries wear out on a schedule, customers replace them without shopping around much, and only two serious players share the organised market. The company is now using the cash that boring business throws off to build something that is anything but boring: a lithium-cell megafactory meant to power the electric vehicles and grid-scale storage of the next twenty years. Whether that is a masterstroke or an expensive distraction is the entire question — and the answer will show up first in how fast new-energy revenue grows and whether the gigafactory ever runs full.

01

Batteries 101: two very different businesses under one roof

No chemistry degree needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole company.

A battery stores electricity as chemistry and releases it on demand. The old, proven, cheap workhorse is the lead-acid battery: lead plates sitting in acid, a design more than 150 years old that still starts almost every petrol and diesel vehicle on earth. It is heavy and low-tech, but it is reliable, recyclable and dirt-cheap per unit of power delivered in short bursts — which is exactly what starting an engine or riding out a power cut needs. Amara Raja makes lead-acid batteries in two families that look similar but sell into completely different worlds.

The two halves of the lead-acid business
FamilyWhat it is — and why it matters here
AutomotiveThe 12V starter battery under the bonnet of cars, bikes, trucks, tractors — plus home inverter batteries. Sold two ways: to vehicle makers on the assembly line (OEM) and, far more profitably, to owners replacing a dead battery (the aftermarket). This is the AMARON brand's home turf.
IndustrialLarge stationary batteries that provide backup power: telecom towers, data centres, UPS systems, railways, solar off-grid. Sold to businesses and infrastructure operators, often on multi-year contracts. Steadier, more technical, and the leg most casual observers forget Amara Raja even has.
Why lead-acid refuses to die

Lithium gets the headlines, but lead-acid still wins wherever you need cheap, dependable power in short bursts and don't care about weight. A starter battery has to deliver a huge jolt of current for a second or two to crank an engine, then sit and recharge — a job lead-acid does superbly for a fraction of lithium's cost. The same is true for a telecom tower that mostly runs on the grid and only needs its battery during outages. Lead-acid also has an unusually clean recycling loop — spent batteries are ~99% recyclable, and the recovered lead feeds straight back into new ones. So even as lithium takes over the jobs where energy density matters (phones, EVs, grid storage), lead-acid keeps the jobs where cost and reliability per burst matter — a much slower erosion than the "lithium eats everything" story assumes.

The three units you'll see repeatedly

  • OEM vs aftermarket. An OEM sale goes to a carmaker fitting a new vehicle — high volume, thin margin, and you win it on price and quality. An aftermarket (or "replacement") sale goes to the owner of an existing vehicle whose battery has died — higher margin, brand-driven, and vastly larger, because there are far more cars on the road than rolling off the line. The aftermarket is where the real money is.
  • GWh (gigawatt-hours). The unit of the new lithium world — how much energy a cell factory can produce a year. Amara Raja's gigafactory is planned at up to 16 GWh of cells. Remember this term for the pivot; it does not describe the lead-acid business, which is measured in units and value.
  • The lead price. Lead is a globally traded commodity and the single biggest raw material in a lead-acid battery. When lead (and the currency it's priced in) moves, Amara Raja's margins move with it — a swing management can only partly pass on to customers, and the main reason FY26 margins slipped.

Hold those two families — automotive and industrial — and the two economics — OEM (thin) and aftermarket (fat) — in your head, and the rest of the company falls into place. Everything Amara Raja does today is either serving that lead-acid base or trying to build a lithium future beside it.

02

What Amara Raja actually sells: three revenue lines, one brand

The business decoded — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Amara Raja's revenue splits into three streams with very different characters: the big, brand-driven automotive lead-acid business; the steadier, contract-driven industrial lead-acid business; and the small but fast-growing new-energy business (lithium packs, chargers, storage). The first two are the mature engine that pays the bills today; the third is the option on tomorrow.

The three lines, decoded

  • Automotive lead-acid — the biggest earner, and the home of the AMARON brand (plus the value brand Powerzone). Amara Raja's leap to the front rank came from a genuinely disruptive product: the maintenance-free, factory-charged battery that you fit and forget, versus the old batteries that needed topping up with water. In automotive, the money is split between OEM supply (to carmakers and two-wheeler makers — Amara Raja grew OEM >20% in FY26) and the far more valuable aftermarket, where owners replacing a dead battery pay a brand premium. A strong dealer and service network — the batteries have to be everywhere, because a dead battery is an emergency purchase — is the real moat here.
  • Industrial lead-acid — where Amara Raja is the domestic leader. These are the big stationary batteries that hold up telecom towers (its historic anchor customer base), UPS systems for offices and data centres, and backup for railways and utilities. It is a more technical, relationship-and-contract business than consumer batteries, less brand-driven and more about reliability, service and total cost of ownership — and it is the leg outsiders routinely underrate.
  • New energy — the future in miniature. Today this includes lithium-ion battery packs (assembled from imported cells, sold into two-/three-wheeler EVs, telecom and storage), EV chargers, and energy-storage systems. It is a small share of revenue but grew ~60% in FY26, and it is the beachhead the giant lithium-cell gigafactory is meant to feed. Crucially, packs and chargers are the near-term new-energy money; the cell factory is the long-term bet.
Why the brand does real work

In most of what Amara Raja sells, the buyer is not an engineer comparing spec sheets — it is a car owner whose battery just died in a parking lot, or a mechanic recommending a replacement. In that moment, a trusted name and a battery that is physically in stock at the nearest shop matter more than a marginal price difference. AMARON spent two decades building exactly that trust plus a dense distribution network, and that is why the aftermarket is both large and defensible: you cannot disrupt it with a cheaper battery nobody can find when they urgently need one. The industrial leg works differently — there the "brand" is a track record of towers that never went dark — but the logic is the same: batteries are bought on trust because the cost of one failing is high.

03

The replacement annuity: why batteries die on a clock

The single mechanism that makes this a quality business — and it is easy to miss.

The most important thing to understand about Amara Raja is that its best business is not selling batteries to carmakers — it is re-selling them to the people who already own a vehicle. A lead-acid battery has a hard physical lifespan: in Indian conditions it typically lasts 3 to 5 years before it can no longer hold a reliable charge and must be swapped out. That single fact converts the entire installed base of vehicles into a rolling, recurring source of demand.

Here is the mechanism, step by step. India has an enormous and growing parc — the total number of vehicles on the road. Every one of them carries a battery. Every one of those batteries will die within a few years and be replaced, whatever the economy is doing, whether or not anyone buys a new car that year. So even in a weak year for auto sales, the replacement stream keeps flowing — it is tied to the huge base of existing vehicles, not to the thin flow of new ones. And because a dead battery is a distress purchase, the replacement buyer reaches for the brand they trust and the product that is in stock. That is a recurring, brand-driven, high-margin annuity hiding inside a commodity product.

The aftermarket is structurally larger and higher-margin than OEM because it draws on the entire fleet, not just this year's new cars — and it recurs on the battery's 3–5 year death clock regardless of the auto cycle. This is the quiet quality inside a "commodity" battery maker.
The device that fits Amara Raja and almost no one else: the death clock

Most manufacturers live and die by whether customers buy something new. Amara Raja mostly lives on the certainty that a product it already sold will break on schedule — and that when it does, the owner will buy another one from the same brand. Every battery it ships is a small future sale booked in advance, dated 3–5 years out. Multiply that across a fleet of hundreds of millions of vehicles and inverters, and you have a demand base that compounds quietly with vehicle ownership and barely notices a bad year for new-car sales. It is the opposite of a fashion business: obsolescence is not a threat here, it is the revenue model. The one thing that would break the clock is a shift to vehicles that don't use a lead-acid battery at all — which is exactly why the electric transition, covered later, is the real question and not the auto cycle.

04

The Galla family, the group, and who controls it

A first-generation industrial house from Andhra Pradesh — with a political thread.

Amara Raja is a first-generation success story built by the Galla family in Tirupati, Andhra Pradesh. Founded in 1985 by Ramachandra Naidu Galla — an engineer who had worked in the United States — it grew from a small industrial-battery maker into a national brand, propelled for years by a technology-and-equity partnership with the American group Johnson Controls (whose battery arm later became Clarios). That partnership brought world-class lead-acid know-how; its unwinding is part of why the company now has to build its own path into new chemistries.

  • 2000s

  • 2010s

  • 2019–2022
    Johnson Controls / Clarios exits its stake; Amara Raja moves toward technological self-reliance. Early new-energy moves begin — lithium packs, chargers, an R&D push.
  • 2023

  • FY25–FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Galla family and associated entities — with the second generation, Jayadev Galla (Chairman & Managing Director) and the next generation (Vikramadityo Galla, Harshavardhana Gourineni) in operating roles. Promoter holding is around 33% (approx.) — notably modest for a family-run Indian manufacturer, meaning a large majority of the company floats with public and institutional investors.
  • The battery-supply nuance: the long Johnson Controls / Clarios association meant Amara Raja historically leaned on a global partner for cutting-edge lead-acid technology. With that partner gone, the company is now developing next-generation chemistry — including lithium — in-house and through new tie-ups. That self-reliance is a source of both ambition and risk.
  • Balance sheet: conservative and cash-rich. FY26 carried only ~₹373 Cr of borrowings against ~₹2,585 Cr of investments — roughly ₹2,200 Cr net cash — with a book value near ₹446/share and ROCE around 13–14%. This is the war-chest that funds the gigafactory without wrecking the balance sheet.
A genuine surprise for a family firm: the promoters own only about a third of Amara Raja. That is unusually low for an Indian promoter-led industrial — most founding families hold 50–75%. It means the Gallas control the company through leadership and history more than through a dominant stake, and it makes the business more exposed to public-shareholder scrutiny (and, in theory, to a change of control) than a typical family-controlled manufacturer.
05

The crux: the lead-acid cash cow funding the lithium bet

The single idea the entire investment case turns on — and it is specific to this company.

Strip away the detail and Amara Raja is a company doing something quietly audacious: using the cash from the technology that electrification threatens to build the technology that electrification rewards. The 12-volt lead-acid battery — the AMARON annuity — is the exact product an all-electric future needs less of. And it is the product whose profits are paying for the ₹9,500 Cr lithium gigafactory meant to matter when that future arrives. The cash cow is financing its own succession.

Every AMARON sold today throws off cash that flows into a plant designed, in part, to make the battery that could one day replace it.

That is the tension, and it is a genuinely two-sided one. On the reassuring side, this is how a smart incumbent should handle disruption: milk the durable, high-return legacy business and redeploy its cash into the next chemistry, rather than paying it all out and being left stranded. Amara Raja has the ideal profile for it — a mature business generating steady cash and a fortress balance sheet, so it can fund the pivot from internal accruals and net cash rather than taking on ruinous debt. On the worrying side, a lithium-cell gigafactory is one of the most brutally competitive, capital-intensive, technology-dependent arenas on earth — and there is no guarantee the returns from lead-acid, however good, can be re-earned inside it.

The way to read Amara Raja: as a hand-off, not a horse race

The mistake is to see the two businesses as competing for your attention. They are running a relay. The lead-acid battery's job is to keep generating cash and hold its ground for as long as possible — every extra year of durable margins is another year of runway for the pivot. The lithium business's job is to be ready to carry the baton before the lead-acid leg tires. The investment question is really about timing: does the cash cow stay healthy for long enough — a decade or more, given lead-acid's slow decline — for the gigafactory to reach the scale, cost and customer wins where it can stand on its own? If yes, this is a rare orderly succession. If the lead-acid margins erode faster than the lithium business matures, the company is funding a plant it cannot yet feed with the profits of a business already fading — and both legs are weak at once.

What makes this more than a slogan is that the two things are physically linked through Amara Raja's own competence: it already knows how to make, sell, service and recycle batteries at scale, and it already sells into telecom, autos and storage — the exact end-markets lithium will serve. So the pivot is not a random diversification; it is the same customers and the same job (store energy, deliver it reliably) done with a new chemistry. That adjacency is the best argument that the cash cow and the lithium bet belong in the same company at all.

06

The Giga Corridor: inside the lithium bet

The big, capital-hungry growth option — what is actually being built.

Amara Raja's new-energy ambition has a physical address: the Giga Corridor at Mahbubnagar, Telangana — a ~260-acre site into which the company plans to pour up to ~₹9,500 Cr through 2031. The end goal is a lithium-cell factory of up to 16 GWh a year plus a battery-pack assembly capacity of up to ~5 GWh — one of India's largest planned home-grown cell operations. This is the plant the cash cow is funding, and it is being built the sensible way: in stages, proving each step before scaling.

The build-out, stage by stage

  • Battery-pack assembly first. The nearest-term, lowest-risk step: assembling imported lithium cells into finished packs for two-/three-wheeler EVs, telecom and storage. This earns revenue now (part of the ~60% new-energy growth) and builds the customer relationships the cell plant will later need.
  • The Customer Qualification Plant (~60 MWh). Commissioned with an initial small capacity, this is a pilot line where Amara Raja makes lithium cells that customers can test and validate before committing to large orders. It is the crucial trust-building step — no serious buyer commits to gigawatt-hours of cells from a maker whose product they haven't qualified. Built for around ₹500 Cr, it is the toe in the water before the plunge.
  • The gigacell factory (toward 16 GWh). The main event — full-scale lithium-cell manufacturing, targeted to build out toward 16 GWh by around FY30. This is where the bulk of the ₹9,500 Cr goes, and where the returns will be made or lost.
  • EV chargers & energy storage. Alongside cells and packs, Amara Raja sells EV chargers and is building energy-storage-system capability — broadening "new energy" from a battery product into an energy-systems offering that plays to its industrial and grid relationships.
Why the staged approach is the tell

A company can announce a 16 GWh gigafactory in a press release in an afternoon. What separates intent from execution is the boring middle: an R&D centre, a qualification line, cells that customers actually approve, and pack orders that turn into repeat business. Amara Raja is deliberately walking that ladder — packs, then a 60 MWh qualification plant, then the big cell lines — rather than betting the balance sheet on a giant plant before a single customer has validated its cells. That sequencing is prudent, and it is also slow: it means the gigafactory's real contribution to profits is years out, and the lead-acid business has to keep the lights on and the cash flowing for the whole climb. The staged plan lowers the risk of a catastrophic write-off; it does not remove the risk that the finished plant, when it arrives, earns a poor return in a brutally competitive market.

The uncomfortable comparison: Amara Raja is not building a lithium plant in a vacuum. It is entering a field where the incumbents are Chinese and Korean cell giants operating at 100+ GWh scale with a decade's head start on cost and yield. India's answer is a scrum of new entrants — from Exide's own cell venture to Ola, Reliance and others chasing government incentives. The bet is that domestic demand, "make-in-India" preference, import friction and Amara Raja's existing customer base give a home-grown 16 GWh plant a viable niche. It is a credible bet. It is not a safe one, and the cost of capital in this business is unforgiving.

07

Does the electric vehicle kill the battery business?

The existential question — with an answer more nuanced than the headline.

The bear's opening line writes itself: electric vehicles don't have engines to start, so they don't need a 12-volt lead-acid starter battery, so Amara Raja's core annuity is on a countdown to zero. There is real truth in this — a fully electric fleet needs far fewer traditional starter batteries, and that is the deepest long-term risk to the lead-acid cash cow. But the reality is slower and more layered than "EVs arrive, lead-acid dies."

The surprise buried in the bear case

Here is the counter-intuitive fact that reframes the whole worry: even an electric car usually still has a small 12-volt battery. The giant lithium traction pack drives the wheels, but a separate low-voltage battery runs the lights, the locks, the computers and — critically — is what lets the car "wake up" and connect its main pack in the first place. Many of these are still lead-acid. So electrification does not switch the lead-acid battery off overnight; it shrinks and slowly transforms the job. Add India's enormous, slow-turning fleet of existing petrol and diesel vehicles — each needing replacement batteries for another 15–20 years — and the erosion of the cash cow is a long, gentle slope, not a cliff. That is precisely what gives the lithium relay (Section 05) time to run. The threat is real; the timeline is the mercy.

None of this makes the lead-acid business a growth engine — its best case is durability, not expansion. But "slow structural decline over 15+ years, cushioned by a still-needed 12V battery and a huge legacy fleet" is a very different investment picture from "obsolete tomorrow." The truer framing is that Amara Raja is racing a clock, not standing in front of a bus — and it started building the next chemistry while the clock still has plenty left on it.

08

The industrial leg most people forget

The quieter, steadier half of lead-acid — and a natural bridge to storage.

Ask a casual observer what Amara Raja does and they'll say "car batteries." But the company was born in industrial batteries, it leads the domestic market in them, and this leg is a big part of why the business is steadier than a pure consumer battery maker would be. It is also the part of the old business that maps most cleanly onto the new-energy future.

Industrial batteries are the large stationary units that provide backup power when the grid fails. The anchor customer historically has been telecom — every mobile tower needs a battery bank so the network stays up during outages, and India has hundreds of thousands of towers. Around that sit UPS systems for offices, hospitals and data centres, plus backup for railways, utilities and solar off-grid installations. These are sold on contracts and relationships, judged on reliability and service rather than brand advertising, and they generate a steadier, less cyclical stream than consumer batteries.

Why the industrial leg is the bridge, not just the ballast

The industrial customers Amara Raja already serves — telecom operators, data centres, utilities — are precisely the buyers of the next generation of energy storage. A telecom tower that today runs on a lead-acid bank is a candidate to run on a lithium pack tomorrow; a grid operator that buys standby batteries is a candidate for grid-scale storage. So the industrial relationships are not just a stable earnings base — they are a warm channel into which Amara Raja can sell its new lithium packs and storage systems. The company doesn't have to find new customers for its pivot; it can walk its existing industrial ones up the chemistry curve. That is a quieter, less glamorous synergy than "gigafactory," but it may be the more reliable one.

09

The industry and the rivals

A cosy lead-acid duopoly — and a crowded, brutal lithium scrum.

Amara Raja competes in two utterly different competitive worlds. In lead-acid, it is one of two scaled players in a comfortable, consolidated market. In lithium cells, it is one of many hopefuls in a global bloodbath. Understanding the company means holding both pictures at once.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

In lead-acid, Amara Raja and Exide behave like a stable two-firm market: they compete hard but rationally, the market is too consolidated and too reliant on brand and distribution for a newcomer to crack easily, and both earn healthy returns. That is the good, defensive half. In lithium, the picture inverts — Amara Raja is a challenger, not an incumbent, against rivals with more scale (the global giants) or the same standing start (the Indian entrants), including its own lead-acid twin Exide, which is further down the cell-manufacturing road. The sharpest read is that Amara Raja's lithium venture should be judged not against "can it beat CATL" (it can't, and doesn't need to) but against "can it earn an acceptable return supplying Indian EV, telecom and storage demand behind the shelter of localisation and its existing relationships." That is the honest bar for the pivot.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cash-rich incumbent racing a slow clock — with the pivot cheaply priced.

Amara Raja enters the next phase from a position of financial strength that most companies attempting a technology transition would envy: a durable, high-return legacy business, a fortress balance sheet, and a lithium plan being built in careful stages rather than one reckless leap. The question is not survival — it is whether the pivot earns its keep before the lead-acid annuity fades, and whether lead costs stop eating the margin in the meantime.

The bull case

  • Durable cash annuity — the lead-acid replacement market recurs on a 3–5 year clock across a vast, growing vehicle fleet, cushioned even in EVs by the 12V auxiliary battery.
  • Duopoly economics — a stable two-firm lead-acid market (with Exide) that protects pricing and returns; AMARON brand and distribution are hard to dislodge.
  • Fortress balance sheet — ~₹2,200 Cr net cash funds the gigafactory internally, no debt strain; the pivot is self-financed.
  • Cheaply priced option — at ~21× with a lot of the value in cash and a steady core, the lithium upside is close to free in the valuation.
  • Adjacency, not diversification — same customers (auto, telecom, storage), same job (store and deliver energy), new chemistry; the industrial leg is a warm channel for new-energy sales.

The bear case

  • Structural EV erosion — the core auto-battery pool shrinks over the long run as the fleet electrifies; the cash cow's best case is durability, not growth.
  • Lithium execution & capital risk — a 16 GWh gigafactory is a brutally competitive, capital-intensive bet against global giants and Indian rivals (incl. Exide); it may never earn its cost of capital.
  • Lead & FX squeeze — lead is a traded commodity; a rising lead price (as in FY26) compresses margins faster than they can be passed on.
  • Margin already slipping — FY26 EBITDA margin fell ~175 bps and PAT was flat; the earnings engine stalled even as revenue grew.
  • Slow pivot payoff — the staged, prudent build means the gigafactory's real profit contribution is years away, and new energy is still a small share of revenue.
The bottom line

Amara Raja is a rare thing: an incumbent facing disruption that actually has the money, the balance sheet and the customer base to fund its own reinvention — and is doing it in careful, provable stages rather than a bet-the-company leap. The lead-acid annuity is not about to collapse; India's fleet, the 12V auxiliary battery and the industrial backup business give it a long, gentle runway, and the stock is priced modestly enough that you are not paying up for a miracle. What is unproven is the hand-off: whether the Giga Corridor's cells win real customers and run near full before the legacy margins fade, and whether the lead-cost squeeze of FY26 was a passing headwind or a new normal. The next few years answer it in one checkable trend — new-energy revenue climbing from a rounding error toward a double-digit share of the total, with the gigafactory's qualification wins and utilisation to match. Watch that mix print each year; it will tell you whether Amara Raja is executing an orderly succession or funding a plant it cannot yet fill.
