00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Sonata Software is a Bengaluru-headquartered technology company that reported ₹10,701 crore of consolidated revenue in FY26. That number is the single most misleading figure in this primer — because it fuses two businesses that have almost nothing in common. The first, International IT Services (IITS), is a real global digital-transformation firm: cloud, data, AI, application engineering and Microsoft Dynamics 365 work sold to enterprise clients across the US, Europe and Australia, at healthy software-services margins. The second, Domestic Products & Services (DPS), is essentially a distributor — it buys software licences, cloud subscriptions and hardware from the likes of Microsoft and Adobe and resells them to Indian companies, booking enormous revenue at almost no margin. Read the consolidated line and you will value the wrong company.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Most of the "revenue" isn't the business you care about. Of that ₹10,701 Cr, roughly ₹7,772 Cr (73%) is DPS resale — pass-through distribution turnover. The engine that actually creates value, IITS, is only ₹2,948 Cr (27%). Flip to profit and the picture inverts: IITS generated ~₹544 Cr of the group's ~₹741 Cr EBITDA (73%), while all of DPS produced barely a quarter of it. The headline top-line is mostly noise.
  • The engine stalled in dollars this year. Here is the surprise a glance at the rupee figure hides: IITS revenue in its reporting currency actually fell — from $335.5 mn in FY25 to $328.4 mn in FY26 (about −2%). The consolidated line "grew 5.4%" mostly on a weaker rupee and on DPS turnover. The part of Sonata worth owning shrank slightly even as the group looked like it grew.
  • It is cheap, net-cash, and acquisition-built. Sonata trades near ₹7,740 Cr market cap at ~17× earnings — a steep discount to mid-cap IT peers on ~30-40× — with a net-cash balance sheet and RoCE above 30%. But its IITS growth over the last five years was bought: a string of US acquisitions (Quant Systems, Encore, Sopris, Scalable) funded largely with cash and debt, leaving goodwill and integration risk on the books.
In plain terms

Two companies wear one ticker. Imagine a boutique consulting firm sharing an office and a bank account with a wholesale electronics dealer, then stapling their sales together on one page. The dealer's turnover dwarfs the consultancy's fees, so the combined "sales" look huge — but the dealer keeps two cents on the rupee, while the consultancy keeps eighteen. If you value the pair on their blended sales you will badly misprice both. The entire craft of reading Sonata is learning to un-staple that page: judge IITS on its own revenue, growth and margin, and treat DPS as the low-margin, cash-cycle sideshow it is.

01

Mid-cap Indian IT 101: how the money is actually made

The handful of terms and mechanics that drive every company in this space — Sonata included.

India's IT-services industry sells one thing above all: skilled engineering time, at a margin. A firm hires software people in India (and increasingly onshore), rents them out to global enterprises to build and run software, and pockets the gap between what the client pays per hour and what the engineer costs. Everything else — cloud, data, AI, "digital transformation" — is a label for higher-value versions of that same trade. To follow Sonata, you need six ideas.

The only vocabulary you need

Because IITS bills mostly in US dollars but reports in rupees, a falling rupee can flatter the rupee revenue even when dollar revenue is flat or down. Pass-through revenueMoney that flows through a company's books but isn't really "earned" — a reseller buys a ₹100 licence and sells it for ₹103. All ₹103 shows up as revenue, but only ₹3 is gross value added. DPS is built on this.

The economics that decide everything: value added, not turnover

In a pure services firm, revenue and value are close cousins — most of what a client pays becomes gross profit before salaries. In a reseller, they diverge violently: the "revenue" is mostly other people's product cost simply passing through. This is why blended revenue is a trap for any company that mixes the two. The number that matters is not how much money moves through the accounts but how much value the company adds to it — and value added shows up as gross margin and EBITDA, not as top-line. Hold that distinction and Sonata's odd financial shape stops being confusing and becomes obvious.

Who buys from a firm like Sonata

  • Global enterprises & ISVs — retailers, distributors, manufacturers, travel and software firms across the US, UK, Europe and Australia buying digital-transformation and product-engineering work. This is IITS's customer base.
  • Microsoft's ecosystem — clients who have standardised on Microsoft Dynamics 365, Azure and the Power Platform and need a specialist partner to implement it. Sonata is one of Microsoft's most decorated partners, and this pulls a large share of IITS work.
  • Indian corporates & government — the buyers of DPS: companies that need software licences, cloud subscriptions and hardware, and buy them through a value-added reseller rather than direct. Huge in rupee terms, thin in margin.
02

The two-headed company: the optical trap at the centre of Sonata

The single most important idea in this primer — decode it once and every other number falls into place.

Sonata reports two segments, and they are not just different divisions of one business — they are different kinds of business that happen to sit under one holding company. Getting this wrong is the classic first-timer error. So set the two side by side and look at what each contributes to revenue versus what it contributes to profit.

The device that fits only Sonata: strip the wrapper, weigh the engine

Most companies you can value on one revenue number. Sonata forces you to do subtraction first. Take the ₹10,701 Cr, remove the ₹7,772 Cr of DPS resale, and you are left with ₹2,948 Cr — the IITS engine, the only part that behaves like the mid-cap IT firm the market files Sonata under. Value that: its dollar growth, its ~18.5% margin, the goodwill and net-cash left behind by the deals that built it. The reseller wrapper doesn't add value; it adds turnover, a working-capital cycle, and optical confusion. Once subtracted, Sonata is a ~₹2,950 Cr services business trading inside a ₹10,700 Cr revenue costume — and the costume is exactly why a first-timer misprices it.

03

Inside IITS: the engine that actually creates value

Sonata's real business — what it does, who it sells to, and why the margin is respectable.

International IT Services is the Sonata a technology investor is trying to buy. It is a mid-sized global digital-services firm — roughly $328 mn of annual revenue and around 8,000 people group-wide — that helps enterprises modernise their software estate. Its self-styled brand for this is "Platformation": the idea that a client shouldn't just move systems to the cloud piecemeal but re-platform around modern, connected, data- and AI-ready foundations.

What IITS actually delivers

  • Cloud & application modernisation — rebuilding legacy business software to run on the cloud (chiefly Microsoft Azure), the bread-and-butter of the digital-services trade.
  • Data & AI — wiring up a client's data so it can be analysed and, increasingly, fed into AI and generative-AI applications. Management now frames a growing share of new work as "AI-led," and it is the fastest-growing theme in the pipeline.
  • Microsoft Dynamics 365 — implementing Microsoft's flagship enterprise-applications suite (ERP + CRM). Sonata is a top-tier Dynamics partner, and this is one of its most differentiated capabilities.
  • ISV / product engineering — building and modernising software for software companies. A stickier, specialist niche that carries better margins than commodity staffing.
  • Vertical focus — concentrated on a handful of industries (retail & distribution, manufacturing & CPG, TMT/software, BFSI, travel & healthcare) rather than trying to serve everyone.
Why the ~18.5% margin is the quality signal

Strip out DPS and IITS earned about ₹544 Cr of EBITDA on ₹2,948 Cr of revenue — an ~18.5% margin, up more than a percentage point on the prior year. That is a perfectly respectable mid-cap IT-services margin, and it is the clearest evidence that IITS is a genuine services business and not a body-shop. It sits below the ~24-27% that the very best Indian IT firms print, which is fair: Sonata is smaller, more acquisition-stitched, and more concentrated. But an ~18.5% margin on a business growing (in better years) faster than the industry is the number that justifies calling IITS "the engine." When you build a valuation, you are really buying this margin times this revenue times whatever growth you believe.

The tension in FY26 was that this engine idled. IITS dollar revenue slipped ~2% for the year as global IT demand softened and one part of the client base pulled back on discretionary spend. Encouragingly, the exit rate improved — Q4 IITS grew about 1.1% year-on-year in constant currency and management pointed to a firmer deal pipeline and AI-led wins into FY27. Whether that inflection is real is, quite literally, the thing to watch each quarter.

04

DPS: the distribution wrapper that inflates the top line

The other half of the page — huge in rupees, tiny in profit, and best understood as a channel business.

Domestic Products & Services is the segment that makes Sonata's revenue look enormous. It is, in plain description, a value-added reseller and distributor: it buys technology products — software licences, cloud subscriptions, and hardware — from vendors such as Microsoft, Adobe and others, and sells them to Indian enterprises and government buyers, adding a thin layer of advisory, provisioning and support on top. In FY26 it booked ₹7,772 Cr of revenue, up ~5.9% — and produced barely ₹200 Cr of EBITDA in doing so.

The blended ~6.9% margin describes neither business. It is simply the mathematical average of a real ~18.5% services margin and a ~2.5% distribution margin, weighted by DPS's outsized revenue. Any analysis that quotes the blended figure as "Sonata's margin" has already made the mistake.

Why keep a business that earns almost nothing per rupee of sales? Because distribution is not valueless — it just isn't a margin business. It throws off cash and volume, cements relationships with the same vendors (notably Microsoft) that feed the IITS side, and gives Sonata reach into thousands of Indian accounts. In a good year it is a steady, capital-light annuity of small spreads on very large flows. The problem is optical: because it turns over billions of rupees, it dominates the headline and drags the blended margin into single digits, making a decent services company look like a low-quality one.

The hidden cost of a fat-turnover reseller: working capital

A distributor's real balance-sheet footprint isn't profit — it's working capital. To resell billions of rupees of licences and hardware, DPS must hold inventory and, above all, carry receivables: it pays vendors and waits for Indian corporate and government customers to pay it. Those flows swing with quarter-end buying patterns and can whip the group's cash conversion around even when the services business is steady. So DPS distorts Sonata twice over — once by inflating revenue and crushing the blended margin, and again by injecting a lumpy, seasonal cash cycle that has nothing to do with the services engine you actually want to own. When you see Sonata's operating cash flow lurch in a given quarter, DPS is usually the reason.

05

Growth that was bought, not built: the acquisition machine

How IITS scaled — and the goodwill, earn-outs and integration risk that came with it.

Here is the second surprise about Sonata: a large share of the IITS engine did not grow organically — it was acquired. Over roughly 2015-2024 Sonata bought a string of mostly US and specialist firms, bolting their revenue, clients and capabilities onto the IITS platform. This is a legitimate, common mid-cap strategy — but it means the "growth" in the services business has repeatedly been purchased with cash and debt, and it leaves goodwill, earn-out liabilities and integration work on the books.

Why acquisition-led growth is a double-edged sword

Buying growth works beautifully when deals are integrated cleanly and the acquired clients stay. It cuts the other way in three specific ways an investor must price. First, goodwill: pay a premium for a firm and the excess sits on the balance sheet, exposed to a writedown if the business underperforms. Second, earn-outs: deals like Quant Systems defer part of the price against future milestones, so a good outcome triggers cash payouts and a bad one triggers disputes. Third, integration drag: stitching a US analytics firm and an Australian Dynamics shop into one delivery platform absorbs management attention and can dent margins mid-transition. Sonata's IITS story is genuinely attractive — but a fair reading treats the growth as partly capital that was spent, not purely value that was created, and keeps one eye on the goodwill line.

A quick untangling to avoid a common mix-up: Sonata's deal history is often confused with Quinnox — a Bengaluru-linked digital-solutions firm that was, in fact, acquired by US-listed ASGN, not by Sonata. Sonata's own trail is Quant Systems, Encore, Sopris, Scalable and the earlier tuck-ins above. When you research the acquisition history, keep those separate.
06

The Microsoft tie: Sonata's moat and its dependence

A 30-year partnership that opens doors on both sides of the company — and concentrates the risk.

Thread running through both halves of Sonata is Microsoft. On the IITS side, Sonata is one of Microsoft's most decorated implementation partners — a member of Microsoft's elite Inner Circle (roughly the top 1% of partners globally) with a 30-plus-year relationship, deep in Dynamics 365, Azure and the Power Platform. On the DPS side, a large share of the licences and cloud subscriptions it resells in India are Microsoft's. The same vendor anchors the high-margin engine and the low-margin wrapper.

This is a real competitive advantage. Enterprises standardising on Microsoft's stack want a partner who lives inside that ecosystem, and Sonata's certifications, Inner Circle status and Dynamics depth are hard-won credentials that win work and defend margins. It is precisely the kind of specialisation that lets a mid-cap punch above its size against generalist rivals.

The flip side of a great partnership: concentration

A moat built on one vendor is also a dependence on one vendor. Two things follow. First, channel economics: Microsoft periodically reshapes how partners are paid — margins on cloud resale, incentive structures, the split between direct and partner-led sales. A shift that trims partner economics would hit both DPS's thin resale spread and parts of IITS's Dynamics/Azure work. Second, strategic direction: if Microsoft leans harder into selling and implementing directly, or reprices its programs, a partner as tied-in as Sonata feels it disproportionately. The Microsoft relationship is genuinely a strength — but it is a concentrated one, and "what is Microsoft doing to its channel?" is a question a Sonata holder can never fully stop asking.

Client concentration, the older wound: beyond vendor dependence, Sonata has historically carried meaningful customer concentration in IITS — a small number of large accounts contributing an outsized share of services revenue. When a top account has derisked or scaled back in the past, it has visibly dented IITS growth. Track the top-client and top-5/top-10 client shares in the disclosures; a wobble there shows up in the numbers before anything else does.
07

The company, the history, and who owns it

A long-listed Bengaluru IT house, professionally run, reshaped around IITS.

Sonata Software is one of India's longer-listed IT companies, with roots going back to the 1980s and a Bengaluru base. For much of its life it was known as much for the domestic products/distribution business as for services. The story of the last decade is the deliberate tilt toward IITS — building, buying and branding a global digital-services firm on top of the older distribution franchise.

Timeline

  • 1980s-90s
    Sonata emerges as an Indian software and technology-products company; the domestic distribution business (reselling third-party software and hardware) is an early and enduring pillar.
  • 1990s-2000s
    Listed on BSE (scrip 532221) and NSE (SONATSOFTW); builds an international IT-services arm alongside the domestic products business.
  • 2015
    Acquisitive phase begins in earnest — niche tuck-ins (Rezopia, Halosys and others) add platform and vertical capabilities to services.
  • 2022
    Leadership transition — Samir Dhir takes over as CEO & MD, sharpening the "Platformation" digital-services strategy and the push into cloud, data and AI.
  • 2023
    Largest deal to date — Quant Systems (~$160 mn) — bolts scaled US data-analytics and cloud-modernisation onto IITS; goodwill and earn-outs rise.
  • FY24-FY25
    IITS scales past $330 mn; Encore, Sopris and Scalable deepen cloud and Dynamics capability; then global IT demand softens.
  • FY26
    Consolidated revenue ₹10,701 Cr (+5.4%), PAT ₹464 Cr (+9.3%); IITS dollar revenue dips ~2% to $328.4 mn but Q4 turns positive in cc; net-cash balance sheet; final dividend of 415% on the ₹1 face value.

Ownership & governance

  • Promoter holding: the promoter group holds roughly ~28% (approx.) — a modest stake by Indian promoter standards, which means a large free float and a shareholder register weighted toward institutions and the public. Verify the exact figure and any pledge against the latest shareholding filing.
  • Management: Sonata is a professionally managed company rather than a founder-run one — CEO & MD Samir Dhir (ex-industry services leadership) drives strategy, with the promoter group in an ownership rather than day-to-day operating role.
  • Structure to keep straight: the domestic distribution business runs through a subsidiary (historically Sonata Information Technology Ltd), while IITS is delivered through Sonata's US and international subsidiaries built up by acquisition. The consolidated accounts fold both together — which is exactly the fusion this primer keeps prising apart.
The governance angle worth a glance: a company built by acquisition, holding earn-out liabilities and goodwill, with a low promoter stake and a professional management team, lives or dies on capital-allocation discipline. The questions that matter here are whether the deals earn their cost of capital, whether earn-outs are being met without disputes, and whether cash is returned (the healthy dividend suggests some discipline) rather than ploughed into ever-pricier acquisitions. Watch how management funds its next deal.
08

Demand softness and the GenAI question

The industry backdrop that idled the engine in FY26 — and the technology shift hanging over all of IT services.

Sonata does not operate in a vacuum. Two forces outside its control shaped FY26 and will shape the next few years: a cyclical demand slowdown in global IT spending, and a structural question mark over what generative AI does to the services model. Both cut to the heart of the IITS engine.

The AI question, put plainly for a services firm

Generative AI is the one force that could genuinely re-write what a company like Sonata is worth, and it points both ways at once. On the bear side, if AI tools let clients build and maintain software with far fewer billable hours, the industry's core trade — renting out engineering time — compresses, and a mid-cap without enormous scale has less cushion to absorb it. On the bull side, every enterprise that wants to deploy AI on its own data needs exactly the cloud-plumbing, data-engineering and application work that IITS sells — and Sonata's Microsoft/Azure/Dynamics anchoring places it right where that demand lands. Which effect dominates is unknowable today; what is knowable is that Sonata is small enough to be hurt and specialised enough to benefit. The AI-led share of new bookings is the metric that will, over time, tell you which way it broke.

The rivals: where Sonata sits in the mid-cap IT pack

A crowded field of Indian mid-caps — and a Microsoft-partner niche Sonata carved for itself.

India's mid-cap IT space is deep and competitive — a cluster of firms all chasing digital-transformation budgets from global enterprises. Sonata competes on two fronts: broadly, against the mid-cap pack for services work; and specifically, against other Microsoft Dynamics/Azure specialists for the ecosystem work that is its edge. The trick, again, is to compare on IITS — not on the distribution-inflated consolidated line.

Two takeaways. First, on a like-for-like IITS basis Sonata is a sub-scale mid-cap — smaller than Coforge or Birlasoft — so it must win on specialisation, not size, and its Microsoft Inner-Circle status and Dynamics depth are precisely that specialisation. The relevant peer multiple for valuing Sonata is what these mid-caps trade at on their services earnings — which is exactly why Sonata's headline "cheapness" against them narrows once you value it on IITS rather than on distribution-padded revenue.

Financials & valuation

A cheap, net-cash, dividend-paying stock — cheap for a reason the segments explain.

FY26 was a year of steady consolidated numbers hiding a soft engine: revenue and profit ticked up, the balance sheet stayed net-cash, dividends kept flowing — while the IITS dollar line quietly slipped. The stock trades at a large discount to peers, and the whole valuation debate is whether that discount is a distortion to exploit or a fair mark on an acquisition-dependent name with a stalled engine.

The one number that settles the thesis: IITS constant-currency growth × IITS margin

Forget the consolidated revenue; it is the most-quoted and least-useful figure Sonata publishes. The case turns on a single compound gauge — IITS constant-currency revenue growth, read together with the IITS EBITDA margin, net of the goodwill and net-cash the acquisitions left behind. If IITS reaccelerates from its FY26 dip back toward double-digit cc growth while holding ~18-19% margins, then a ~17× multiple on a net-cash, 30%-plus-RoCE services engine is genuinely cheap, and the distribution optics were hiding value. If IITS stays flat-to-shrinking in dollars — client concentration bites, or GenAI compresses the work — then the discount is the market correctly pricing an acquisition-dependent name whose engine has stopped compounding. Every quarter prints those two IITS numbers; they, not the ₹10,701 Cr headline, are the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap net-cash services engine wearing a distribution costume — asked to prove the engine still runs.

Sonata enters FY27 as a paradox: a company that looks statistically cheap and financially clean, wrapped around a services engine that just spent a year going backwards in its own currency. The debate is not whether the business exists or whether it is well-financed — both are settled. It is whether IITS reaccelerates fast enough, at margin, to prove the discount was an optical illusion rather than a fair verdict.

The bull case

  • A real ~18.5%-margin engine, mispriced — value Sonata on IITS not consolidated revenue and a net-cash, 30%+-RoCE services firm at ~17× looks cheap versus 30-40× peers.
  • Microsoft moat — Inner-Circle status and deep Dynamics/Azure capability win differentiated, defensible work as enterprises standardise on Microsoft.
  • AI-led inflection — Q4 IITS cc growth turned positive and the pipeline is tilting to AI-enablement, exactly where its Azure/data anchoring places it.
  • Clean, cash-returning balance sheet — net-cash, healthy dividends (415% FY26), room to fund the next capability deal without strain.
  • Optionality in the wrapper — DPS is low-margin but cash-generative and cements the Microsoft channel that feeds services.

The bear case

  • The engine shrank — IITS dollar revenue fell ~2% in FY26; the discount may simply price a services business that has stopped compounding.
  • Acquisition & goodwill risk — growth was bought; a misstep, a missed earn-out, or a writedown on deals like Quant Systems would hurt.
  • Client & Microsoft concentration — reliance on a few large accounts and on one vendor's channel economics leaves the engine exposed to a single decision elsewhere.
  • The GenAI overhang — a sub-scale mid-cap has less cushion if AI compresses billable services hours across the industry.
  • DPS distorts everything — thin, volatile margins and a lumpy working-capital cycle muddy the optics and the cash flow.
The bottom line

Sonata is the rare stock where the first job is arithmetic, not judgement: subtract ₹7,772 Cr of distribution turnover from a ₹10,701 Cr top line and you finally see the company you're being asked to value — a ~₹2,950 Cr, ~18.5%-margin, net-cash, Microsoft-anchored digital-services firm that grew by buying capability and then, in FY26, stalled in dollars. Everything hinges on whether that stall was the cycle or the ceiling. The checkable answer arrives four times a year in two figures printed side by side — IITS constant-currency growth and IITS EBITDA margin. Watch them cross back into double-digit growth at ~18%-plus margin and the cheap multiple was the optical trap doing its work; watch them flatline and the market was reading the engine correctly all along. Read past the ₹10,701 Cr, and Sonata stops being confusing — and starts being a single, answerable question.
