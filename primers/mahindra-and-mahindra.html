Mahindra & Mahindra, the two-engine comeback machine

A complete walk through the Mahindra group's flagship — the company that builds India's best-selling SUVs and the world's best-selling tractors. How a near-forgotten carmaker became the country's #2 passenger-vehicle brand, why a ruthless "18% ROE or exit" discipline re-rated the whole stock, and how a net-cash auto-and-farm core self-funds a ₹16,000-crore electric-SUV bet — sitting atop a federation of listed "growth gems."

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Mahindra & Mahindra is the listed flagship of the Mahindra group and, at its heart, a two-business manufacturer: it makes SUVs (Thar, Scorpio-N, XUV700, XUV 3XO, Bolero) and it makes tractors (under the Mahindra and Swaraj brands). Remarkably, it is #1 in both — India's largest SUV maker by revenue share and the world's largest tractor maker by volume — and in FY26 (the year ended March 2026) it overtook Hyundai to become India's #2 passenger-vehicle brand overall, behind only Maruti Suzuki.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Two strong cylinders, firing together. FY26 consolidated revenue rose 25% to ₹1,98,639 crore and profit attributable to owners ~32% to ₹17,099 Cr. The auto business hit a record 25.3% SUV revenue market share (up 260 basis points); the farm business sold an all-time-high 5,05,930 tractors (43.6% share). Each is a clear market leader, and crucially they peak on different cycles — autos on urban incomes and fashion, tractors on the rural/monsoon cycle.
  • A capital-discipline transformation. Under group CEO Anish Shah, Mahindra exited ~15 loss-making businesses in roughly 18 months (including Korea's SsangYong) and imposed a hard rule: every business must earn an 18% return on equity or be fixed or sold. That 18% target — once a stretch — was hit in about a year and a half, and FY26 ROE of ~20–21% shows it held. The re-rating that followed is a big part of the stock's story.
  • A funded EV option on top. The auto-and-farm core is essentially net-cash (the group's large consolidated debt of ₹1.34 lakh crore sits almost entirely in its lending arm, not its factories), which lets it self-fund a serious electric-SUV push — the "Born Electric" XEV 9e, BE 6 and XEV 9S — ring-fenced in a subsidiary independently valued at up to ₹80,580 crore (~$9.8bn) with Temasek and BII as investors.
In plain terms

Think of Mahindra as a company with two reliable money-making engines that rarely stall at the same time: rugged, desirable SUVs for India's car buyers, and tractors for its farmers. For years the group diluted those gems with sprawling, money-losing side-ventures; a new CEO cut the dead weight and demanded every rupee earn a real return, which lifted profitability and the share price together. Now the cash those two engines throw off is funding the next bet — stylish, ground-up electric SUVs — without piling debt on the core, while a cluster of separately-listed subsidiaries (Tech Mahindra, Mahindra Finance and others) adds a layer of "hidden" value on top. The risks are the obvious ones for any carmaker-cum-tractor-maker: both core businesses are cyclical, competition is fierce, and the EV transition is expensive and not yet proven to be profitable at scale.

01

The two-cylinder engine: how Auto + Farm work

Two different businesses, two different cycles — and why running both is an advantage.

Mahindra's core is genuinely two businesses under one roof, each a leader in its market, each earning money in a slightly different way. Understanding how they differ — and why they don't usually slump together — is the key to the whole company. A third profit pool, the rural finance arm, sits alongside and quietly enables the other two.

Why two cylinders beat one — the natural hedge

The two engines respond to different drivers. SUV demand tracks urban incomes, fuel prices, interest rates and fashion; tractor demand tracks the monsoon, crop prices, government support prices and rural sentiment. A weak farm year (a poor monsoon) can coincide with a perfectly decent urban-auto year, and vice-versa — so the two tend to smooth each other out. This diversification is a real structural advantage over a pure-play carmaker: when one business is in a down-cycle, the other frequently isn't, giving Mahindra steadier group cash flows than its single-product rivals. The flip side is complexity — running a world-class SUV business and a world-class tractor business demands two very different sets of skills, suppliers, dealers and customers, and management attention is a finite resource.

A useful way to read the FY26 numbers: the farm business is the more profitable cylinder (19.9% PBIT margin versus 9.3% for auto), but the auto business is the bigger one by revenue and the one carrying the growth story. Notice too that the auto margin actually dipped slightly (9.5% → 9.3%) even as volumes surged — that's the cost of the EV ramp, where electric SUVs are currently lower-margin than petrol/diesel ones. The farm margin, by contrast, expanded sharply as a strong rural cycle let Mahindra sell more tractors over a fixed cost base. That is the cyclical leverage of the tractor business in a single line.

02

The company and who controls it

A professionally-run group with a famous chairman — and an unusually low promoter stake.

Mahindra is one of India's oldest and most respected business houses, today run by professional management under a celebrated chairman, Anand Mahindra. Unusually for a large Indian group, the promoter family owns only ~18.5% — Mahindra is, in effect, a widely-held, professionally-managed company with a guiding family rather than a tightly-controlled family firm. That matters: it means decisions are made by a professional CEO accountable to a broad institutional shareholder base, and it is part of why capital discipline (§05) could be imposed at all.

  • 2022

  • 2024–25

Ownership & leadership

  • Promoter ~18.45%: the Mahindra family and group entities. A relatively low stake that makes Mahindra effectively a professionally-managed, institution-owned company; the rest is held by foreign and domestic funds and the public.
  • Anand Mahindra (Chairman): the public face and culture-setter, author of the group's "RISE" philosophy, now in a non-executive role; strategy and operations sit squarely with professional management.
  • Anish Shah (Group CEO & MD): the architect of the capital-allocation reset and the "growth gems" framework — the executive most associated with the re-rating.
  • Rajesh Jejurikar (Executive Director & CEO, Auto & Farm Sectors): runs the two core businesses — the product turnaround in SUVs and the tractor franchise both sit under him.
03

The SUV resurgence

The comeback that re-made the company — from also-ran to India's #2 carmaker.

A decade ago, Mahindra's cars were rugged but unfashionable, and the company was steadily losing relevance in passenger vehicles. Then came a string of hit SUVs — the reborn Thar (2020), the Scorpio-N and XUV700 (2021–22), the XUV 3XO and the Thar Roxx (2024) — that turned the brand into India's hottest SUV maker. In FY26 it passed Hyundai to become the country's #2 passenger-vehicle brand, an extraordinary turnaround for a company once written off in cars.

India's market is shifting structurally toward SUVs (now well over half of all passenger vehicles) — and Mahindra, as a pure SUV specialist, is the cleanest beneficiary of that shift.

What's actually selling — and why it's a quality problem

The line-up is unusually coherent. The Thar was repositioned from a niche off-roader into an aspirational lifestyle vehicle and now sells in volumes a niche product never could; the longer five-door Thar Roxx extended that. The Scorpio-N and XUV700 carried multi-month order backlogs through much of FY25–FY26 — a "good problem" of demand outrunning supply that pushed Mahindra to expand SUV capacity from ~64,500 units/month toward ~68,000 in H1 FY27. The XUV 3XO brought the brand into the high-volume compact-SUV space, and the Bolero remains a rural/SME workhorse. The result: Mahindra clocked its highest-ever SUV volumes in FY26 and a ~13.4% retail PV market share.

Why the SUV win matters so much

Two structural reasons make this more than a lucky streak. First, India is going SUV: utility vehicles have grown from a minority to well over half of all passenger vehicles sold, as buyers trade up to higher driving positions, rugged styling and more space. Mahindra is a pure SUV specialist — it deliberately doesn't make small hatchbacks — so the whole industry's mix-shift flows straight to its strength rather than against it. Second, SUVs carry higher prices and fatter margins than small cars, so winning here is far more profitable than winning the hatchback war Maruti dominates. The risk is the mirror image: SUV success invites everyone in, the next wave of launches must keep landing, and a desirable brand can cool as fast as it heated. But for now, Mahindra owns the segment India most wants to buy — and it owns it on desirability, not on price.

The order-book signal: Mahindra's SUV strength has repeatedly shown up as a long waiting list rather than discounting — the opposite of a brand having to push metal. That is the healthiest possible kind of demand, but it also means reported volumes have at times been capacity-limited, not demand-limited: the swing factor for the auto business is increasingly how fast new plants come online, not whether buyers turn up.
04

Tractors and the rural cycle

The world's #1 tractor maker — a high-margin cash machine tied to the monsoon.

Mahindra is the largest tractor maker on earth by volume, and its farm-equipment business is the quieter, higher-margin half of the company. In FY26 the Mahindra and Swaraj brands together sold a record 5,05,930 tractors domestically (+24%), holding a commanding 43.6% market share as India's tractor industry crossed the historic one-million-unit mark (wholesale dispatches of ~11.6 lakh) for the first time in a single year.

Why this is such a good business — the economics

Tractors are, structurally, a wonderful business for Mahindra, for four reasons. Scale: as the largest player by a distance, it spreads fixed costs (R&D, plants, dealer network) over more units than anyone else, giving it cost leadership. Brand & reach: the Mahindra and Swaraj names carry deep trust in rural India, sold through a dense dealer-and-finance network that competitors find hard to replicate. Two brands, one company: running both Mahindra and Swaraj lets it cover different price points and regions without cannibalising itself. And margins: the farm segment earns ~20% PBIT margins — roughly double the auto business — on relatively modest capital intensity, which makes it the group's prized cash engine. That cash is part of what funds the SUV capacity and the EV bet.

The catch: it's a weather-and-credit business

The single most important thing to understand about tractors is that demand is cyclical and weather-dependent. A good monsoon, strong crop prices, healthy government support prices and easy rural credit drive a tractor up-cycle; a drought, weak farm incomes or a credit squeeze can cut volumes sharply and quickly. FY26's record was helped by a genuinely favourable rural cycle — so investors should treat the farm line as lumpier than the auto line over time, and be wary of extrapolating a peak year forever. This is also precisely why the two-engine model earns its keep: a weak tractor year is often cushioned by a strong SUV year, and the group rarely sees both cylinders misfire at once. Mahindra's share lead, importantly, has stayed remarkably durable across cycles even as absolute volumes swing.

The structural tailwind: Indian agriculture is slowly mechanising and farm holdings are gradually consolidating — a long-run driver of tractor demand and, importantly, of a mix-shift toward higher-horsepower, higher-value machines. Mahindra is also pushing beyond tractors into farm machinery (harvesters, rotavators, implements) and into export markets, extending a dominant domestic franchise into adjacent, somewhat less weather-dependent revenue pools. If it succeeds, the farm business becomes both bigger and a little less cyclical over time.
05

The capital-allocation reset

The management change that quietly re-rated the entire stock.

For all its great products, Mahindra spent much of the 2010s diluting its returns with a sprawl of loss-making subsidiaries and overseas adventures. The single most important corporate event of the past five years wasn't a car launch — it was the arrival, under Anish Shah in 2021, of a hard capital-allocation discipline that fixed the returns and changed how the market valued the company. As Shah reframed the group's "RISE" ethos, it became as much about knowing when to walk away as about growth.

Why this is the heart of the investment story

Stock prices are driven by two things multiplied together: how much a company earns, and how much the market is willing to pay for each rupee of those earnings (the multiple). Mahindra's reset improved both at once. Cutting the loss-makers lifted reported profit and ROE directly — you stop bleeding money. And demonstrating that management would be ruthless with capital earned the company a higher multiple, because investors no longer feared the next overseas misadventure torching value. That combination — better earnings and a re-rating — is why Mahindra compounded so powerfully over 2021–2026. The forward question is whether the discipline holds now that the company is making bigger, bolder bets again (EVs, new plants, scaling the gems). Capital discipline is easiest to praise and hardest to sustain precisely when growth beckons — so the credibility of "18% or exit" is something to keep watching, not assume.

06

The Born-Electric bet

A funded, fast-starting EV push — and a subsidiary worth ₹80,000 crore on paper.

Mahindra's answer to the EV question is its "Born Electric" range — ground-up electric SUVs (the XEV 9e and BE 6, then the larger XEV 9S) launched in 2024–25 that, unlike many legacy-maker EVs, were designed as EVs from a clean sheet rather than converted from a petrol model. In their first full year they crossed ~65,000 dispatches to dealers, helping make Mahindra India's second-largest EV maker by volume almost overnight — and EV penetration of its own SUV sales jumped to 9.6% in Q4 FY26, with a target of 18–20% over the next few years.

Why this bet is structured cleverly — and where the risk lies

Two things make Mahindra's EV approach notable. First, it's self-funded: the cash-generative, near-debt-free auto-and-farm core can pay for the EV build-out without endangering the balance sheet — a luxury many EV hopefuls simply don't have. Second, by ring-fencing EVs in a separate, independently-valued subsidiary (with marquee global investors like Temasek and BII), Mahindra does three things at once: it raises outside capital, it shares the risk, and it creates a visible, separate value pool the market can price — potential sum-of-the-parts upside if EVs scale. The risk is the universal EV risk: the segment is fiercely competitive and not yet clearly profitable (recall the auto margin dipped as EV mix rose), battery economics and pricing are volatile, and ambitious volume targets are far from guaranteed. The EV leg is best read as real, well-started optionality — funded, ring-fenced, already selling — but it is an option, not yet a proven profit engine.

07

The listed group & the sum-of-the-parts

Why M&M is more than Auto + Farm — and why its consolidated debt can mislead.

Mahindra isn't just SUVs and tractors; it sits atop a federation of listed and unlisted businesses. These "growth gems" add real, sometimes under-appreciated value — but they also distort the consolidated accounts in ways that catch out first-time readers, especially on debt. Understanding the federation is the key to valuing the whole thing.

The debt trap to avoid: Mahindra's consolidated balance sheet shows ~₹1.34 lakh crore of borrowings — which looks alarming for a manufacturer. But the vast majority of that is the loan book of Mahindra Finance, an NBFC whose business is borrowing money in order to lend it out. The actual Auto & Farm manufacturing core is essentially net-cash. Reading group debt as if it were factory debt badly misjudges the company — always mentally separate the lending subsidiary's borrowings from the industrial core.
How to value the whole thing — sum-of-the-parts (SOTP)

Because of this layered structure, Mahindra is best valued sum-of-the-parts rather than on a single blended multiple. The pieces: (1) the core Auto & Farm business on an earnings multiple; plus (2) the market value of M&M's stakes in its listed entities — its ~35% of Tech Mahindra (~₹1.4 L Cr company), ~52% of Mahindra Finance (~₹39,000 Cr), ~51% of Mahindra Lifespaces, plus Logistics and Holidays; plus (3) the independently-valued EV subsidiary (MEAL, valued up to ₹80,580 Cr); minus (4) the industrial net debt, which is small. The capital-allocation reset (§05) was largely about making this federation earn its keep rather than dilute the core — and the listed-subsidiary stakes are a meaningful, sometimes overlooked, chunk of Mahindra's total value. The flip side is the standard "holding-company discount": markets typically pay less than the full SOTP for a conglomerate, on the view that the parent can't easily monetise the stakes and that complexity has a cost.

08

The industry and the rivals

Leader in both arenas — but facing the best of Indian and global manufacturing.

Mahindra competes in two distinct battlegrounds. In SUVs and passenger vehicles it faces the full force of India's car industry; in tractors it faces a smaller, more entrenched set of rivals. It leads both — but neither is uncontested, and the two leaderships are very different in quality.

The competitive reality: Mahindra's SUV leadership rests on a current hit-product cycle, and the passenger-car industry is unforgiving — Tata, Maruti, Hyundai, Kia and Toyota are all pouring fresh SUVs and EVs into the market, and a stale line-up can shed share quickly. In tractors, by contrast, the lead is more structural and durable, anchored in scale and rural distribution. The strategic test is doing three hard things at once: sustaining SUV desirability, winning the EV transition, and defending the tractor fortress. Few companies in the world are trying to lead all three races simultaneously.

Financials & valuation

Strong, broad-based growth — at a more reasonable multiple than most quality peers.

Mahindra's recent financials are the picture of a company firing on all cylinders: double-digit revenue growth, expanding (group) margins, rising returns, and an industrial balance sheet that's essentially net-cash. Notably, it trades at a lower multiple than many "quality" consumer compounders — a function of its cyclicality and conglomerate structure rather than any weakness in the franchises themselves.

The board declared a dividend of ₹33 per share for FY26 (up 30%), on EPS of ~₹152 — a modest payout ratio (~22%) that keeps most of the cash inside the business to fund SUV capacity and the EV ramp.

How to think about the valuation

At ~22× earnings, Mahindra trades at a discount to many "quality compounder" consumer names (a Titan or a Bajaj Finance) despite leading two markets and posting 20%+ ROE. Why the lower multiple? Two reasons. It's a cyclical manufacturer — autos and tractors both swing with the economy and the monsoon, so the market won't pay a steady-grower premium. And it's a conglomerate with a lending subsidiary, which usually attracts a holding-company discount and makes the accounts harder to read. The bull case is that the market under-rates three things: the durability of its two leaderships, the SOTP value of its listed stakes plus the EV subsidiary, and the staying power of the capital discipline — leaving room for further re-rating. The bear case is that a cyclical, capital-intensive auto/tractor maker simply shouldn't trade much higher, and that FY26's numbers are flattered by a double peak — a record tractor year stacked on a hot SUV cycle. The whole debate, in the end, is about how durable that dual peak proves to be.

Four stacked engines — and a disciplined hand on the capital.

Three drivers to watch

  • SUV launches, share & capacity — sustaining the hit-product cycle and bringing new plant capacity online to convert the order backlog into volume and defend the #2 PV position.
  • EV ramp & its profitability — hitting the ~18–20%-of-SUV electrification target without wrecking margins; the Born-Electric scale-up and the MEAL value crystallising.
  • Tractor cycle & farm machinery — riding the rural up-cycle while extending beyond tractors into implements, harvesters and exports to make the farm line bigger and a little less weather-dependent.
The next chapter is about doing three hard things at once — keep the SUV streak alive, win the EV transition, and defend the tractor fortress — all without losing the capital discipline that got it here.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine dual leader, re-rated on discipline — now priced for the cycle to hold.

Mahindra is a rare thing: a company that leads two large markets, has visibly fixed its capital discipline, and self-funds an ambitious EV bet from a net-cash core — with a federation of listed subsidiaries on top. The investment debate is therefore less about whether the franchises are real (they plainly are) and more about cyclicality and whether the current peak can be sustained.

The bull case

  • Dual leadership — #1 SUV revenue share and #1 tractor share; two engines on different cycles smoothing group cash flows.
  • Capital discipline — the "18% ROE or exit" reset lifted returns to ~20–21% and earned a structural re-rating that may not be done.
  • SUV mega-trend — a pure SUV specialist riding India's decisive, structural shift toward utility vehicles, winning on desirability not price.
  • Funded EV optionality — Born-Electric scaling fast (already #2 in EVs), ring-fenced in an ~₹80,580 Cr subsidiary with Temasek/BII, self-financed.
  • SOTP upside — a net-cash industrial core plus valuable listed stakes (Tech Mahindra ~₹1.4 L Cr, Mahindra Finance, Lifespaces) the market may under-credit.

The bear case

  • Double-peak cyclicality — a record tractor year stacked on a hot SUV cycle; FY26 may flatter near the top of both.
  • SUV competition — leadership rests on a current hit cycle; Tata, Maruti, Hyundai, Kia and Toyota are all attacking with new SUVs and EVs.
  • EV economics — fiercely competitive and not yet clearly profitable; the auto margin already dipped as EV mix rose, and volume targets are ambitious.
  • Discipline drift — bigger, bolder bets (EVs, new plants, scaling gems) could test the capital discipline that drove the re-rating.
  • Conglomerate complexity — many subsidiaries and an NBFC make the accounts hard to read and justify a persistent holding-company discount.
The bottom line

Mahindra & Mahindra is a genuinely strong, dual-market leader transformed by capital discipline into a higher-return, better-valued company — with a net-cash core funding a credible EV bet and a federation of listed gems on top. The business quality is not really in doubt; the open questions are cyclical and competitive. The story over the next few years turns on three numbers — SUV market share (does the hit streak last as rivals pile in?), the EV ramp and its profitability (does Born Electric scale and eventually pay?), and the tractor cycle (how durable is the rural up-cycle?). Keep the discipline and win those, and a cyclical maker keeps compounding; lose the streak or the discipline, and a peak-cycle multiple has plenty of room to compress.
