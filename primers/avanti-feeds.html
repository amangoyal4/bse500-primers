Avanti Feeds, the company that feeds India's shrimp

A complete walk through India's largest shrimp-feed maker and a major shrimp exporter — how farmed shrimp actually gets grown, why selling the feed that fills nearly every pond is the steadier, higher-share business, and why the frozen-shrimp export arm bolted onto it swings with US duties, global shrimp prices, disease and the dollar.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Avanti Feeds makes the feed that farmed shrimp eat — the single biggest cost a shrimp farmer carries — and it is by a distance India's largest shrimp-feed manufacturer, with roughly 45–50% of the domestic market. That feed business is the steady, dominant, cash-generating engine. Bolted onto it is a second, very different business: a shrimp processing-and-export arm (Avanti Frozen Foods) that buys raw shrimp, freezes and value-adds it, and ships it mainly to the United States and Europe. One leg is a high-share domestic staple; the other is a cyclical, duty-exposed, dollar-earning exporter. Understanding Avanti means keeping those two engines separate in your head.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Feed is the moat; export is the option. Feed contributes roughly four-fifths of revenue and the bulk of steady profit, sold to thousands of Indian farmers who need it every crop cycle regardless of who ultimately profits from the pond. Processing and export is roughly the remaining fifth — lumpier, thinner-margin, and hostage to forces entirely outside India.
  • The export leg lives under a US duty cloud. The USA takes the majority of Avanti's exported shrimp, and Indian shrimp entering the US now faces a stacked burden — long-standing anti-dumping and countervailing duties plus the 2025 reciprocal tariffs — that at its August-2025 peak implied an effective duty well above 50%. Every twist in that trade file moves the export half of this company.
  • It is a fortress balance sheet. Avanti is essentially debt-free (borrowings ~₹13 Cr) and sits on a large treasury of cash and investments, with ROCE around 24%. FY26 revenue rose ~8% to ~₹6,066 Cr and net profit rose to ~₹657 Cr. The stock trades near 19–22× earnings — not cheap for an aquaculture name, not demanding for a category leader with net cash.
In plain terms

During a gold rush, the person who reliably makes money is not the prospector but the one selling shovels to every prospector on the field. Avanti sells the shovels of Indian shrimp farming — the feed that goes into most of the country's ponds. It does not need any particular farmer to strike it rich; it needs farmers, collectively, to keep stocking ponds and buying feed. On top of that steady toll sits a second business — freezing and exporting shrimp to America — which is where the excitement, and the danger, live: US duties, global shrimp prices, disease outbreaks and the rupee-dollar rate can make that arm swing hard from year to year. The feed pays the bills; the export decides whether it was a good year or a great one.

01

Shrimp farming 101: how a pond makes money

No aquaculture background needed — just how the crop works and where the money leaks.

Farmed shrimp is grown much like a crop, but in water. A farmer floods a coastal pond, buys tiny hatchery-bred shrimp larvae (called seed or post-larvae), stocks them, and then feeds them for roughly 100–150 days until they reach market size and are harvested. India's farmed shrimp is almost entirely one fast-growing species — Litopenaeus vannamei, the Pacific white shrimp — which the country adopted around 2009 and which turned India into one of the world's biggest shrimp exporters. The economics of that pond are where Avanti's whole business is rooted.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
VannameiThe Pacific white shrimp — the fast-growing, disease-tolerant species that dominates Indian farming. Its 2009 arrival created the modern industry Avanti serves.
Seed / post-larvaeBaby shrimp bred in a hatchery and sold to farmers to stock a pond. Avanti runs its own hatchery, but this is a small line.
Feed / FCRThe pelleted feed shrimp eat, and the feed conversion ratio — kilograms of feed per kilogram of shrimp grown. Feed is the farmer's single largest cost, often 50%+ of the cost of raising a crop. This is Avanti's core product.
Stocking / crop cycleHow many ponds get filled, and how often. More stocking = more feed sold. Weak farm-gate prices or disease fear make farmers stock less — the demand lever for feed.
Farm-gate priceWhat the farmer is paid per kg of harvested shrimp. Set by global export demand. When it falls, farmers stock fewer ponds — which eventually feeds back into feed volumes.
The mechanism that decides feed demand: the farmer's confidence

A shrimp farmer only fills a pond and buys feed if they expect the harvest to sell for more than the crop cost. That expectation is driven by the farm-gate price of shrimp, which is in turn set thousands of miles away by US and EU demand, competing supply from Ecuador, and freight and duty costs. So even Avanti's steady feed business is indirectly tied to the same export cycle that whipsaws its processing arm — just with a lag and a cushion. When farm-gate prices are healthy, ponds get stocked, feed flows, and Avanti's core engine hums. When a price crash scares farmers off stocking, feed volumes soften. The feed business is steadier than export, but it is not immune to the global shrimp cycle; it is one step removed from it.

The chain, from hatchery to America's dinner plate

  • Hatchery breeds the seed → farmer stocks the pond and buys feed → feeds the crop for months → harvest → sold to a processor → frozen, value-added and exported. Avanti earns at the feed step for almost every pond, and again at the processing step for the shrimp it buys and ships.
02

Why feed is the picks-and-shovels play

The heart of the thesis — and the reason feed, not export, is the durable business.

Here is the structural insight that defines Avanti. Farming shrimp is risky and cyclical: a farmer can lose a whole crop to disease, get crushed by a price crash, or make a fortune in a good year. But whichever way the pond turns out, the farmer had to buy the feed first. Avanti sits at that unavoidable step. It is not betting on any single farmer, region, or crop — it is selling into the entire industry, taking a toll on aggregate shrimp production rather than on any one pond's profit.

Two forces make feed the durable engine. First, share: at roughly 45–50% of the Indian market, Avanti is the default supplier — a scale, distribution and trust advantage a new entrant cannot easily buy. Second, the nature of feed demand: it recurs every crop cycle, it is a consumable (not a one-time capital purchase), and it grows structurally as Indian shrimp output grows. The margin on feed is thin per kilo, but the volume is enormous and the customer keeps coming back.

The device that only fits Avanti: sell to the field, not the winner

Most consumer or agri companies you can size by asking "will their product win?" Avanti flips that. Its feed doesn't need to back the winning pond — it goes into most of the ponds, so it profits from Indian shrimp production in aggregate, not from any farmer's success. A disease outbreak that ruins one region's crop still consumed Avanti's feed on the way in; a boom that mints another region's farmers buys even more of it. The thesis-settling number for the core business is therefore not any single price or margin but feed volume (in MT) multiplied by feed margin per kg — how much shrimp India is growing, times how much Avanti keeps on each kilo of feed. 03

Two engines under one roof: feed vs export

Same company, opposite economics — the single most important distinction to hold.

Avanti reports as one company, but it runs two businesses whose economics point in different directions. Read this section once and the earnings pattern — steady feed profits jolted around by export swings — stops being confusing.

The two engines compared

Notice how the group's thin consolidated operating margin (~12%) makes sense once split: feed is a high-turnover, low-margin staple, and processing is a pass-through-heavy exporter where much of the cost is the raw shrimp itself. Avanti is not a fat-margin business — it is a high-return-on-capital, low-margin, cash-light-inventory business. It earns strong ROCE (~24%) not by charging a lot per kilo but by turning capital quickly and carrying almost no debt.

Why the two-engine design is deliberate, not accidental

The feed business and the export business are two sides of the same shrimp. When export demand is strong, farm-gate prices are healthy, farmers stock aggressively, and both feed volumes and export throughput rise together. When the US market sours — a duty hike, a price crash — export margins compress and, with a lag, farmers pull back on stocking, softening feed too. So the two engines are correlated on the way down, which is the uncomfortable part: in a genuinely bad shrimp year, both legs can weaken at once. The saving grace is that feed is far less sensitive and recovers faster, because a farmer skips a crop long before they exit farming entirely. Feed is the shock absorber; export is the shock.

04

The company, the family, and the Thai Union partnership

An Andhra promoter family, a Thai seafood giant, and a technology bridge that built the business.

Avanti Feeds is a Hyderabad-headquartered company promoted and run by the Alluri family — founder-chairman Alluri Indra Kumar has led it for decades. But the fact that makes Avanti unusual is its long partnership with Thai Union Group, the Thailand-based global seafood major (owner of brands like John West and Chicken of the Sea). Thai Union has been Avanti's technical partner and an equity holder for many years, and it owns 40% of the processing subsidiary, Avanti Frozen Foods. That relationship is not cosmetic — it is how the feed technology and the export-market access were built.

  • 2016–17

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Alluri family and associated entities hold roughly 43% of Avanti Feeds. Thai Union is a long-standing equity holder in the parent as well; the exact current stake percentage in the listed company is held here as sources differ, but Thai Union's clearly disclosed holding is the 40% of Avanti Frozen Foods, the export subsidiary.

A genuine surprise: one company feeds roughly half of India's farmed shrimp — and a Thai seafood conglomerate owns a slice of it. Stranger still, the direction of dependence has started to reverse: in 2026 Avanti bought a 10% stake in Thai Union's own feed mill in Ecuador. The Indian pupil that learned feed technology from Thai Union in the 1990s is now co-investing alongside its teacher in the world's largest shrimp-farming country.
05

The export leg and why it lurches

The volatile engine — where US trade policy, global prices, disease and the dollar collide.

The processing-and-export arm is the part of Avanti that makes headlines and moves the stock in bursts. It is a smaller slice of revenue, but it carries almost all of the drama, because it is exposed to four forces Avanti cannot control — and the biggest of them sits in Washington.

1 · The US duty stack

The United States is the destination for the majority of Avanti's exported shrimp — and Indian shrimp entering the US now faces a stacked set of charges rather than one. There is a long-standing anti-dumping duty and a countervailing duty (a levy against alleged subsidies), and on top of those the 2025 US reciprocal tariffs were layered on. At the August-2025 peak, industry estimates put the combined effective burden on Indian shrimp above 50%.

A duty this large does not simply reduce Avanti's export margin — it reshapes the whole trade. It makes Indian shrimp costlier than Ecuadorian shrimp in the US, shifts negotiating power to American buyers, and pressures the farm-gate price Indian farmers receive, which loops back into feed demand. The single most important variable for the export leg over FY26–27 is which way this duty file moves.

2 · The global shrimp price cycle

Shrimp is a globally-traded commodity, and its price swings with supply — above all from Ecuador, which has scaled production enormously and can flood the world market with cheap shrimp. When global supply is heavy, farm-gate prices fall, exporters' realizations shrink, and Indian farmers stock fewer ponds. Avanti's export arm is a price-taker in this global market; it cannot set the price of a kilo of frozen shrimp any more than a wheat farmer sets the price of grain.

3 · Disease

Shrimp farming is periodically hit by disease outbreaks that can wipe out a crop across a whole region — historically white-spot syndrome virus, and more recently EHP (a microsporidian parasite that stunts growth). A bad disease year cuts harvest volumes, hurts farmers, and reduces the raw shrimp available to processors — a genuine tail risk to both engines at once.

4 · The dollar

Export revenue is earned in US dollars and costs are largely in rupees, so the rupee-dollar rate is a direct swing factor on export profitability. A weaker rupee helps; a stronger rupee hurts — a variable entirely outside Avanti's control.

Why the export arm is best read as an option, not a base

Value the feed business on its steady volume and share, and treat the export arm as variable upside layered on top. In a benign year — reasonable US duties, firm shrimp prices, a soft rupee — processing adds a meaningful kicker to profits. In a hostile year — a duty spike, an Ecuador-led price crash, a disease scare — it can go from contributor to drag. The export leg's earnings are real but episodic; anchoring a valuation to a good export year is how investors get hurt in this stock. Size the company on feed; let export surprise you.

06

The raw-material cost cycle

What goes into the feed pellet — and why input prices are the swing factor on feed margin.

Feed is a low-margin, high-volume business, which means its profitability is unusually sensitive to the cost of what goes into the pellet. The main ingredients are fishmeal (protein, the priciest input), soybean meal, wheat flour, and vitamin/mineral premixes. When these rise, Avanti's feed margin gets squeezed — because it cannot always pass the full increase on to price-sensitive farmers.

Fishmeal and soybean meal are the swing costs — both are globally-traded commodities. A spike in either (as in 2020–22) compresses feed margin until Avanti can reprice, which it does only carefully because farmers are cost-sensitive.

The pricing dynamic is the crux. Avanti has scale-buying power on inputs and enough share to lead pricing, but it competes for the loyalty of farmers who watch every rupee of crop cost. So it tends to absorb some input inflation before repricing, protecting volume and market share at the expense of near-term margin — and to pass through price cuts when inputs ease, as it did with a ₹4/kg reduction from April 2025. That is why feed margin, not feed volume, is the wobblier of the two core numbers: volume grows with the industry, but the margin per kilo breathes in and out with fishmeal and soya.

The counter-intuitive bit: a feed price cut is not necessarily bad news for Avanti. Lowering feed price when inputs allow keeps farmers stocking and defends the ~45–50% share against rivals — it buys volume and loyalty. The company is often willing to trade a little margin per kilo to keep the whole field buying its shovels. In a share-led staple business, defending the base can matter more than squeezing each unit.

The uncomfortable correlation to keep in view

The reassuring story is that feed diversifies away export risk. That is only half true. In a mild wobble — a duty tweak, a soft quarter — feed genuinely cushions the blow. But in a severe shrimp downturn, the risks converge: a US duty shock crushes export margins and pushes farm-gate prices down enough that Indian farmers stock fewer ponds, softening feed volumes too. The two engines are positively correlated in the tail, not independent. What protects Avanti in that scenario is not diversification but its balance sheet — no debt, a large cash treasury, and market leadership let it out-wait a bad cycle while weaker rivals struggle. Resilience here comes from the fortress, not from the mix.

08

The cash treasury

The quiet superpower — a debt-free balance sheet with a large investment pile.

In a cyclical, externally-buffeted business, the balance sheet is the difference between riding out a bad cycle and being forced to sell into it. Avanti's is a genuine fortress: essentially no debt (borrowings ~₹13 Cr against reserves of ~₹3,286 Cr) and a substantial treasury of cash and investments built up over years of cash generation.

Why net cash is the real moat in a cyclical trade

Two things flow from the treasury. First, staying power: when a duty shock or price crash hits, Avanti can keep buying inputs, hold prices to defend farmer loyalty, and out-last thinly-capitalised competitors — turning a downturn into share gains. Second, optionality: the cash funds new lines (the pet-food push, the Ecuador feed-mill stake) and a generous dividend without touching debt. The flip side, which value-conscious investors do flag, is that a large idle cash pile earning treasury returns can drag on return on equity — capital sitting in mutual funds rather than compounding in the business. It is a fortress that is arguably slightly over-provisioned; but in this trade, over-provisioned beats under-capitalised every time.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

In feed, Avanti's edge compounds: leading share funds the widest distribution and the best input-buying scale, which lets it price keenly, which defends the share — a self-reinforcing loop that a #2 struggles to break. In export, there is no such moat; Avanti is a capable, well-run processor but ultimately a price-taker against Ecuador and its own Indian peers. So the durable competitive advantage lives entirely on the feed side. When you hear Avanti called a "quality franchise", that quality is the feed leadership and the balance sheet — not the frozen-shrimp business, which is a competent commodity operation riding the same waves as everyone else.

The one number that settles the thesis: feed volume × feed margin, read against export

At ~19–22× earnings the market is paying up for the feed franchise and the fortress balance sheet, not for the export leg. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the core engine's earnings power — feed volume (MT) multiplied by feed margin per kg — read alongside three cross-checks: market share (is the ~45–50% holding as CP and others push?), export realizations and the US duty file (is the volatile leg a kicker or a drag this year?), and raw-material costs (fishmeal/soya, which set the feed margin). If feed volume keeps climbing with Indian shrimp output while Avanti defends share and margin, the steady engine justifies the multiple on its own, and any benign export year is upside. If a duty shock or price crash both compresses export and scares farmers off stocking, feed volume stalls just as export sinks — and a full multiple on depressed earnings is where the pain comes from. Feed volume × feed margin is the scoreboard; export is the weather.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A dominant feed franchise with a volatile export bolt-on — asked to grow the core while the trade file churns.

Avanti enters FY27 as it has entered most years: the unquestioned leader of Indian shrimp feed, sitting on a fortress balance sheet, with a capable but externally-buffeted export arm attached. The real questions are whether it can keep growing feed volume and defending share as CP pushes, whether it can add new legs (pet food, overseas feed) that matter, and which way the US duty file breaks.

The bull case

  • Dominant feed franchise — ~45–50% share of a structurally growing market; a self-reinforcing scale-and-distribution moat that a #2 can't easily crack.
  • Picks-and-shovels exposure — earns on aggregate Indian shrimp output every crop cycle, not on any single pond's success.
  • Fortress balance sheet — debt-free with a large cash treasury; can out-wait any downturn and take share from weaker rivals.
  • Thai Union partnership — feed technology and export-market access from a global seafood major; now co-investing abroad (Ecuador).
  • Export optionality — a benign US duty year plus firm shrimp prices turns the processing arm into a real profit kicker.

The bear case

  • US duty / tariff shock — a stacked duty above 50% at its 2025 peak can gut export margins and pull down farm-gate prices, hitting both engines.
  • Correlated downside — in a severe shrimp downturn, export and feed weaken together; the diversification is thinner than it looks.
  • Thin, input-driven margins — a fishmeal/soya spike can halve profit (as in FY22) with little pricing power over cost-sensitive farmers.
  • Disease & price cycle — EHP/white-spot outbreaks and Ecuador-led oversupply are recurring tail risks outside Avanti's control.
  • Full valuation for a cyclical — ~19–22× earnings leaves the stock exposed if a bad shrimp year compresses both earnings and the multiple; idle cash also drags on ROE.
The bottom line

Avanti is really two companies stapled together: a dominant, boring, cash-machine feed business that profits from nearly every shrimp pond in India, and a competent-but-commodity export arm whose fortunes are written in Washington, Guayaquil and the fishmeal market. The durable value is the first; the volatility is the second. The company's edge in a rough cycle is not its business mix but its balance sheet — no debt, a deep cash reserve, and the market leadership to gain share while weaker players bleed. The next few years turn on two checkable things: whether feed volume keeps climbing toward and past FY26's 5,62,060 MT while share holds near half the market, and which way the US shrimp-duty file settles for FY27. Watch the feed-volume print each year and the tariff headlines each quarter — between them, they tell you whether Avanti had a good year or merely a steady one.
