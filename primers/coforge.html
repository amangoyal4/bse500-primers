The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Coforge is a mid-cap Indian IT-services company — it writes software, runs technology systems and, increasingly, builds AI capabilities for big global corporations. Unlike the giants (TCS, Infosys) that sell to everyone, Coforge is a focused specialist: it goes deep in three industries — Travel/Transportation/Hospitality, Banking & Financial Services, and Insurance — rather than wide across many. Formerly NIIT Technologies, it rebranded to Coforge in 2020.

Four facts frame everything:

  • The industry's growth leader. FY26 (year ended March 2026) revenue was $1,870 Mn (₹16,403 Cr), up a striking 29.2% in USD terms and 35.9% in rupees — several times faster than the large-cap Indian IT peers, and among the fastest organic-plus-inorganic growth in the sector.
  • It has literally no promoter. After private-equity owner Baring (BPEA EQT) exited completely in 2023, Coforge became one of the very few large Indian IT names with 0% promoter holding — it is entirely owned by institutions and the public. That shapes its governance and its unusually bold appetite for acquisitions.
  • It bought its way bigger — on purpose. The Cigniti acquisition (AI-led software testing / quality engineering) and a string of tuck-ins turbo-charged growth, but added debt and temporarily pulled reported margins down. FY26 EBIT margin was 14.4% — optically below peers, but rising fast (+370bps YoY) as the deals integrate.
  • A record order book underwrites it. A landmark $1.56bn, 13-year Sabre deal in the travel vertical, $2,262 Mn of FY26 order intake, and a $1.75bn 12-month executable order book give unusual visibility. The market pays up: ~₹62,950 Cr market cap, a ~38× P/E, and a small dividend.
In plain terms

Think of Coforge as a surgical specialist in a market of general physicians. Where the IT giants are broad and slow-growing, Coforge picks three industries it knows intimately — airlines and travel systems, banks, and insurers — and becomes the go-to technology partner inside them. It grows faster than anyone else in the sector by combining that domain depth with aggressive dealmaking: winning enormous long-term contracts and buying companies to add capability. The catch is that fast growth and integration cost money up front, so its profit margins look lower than a TCS even though the business is compounding faster. The whole investment question is whether that growth converts into the fat margins later — and whether a company with no controlling owner keeps making disciplined bets.

01

IT-services economics 101: how the money actually works

No coding required — just the handful of levers that drive an IT-services P&L.

An IT-services company is, at heart, a people business that rents brains by the hour. It hires engineers, bills them out to client projects, and earns the gap between what a client pays and what the engineer costs. Almost everything about the economics flows from a few simple levers.

The only levers you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters)
HeadcountThe number of billable engineers — the raw capacity to do work. Coforge ended FY26 with ~35,777 people. More people can mean more revenue, but only if they're kept busy.
UtilisationWhat share of employees are actually billing a client vs sitting "on the bench". High utilisation = more revenue on the same payroll. It's the IT equivalent of a hospital's occupancy.
Realisation / billing rateThe price per hour of an engineer's time. Higher-value work (AI, consulting, complex systems) commands more than commodity coding.
AttritionHow fast staff quit. High attrition means constant re-hiring and re-training, which is expensive and disruptive. Coforge's FY26 attrition was a healthy ~10.8% (last-twelve-months) — low by industry standards.
Order book / TCVSigned-but-not-yet-delivered future work (Total Contract Value). It's the pipeline that tells you how much revenue is already booked. The bigger and longer, the more visibility.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Revenue ≈ Billable people × Utilisation × Billing rate, and Margin ≈ that revenue minus employee cost. Because staff salaries are the dominant cost (typically ~60% of revenue), the game is to (a) keep people billing, (b) push them onto higher-value work, and (c) resist wage inflation and rupee swings. When Coforge signs a huge multi-year deal, it must hire ahead of the revenue — which is why a growth phase temporarily depresses margins before the leverage kicks in. Understand this front-loading and Coforge's "low margin, high growth" profile immediately makes sense.

Why "constant currency" (CC) matters

Coforge is paid mostly in dollars, pounds and euros but reports in rupees. When the rupee weakens, reported INR growth looks bigger than the true underlying growth. So analysts watch constant-currency (CC) growth — the "real" volume growth stripped of exchange-rate noise. In FY26 Coforge grew 29.2% in USD terms while INR growth was 35.9%; the gap is the currency effect. When you compare it to peers, always compare CC or USD numbers.

Why deep beats wide for a mid-cap

  • Domain becomes a moat. Knowing exactly how an airline's reservation system or an insurer's claims engine works is worth far more than generic coding skill. Clients pay a premium for a partner who already speaks their language, and rivals can't cheaply replicate two decades of vertical know-how.
  • Reference selling. Win a marquee airline and every other airline listens. Deep credentials in one client become a sales weapon across the whole industry — a compounding advantage a generalist lacks.
  • Focused R&D and IP. Coforge builds reusable platforms and accelerators for its chosen verticals (travel, insurance, banking), which raise value-add and margins over time.
  • The trade-off: concentration. Depth cuts both ways. If travel demand collapses (as in COVID) or a big banking client cuts spend, a focused player feels it harder than a diversified giant. Coforge's answer is to spread across three unrelated verticals so they don't all wobble together.
The mid-cap sweet spot

Coforge sits in a deliberate niche: big enough to win large, complex deals that a tiny firm can't service, yet small and hungry enough to grow fast and to matter to a client the way a $300bn giant never could. A $1.56bn contract is transformational for a ~$1.9bn-revenue company but a rounding error for TCS — so Coforge can (and must) fight harder for exactly the deals that move its needle. This is why its growth rate structurally outpaces the majors, and why the whole thesis rests on it continuing to win outsized deals relative to its size.

03

The Travel signature — the vertical few rivals can match

A bespoke strength: Coforge's decades-deep roots in airline and travel technology.

If you want to understand what makes Coforge different from every other Indian IT firm, start with Travel. Most peers are strongest in banking and technology clients; Coforge is one of the very few with a genuine, deep, hard-to-copy franchise in the airline and travel-technology world — reservation systems, passenger service systems (PSS), global distribution systems (GDS), airline operations and loyalty. This is its calling card.

The jargon, decoded

  • PSS (Passenger Service System) — the software that manages your booking, ticket, check-in and boarding. The beating heart of an airline's IT.
  • GDS (Global Distribution System) — the giant middleman networks (Sabre, Amadeus, Travelport) that connect airlines, hotels and travel agents so a fare shows up when you search. Coforge builds and runs technology for these platforms.
  • Why it's sticky. These systems are mission-critical, safety-relevant and eye-wateringly complex. Once a partner is embedded, switching is risky and expensive — so relationships run for years and expand deal by deal.

Why the Travel edge is genuinely rare

Airline IT is a small, specialised club. Very few firms have the credentials to run a carrier's core reservation platform or to build for a GDS like Sabre — it takes years of proven, incident-free delivery on systems where an outage grounds planes. Coforge inherited this strength from its NIIT Technologies days and has compounded it into a defensible position. It's why the $1.56bn Sabre partnership — the largest deal in Coforge's history — landed with Coforge rather than a bigger rival: not because Coforge is cheapest, but because it is one of the few that could credibly do the work. This is domain-depth-as-moat in its purest form.

04

From NIIT to no-promoter: the private-equity chapter

The most unusual thing about Coforge's ownership — and why it matters.

Almost every large Indian IT company has a founding family or a controlling group behind it. Coforge is the striking exception: it has no promoter at all. Understanding how it got there — via a private-equity buyout and a clean exit — explains its governance, its dealmaking appetite, and a real question about accountability.

Timeline

  • 1992
    Incorporated as NIIT Technologies, the software-services arm of the NIIT training group (founders Rajendra Pawar & Vijay Thadani).
  • 2019
    Baring Private Equity Asia (BPEA) buys the NIIT promoter stake (~30.6%) and, via an open offer, builds a controlling ~70% position — turning a family-linked company into a PE-owned one.
  • 2020
    Rebranded from NIIT Technologies to Coforge — a clean break signalling the new, focused digital-services identity.
  • 2020–23
    Baring sells down its stake in tranches through the market (block deals), professionalising the board and sharpening the growth strategy along the way.
  • Aug 2023
    Baring exits completely. Coforge becomes a company with 0% promoter holding — fully owned by institutions and public shareholders.
  • FY25–26
    The Cigniti acquisition and the $1.56bn Sabre deal drive sector-leading growth; revenue crosses $1.87bn, on track toward a $2bn+ firm.

What "no promoter" actually means

  • Fully institutional/public float. As of mid-2026, the register is dominated by domestic institutions (~43%), foreign institutions (~25%) and the public (~33%) — with zero promoter. Effectively a widely-held, "ownerless" company in the Western sense.
  • Governance cuts both ways. On the plus side: professional, board-driven management with no dominant family agenda, and a cap table that's a clean takeover-friendly structure. On the minus side: no anchor shareholder with deep skin in the game to guard against empire-building or short-termism — accountability rests entirely on the board and institutional investors.
  • An acquirer's — and a target's — profile. No controlling block makes Coforge both an unusually willing acquirer (it can raise equity and issue stock for deals without a promoter's dilution worries) and, theoretically, an acquirable company — a rarity among Indian IT majors.
The governance question to keep in view: a promoter-less company living on aggressive M&A is a distinctive combination. It has powered Coforge's growth, but it puts a premium on board discipline and capital allocation. Watch for signs of value-accretive vs value-destructive dealmaking — with no family fortune on the line, the checks are institutional, not personal.
05

The inorganic engine: buying capability, adding debt

How acquisitions — Cigniti above all — reshaped the company and its margins.

Coforge's growth is not just organic. A defining part of its strategy is inorganic growth — acquiring companies to add capability, clients and scale faster than it could build alone. The headline example is Cigniti Technologies, an AI-led software-testing and "quality-engineering" specialist, whose absorption is central to Coforge's leap toward a $2bn+ business — and to why reported margins dipped before recovering.

What Cigniti brought

  • Quality engineering / software testing. Before software ships, it must be tested for bugs, security holes and performance. Cigniti is a leader in this "digital assurance" niche, increasingly using AI to automate testing — a capability Coforge can now sell across its own client base.
  • New clients & geographies. Cigniti added tenured relationships, deepened Healthcare exposure, and expanded Coforge's US Midwest/West footprint — complementary rather than overlapping.
  • Cross-sell proof. Management has pointed to the integration working: the acquired business's EBITDA margin reportedly climbed from ~11% to ~19% within six quarters, and the top two Cigniti clients scaled from ~$25m to ~$75m of combined annual revenue as Coforge cross-sold — a textbook example of "buy capability, then grow it".
Why a good acquisition still dents margins first

Buying a company does three things to the P&L that make near-term margins look worse even when the deal is smart. (1) Debt & interest. Coforge funded acquisitions partly with borrowing, so interest costs rose and the debt-free profile shifted. (2) Lower-margin mix. An acquired business often starts at a lower margin than the parent, dragging the blended average down until it's optimised. (3) Integration & amortisation. One-off integration costs and the accounting amortisation of "intangibles" (client relationships, brand) both hit reported profit. The bet is that all three fade — debt gets repaid, the acquired margin rises, integration ends — leaving a bigger, more capable company at a higher margin. FY26's 370bps EBIT-margin jump to 14.4% is exactly this playing out; management guides consolidated FY27 EBITDA to 20.5–21%, signalling further recovery.

The Sabre deal, explained

In FY26 Coforge signed a 13-year, ~$1.56bn strategic partnership with Sabre, the US travel-technology giant behind one of the world's biggest GDS/airline platforms. Structurally it began as a product-development relationship and matured into a long-horizon innovation partnership centred on AI in travel. Early on it is expected to add roughly $140–150m of annual revenue — material for a ~$1.9bn company — and it cements Coforge's travel-vertical leadership. For a mid-cap, landing a contract of this size is transformational: it front-loads years of revenue visibility and validates the "depth-over-breadth" strategy.

Why a big order book is worth a premium

Revenue you've already signed is worth more than revenue you hope to win, because it removes guesswork. A $1.75bn 12-month executable book means most of next year's growth is effectively pre-committed — the company just has to deliver. This is precisely why the market tolerates Coforge's optically-lower margins and pays a rich multiple: the growth isn't a forecast, it's a backlog. The flip side is concentration risk — a mega-deal like Sabre means one client and one vertical carry outsized weight, so a re-scoping, delay or dispute on a flagship contract would hurt more than a diversified book would.

The number to watch each quarter: order intake (new signings) and the executable book together tell you whether the growth engine is still firing. As long as intake keeps replacing delivered revenue and the executable book grows, the sector-leading topline is well-supported. A stall in large-deal wins would be the first crack in the thesis.

The AI question: threat, tool, and sales pitch all at once

The defining industry force — and Coforge is positioning to be on the right side of it.

For every IT-services company, generative AI is the strategic question — because the industry's core product is human effort billed by the hour, and AI can automate exactly that. If AI lets clients do more with fewer engineers, the naïve fear is that services revenue shrinks. Coforge's answer, like the sector's, is to make AI a growth lever rather than a threat — using it to win work, deliver faster, and lift its own margins.

Coforge's three AI angles

  • Quasar — its own GenAI platform. Coforge built Quasar, an enterprise AI platform with 100+ APIs and an agentic framework (document AI, graph AI, speech/vision), designed to let clients build and deploy AI applications at scale. Owning a platform lets Coforge sell AI outcomes, not just AI hours.
  • Hyperscaler & chip partnerships. It partners with the big cloud and AI infrastructure providers (and supports NVIDIA microservices/models within Quasar), positioning as an implementation partner for enterprises rolling out AI — a large, growing pool of work.
  • AI in its own delivery. Crucially, Coforge uses AI internally to lower its own delivery cost — automating testing (via Cigniti), code and operations — which is a big part of why management can guide margins up even while growing fast. AI adoption in delivery is explicitly cited as a margin driver.
The bull and bear of AI, in one frame

Bull: AI is a once-in-a-generation demand wave. Enterprises need help building, integrating and governing AI systems, and a domain-deep specialist that also owns a platform (Quasar) and a testing engine (Cigniti) is well placed to capture that spend. Meanwhile AI cuts Coforge's own costs, so margins rise. Bear: AI could compress billing rates and shrink the number of engineers a project needs, deflating the whole services model — and a mid-cap has less R&D firepower than the giants to stay ahead. The honest reality is that both are true at once: AI simultaneously expands the pie (new work) and erodes the old model (fewer hours per task). Coforge's growth-and-margin trajectory in FY26–27 is, in effect, a live test of whether the expansion is outrunning the erosion. So far, it is.

The paradox of AI for IT services: the same technology that could shrink billable hours is also the biggest new source of billable work — and the tool that lifts margins. The winners are those who ride all three at once.

The growth-versus-margin trade-off

Why the fastest-growing name in the sector has margins that look lower — on purpose.

Here is the apparent contradiction at the heart of Coforge: it grows faster than any large peer, yet its reported operating margin (14.4% EBIT in FY26) sits below the 20%+ that a TCS or Infosys earns. This isn't a weakness the company is hiding — it's a deliberate choice, and understanding the mechanism is the key to reading the numbers.

Three reasons the margin looks lower

  • Hiring ahead of revenue. To staff mega-deals like Sabre, Coforge recruits and trains people before they bill — a cost drag that reverses once the deal ramps to full run-rate.
  • Acquisition dilution. Cigniti and other deals started at lower margins and carried integration costs and debt interest, pulling the blended margin down until optimised (see Part 05).
  • Deal-win investment. Winning outsized contracts relative to its size means upfront transition, ramp and sometimes discounted early-phase pricing — front-loaded cost for back-loaded profit.
Why this can be the smarter play

A mid-cap that maximised margin today would have to stop growing fast — declining risky deals, avoiding acquisitions, under-investing in AI. Coforge is doing the opposite: sacrificing a few points of near-term margin to compound revenue at multiples of the peer rate, on the logic that a much bigger business at a recovered margin is worth far more than a smaller one at a fat margin now. The evidence that it's working: FY26 EBIT margin rose 370bps even during the growth push, and management guides FY27 consolidated EBITDA to 20.5–21% — i.e., the margin is expected to climb back toward peer levels without giving up the growth. If that convergence happens, Coforge gets the best of both worlds; if it doesn't, the "low margin" becomes a genuine problem rather than a temporary phase.

The industry and the rivals

Where a focused mid-cap sits between the giants and the boutiques.

Indian IT services is a ~$250bn export industry built on the offshore-delivery advantage: skilled engineers at a fraction of Western cost. It splits into tiers — the mega-caps that do everything, the focused mid-caps that specialise, and the boutiques. Coforge is a leader of the focused mid-cap cohort, and its rivals differ by tier.

The structural backdrop that matters

Three forces shape the whole industry and therefore Coforge. (1) Global tech-spend cycles. Most revenue comes from US and European clients, so a Western slowdown, higher-for-longer interest rates, or a recession directly hits discretionary IT budgets — a demand risk no Indian IT firm escapes. (2) The rupee. A weaker rupee flatters reported margins and revenue (costs are in rupees, billing in dollars); a strengthening rupee squeezes them. (3) Visa & onshore-cost pressure. US immigration/visa policy and rising onshore delivery requirements raise costs and are a perennial regulatory wildcard. Coforge's mid-cap agility helps it navigate cycles, but it cannot opt out of them.

The client-concentration and geography angle: Coforge earns the bulk of its revenue from the Americas and EMEA, and its largest relationships (now including Sabre) carry meaningful weight. This concentration is the price of the "land big deals" strategy — great when the flagship accounts grow, a vulnerability if one contracts.

How to think about the ~38× multiple

At roughly ₹62,950 Cr market cap on ~₹1,745 Cr of FY26 profit, Coforge trades around 38× trailing earnings — a clear premium to the mid-cap IT average and even to some large-caps. 11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely high-growth franchise — priced for the growth to continue and the margin to converge.

Coforge is one of the more distinctive stories in Indian IT: a focused, vertical-deep mid-cap growing far faster than the sector, run by a professional board with no promoter, and underwritten by a record order book. The investment debate is almost entirely about execution and price — whether the margin recovers as guided and whether the mega-deal-driven growth holds — not about whether the business is growing.

The bull case

  • Sector-leading growth — ~29% USD revenue growth in FY26, multiples of the large-cap peers, underwritten by a $1.75bn executable book.
  • Vertical moats — rare, deep travel/airline franchise (the $1.56bn Sabre deal) plus anchored BFS and insurance positions rivals can't cheaply copy.
  • Margin convergence — EBIT up 370bps in FY26; management guides FY27 EBITDA to 20.5–21% as Cigniti integrates and AI cuts delivery cost.
  • AI as a tailwind — Quasar platform, hyperscaler/NVIDIA ties, and Cigniti's AI-testing engine position it to sell into the AI wave and lower its own costs.
  • Clean, flexible structure — no promoter overhang, professional board, and equity/M&A flexibility to keep compounding inorganically.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~38× earnings prices in flawless execution; vulnerable to a sharp de-rating on any stumble.
  • Concentration risk — mega-deals and top clients (now incl. Sabre) and the travel vertical carry outsized weight; a re-scope or delay hurts disproportionately.
  • M&A dependence & debt — serial acquisitions add integration, goodwill and leverage risk; a bad deal would dent both growth and the balance sheet.
  • Demand & macro — reliant on Western (US/EMEA) tech budgets and the rupee; a discretionary-spend slowdown or currency swing bites.
  • AI & no-promoter — AI could compress billing rates over time, and an ownerless company betting big on M&A leans entirely on board discipline.
The bottom line

Coforge is a structurally advantaged, fastest-in-class mid-cap IT franchise — focused where it's deep, aggressive where it can win, and riding the AI wave from a credible position. The business momentum is real and the order book de-risks the near term. The risk is not growth; it's price and convergence. The story over the next few years will be told in three numbers each quarter: order intake (are big deals still landing?), EBIT/EBITDA margin (is it climbing toward the guided 20%+?), and large-deal delivery (is Sabre ramping on plan?). Get those right and a ~30%-growth compounder can grow into its premium; stumble on any, and a 38× multiple has room to fall.
