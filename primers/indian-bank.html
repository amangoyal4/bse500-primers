00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Indian Bank is one of India's largest — and best-run — public-sector banks. It is a Chennai-headquartered, government-controlled lender, born in 1907 out of the Swadeshi movement, that takes deposits from savers nationwide and lends to households, farmers, small businesses and corporates. After the 2020 amalgamation that folded Allahabad Bank into it, Indian Bank roughly doubled in size to become India's seventh-largest bank, with a ~₹14.95 lakh crore total business (deposits + advances) and a franchise that now spans its Southern heartland plus a new North, Central and Eastern reach.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is the quality leader of the PSU pack. This is the whole differentiator. Among the large state-owned banks, Indian Bank has arguably the cleanest book and the most conservative provisioning: FY26 gross NPAs of 1.98%, a barely-there net NPA of 0.15%, and a provision coverage ratio of ~98% — among the highest of any bank in the country. Its funding is enviable too — a ~40% domestic CASA and a cost of deposits of just 4.97% — which, combined with the clean book, produces a ~1.3% return on assets that stands with the better private banks and above most PSU peers.
  • Strong, steady growth with a live margin squeeze. FY26 delivered ₹12,156 Cr net profit (+11.3%) on advances up 13.4% and deposits up 12.3%. But the net interest margin eased to 3.24% globally (3.36% domestic) from ~3.4%, as RBI rate cuts and a deposit war press on the spread — and management guides FY27 NIM lower still, to 3.10–3.25%.
  • It is ~73.8% owned by the Government of India. That is the double-edge the whole valuation turns on: sovereign backing makes its deposits ultra-safe and cheap, but state ownership, social-lending mandates and the memory of past PSU crises keep the stock at a P/E of ~9× and ~1.4× book — a discount to private peers like HDFC and ICICI, even though Indian Bank's fundamentals are close to theirs.
In plain terms

Think of Indian Bank as a ₹8.3-lakh-crore deposit machine wired to a ₹6.7-lakh-crore loan book, run by the government — but run unusually well. It pays savers a little, lends that money out for more, and pockets the gap. What makes it stand out from the other state banks is not a flashy side-business; it is discipline. Its bad loans are among the lowest, it has set aside money against almost every rupee of them (98% coverage), its funding is cheap and sticky, and so it earns close to the ~1.3–1.5% return on assets that marks a genuinely good bank — while still trading at a "government-owned" discount. The open questions: can it defend that margin as rates fall, and does the market ever pay a quality premium for a quality PSU bank?

01

Bank economics 101: how a bank actually earns

No finance degree required — just the numbers that drive everything.

A bank is, at its core, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors) and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap between what it earns on loans and pays on deposits — scaled to its assets — is the engine. A handful of terms tell you almost the whole story, and they matter doubly for Indian Bank, whose investment case rests on being the bank that does each of these things a notch better than its PSU peers.

The only banking units you need

PCRProvision Coverage Ratio — how much of the bad loans the bank has already set money aside against. Indian Bank: ~98.2% — near-total pre-funding, best-in-class among PSU banks.
CAR / CET-1Capital Adequacy — the equity cushion versus loans, the buffer against losses. Indian Bank: CRAR ~17.9% — comfortably above the regulatory floor.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. So a bank gets richer four ways — lend more (grow advances), fund cheaper (more CASA, lower deposit cost), lose less (low NPAs and low credit cost), and earn fees (cards, insurance, forex, distribution). The reason Indian Bank stands out is that it does well on all four levers at once, and especially on the two hardest — funding cheaply and losing very little. That combination is exactly what produces its ~1.3% return on assets, a level most PSU banks struggle to reach.

Why "loan losses" are the swing factor for a PSU bank

  • Provisioning is the hidden lever. When a loan turns bad, the bank must set aside money to cover the expected loss — a provision, charged straight against profit. In the FY16–18 crisis, PSU banks were provisioning tens of thousands of crores; as books cleaned up, that charge collapsed and profit surged. Indian Bank went further than most: it kept provisioning even after loans were covered, pushing its PCR to ~98% — meaning almost nothing left to surprise it. That conservatism is the foundation of its "quality" label.
  • Slippages are the leading indicator. A "slippage" is a loan freshly turning bad. Indian Bank targets slippages below 1% of the book — the early-warning gauge that a clean book stays clean. Watch this each quarter.
  • The rate cycle sets the margin. Because roughly half of Indian Bank's loans are linked to external benchmarks (the RBI repo rate), they reprice quickly when the RBI cuts — but deposits reprice more slowly. So a rate-cut cycle squeezes NIM first: loan yields fall before funding costs do. That is precisely the pinch showing up in FY26 and guided to continue into FY27.
02

How the money flows: deposits → loans → margin

Read this once and the whole bank clicks into place.

A bank is a conveyor belt for money. Savers' deposits come in one end; loans go out the other; the spread between the two is harvested as net interest income. Two things govern how much profit drips out: how cheap the deposits are (the CASA mix and deposit cost) and how much more the loans yield than the deposits cost (the margin). Indian Bank runs this belt better than most PSU peers because the funding end is unusually cheap — and that is the whole reason its margin and returns are higher.

When the RBI cuts rates, Stage-3 loan yields fall almost immediately while Stage-1/2 deposit costs lag — so the spread narrows first. A high CASA and low deposit cost is what lets Indian Bank hold a wider margin than its peers through that squeeze.

Why the CASA mix and deposit cost are the whole game

A current account pays 0% and a savings account ~3%; a fixed deposit pays 6.5–7.5%. The more of your funding that sits in low-cost CASA, the cheaper your money — and the wider your margin on the same loan. Indian Bank's domestic CASA of ~39.7% is on the higher side for a large PSU bank (Canara runs ~30%), and its blended cost of deposits of just 4.97% — trimmed 15 basis points during FY26 — is among the lowest in the state-owned pack. That cheap, sticky funding is the quiet engine behind its superior margin and returns. It is the difference between a bank that has to "buy" expensive deposits to grow and one that gathers them cheaply from a loyal, largely retail base.

Why a great deposit franchise beats a great loan book

Anyone can lend money; the hard, valuable part of banking is gathering deposits cheaply and reliably. A bank with a sticky, low-cost deposit base can lend profitably through any cycle; one that has to bid up for deposits at high rates is always squeezed. Indian Bank's edge is precisely here: a deep, largely retail Southern deposit base, ~40% of it in near-free CASA, funded at under 5%. That is why — even with the same RBI rate cuts hitting everyone — Indian Bank's margin (~3.24%) sits comfortably above a Canara (~2.5%) and near the top of the PSU pack. The whole "quality" thesis starts with this: cheap funding + clean lending = durable, above-average returns.

03

The company and who controls it

A 118-year-old Swadeshi-era institution, majority-owned by the state.

Indian Bank was founded in 1907 in Madras (now Chennai) by the lawyer V. Krishnaswamy Iyer, born directly out of the Swadeshi movement after the collapse of the European-run Arbuthnot Bank left Indians wary of foreign-controlled finance. It commenced business on 15 August 1907. It was nationalised in 1969 alongside India's other large banks. Today it is a professionally-run public-sector bank headquartered in Chennai, whose controlling shareholder is the Government of India (~73.8%), operating ~6,000+ domestic branches and a modest overseas network. Its deep roots in Tamil Nadu and the South remain its franchise DNA — and its low-cost deposit advantage.

Ownership & management

  • Government of India ~73.8%. The state is the promoter and controlling shareholder. This anchors the stock's identity as a "PSU bank" and drives both its funding advantage and its valuation discount. It also means the government must eventually sell down to meet the SEBI minimum-public-float rule — a live dilution overhang (see §09).
  • Public & institutional float ~26%. Foreign and domestic institutions (LIC, mutual funds) plus retail hold the rest. The government's high stake means periodic dilution risk — an OFS, QIP or FPO — hangs over minority holders. The board has already cleared a ₹5,000 Cr capital-raise enabling resolution.
  • Board-driven, government-appointed leadership. Like all PSU banks, top management (MD & CEO, executive directors) is appointed via the government's selection process, with fixed tenures — a governance model that differs from the founder- or professional-CEO continuity of private banks, though Indian Bank's execution has been notably steady.
  • A focused, low-frills bank. Indian Bank is fundamentally a plain-vanilla lender-and-deposit-taker — no large listed subsidiaries to unlock. Its edge is operational quality, not financial engineering. That makes the "quality leader" thesis a story about how well it runs the core bank, not about hidden assets.
04

The quality-leader thesis — why Indian Bank is the best of the PSU pack

The single feature that sets Indian Bank apart — it is arguably the cleanest, best-provisioned, highest-return of the large public-sector banks.

Here is what makes Indian Bank genuinely different from Canara, Bank of Baroda, PNB or Union Bank. It is not a subsidiary IPO or a flashy merger — it is discipline. On the three metrics that separate a good bank from a merely large one — asset quality, provisioning conservatism, and returns — Indian Bank consistently ranks at or near the top of the entire public-sector group. An analyst covering it leads not with "cheap PSU bank" but with "the PSU bank that behaves like a good private bank". The question is whether the market ever pays up for that.

Why the market may still under-rate it

Here is the puzzle. Indian Bank earns a private-bank-like ~1.3% ROA and ~16% ROE, has the cleanest book and the fattest provisions of the PSU pack, and funds itself cheaply — yet it trades at ~9× earnings and ~1.4× book, a discount to HDFC or ICICI (18–22× earnings, 2–3× book). The market is applying a blanket "PSU discount" (§09) that treats every state-owned bank as government-directed and cyclically fragile. But Indian Bank arguably deserves to be treated as an exception — a well-run bank that happens to be state-owned. When the market grants that distinction, the stock re-rates; when it lumps all PSU banks together, it stays cheap.

Why quality translates into durable returns

These metrics are not just trophies — they compound into real, durable profit. Start with cheap funding (~5% deposit cost, ~40% CASA): that hands Indian Bank a wider margin than peers on the very same loans. Add conservative provisioning (98% coverage): its future profits can't be ambushed by old loans suddenly needing more cover, because it already over-provided. Add a clean, granular book (0.15% net NPA, RAM-heavy): credit costs stay low and predictable. Stack those together and you get a bank whose ~1.3% ROA is not a lucky one-year number but a structural outcome of how it is run. That durability is exactly what a re-rating rewards — the market pays more for earnings it can trust. Indian Bank's whole bull case is that it has quietly built private-bank-quality earnings inside a PSU-bank valuation.

Why this is the section that matters: every other force in this primer — the merger, the margin, the RAM book, the discount — ultimately feeds this one question. Is Indian Bank a generic PSU bank that got lucky, or a genuinely superior bank that the market is mis-filing? The evidence (best-in-class PCR, lowest-ish net NPA, top-of-pack ROA and cheap funding, held steadily for years) points to the latter. Watch whether it sustains the lead — PCR, net NPA and ROA each quarter — because the whole thesis is that the quality is durable, not a flash.
05

The 2020 Allahabad Bank amalgamation

The merger that doubled Indian Bank — and turned a Southern bank into a national one.

On 1 April 2020 — at the very start of the pandemic lockdown — the government folded Allahabad Bank, India's oldest joint-stock bank (founded 1865, headquartered in the North), into Indian Bank. This was a defining consolidation: it roughly doubled Indian Bank overnight to ~₹8 lakh crore of business, ~6,000 branches, ~43,000 staff and 120 million+ customers, making it India's seventh-largest bank. Crucially, it combined Indian Bank's strong Southern base with Allahabad's Northern, Central and Eastern reach — geographies where a Chennai-rooted bank had been thin.

Why the government did it — and why it fit

  • PSU consolidation. India had too many small, sub-scale state banks. The 2019–20 mega-merger wave collapsed ten PSU banks into four strong anchors. Merging Allahabad into Indian Bank created a bigger, more competitive lender and stripped out duplicated costs — the same logic that folded Syndicate into Canara and Vijaya/Dena into BoB.
  • Complementary geographies, not overlapping ones. This is what made it a smart pairing. Indian Bank was concentrated in the South; Allahabad was strong in the North, Central and East (Uttar Pradesh, West Bengal, Bihar). Rather than duplicating branches, the merger extended Indian Bank's map — giving a strong Southern deposit franchise a genuine national footprint.
  • Scale & a broader deposit base. The combined entity gained a far larger, more geographically diversified deposit franchise and branch network — deepening the very low-cost funding advantage that underpins today's quality thesis.

The merger's legacy in today's numbers

This was a government-engineered consolidation of two banks with complementary maps, which made the strategic logic clean even if the integration work (unifying systems, branches and ~12 crore accounts) took a couple of years. The lasting effect is scale plus geographic diversification: today's Indian Bank is a genuinely national bank whose home turf remains the South — the source of its sticky, low-cost deposits — but which now also lends and gathers deposits across the North and East. When you look at Indian Bank's post-FY20 figures, remember they step up sharply versus its pre-merger history — comparisons to earlier years can mislead, because you are really looking at two banks in one. The remarkable part is how cleanly it absorbed a large, weaker partner without letting its asset quality slip — itself evidence of the operational discipline at the heart of the quality thesis.

06

The deposit franchise, NIM and the margin squeeze

Indian Bank's cheap funding is its edge — but the rate-and-deposit cycle is pressing on the spread.

With the bad-loan problem long solved, the swing factor for Indian Bank's earnings has shifted to the margin — and here Indian Bank starts from a position of strength. Its NIM of ~3.24% globally (3.36% domestic) is among the healthiest of the large PSU banks, built on that ~40% CASA and sub-5% deposit cost. But the margin is now being squeezed: it eased from ~3.4% a year earlier, and management guides FY27 NIM lower still, to 3.10–3.25%. The RBI's rate-cut cycle drags loan yields down quickly, while a deposit war keeps funding costs from falling as fast.

Why margins are hard to defend right now

  • Loans reprice faster than deposits. Roughly half of Indian Bank's loans are linked to external benchmarks (the RBI repo rate). When the RBI cuts, those loan rates drop almost immediately — but existing fixed deposits keep paying their old, higher rates until they mature. So in a rate-cut cycle, income falls before costs do, and NIM compresses first. Management explicitly flagged "limited pricing flexibility" from this benchmark linkage.
  • The deposit war. Indian households are increasingly moving savings into mutual funds, equities and higher-yield products. That shrinks the pool of cheap bank deposits, so every bank must offer higher fixed-deposit rates. Higher deposit costs eat directly into the spread — the reason cost of deposits fell only modestly (to 4.97%) despite RBI cuts.
  • But a high CASA is the shock-absorber. Here is where Indian Bank differs from lower-CASA peers: because ~40% of its funding sits in near-free current and savings accounts, a rise in term-deposit rates hurts it less than a bank funding itself mostly with fixed deposits. Its cheap, sticky base is precisely what keeps its margin near the top of the pack even as the cycle bites.

Why the deposit franchise is the ultimate moat

Gathering deposits cheaply and reliably is the hard, valuable part of banking. Indian Bank's advantage here is its deep Southern retail branch network and sovereign backing: as a government-majority bank rooted in Tamil Nadu, its deposits are perceived as ultra-safe, so savers park money with it even at slightly lower rates — and a big chunk of that money sits in low-cost CASA. That is the moat. The margin squeeze is real, but Indian Bank fights it from higher ground than most PSU peers. Watch the cost of deposits and CASA ratio each quarter: if CASA holds around 40% while the rate-cut cycle ends, the margin can stabilise near the top of guidance; if CASA bleeds toward term deposits, the squeeze deepens.

07

The RAM book and priority-sector strength

A granular, retail-agri-MSME-tilted loan book — safer, higher-yielding, and true to Indian Bank's roots.

The other half of Indian Bank's quality story is the shape of its loan book. Two-thirds of its domestic advances sit in the "RAM" book — Retail, Agriculture and MSME (₹4.04 lakh crore, 65.45% of domestic advances, growing ~15%). The FY16–18 crisis was a corporate-lending disaster — a handful of large infrastructure and industrial borrowers defaulted and blew holes in every PSU bank's book. The lesson was universal: grow the granular, secured, diversified RAM book, where losses are small and spread across millions of borrowers. Indian Bank leans into this harder than most — and it plays perfectly to its heritage as a strong agri and priority-sector lender in South India.

Why a RAM-heavy book is both safer and more profitable

  • Diversification of risk. A book spread across millions of small home, farm and MSME loans can't be sunk by a handful of big defaults the way a corporate-heavy book can. When Indian Bank's sector-wise NPAs are examined, its retail GNPA is a tiny ~0.64% — a testament to how safe the granular book is — while the somewhat higher agri (~4.7%) and MSME (~4.1%) NPAs are the well-understood, well-provisioned cost of priority-sector lending.
  • Higher yield. Retail and MSME loans (home, auto, personal, small-business) typically earn more than large corporate loans, which are priced razor-thin because big companies can shop around. So a RAM-heavy book quietly lifts the average loan yield — a direct support to the margin.
  • Priority-sector fit. As a PSU bank, Indian Bank must meet priority-sector lending targets (agriculture, small business, weaker sections). Its long agri heritage means it does this as a strength, not a chore — the granular priority book is both a regulatory requirement met and a genuine, profitable franchise.
Why the RAM shift is a quiet quality defence

Here's the elegant part: the same strategy that makes Indian Bank safer also makes it more profitable per rupee lent. By keeping ~65% of the book in granular retail, agri and MSME loans — and growing that faster than the total — Indian Bank spreads its risk across millions of borrowers and earns a better average yield than a corporate-heavy lender. That combination is a big reason its asset quality (0.15% net NPA) and margin (~3.24% NIM) both sit near the top of the PSU pack at the same time — the two usually trade off, but a well-run granular book gets you both. The risk to watch is the fast-growing retail slice, where a consumer downturn or a build-up of unsecured personal lending can bite — but Indian Bank's retail GNPA of ~0.64% suggests underwriting has stayed tight.

08

Digital distribution and the cost engine

How a 6,000-branch bank keeps its cost-to-income low — and reaches customers cheaply.

A quiet part of Indian Bank's quality edge is efficiency. It runs a cost-to-income ratio around 45% — lean for a large PSU bank — and it has leaned hard into digital to keep it there. Its mobile-banking super-app IndOASIS, alongside internet banking and UPI, now carries the bulk of the bank's transactions: 94% of FY26 business was digital, with 2.36 crore mobile-banking users (+22%) and 2.64 crore UPI users (+21%). For a bank whose branches are concentrated in the South plus the post-merger North/East, digital is how it grows the customer count and cross-sells without proportionally growing cost.

Why digital matters for a PSU bank's economics

  • Cheaper distribution. Onboarding customers, opening accounts and disbursing small retail loans through an app costs a fraction of doing it through a branch. For a bank with ~6,000 branches and a huge rural/agri base, digital is the way to grow without ballooning the cost base — which is exactly what keeps the cost-to-income ratio near 45%.
  • Deposit gathering and CASA defence. A good app helps gather and retain low-cost CASA deposits — the very funding advantage at the heart of the quality thesis. Every customer who keeps their salary account and everyday balances on IndOASIS is cheap, sticky funding.
  • Data-driven, safer lending. Digital footprints and account data let the bank underwrite small retail and MSME loans faster and more accurately — supporting the granular-book strategy that keeps NPAs low. Analytics-led collections also help keep slippages under the 1% target.

Why efficiency compounds with the quality thesis

Cost efficiency is the fourth quiet lever behind Indian Bank's returns. Recall the profit equation: profit rises when you fund cheaper, lose less, and spend less running the bank. A ~45% cost-to-income means Indian Bank keeps a bigger share of its net interest income as pre-provision profit — which, on top of low credit costs and cheap funding, is why the ~1.3% ROA holds. Digital is what makes that efficiency sustainable as the bank grows: it lets Indian Bank add customers and loans across its enlarged national footprint without adding branches and staff at the same pace. It is not a headline-grabbing story, but it is a real part of why this bank out-earns its peers.

09

Government ownership and the PSU-discount debate

Why a clean, ~1.3%-ROA bank still trades at ~9× earnings — and whether that changes.

Here is the puzzle at the heart of the Indian Bank stock: it earns ~1.3% on assets, ~16% on equity, has fortress ~98% provision coverage and a 0.15% net NPA — yet the market prices it at a P/E of ~9× and ~1.4× book, while private peers HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank trade at ~18–22× earnings and 2–3× book. That gap — the "PSU discount" — is not an accident. It reflects real structural concerns about government-owned banks. But the Indian Bank twist is that its fundamentals are the closest of any large PSU bank to a private bank's — so the debate over whether the discount is deserved is sharper here than anywhere.

Why PSU banks trade at a discount

  • Governance & non-commercial mandates. The government is both owner and policymaker. PSU banks can be asked to support social schemes, farm-loan waivers, priority-sector lending and national-priority projects — lending that may not maximise shareholder returns. Management is government-appointed on fixed tenures, so continuity and incentives differ from a founder-led private bank.
  • The NPA scar tissue. Institutional investors remember the FY16–FY18 crisis, when PSU banks' hidden bad loans destroyed years of profit. Even with clean books today, that credibility gap lingers in the multiple — though Indian Bank, having weathered that crisis better than most, carries less of it.
  • Dilution risk. With the government at ~73.8%, Indian Bank must eventually sell down to meet SEBI's 25% minimum-public-float rule. That guarantees future supply of stock (an OFS), and the board has already cleared a ₹5,000 Cr capital-raise resolution — a real overhang that can cap the price near-term.
  • Slower structural growth. Private banks have taken market share from PSU banks for two decades on service, technology and underwriting. The market extrapolates that trend onto the whole PSU group — arguably unfairly to a well-run bank like Indian Bank.
The re-rating case vs the value-trap case

The bull ("quality gets recognised"): Indian Bank's fundamentals — ~1.3% ROA, ~16% ROE, 0.15% net NPA, ~98% PCR, cheap funding — are genuinely private-bank-like, and better than any other large PSU bank. If the market ever stops treating all PSU banks as one undifferentiated block and prices Indian Bank on its own quality, the stock re-rates meaningfully above its current ~1.4× book. It has already earned a premium to Canara; the bull says it deserves more.

The bear ("the discount is structural"): PSU banks have looked "cheap" for a decade and the discount has never fully closed, because the structural reasons — government control, mandate risk, forced dilution to meet float rules, slower growth — never go away. A clean book and a great PCR are nice, but without a change in ownership structure or governance, the discount is largely permanent, and the guaranteed future equity supply from the government sell-down caps the upside.

How to hold both ideas: Indian Bank can be a genuinely excellent, cheap bank and a stock whose discount only partly closes. The realistic middle case is a modest re-rating (holding above 1.4× book as its quality lead persists) plus a healthy ~2%+ dividend yield — a decent return — rather than full convergence to private-bank multiples. The swing factor is credibility and the sell-down: every quarter of best-in-class results narrows the gap; the eventual government OFS/QIP to meet the float rule is the overhang that widens it. Net, the quality thesis argues Indian Bank should trade at the top of the PSU range, not the middle.

Clean book, top-of-pack returns, at a modest discount — the debate is margins and the sell-down.

Indian Bank's financials read like the best-run of the PSU banks: steady low-teens loan growth, the cleanest balance sheet in the pack, a private-bank-like ~1.3% return on assets and ~16% return on equity — but with a margin now easing, and a valuation that still applies a PSU discount even as it grants Indian Bank a premium over cheaper peers. The debate is less "is it a healthy bank" (it plainly is, and the best of its group) and more "how durable are the returns, and does the market ever pay a full quality premium".

How to think about the valuation

Indian Bank trades around a ~₹1.10 lakh crore market cap (~₹815/share) on FY26 earnings — roughly 9× earnings, ~1.4× book value (book ~₹581/share), with a ~2.2% dividend yield. For a bank, the multiple that matters most is price-to-book against ROE: a bank earning ~16% ROE would, in a "normal" world, trade well above 1.4× book. Indian Bank's valuation is the market applying a "PSU discount" (§09) — but a smaller one than it applies to weaker PSU peers, because Indian Bank's quality is visible. The whole valuation question is whether that discount shrinks further as the market recognises Indian Bank as the exception — a well-run bank that happens to be state-owned — or whether the structural PSU factors (mandate risk, the guaranteed government sell-down to meet float rules) keep a lid on the multiple. Unlike a private bank, the debate here is more about the multiple than the earnings — the earnings are already excellent.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

The best-run PSU bank at a PSU discount — the debate is margins and the government sell-down.

Indian Bank has done the hard part — and done it better than its peers. It runs the cleanest book, the most conservative provisions, a cheap and sticky deposit franchise, and top-of-pack ~1.3% returns on assets, while having cleanly absorbed a bank its own size in the 2020 Allahabad merger. The investment debate now is almost entirely about whether those returns are durable (can it defend a margin easing toward ~3.1% as rates fall?) and whether the market ever pays a full quality premium for a government-owned bank facing a forced sell-down.

The bull case

  • Quality leader of the PSU pack — cleanest book (0.15% net NPA), best provisioning (~98% PCR), top-of-pack ~1.3% ROA and ~16% ROE.
  • Cheap, sticky deposit franchise — ~40% CASA and ~4.97% cost of deposits give a wider, more defensible margin than peers.
  • Balanced, granular growth — advances +13.4%, deposits +12.3%, RAM book +15% and 65% of the book; safer and higher-yielding.
  • Fortress balance sheet — CRAR ~17.9%, near-total provision coverage; earnings unlikely to be ambushed by old loans.
  • Re-rating optionality — if the market prices Indian Bank on its own quality rather than as a generic PSU bank, the discount narrows.

The bear case

  • Margin easing — NIM slipped to ~3.24% and is guided lower (3.10–3.25%) as rate cuts and the deposit war bite; half the book reprices fast.
  • Government sell-down overhang — at ~73.8%, the state must dilute to meet SEBI's 25% float rule; the ₹5,000 Cr raise is a real supply overhang.
  • The PSU discount may be structural — government control, social mandates and slower growth have capped PSU multiples for a decade regardless of quality.
  • Priority-sector NPA pockets — agri (~4.7%) and MSME (~4.1%) GNPAs are the cyclical soft spots; a rural or SME downturn would test them.
  • Cyclical & governance risk — asset quality can turn in a downturn; government-appointed management and mandate risk cap the quality premium the market will pay.
The bottom line

Indian Bank is the quality leader of India's public-sector banks — a lender that behaves like a good private bank while trading at a state-owned discount. The business risk is genuinely low: the book is the cleanest in the pack, provisions are near-total, funding is cheap and sticky, and returns are top-of-group and durable. The two live questions are: can it defend its margin as the rate-cut cycle plays out (its high CASA gives it more room than peers, but the squeeze is real); and will the market ever pay a full quality premium for a government-owned bank that must, at some point, sell down to meet the public-float rule. The story over the next few years will be written in a handful of numbers each quarter — NIM and cost of deposits, CASA, net NPA and slippages, and the timing of the government sell-down. Get those right and the best-run PSU bank in the country can re-rate toward the top of its range and pay you a steady dividend while you wait; let margins slip or the dilution land badly, and it stays exactly what the market fears — a good bank the market refuses to fully reward.
