00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Power Finance Corporation (PFC) is a non-banking finance company (NBFC) that lends almost exclusively to India's power sector — the companies that build power stations, string transmission lines, run the distribution network that reaches your home, and increasingly build solar and wind farms. It is owned ~56% by the Government of India, holds Maharatna status (the top tier of Indian state-owned enterprises), and is India's largest infrastructure-finance NBFC. Through its ~52.6% stake in REC Limited — another huge power financier — PFC anchors what is effectively a duopoly that funds most of India's electricity build-out.

Four facts frame everything:

  • It is a spread business built on cheap, quasi-sovereign money. PFC borrows at low rates because it carries top domestic credit ratings and implicit government backing, then re-lends to power projects at a higher rate. The gap — the "spread" (~2.5% in FY26) — is the profit engine. Standalone loan book: ₹5.80 lakh crore; consolidated (with REC): ₹11.53 lakh crore, the biggest loan book of any Indian NBFC.
  • Consolidated vs standalone matters more here than almost anywhere. PFC alone earned ₹20,051 Cr profit in FY26; consolidated with REC it earned ₹33,625 Cr. Roughly 40% of the group's profit comes from a subsidiary PFC doesn't fully own — so which number you look at changes the whole picture.
  • It just cleaned up a bad-loan mess. A decade ago PFC was buried in stressed thermal-power loans. By FY26, standalone gross NPAs fell to 1.09% and net NPAs to 0.15% — with more provision write-backs still flowing through as old cases resolve.
  • It's cheap, pays a fat dividend, and is about to merge with REC. The stock trades at roughly 5–5.5× earnings, ~1.1× book, with a ~4.4% dividend yield — the classic "PSU discount". And in June 2026 the government approved merging REC into PFC at an 88-for-100 share swap, to create a single ~₹11-lakh-crore power-finance giant.
In plain terms

Think of PFC as the wholesale bank for India's electricity system. It doesn't take your deposits or run branches; instead it raises enormous sums from bond markets and banks — cheaply, because lenders treat it as almost as safe as the government itself — and channels that money into the power plants, grids and solar farms the country needs. It earns the difference between what it pays to borrow and what it charges to lend. Because the government owns it, funds it cheaply, and steers national power policy through it (PFC is an official "nodal agency" for big reform schemes), it sits at the centre of one of India's largest, most policy-driven investment cycles. The catch: its fortunes are tied to state electricity boards and government direction, and the market has long refused to pay a full price for a company it sees as an arm of the state.

01

How a power financier actually earns money

No finance degree required — just the four ideas that drive an NBFC like this.

PFC is a lender, not a bank. The distinction matters: a bank funds itself mostly with customer deposits; an NBFC like PFC funds itself by borrowing in the market — issuing bonds and taking loans — and re-lending that money. Strip away the jargon and four numbers explain nearly the whole business.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and PFC's number)
Loan book / AUMThe total money it has lent out and is earning interest on. Standalone ~₹5.80 lakh crore; consolidated (with REC) ~₹11.53 lakh crore — the largest NBFC book in India.
YieldThe average interest rate it charges borrowers — around ~10%. This is its top-line income per rupee lent.
Cost of fundsThe average interest rate it pays to borrow. Because it's a top-rated, government-backed PSU, this is unusually low — the heart of its advantage.
Spread / NIMYield minus cost of funds = the spread (~2.46% in FY26). A close cousin, net interest margin (NIM, ~3.55%), measures the same profit on the whole balance sheet. This gap, multiplied across ₹5.8 lakh crore, is the money machine.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Net interest income ≈ Loan book × Spread. So PFC grows profit in three ways — lend more (grow the book), keep the spread wide (borrow cheap, price loans well), and lose less to bad loans (credit costs). Because its borrowers are largely government-linked utilities and its funding is quasi-sovereign, PFC runs an enormous book on a thin spread — the opposite of a small NBFC that earns a fat margin on a small book. Its edge is scale plus cheap funding, not high pricing. That's why the single most important thing to watch is the cost of funds relative to the yield.

Why "infrastructure finance" is a different animal

  • Huge, long-dated loans. A power plant or transmission line is financed over 10–25 years in tickets of hundreds or thousands of crores — very different from a car loan or a credit card. PFC's balance sheet is built to match long assets with long, cheap borrowings.
  • Concentrated, policy-linked borrowers. Around three-quarters of the book is government-linked — central and state power utilities — with the rest to private developers. That concentration is a strength (implicit backing, low ultimate losses) and a risk (fortunes tied to state discom finances and government direction).
  • Leverage is the model. PFC operates at a debt-to-equity of roughly 5–6× — normal and healthy for a financier, but it means the spread and asset quality must be managed tightly, because a small book-wide problem is amplified across a giant balance sheet.
02

The moat: quasi-sovereign funding & the nodal-agency role

The single most important reason PFC exists and earns — read this once and the whole model clicks.

A financier's only real edge is the cost at which it can raise money. Anyone can lend; the winner is whoever can fund most cheaply and reliably. PFC's moat is that it borrows almost as cheaply as the Indian government itself — because it is the government, in commercial clothing. This "quasi-sovereign" funding advantage, plus its official role in national power policy, is what makes PFC hard to dislodge.

Where the cheap money comes from

  • Top domestic credit ratings. PFC carries the highest domestic long-term ratings (AAA-equivalent from Indian agencies; its international ratings sit at India's sovereign ceiling). High rating = low borrowing cost. Lenders demand little extra return because they view a default as almost unthinkable for a majority-government-owned Maharatna.
  • Implicit sovereign backing. With the Government of India owning ~56%, the market prices PFC's bonds as if the state stands behind them. That belief — not a formal guarantee, but a powerful one — shaves the cost of every rupee it borrows.
  • Diversified funding channels. PFC raises money across domestic bonds (the bulk, ~57% of borrowings), bank/institutional loans (~18%), and foreign-currency borrowings (~25%), plus tax-free bonds and capital-gains (54EC) bonds retail investors buy for tax reasons. This breadth keeps funding available even when one market tightens.
Why a thin spread on cheap money beats a fat spread on dear money

Imagine two lenders. Lender A borrows at 7% and lends at 10% — a 3% spread. Lender B borrows at 12% and lends at 16% — a 4% spread. B looks more profitable per rupee, but B can only lend to riskier borrowers (to justify 16%) and its funding can dry up in a crisis. A — that's PFC — borrows cheaply and safely, so it can lend safely to giant, low-risk power projects and still make money on a modest spread. Then it does this across ₹5.8 lakh crore. Cheap, dependable funding is the entire game, and it's the one thing a new private rival cannot replicate, because a private NBFC can never borrow at a government-owned PSU's rate.

The nodal-agency role — policy power on top of lending

PFC is not just a lender; it is an instrument of national power policy. The government routes its flagship power-sector reform programmes through PFC (and REC) as designated "nodal agencies" — most importantly the Revamped Distribution Sector Scheme (RDSS), a ~₹3.04 lakh crore programme (FY22–FY26) to fix India's loss-making electricity distribution companies with smart meters and loss-reduction infrastructure.

Why nodal-agency status is a quiet moat: being the government's chosen channel for schemes like RDSS gives PFC a first look at a pipeline of state-backed lending, a policy-anchored reason for utilities to borrow from it, and deep relationships with every state discom in the country. Combined disbursement opportunity for PFC and REC under RDSS alone was estimated at ~₹2 lakh crore. It's business a private lender simply cannot access.
03

The company and who controls it

From a 1986 policy vehicle to a Maharatna at the centre of power finance.

PFC was set up in 1986 as a dedicated financier for India's power sector, at a time when the state was building generation capacity and needed a specialised lender to fund it. Four decades on, it is a listed Maharatna (the highest autonomy tier for Indian PSUs), the parent of REC, and the anchor of the country's power-finance system.

Timeline

  • 1986
    PFC incorporated as a government financial institution dedicated to funding India's power sector.
  • 2007
    IPO / listing on NSE & BSE (BSE code 532810), as the government part-privatises to bring market discipline while retaining majority control.
  • 2010s
    Book balloons on the back of a huge thermal-power capex boom — which later turns sour as many private thermal projects stall (the NPA problem of §07).
  • Mar 2019
    PFC acquires the government's 52.63% stake in REC Limited for ~₹14,500 Cr — folding India's #2 power financier under its own roof and creating the group structure.
  • 2021
    Government designates PFC & REC nodal agencies for the RDSS distribution-reform scheme (~₹3.04 lakh Cr outlay).
  • FY26
    Record year: standalone PAT ₹20,051 Cr, consolidated ₹33,625 Cr; consolidated loan book crosses ₹11.5 lakh Cr; standalone net worth crosses ₹1 lakh Cr.
  • Jun 2026
    Boards approve — and the President of India approves (10 Jun 2026) — a merger of REC into PFC at an 88:100 share swap, to create a single power-finance behemoth (final scheme & approvals pending).

Ownership & the group structure

  • Government of India: ~55.99%. The state is the controlling promoter — it appoints management, can direct strategy, and receives the bulk of PFC's large dividends (a meaningful line in the government's own budget).
  • Institutions: ~35%. Foreign institutional investors hold ~19–20% and domestic institutions ~15% — a heavily institution-owned float, reflecting PFC's index weight and yield appeal.
  • PFC owns ~52.63% of REC Limited — making REC a subsidiary that PFC consolidates. This one fact is so central it gets its own section next.

The trap — and how to read it correctly

When accounting rules make you consolidate a subsidiary you control, you add in 100% of REC's loan book and profit — even though PFC only owns ~52.6% of it. The ~47% that belongs to REC's other shareholders is then subtracted lower down as "minority interest". So the consolidated loan book (₹11.53 lakh Cr) overstates what PFC's own shareholders truly own, while the consolidated PAT of ₹33,625 Cr still includes profit attributable to REC's minorities. The practical rule: use consolidated to see the scale and reach of the group (it's genuinely the dominant force in Indian power finance); use standalone plus PFC's stake in REC to think about value per PFC share. Screener-style headline ratios (EPS ₹78, P/E ~5.5) are usually on the consolidated basis.

PFC + REC = the power-finance duopoly

PFC and REC do broadly the same thing — lend to generation, transmission, distribution and renewables — and between them they finance the lion's share of India's power build-out. REC was originally focused on rural electrification and distribution; PFC on generation. Today they overlap heavily, which is precisely why the government wants to merge them (see the timeline): one balance sheet, less duplication, more scale, and a cleaner structure than a parent and a 52%-owned twin both listed separately. The approved 88:100 swap (REC holders receive 88 PFC shares per 100 REC shares) is the mechanism; the final scheme and regulatory clearances were still pending as of mid-2026.

Why the merger is a genuine swing factor: collapsing REC into PFC removes the minority-interest leakage, creates a single ~₹11-lakh-crore lender with unmatched scale, and could simplify the story enough to help close the valuation discount (§10). But the swap ratio, tax treatment and timing all carry execution and relative-value risk for holders of each stock — it is the biggest single event on PFC's horizon.

The loan book, decoded

Who PFC lends to, and the quiet pivot from coal to clean energy.

PFC's ~₹5.80 lakh crore standalone book is spread across the four links of the electricity chain — generation, transmission, distribution and renewables — with a clear structural shift underway toward green energy. Understanding the mix tells you where the growth is coming from and where the risk sits.

The four segments in plain English

  • Generation — money for building power stations: coal (thermal), hydro, nuclear and gas. Historically PFC's core, and the source of its old bad-loan problem (stalled private thermal projects). Still ~a third of the book, but no longer the growth driver.
  • Transmission & distribution (T&D) — the wires: the high-voltage grid that moves power across states, plus the local distribution networks (discoms) that deliver it to homes and factories. Now the largest slice (~46%), powered by grid expansion and the RDSS distribution-reform push.
  • Renewables — solar parks, wind farms, and emerging areas like green hydrogen and battery storage. The fastest-growing segment and the strategic future; the group's renewable book is ~₹1.65 lakh crore and rising as India races toward its 500 GW non-fossil target.
  • Infrastructure & others — a newer, diversifying bucket: e-mobility, logistics, ports and other infrastructure, letting PFC grow beyond pure power.
Why the coal-to-green shift is the story to watch

For most of its history PFC was, in effect, India's coal-power banker. That is changing fast. New lending is tilting decisively toward renewables and the grid, because (1) India's energy transition means most new generation capacity is now solar and wind, and (2) green loans to strong developers carry lower credit risk than the marginal thermal projects that once burned PFC. The strategic bet is that PFC rides the energy-transition capex wave — financing the solar farms, transmission lines and storage that a decarbonising grid needs — while its legacy thermal exposure runs down. Get this transition right and PFC swaps a risky, politically-fraught coal book for a cleaner, faster-growing green one. That is genuinely re-rating-worthy — if the market chooses to believe it.

06

The power-capex & energy-transition tailwind

The multi-year supercycle that gives PFC something to lend into.

A lender only grows if there is something to finance. PFC's biggest structural tailwind is that India is in the middle of a massive, multi-year power-sector investment cycle — building generation, rebuilding the grid, financing renewables, and fixing distribution. That capex has to be funded, and PFC + REC are the natural, government-anchored channel for a large share of it.

Where the demand for PFC's money comes from

  • Renewables build-out. Hitting India's 500 GW non-fossil goal means hundreds of gigawatts of new solar and wind — capital-intensive projects that need long-dated debt. This is the fastest-growing pool of lending demand and squarely in PFC's wheelhouse.
  • Grid & transmission. A greener grid needs far more wires — renewables are built where the sun and wind are, not where the demand is, so power must be moved long distances. India's transmission-expansion plans imply years of T&D financing.
  • Distribution reform (RDSS). The ~₹3.04 lakh crore RDSS scheme funnels smart-metering and loss-reduction spending through PFC and REC — a policy-guaranteed pipeline aimed at fixing the chronically loss-making discoms that are the sector's weak link.
  • Diversification. Beyond power, PFC is extending into broader infrastructure (e-mobility, logistics, ports), widening the runway if power-sector growth ever slows.
The tailwind, and its honest limit

The bull case is simple: India must spend enormous sums on power for the next decade, and PFC is positioned to fund a big chunk of it cheaply — so the loan book can keep compounding at high-single to low-double digits (management guided ~10% growth for FY27). The honest limit is twofold. First, competition from banks: when banks are flush with deposits, they undercut PFC on price for the best power projects, squeezing the spread (exactly what happened in FY26 — see §08). Second, this is a spread business, not a fee bonanza: growth comes from more loans at a thin margin, so PFC needs volume and discipline on funding cost to convert the capex wave into profit growth. The tailwind is real; it is not automatic.

07

Asset quality: the great thermal-NPA clean-up

The decade-long turnaround that quietly transformed the earnings story.

Not long ago, the biggest worry about PFC was its bad loans. During the 2010s thermal-power boom, PFC lent heavily to private coal-power projects — many of which stalled over fuel shortages, cancelled power-purchase agreements, and cost overruns. A chunk of the book went non-performing, and PFC had to set aside huge provisions against it. The FY26 numbers show how far that clean-up has come.

Why the clean-up is a hidden profit engine — and a warning

When old bad loans get resolved — a stalled project is sold, restructured, or recovers — PFC can write back provisions it had previously set aside. Those write-backs flow straight to profit. In FY26, roughly ₹1,800 crore of provision reversals boosted standalone earnings, and this has been a recurring feature as legacy cases close. That's genuinely good news — the risk is real and shrinking. But it also carries a warning for the analyst: part of recent profit growth is "one-off" in nature (write-backs, not fresh spread income). As the stressed book empties out, this tailwind fades, so it's important to look at the underlying net-interest-income growth (~13% in FY26) separately from the provision-reversal sugar rush. Clean books are great; just don't extrapolate the write-backs forever.

On a consolidated basis (with REC), asset quality looks even cleaner — gross NPA of ~0.66% and net NPA of ~0.13% — because REC's book has also normalised. The upshot: what was once PFC's central risk is now, largely, behind it, and the story has shifted from "will the bad loans sink it?" to "how fast can it grow the clean book?"

08

Borrowing profile & the cost-of-funds squeeze

The lever that decides whether loan growth becomes profit growth.

Because PFC's edge is cheap funding, the cost of funds is the number that makes or breaks a year. In FY26 that lever tightened: even as the book grew and NPAs fell, the spread narrowed to 2.46% (from 2.58%) as banks competed aggressively on price and hedging costs rose. Understanding the borrowing mix explains both the strength and the pressure.

The FY26 squeeze, explained

Here's the mechanism. When Indian banks are flush with deposits and looking to lend, they chase the same big, safe power projects PFC wants — and they can undercut PFC on rate. To keep winning good loans, PFC has to accept a slightly lower lending yield. Meanwhile, its ~25% foreign-currency borrowing has to be hedged against rupee moves, and when the rupee is volatile, that hedging gets expensive — raising the effective cost of funds. Squeeze the yield and lift the cost, and the spread compresses — which is why FY26's spread slipped to ~2.46% despite everything else going right. Management guided the spread to hold around 2.4–2.5% in FY27. This is the tension at the heart of the model: PFC's cheap-funding moat is real, but it is relative — and in a competitive, high-liquidity banking market, the gap to banks narrows.

A well-capitalised, top-rated lender can keep borrowing cheaply and keep lending — the spread pressure is a headwind on margin, not a threat to solvency.

How to think about the valuation

At roughly ₹430/share and a ~₹1.42 lakh crore market cap on ~₹33,600 Cr of consolidated profit, PFC trades around 5–5.5× earnings and ~1.1× book value, with a ~4.4% dividend yield and a ~20% return on equity. For a lender, the ratio that matters most is price-to-book against ROE: a financier earning ~20% on equity would, in a "normal" market, trade well above 1× book — a private NBFC with those returns might fetch 2–3×. PFC's barely-above-book multiple is the market applying a heavy "PSU discount": it prices in government control, policy/direction risk, the thin (and this year shrinking) spread, and lingering memory of the thermal-NPA era. The bull case is that a clean book, a ~20% ROE, a fat dividend and the REC-merger simplification eventually force a re-rating; the bear case is that the discount is structural and correctly prices lower-quality, government-steered earnings. Here, the debate is far more about the multiple than the earnings.

10

The dividend & the PSU-discount debate

Why PFC is a favourite of income investors — and why it stays cheap.

Two things define how the market treats PFC: it is a high-dividend-yield stock, and it wears a persistent PSU discount. The two are linked — the low price is exactly what makes the yield so high.

The dividend

  • ~₹18.55 per share paid for FY26 (final dividend ₹3.95 plus interims), against a share price around ₹430 — a dividend yield of ~4.4%, well above the market average.
  • Why the payouts are generous: as the majority owner, the Government of India relies on dividends from PSUs like PFC as a budget resource, so there is a structural incentive for steady, sizeable distributions. For a minority investor, that means a dependable income stream on top of any capital gain.
  • A yield and growth story: unusually, PFC combines a bond-like ~4%+ yield with a business still compounding profit at mid-teens — an income stock that also grows, which is rare.

The re-rating debate in one paragraph

Bulls argue PFC is a mispriced compounder: a ~20% ROE, a clean balance sheet, a structural power-capex tailwind, a fat dividend, and a coming REC merger that simplifies the story — all for ~1.1× book. On that view, even a modest narrowing of the discount, on top of the yield and book-value growth, is a strong total return. Bears counter that the discount has persisted for a decade for good reasons — government control, mandate risk and a thin spread — and that "cheap for a reason" PSUs can stay cheap indefinitely, making PFC a value trap that pays you to wait but never re-rates. The truth usually sits in between and moves with the cycle: in power-capex up-cycles and reform pushes the discount narrows; when spreads compress or policy risk flares, it widens again.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, cleaned-up, sovereign-backed lender — the debate is spread, direction and the discount.

PFC has done the hard part: it climbed out of a bad-loan crisis to a clean book, record profits and a ~20% ROE, sitting at the centre of a multi-year power-investment cycle with the cheapest funding in the sector. The investment debate now is about whether the spread holds, whether government direction helps or hurts, and whether the market ever pays a fair multiple.

The bull case

  • Cheap, quasi-sovereign funding — a top-rated, government-backed cost of funds no private rival can match; the durable core moat.
  • Structural capex tailwind — renewables (500 GW target), grid expansion and RDSS give years of lending demand, with PFC as the nodal channel.
  • Clean balance sheet — GNPA 1.09%, NNPA 0.15%, ~86% coverage; the thermal-NPA risk is largely resolved.
  • Cheap + high-yield — ~5.5× earnings, ~1.1× book on a ~20% ROE, with a ~4.4% dividend — a rare income-plus-growth combination.
  • REC merger optionality — a simpler ~₹11-lakh-crore giant could help close the discount.

The bear case

  • Spread compression — banks undercut on price and hedging costs rose; the spread slipped to ~2.46% and may stay ~2.4–2.5%, capping margin.
  • The PSU discount may be permanent — government control and mandate risk have kept the multiple near book for a decade.
  • Concentration & policy risk — ~75% government-linked exposure tied to state discom health and government direction.
  • One-off flattered profits — ~₹1,800 Cr of provision reversals boosted FY26; that tailwind fades as legacy cases close.
  • Merger execution — the REC swap ratio, tax treatment and timing carry relative-value and integration risk.
The bottom line

Power Finance Corporation is a cleaned-up, cheaply-priced, sovereign-backed lender sitting at the centre of India's power build-out. The business risk is far lower than a decade ago — the book is clean, capital is abundant, funding is cheap, and the capex tailwind is real. The two live questions are: can it defend the spread against bank competition and hedging costs so that strong loan growth becomes profit growth; and will a clean record plus the REC merger finally narrow the PSU discount. Watch a handful of numbers each quarter — the spread and cost of funds, loan-book growth and disbursements, fresh slippages and provision write-backs, and progress on the REC merger. Hold the spread and simplify the structure, and a ~20% ROE lender at ~1.1× book can re-rate while paying you a healthy dividend to wait; let the spread bleed and the discount hold, and it stays exactly what the market fears — a high-yield value trap.
