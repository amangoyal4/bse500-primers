00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

United Spirits Ltd (USL), which trades and markets itself as Diageo India, is India's largest spirits (hard-liquor) company. It makes, bottles and sells whisky, brandy, rum, vodka and gin — brands most Indians will recognise: McDowell's No.1, Royal Challenge, Signature, Antiquity and Black Dog, plus, through its parent Diageo, the imported icons Johnnie Walker, Black & White, VAT 69, Smirnoff, Ketel One and Tanqueray, and its own home-grown luxury single malt, Godawan. It is majority-owned (~56%) by the global drinks giant Diageo.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It's a premiumisation story, not a volume story. Volumes barely grow; the engine is mix — shifting the business toward higher-priced "Prestige & Above" (P&A) spirits. In FY26 (year ended Mar 2026), P&A was 89.6% of net sales, net sales value (NSV) rose ~7.7% to ₹12,467 Cr, and the standalone EBITDA margin expanded to ~18.4% with gross margin at 46.4% (+172 bps). Profit (standalone PAT) grew ~17.5% to ₹1,830 Cr.
  • Its single biggest external force is the government — 28 of them. Alcohol in India is a state subject: each state sets its own excise duties, fixes or approves prices, and in several states is the sole wholesaler or retailer. FY26 was a live demonstration — a new Maharashtra levy hammered the Popular segment while a re-entry into Andhra Pradesh added growth. USL cannot freely raise prices; it must ask 28 different governments.
  • A high-quality, high-return, richly-priced franchise. USL earns a ~27% ROCE, is essentially debt-light, throws off cash, and pays dividends — but at ~₹1.0 lakh Cr market cap on ~₹1,830 Cr of profit it trades near ~55× earnings. That premium multiple is paying for a decade more of premiumisation.
In plain terms

Think of USL as the company that owns India's drinks cabinet — but with a landlord (the state) that takes the biggest slice of every bottle before USL sees a rupee. Its growth trick isn't selling more bottles; it's getting India to "drink better, not more" — trading a drinker up from a cheap McDowell's peg to a Signature, then a Black Dog, then a Johnnie Walker. Each step up is worth far more revenue and far more margin for roughly the same litre of liquid. The catch: the government sets the rules of that game state by state, and can change them overnight.

01

Spirits economics 101: how a whisky business earns

No tasting notes required — just the four levers that drive the whole model.

A spirits company is, at heart, a branding-and-distribution business wrapped around a distilled liquid. It buys or produces alcohol, blends and bottles it, brands it, and sells it through a heavily-regulated channel. Four levers drive the economics.

The four levers of a spirits business
LeverWhat it means (and USL's number)
Volume (cases)How many cases are sold. A "case" is 9 litres. USL sells on the order of ~80 million cases a year — but volume growth is low-single-digit; this is not the growth engine.
Realisation / caseNet revenue per case. The premiumisation lever: richer mix lifts this even with flat volume. USL's realisation has climbed steadily (well past ₹1,900/case) as P&A share rose.
Gross marginNSV minus the cost of the liquid, glass and packaging. USL's FY26 gross margin was 46.4% — sensitive to input costs (ENA, glass, grain) and to mix.
A&P + overheadsAdvertising, promotion and fixed costs. USL spends heavily behind premium brands (~₹1,295 Cr A&P in FY26). What's left after this is EBITDA (~18.4% margin).
The simple profit equation

Roughly: NSV ≈ Cases × Realisation-per-case, then EBITDA ≈ NSV × Gross-margin − A&P − overheads. Because USL's volume barely moves, essentially all the growth comes from the second term — pushing realisation up via premium mix — and from holding costs in check. That's why "premiumisation" isn't marketing fluff for this company: it is, almost literally, the entire growth algorithm. Sell the same number of cases, but make each one a more expensive, higher-margin bottle, and both revenue and profit rise.

Why spirits is a "good" FMCG category — and a strange one

  • Brands are durable and pricing-powerful. A Johnnie Walker or a McDowell's No.1 is a decades-old trust mark. Consumers are loyal, and premium drinkers are relatively price-insensitive — a genuine moat.
  • Whisky dominates India. Whisky is by far the largest spirit by value in India, and USL is the market leader in whisky. That concentration plays to USL's strongest brands.
  • But it is uniquely un-free. Unlike soap or biscuits, a spirits maker cannot advertise on mass media (surrogate advertising only), cannot set its own retail price in many states, and sells through channels the government owns or tightly licenses. The "FMCG" label hides a business that is half consumer-brand, half regulated utility.
02

Gross sales vs Net Sales Value — the number that matters

Read this once and every headline about a liquor company makes sense.

Here is the single most important accounting quirk of an Indian spirits company. Its headline "revenue from operations" is enormous and misleading, because it includes the huge state excise duty that USL merely collects and hands to the government. The meaningful revenue line is Net Sales Value (NSV) — sales after stripping out that excise.

Roughly half or more of the gross figure is duty. NSV — what USL actually keeps as revenue — is the number every analyst, and this primer, uses.
Why NSV, not gross revenue

Imagine a bottle sells for ₹1,000 at the shop. The state might take ₹550 of that as excise duty; USL records the ₹1,000 as gross revenue but immediately pays the ₹550 to the government. It never keeps that money — it's a pass-through, like GST on your restaurant bill. So the ₹1,000 "revenue" tells you almost nothing about USL's business; the ₹450 NSV is what actually funds the liquid, the marketing and the profit. When you see "United Spirits revenue ₹27,816 Cr," mentally halve it: the business you're valuing is a ~₹12,500 Cr NSV company. Every growth, margin and per-case figure in the industry is quoted net of excise for exactly this reason.

This is also why margins look the way they do. USL's ~18% EBITDA margin is measured on NSV. Measured on gross revenue it would look like ~8% — which is why headlines sometimes quote a small-looking margin (e.g. "8.64% in Q4"): that's the gross-revenue basis. Same profit, different denominator. Throughout this primer, margins are on NSV unless stated.

03

The premiumisation engine — the master lever

The core of the entire investment case. Understand this and you understand USL.

USL divides its portfolio into two buckets, and the whole company is organised around moving value from one to the other. "Prestige & Above" (P&A) is the premium tier — the brands people trade up to. "Popular" is the cheap, mass, low-margin tier. Premiumisation is the deliberate, sustained shift of the business toward P&A — and it is the single biggest driver of both growth and margin.

How premiumisation actually creates value

  • Higher price per case. A P&A bottle sells for multiples of a Popular one. Shifting one case from Popular to P&A lifts realisation-per-case sharply — USL's blended realisation has climbed well past ₹1,900/case (from ~₹1,600 a few years earlier).
  • Higher margin per case. Premium brands carry richer gross margins (brand power, less price regulation at the top end), so the mix-shift expands margins even without cost cuts — visible in FY26's gross margin rising to 46.4%.
  • A structural demand tailwind. India has a large, young, growing legal-drinking-age population with rising incomes and urbanising tastes — the classic "premiumisation" set-up. Consumers are choosing to drink better, not more: fewer, nicer drinks. That secular trend is precisely what P&A is built to capture.
The "drink better, not more" driver

India's alcohol volumes grow slowly — social, health and regulatory factors cap how much the country drinks. But value can grow much faster than volume if drinkers keep upgrading. A young professional who once bought a regular whisky now buys a premium one; a premium drinker moves to Scotch; an aspirational consumer buys an Indian single malt as a status object. Each step is more NSV and more margin on the same litre of alcohol. This is why USL can post ~8% NSV growth on ~flat volumes, and why the ~90% P&A mix is treated as the company's crown jewel: it is the machine that converts India's rising affluence into profit, one trade-up at a time.

Watch the "excluding policy states" number. USL flagged that in FY26 its P&A segment grew ~11%+ excluding the two policy-affected states (Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh). That gap between headline growth (~7–8%) and underlying growth (~11%) is the tell: the premiumisation engine is running hard; state-level policy is the thing periodically standing on the brake.

The layers, decoded

  • Owned Prestige & Above — the volume backbone: McDowell's No.1 (one of the world's largest-selling whisky brands by volume), Royal Challenge, Signature, Antiquity, Black Dog. These are made and owned by USL and carry good margins.
  • Imported / "BII" (bottled-in-India) Diageo brands — USL manufactures, bottles and distributes Diageo's global portfolio locally: Johnnie Walker, Black & White, VAT 69, J&B (Scotch), Smirnoff, Ketel One, Cîroc (vodka), Tanqueray, Gordon's (gin), Captain Morgan (rum), Baileys. Making these in India (rather than importing finished) cuts duty and price, expanding the addressable market — a key premium growth vector. Smirnoff crossed 1 million cases in FY26.
  • Godawan — USL's home-grown Indian single malt, positioned as a craft/luxury product with "curated experiences." Tiny today, but a strategic halo: it signals USL can create top-of-ladder premium value itself, not just distribute Diageo's.
Why owning the whole ladder is the moat

Most rivals compete on one or two rungs. USL owns the entire staircase — from a ₹500 Popular bottle to a Godawan single malt — plus exclusive Indian access to Diageo's global vault. So when a consumer trades up, USL usually captures both the exit and the destination. That's a structurally better position than a single-brand or single-tier competitor, and it's why premiumisation is such a natural fit for this company specifically: the brands to trade people into are already on its own shelf.

05

The state-excise maze — the defining external force

The most important thing to understand about any Indian liquor company.

In India, alcohol is a state subject — Entry 8 of the State List in the Constitution gives each state government control over the "production, manufacture, purchase and sale of intoxicating liquor." Alcohol is also excluded from GST. The consequence: there is no single national liquor market. There are ~28 different markets, each with its own excise duty, price rules, distribution model and periodic shocks. This is the defining feature of USL's world.

How the state stands between USL and the drinker
Regulatory leverWhat it does — and why it constrains USL
Excise duty & VATEach state levies its own (often very high) duty — frequently the majority of the shelf price. States hike duties to plug budget gaps; a duty rise can price a brand out of a market overnight.
Price approval / MRP fixationUSL often cannot freely raise its own prices. Tamil Nadu sets MRPs via TASMAC; Delhi and others approve cost sheets; Rajasthan notifies prices annually. So when input costs rise, USL may be stuck waiting for a state to sanction a price increase — squeezing margins in the meantime.
State-monopoly distributionIn several states the government is the wholesaler/retailer — e.g. Tamil Nadu (TASMAC), Kerala (Bevco), and others. USL sells to the state, which then controls shelf access and pricing. Route-to-market varies wildly: auction, monopoly, open licence.
Bans & shocksProhibition (full in Bihar, Gujarat, and others), "dry days," the mid-2010s Supreme Court highway-liquor-ban, and abrupt new levies. Each can wipe out volume in a state with little warning.
FY26 in one word: Maharashtra

FY26 was a textbook illustration. Maharashtra introduced a new "Maharashtra Made Liquor (MML)" policy that reshaped duties and hit USL's Popular segment hard — Popular NSV fell ~13% in Q4 largely because of it. At the same time, USL re-entered Andhra Pradesh (which had earlier moved to a state-run model and then reopened to private players), adding a growth kicker. Net result: reported growth was dragged to ~7–8%, while "Rest of India" (excluding Maharashtra & AP) grew ~10.9%. Two states — one hurting, one helping — swung the entire company's headline. That is the excise maze in action: policy, not consumer demand, was the swing factor.

Moat and millstone. The regulation cuts both ways. It is a barrier to entry — the licences, state relationships, distillery footprint and compliance machinery needed to operate in 28 states are a formidable moat that protects incumbents like USL from newcomers and imports. But it is also a permanent constraint on pricing and a source of unpredictable shocks. USL can't simply pass on cost inflation the way a soap maker can; it must negotiate, state by state, with the very governments that also tax it. This is why a "consumer staples" company carries a risk profile closer to a regulated utility.
06

Portfolio-sharpening — pruning the Popular tail

The deliberate decision to sell profitable-but-low-quality revenue.

A counter-intuitive but pivotal move: USL deliberately shed a large chunk of its own Popular business to sharpen focus on premium. In 2022 it sold or franchised 43 Popular-segment brands to Inbrew Beverages — a decision that reduced revenue but improved the quality of the business. Understanding why is understanding how USL thinks about returns.

  • The transaction: in September 2022, USL completed a slump sale of 32 Popular brands (Haywards, Old Tavern, White Mischief, Honey Bee, Green Label, Romanov and others) to Inbrew for ~₹820 Cr, and franchised 11 more (including Bagpiper) for five years, with options for Inbrew to buy them out.
  • The logic: Popular brands are high-volume but low-margin, capital-hungry and heavily price-regulated at the mass end. They tied up management attention, working capital and distillery capacity that earned far better returns behind premium brands.
  • The effect: shedding low-return revenue mechanically lifted USL's margins, ROCE and P&A mix. It's why Popular collapsed to ~9% of NSV and P&A rose toward ~90%. The company chose a smaller, better business over a bigger, worse one.
The ROCE-upgrade logic (why less can be more)

Not all revenue is equal. A rupee of Popular sales might earn a thin margin and require heavy working capital and factory time; a rupee of Prestige sales earns far more and is less regulated. By franchising/selling the Popular tail, USL freed up capital and capacity to redeploy behind premium brands that earn a much higher return on that capital. The reported revenue base shrank, but return on capital employed rose — and a business earning ~27% ROCE on a focused premium portfolio is worth far more per rupee of sales than a sprawling one earning single digits on its mass tail. This is "portfolio-sharpening": trading volume for quality of earnings, a classic premium-FMCG playbook that USL executed decisively.

08

Input costs — ENA, glass and grain

The margin mechanics beneath the premiumisation gloss.

Premiumisation lifts the top of the P&L; input costs decide how much reaches the bottom. USL's gross margin swings on three commodities — and because it often can't re-price freely (see §05), input-cost spikes can bite before prices catch up.

  • ENA (Extra Neutral Alcohol) — the base spirit, distilled from grain or molasses. It's the core raw material. ENA prices move with grain/sugar cycles and with government ethanol-blending policy: when the state diverts alcohol to fuel-ethanol (to blend into petrol), less is available for potable spirits, pushing ENA prices up. India's expanding ethanol programme is a structural upward pressure on ENA.
  • Glass — bottles are a major packaging cost. Glass prices are cyclical and periodically disrupted (energy costs, furnace outages); supply crunches raise packaging costs quickly.
  • Grain — for grain-based spirits, cereal prices feed directly into ENA and gross margin.
How premiumisation absorbs cost shocks

Here's the elegant part of the strategy: a richer premium mix cushions input-cost volatility. A premium bottle has so much brand value baked into its price that a swing in glass or ENA cost is a smaller percentage of the total — so premium margins are more resilient. That's why USL could expand gross margin to 46.4% in FY26 (+172 bps) even amid ENA/glass pressure and ethanol-policy noise: the mix-shift did the heavy lifting. But the cushion isn't infinite — a sharp, simultaneous spike in ENA and glass, arriving while states withhold price increases, is the classic squeeze that has compressed USL's margins in past cycles. Input costs are the reason margins wobble quarter-to-quarter even as the long-term trend is up.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely premium franchise — priced for premiumisation to keep delivering.

USL is one of the cleaner premium-consumer stories in India: market leader, high returns, structural tailwind, world-class parent. The debate is about price and the ever-present hand of the state, not business quality.

The bull case

  • Structural premiumisation — a young, affluent, growing drinking population trading up for a decade-plus; USL owns the whole trade-up ladder.
  • Margin runway — P&A at ~90% and rising realisation keep expanding gross and EBITDA margins even on flat volumes.
  • Diageo firepower — exclusive Indian access to Johnnie Walker, Smirnoff, Tanqueray et al., plus parent strategy and discipline.
  • Focused, high-return franchise — post-pruning, ~27% ROCE, low debt, self-funded, dividend-paying.
  • Regulation as a moat — the 28-state maze that constrains USL also walls out newcomers and imports.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~55× earnings leaves little room for error; vulnerable to de-rating on any stumble.
  • State-policy shocks — a single state (Maharashtra "MML" in FY26) can knock several points off growth; duty hikes and bans are unpredictable.
  • No free pricing — price approvals lag input-cost spikes, squeezing margins in ENA/glass up-cycles.
  • Input-cost volatility — ethanol-blending policy structurally pressures ENA; glass and grain add noise.
  • Slow volumes & tough rivals — growth leans entirely on mix; Pernod Ricard and a premiumising Radico contest the same rupee.
The bottom line

United Spirits is a structurally advantaged premium-spirits leader executing a clear, working strategy: premiumise the mix, prune the tail, lean on Diageo's brands, and grind margins up. The business quality is high and the tailwind is real. The two things to watch each quarter are the premiumisation metrics (P&A growth, realisation per case, gross margin — is the engine still humming?) and state policy (which government just changed the rules, and where?). Get the first right and the compounding can justify the premium; underestimate the second, and a ~55× stock has a long way to fall when a state moves the goalposts. The genius — and the vulnerability — of this business is that it sells aspiration in a bottle, while the government decides the price.
