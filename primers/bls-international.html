00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

BLS International Services is an outsourcing company for governments. When a sovereign state decides it no longer wants to run the tedious, high-volume, front-desk part of issuing visas and passports — the queue, the forms, the fingerprint scanner, the photocopier, the courier — it puts that work out to competitive tender. BLS bids for those mandates and, when it wins, opens and staffs the application centres that travellers actually walk into. It collects a service fee per applicant, hands the completed file to the embassy, and the embassy alone decides whether the visa is granted. BLS never touches the decision. It monetises the paperwork around it.

Four facts frame everything that follows:

  • It is bigger and more global than almost anyone expects. BLS reports serving roughly 46 client governments with operations across 80+ countries, having started in 2005 with a single contract from the Portuguese Embassy in New Delhi. In FY26 it processed 44.1 lakh (4.41 million) applications.
  • FY26 was a record year — but the growth came from two different places. Consolidated revenue rose 36.7% to ₹2,998.2 Cr, EBITDA 30.1% to ₹818.9 Cr, PAT 34.1% to ₹723.8 Cr. Yet the visa business grew only ~11%; the headline number was carried by a digital/e-governance arm that more than doubled, largely through consolidating an acquisition.
  • It is a cash machine with no debt. Asset-light by construction — leased offices, staff, scanners, software — BLS ended FY26 with ₹1,434 Cr of net cash and a visa-segment EBITDA margin of 40.1%, up from 34.5% a year earlier.
  • The market has, nonetheless, halved its enthusiasm. Market capitalisation is around ₹9,700 Cr (approx., mid-2026), down roughly 38% over a year, putting the stock near 13× trailing FY26 earnings (approx.) — a de-rating that says the market is worried about something the P&L doesn't show.
In plain terms

Every time somebody in Delhi, Manila, Casablanca or Johannesburg queues up to submit a Schengen visa application, a private contractor is very likely running that room — checking the form is complete, taking the fingerprints, taking a fee, couriering the file to the embassy. BLS is one of a handful of companies in the world trusted to do that job, and it has been quietly winning country-pair after country-pair for two decades. The business is beautifully simple: applications in, fee per application, and the cost of a leased office and some staff. The complication is that BLS does not own its customers. It rents them, for three or five years at a time, from foreign ministries that can and do put the work back out to tender.

01

Visa outsourcing 101: who actually processes your visa

The industry almost nobody knows exists, explained from scratch.

A visa is a sovereign act. Only a state can decide who may enter it, and that decision — granted, refused, or referred — is made by a consular officer employed by that state. But a visa application is mostly clerical work: a form, a photograph, a passport, proof of funds, hotel bookings, insurance, ten fingerprints and a facial image. Multiply that by hundreds of thousands of applicants a year, in dozens of cities, in languages the embassy doesn't staff for, and it becomes an industrial process that embassies were never designed to run.

From the early 2000s, foreign ministries began separating the two. The decision stayed inside the embassy. The administration — reception, verification of completeness, biometric capture, fee collection, secure transport of documents, tracking, appointment scheduling, call centres, passport return — was contracted out to specialist private firms. That industry, "visa application centre" outsourcing, is what BLS competes in.

Why a government hands this to a contractor at all

Three reasons, and they compound. First, cost: the state gets a national network of application centres without hiring a single civil servant or signing a single lease — the applicant pays for it through the service fee. Second, reach: an embassy in New Delhi cannot economically open counters in fifteen Indian cities, but a contractor can, which widens the tourist and student catchment. Third, surge capacity: summer Schengen demand can be several times winter demand, and a private operator can staff up and down in a way a foreign ministry cannot. What the state keeps is the only thing it cannot delegate — the decision itself, and the legal responsibility for it.

02

How a mandate is won — and why growth arrives in steps, not slopes

The single most important structural fact about this company.

Most listed companies grow by selling a little more to a lot of customers. BLS grows by winning a whole country at a time. A ministry of foreign affairs publishes a tender; bidders are pre-qualified on financial strength, data-security certifications, insurance cover, track record and references from other governments; a technical evaluation is scored; a commercial bid is opened; a contract is awarded for a fixed term, typically three to five years, often with renewal options. On the day BLS wins, revenue from that country-pair goes from zero to a run rate. On the day it loses one at re-tender, the reverse happens just as fast.

This is why BLS's reported growth is jagged rather than smooth, and why the correct mental model is a ledger of mandates rather than a market-share curve. In FY26 and the months around it the ledger moved like this:

  • New wins: the High Commission of Cyprus in South Africa; the Embassy of Portugal for Morocco; the Embassy of Poland for the Philippines; an exclusive agreement with Slovakia.
  • Renewals: Spain renewed — commercially the more consequential event of the two categories, given Spain's Schengen volumes out of India; Qatar renewed and simultaneously extended BLS's attestation work into Uganda, the Seychelles and the DRC.
  • The pipeline: management has publicly sized the tenders expected to come to market over the next one to two years at roughly $1–2 billion of contract value across Europe, North America and elsewhere (management estimate, not a backlog).
The arithmetic that governs this company

BLS's visa income reduces to three numbers multiplied together: how many mandates it holds × how many applications each mandate generates × what BLS keeps per application. Nothing else in the visa business matters much. It is worth internalising because each of the three fails differently. Mandates are lost at re-tender — abruptly, and by political or commercial decision rather than by the company doing anything wrong. Applications per mandate move with travel demand, visa policy and the seasons — cyclically. Revenue per application moves with the contract's fee cap and how many travellers buy the optional extras — slowly, and mostly under BLS's own control. A company whose three growth levers break on three completely different clocks cannot be modelled as a single growth rate, and the market's habit of doing exactly that is part of why the stock swings so violently.

The moat, such as it is, is trust plus track record. A foreign ministry is handing a private company custody of its citizens' — and its applicants' — passports, biometric records and personal data, with the reputational risk of a data breach or a bribery scandal landing on the state, not the contractor. Pre-qualification therefore filters hard: two decades of references, ISO and data-protection certifications, financial capacity to fund fit-outs before revenue arrives, and demonstrated ability to open a compliant centre in an unfamiliar city within weeks. That filter is why the global industry has consolidated into a handful of credible names rather than fragmenting into hundreds of local operators, and why an incumbent with a clean service record usually starts a re-tender ahead.

It is a real barrier. It is not a permanent one. Contracts still end.

03

Visa & consular: the engine room

61% of revenue, roughly 90% of the profit.

The visa and consular segment posted FY26 revenue of ₹1,840.3 Cr, up 11.3%, with segment EBITDA of ₹737.8 Cr and a margin of 40.1% — sharply better than the 34.5% of FY25. Underneath that sit 44.1 lakh processed applications and a disclosed net revenue per application of ₹3,302, up 13.7%.

Put those two disclosures side by side and something important falls out.

Do the division. If revenue per application rose 13.7% while segment revenue rose 11.3%, then application volume did not grow in FY26 — it fell slightly (our arithmetic implies roughly 45 lakh applications in FY25 against 44.1 lakh in FY26, a low-single-digit decline; derived figure, approx.). The entire growth of BLS's core business last year came from charging more per applicant, not from serving more of them. That is a materially different story from the one the 36.7% headline tells, and it is the first thing to check in FY27's disclosures.

What sits inside the segment

  • Visa application processing — the core. Reception, document checks, biometric enrolment, fee collection, secure logistics to and from the mission, passport return. Fees are contractually capped; volumes are seasonal and peak in the northern-hemisphere summer.
  • Passport and citizen services — for governments outsourcing renewals and consular registration to their diaspora abroad, including work for the Indian government's own missions.
  • Attestation and legalisation — document authentication, a steady, unglamorous flow that travels with labour migration rather than tourism, which makes it a partial hedge against a tourism slump. The Qatar extension into Uganda, the Seychelles and the DRC is exactly this.
  • Value-added and premium services — the lever behind the ₹3,302-per-application number: prime-time appointments, premium lounges, doorstep collection, form assistance, photographs, courier. Almost pure incremental margin on a cost base that is already paid for.
  • Adjacent advisory — citizenship- and residency-by-investment advisory acquired via bolt-ons in the Gulf and Turkey (deal specifics approx.), which sell a much higher ticket to a much smaller number of clients.
Why the incremental margin is so high

A visa application centre is a fixed cost that has already been paid: the lease is signed, the counters are built, the biometric scanners are installed, the staff roster is set to cover the peak. Whether 300 or 380 people walk through that door on a Tuesday changes the revenue line meaningfully and the cost line barely at all. That is the whole reason a mature mandate throws off 40% margins while a freshly-won one loses money for a couple of quarters — fit-out and hiring land before the first applicant does. It also means the segment's profitability is unusually sensitive to volume declines in either direction: the operating leverage that makes a good year spectacular makes a soft one ugly.

04

The other half of the P&L: digital and e-governance

Small-town India, banking kiosks and loan sourcing — under the same roof as the Schengen counter.

Alongside the global consular business sits something almost entirely unrelated: a domestic Indian operation delivering government and financial services to people in small towns and villages who cannot easily reach a bank branch or a government office. It is run substantially through BLS E-Services, a subsidiary that was itself listed separately in 2024, and it was the loudest number in FY26 — revenue up 114.4% to ₹1,157.9 Cr.

What this business actually does

  • Business correspondent banking — an operator with a laptop, a fingerprint scanner and a bank tie-up runs a kiosk in a small town where a branch is uneconomic. Villagers deposit, withdraw, open accounts and receive government benefit transfers; the operator earns a commission per transaction and BLS takes a share.
  • Assisted digital / citizen services — helping people who are not comfortable online complete applications for certificates, licences, pensions and welfare schemes. India's e-governance push created millions of such transactions and almost none of the digital literacy needed to do them unaided.
  • Loan distribution — the growth driver of FY26, following the consolidation of Aadifidelis Solutions, a loan-sourcing business. Applications are originated on behalf of lenders; BLS earns a distribution commission.
  • Government project delivery — state-level digitisation and citizen-facing programme contracts.
Read the doubling carefully

Digital revenue did not double because twice as many villagers used a kiosk. It doubled substantially because BLS bought a loan-distribution business and consolidated its revenue into the group accounts. Acquired revenue is still revenue, but it is not the same evidence about the underlying franchise, and it comes at a very different quality: the derived numbers put digital's operating margin near 7% against the visa segment's 40%. Every rupee of growth from this leg therefore dilutes the group margin even as it flatters the growth rate — which is precisely what happened in FY26, when group EBITDA margin slipped to 27.3% from about 28.7% despite the visa business getting substantially more profitable.

Is the digital leg a mistake? Not obviously. Loan distribution and banking correspondence are genuinely large, genuinely under-served markets, and they diversify a company whose fortunes otherwise depend entirely on foreign ministries and airline seats. They are also counter-cyclical to travel in a mild way: a global downturn that suppresses tourism does not necessarily suppress rural benefit transfers.

But the two businesses share almost nothing — not customers, not skills, not competitive dynamics, not margins, not currencies. The visa business competes against two global specialists for sovereign contracts; the digital business competes against fintechs and correspondent networks for commission on small-ticket transactions. Managing both well is a genuine test, and the group's reported margin will keep drifting down for as long as the low-margin half grows faster than the high-margin one. That drift is arithmetic, not deterioration — but it will look like deterioration on a screen, and investors should decompose before concluding.

The company, the Aggarwals, and how it got here

Two decades from one embassy contract to 46 governments.

BLS International is a promoter-controlled, New Delhi-headquartered company built by the Aggarwal family, who together hold roughly 70.4% of the equity (March 2026) — an unusually high level of promoter ownership for a company of this size, and one that cuts both ways. Alignment is high; minority protection depends heavily on governance quality rather than on any balance of voting power.

The origin story is genuinely small. In 2005 the group won a single contract to handle visa applications for the Portuguese Embassy in New Delhi. Everything since has been a compounding of that one credential: each government served became a reference for the next tender, each new country added operating know-how, and the network effect in this industry is almost purely reputational.

The thing that should surprise you

A sovereign government — several dozen of them — has concluded that the safest place for its applicants' passports, fingerprints and personal files is a contractor listed on the Bombay Stock Exchange and controlled by one Indian family. Nothing about the visa counter you walk into in Manila or Madrid signals this. The signage is consular, the queue is official, the staff behave like officialdom. The commercial reality is a services company earning a capped fee per head, with its shares quoted daily, its quarterly margins scrutinised by analysts, and its ability to keep that counter dependent on winning a tender round it does not control. The gap between how governmental the experience feels and how thoroughly commercial the arrangement is remains the most underappreciated fact about this industry.

Group structure, in brief

  • BLS International Services Ltd — the listed parent; holds the global visa, consular, passport and attestation mandates through a web of country subsidiaries and joint ventures.
  • BLS E-Services Ltd — the separately listed domestic digital/e-governance arm, consolidated into the parent's accounts.
  • Acquired units — loan distribution (Aadifidelis) and citizenship/residency advisory bolt-ons, sitting inside the respective segments.

A structure of this shape — many small overseas entities, several partly-owned, plus a separately listed subsidiary and a string of acquisitions — is normal for an operator of country-specific mandates. It is also, unavoidably, harder for an outside investor to audit than a single-entity business, which is where §11's governance discussion begins.

06

Where the FY26 margin actually came from

A quiet operating change worth more than any contract win last year.

The visa segment's EBITDA margin moved from 34.5% to 40.1% in a single year. On ₹1,840 Cr of revenue, those 5.6 percentage points are worth over ₹100 Cr of operating profit — comfortably more than any individual new mandate announced during the year contributed. The company attributes it to cost efficiencies and a transition to a self-managed business model. That phrase deserves unpacking, because it describes a genuine strategic shift rather than a cost-cutting drive.

Historically, a global visa operator expanding into a new country often does not open the centre itself. It appoints a local partner, franchisee or sub-contractor who leases the premises, hires the staff and runs the counter, remitting a share of the fee upward. It is fast, capital-light and low-risk — and it gives away a large slice of the economics permanently. Going self-managed means BLS takes those centres in-house: it signs the leases, employs the staff, owns the operations, and keeps the whole fee rather than a share of it.

The trade being made

Insourcing a centre converts a variable cost into a fixed one. Under a partner model, when applications fall, so does the partner's share — the cost flexes down automatically and BLS's downside is cushioned. Once BLS holds the lease and the payroll, a soft season lands entirely on its own P&L. So the 40.1% margin is not simply "better management"; it is a deliberate exchange of downside protection for upside participation. In a growing travel market that is clearly the right trade. In the next border-closure or travel shock, the same decision will amplify the damage — and it is worth remembering that this business has already lived through one such event, in 2020, when volumes went to nearly nothing.

The second, less discussed contributor is revenue per application. The disclosed ₹3,302 net revenue per applicant is not primarily the base service fee — that is usually capped by the contract and cannot be raised at will. It is the base fee plus whatever share of applicants buy premium appointments, courier returns, form-filling assistance, photographs and lounge access. Raising attachment rates on those extras is one of the few levers BLS can pull unilaterally inside an existing mandate, and it flows through at very high incremental margin because the centre is already staffed and open.

There is a natural ceiling to it. Service fees are regulated by the contracting government precisely to stop applicants being gouged, and premium attach rates cannot rise forever on a price-sensitive base of students and family travellers. A business whose core growth in FY26 came entirely from revenue per head, on flat volumes, is drawing on the shorter of its two reserves.

07

Footprint, geography and the travel tailwind

The demand backdrop — and why it is less uniformly favourable than it looks.

BLS reports operations across 80+ countries serving roughly 46 client governments. That footprint is not evenly valuable: a mandate's worth is set by the volume of the corridor it covers, not by the number of flags on the map. A Schengen mandate covering India — a country sending millions of students, tourists and business travellers to Europe every year — is worth many times a mandate in a small, low-traffic corridor.

The structural demand drivers

  • Outbound travel from emerging markets. Rising middle-class incomes in India, South-East Asia, the Gulf and Africa translate almost mechanically into first-time passport holders and first-time visa applicants — the exact demographic that needs an assisted, physical application centre rather than a self-service portal.
  • Student and labour mobility. Study-abroad and work-migration flows are far less discretionary than leisure travel and generate the associated attestation, document-legalisation and police-clearance work that BLS also monetises.
  • More outsourcing, not less. Consular budgets are flat while application volumes grow; each budget cycle pushes another ministry toward contracting out the front end. Management's $1–2 billion tender-pipeline estimate reflects a market still converting from in-house to outsourced.
  • Biometric mandates. Every regime that requires fingerprints requires physical presence. Regulation, in this one respect, is BLS's friend: it makes the application centre legally necessary rather than merely convenient.
The driver that works in the other direction

Visa policy is set by politics, not by demand, and it moves without notice. A destination country that liberalises — moving to a full e-visa, granting visa-free access, or extending validity so travellers apply once every ten years instead of every two — permanently removes applications from the system for that corridor. The reverse is also true: a tightening regime, or new biometric requirements, creates volume. BLS's addressable market is therefore a policy variable dressed up as a demand variable, and no amount of operational excellence protects a corridor from a bilateral agreement signed in a capital city. The Gulf's steady move toward instant online visas is the live example of this risk; the Schengen area's biometric requirements are the live example of the protection.

Currency deserves a note too. A large share of visa revenue is earned in foreign currencies while a large share of the group's costs — and all of the digital segment — sit in rupees. A depreciating rupee is a quiet tailwind to reported revenue and margin that has nothing to do with the underlying business, and it should be stripped out before extrapolating a growth rate. Similarly, the group's effective tax rate is strikingly low — Q4 FY26 shows profit before tax of ₹203.6 Cr converting to PAT of ₹186.9 Cr, an effective rate under 10% — reflecting where the profits are earned rather than any aggressive domestic structuring. It is a real and durable feature of the model, but it means comparing BLS's PAT growth against a domestic peer's is not comparing like with like.

08

VFS, TLScontact — and the embassy itself

A market with very few bidders and one very large one.

Global visa outsourcing is close to an oligopoly, and BLS is not the largest member of it. The competitive set is short, which is both the industry's great attraction and the source of its sharpest risk: with so few credible bidders, every tender is a genuine coin-toss between a handful of names, and losing one costs an entire country's revenue at once.

Why price competition is dangerous here

In most industries a bidder can accept a thin margin to win volume and improve it later. In sovereign outsourcing the fee is usually capped for the contract's full term, so the price bid on day one is the price for three to five years, with wage inflation and rent escalation absorbed entirely by the contractor. A tender won too cheaply is not a foothold — it is a locked-in low-margin obligation that also consumes the management bandwidth and capital required to fit out and staff a national network. This is why "we bid disciplined" is a more meaningful statement from a visa outsourcer than from almost any other kind of services company, and why a run of contract wins should always be read alongside the segment margin rather than instead of it.

The countervailing point is that incumbency genuinely matters. A ministry mid-way through a functioning contract, with no complaints and no data incidents, has a strong administrative incentive to renew rather than to run a full transition — retendering means new premises, new staff vetting, migration of appointment systems and a real risk of a service disruption that lands in the newspapers. BLS's FY26 renewals with Spain and Qatar are evidence that the incumbency advantage is working. It is evidence about the past, not a guarantee about the next round.

09

The re-tender cliff

The structural risk that no operating improvement can remove.

Everything good about BLS — the 40% segment margins, the negligible capital intensity, the ₹1,434 Cr of net cash, the two-decade compounding of references — rests on a foundation the company does not own. Its revenue is a set of fixed-term contracts with sovereign counterparties, each of which expires. When one expires, the work returns to the open market and BLS bids for its own existing business against VFS, TLScontact and anyone else pre-qualified.

This produces a risk profile with an unusual shape. It is not the gentle erosion of a business losing share to a better product. It is a step function: revenue from a mandate runs at full rate right up to the transition date, and then stops. There is no warning period visible in the operating metrics, because a contract that is about to be lost performs exactly like a contract that is about to be renewed. Application volumes do not decay first. Margins do not compress first. The information arrives as an announcement.

What this means practically. A single large corridor lost at re-tender could remove a meaningful share of visa-segment revenue in one step — and because the cost base of a self-managed centre network is now largely fixed, the profit impact would be proportionally worse than the revenue impact. BLS does not disclose a mandate-by-mandate revenue breakdown, so an outside investor cannot size this precisely. That opacity is itself part of the risk: you can observe the portfolio's aggregate output, but not its concentration.

Three things mitigate it, none of them fully:

  • Diversification across the portfolio. With roughly 46 client governments and 80+ countries of operation, no single mandate should be existential. Breadth is BLS's real insurance policy, and every new country-pair — Cyprus in South Africa, Poland in the Philippines, Portugal in Morocco — thins the concentration a little further.
  • Staggered expiries. Contracts do not come up together, so losses and wins tend to partially offset within any given year rather than arriving as one shock.
  • A high renewal base rate. Incumbents with clean records usually win. Spain and Qatar renewed in FY26.
The scoreboard to keep

Because BLS's growth is a portfolio problem rather than a market-share problem, the most useful thing an investor can maintain is not a revenue model but a ledger: every mandate won, every mandate renewed, every mandate lost, dated. Company announcements and embassy notices make this constructible from the outside, if tediously. Two or three years of that ledger tells you more about BLS's durability than any quarterly result will, because it measures the only thing the business ultimately runs on — whether governments keep choosing it. A year of contract wins with no disclosed losses is a genuinely strong signal. A year of wins alongside a quiet non-renewal in a major corridor is a very different year wearing the same headline growth rate.

The valuation puzzle

Market capitalisation is around ₹9,700 Cr (approx., mid-2026) — roughly 38% below where it stood a year earlier. Against FY26 PAT of ₹723.8 Cr that is about 13× trailing earnings (approx.), and stripping out the ₹1,434 Cr of net cash brings the operating business closer to 11×. For a debt-free, asset-light services company that compounded profit 34% last year with a 40%-margin core, that is not a demanding multiple. It is the multiple of a business the market has decided to distrust.

Two explanations compete, and an investor has to pick one:

  • The market is pricing the contract-portfolio risk correctly. Earnings that depend on renewable sovereign mandates arguably deserve a lower multiple than earnings that depend on owned assets or sticky consumer habits, because the terminal value is contingent on tender outcomes nobody can forecast. On this reading, 13× is not cheap — it is the right price for revenue you rent.
  • The market is pricing something else. Questions have been aired publicly over recent years about the group's related-party dealings, the accounting for its acquisitions, and the transparency of its many overseas subsidiaries; the company has rejected such characterisations. This primer takes no view on the merits, and readers should go to primary filings rather than to commentary. But a persistent governance discount would explain a de-rating that the operating results plainly do not.
The one number that settles it

Neither revenue growth nor the P/E answers the question. The metric that decides whether BLS is a compounding sovereign-services franchise or a rented earnings stream is the visa segment's applications processed × net revenue per application, tracked against the mandate ledger — that is, 44.1 lakh and ₹3,302 in FY26, and what those two numbers do next while the contract portfolio wins, renews or loses. If applications return to growth and revenue per application holds and the ledger shows renewals outnumbering losses, the 40.1% segment margin is durable and the current multiple is an anomaly. If FY27 delivers another year of flat volumes carried by fee increases, the company will have spent its shorter reserve and the market's caution will look like foresight. Everything else in the disclosure — the digital doubling, the acquisitions, the headline growth rate — is commentary on that one line.

Future, risks, bull and bear

What has to go right, what can go wrong, and what to watch first.

BLS enters FY27 with the operating position of a much-admired company and the share price of a suspected one. Management has spoken of roughly doubling the business by the end of the decade (approx., management commentary), funded from internal cash and bolt-on acquisitions rather than borrowing. Whether that happens turns almost entirely on the mandate portfolio and on whether visa volumes resume growing.

The case for

  • A structurally growing market. Consular budgets are flat while applications rise; outsourcing penetration is still incomplete. Management sizes the tenders coming to market at $1–2 billion over one to two years.
  • Economics that are genuinely good. A 40.1% segment EBITDA margin, negligible capital intensity, and an effective tax rate under 10% because profits arise overseas.
  • Net cash of ₹1,434 Cr and no debt — enough to fund fit-outs for several new national networks without touching the equity, and enough to keep bidding through a downturn when a thinly capitalised rival cannot.
  • Renewals are landing. Spain renewed, Qatar renewed and expanded into three new attestation markets — the incumbency advantage is visibly working.
  • Biometrics are regulation on BLS's side. Physical fingerprint capture keeps the application centre legally necessary in most major regimes.
  • A cheap multiple for the growth. ~13× trailing, ~11× ex-cash, after 34% PAT growth.

The case against

  • The re-tender cliff. Revenue is a set of expiring sovereign contracts. A major corridor lost is an immediate step down, with no early warning in the operating metrics and no mandate-level disclosure to size the exposure.
  • FY26's core grew on price, not volume. Applications appear to have declined slightly while revenue per application rose 13.7% (derived, approx.) — the fee lever has less room left than the volume lever.
  • Margin mix is drifting the wrong way. Group EBITDA margin fell to 27.3% because a ~7%-margin digital business is growing far faster than the 40%-margin core, and much of that growth was acquired rather than organic.
  • Self-managed centres cut both ways. The insourcing that lifted margins also converted variable cost into fixed cost, removing the cushion that protected the P&L in the last travel shock.
  • Policy risk is uninsurable. An e-visa regime, a visa-waiver agreement or longer validity periods can delete a corridor's volume by treaty, regardless of how well BLS operates.
  • Governance questions have been aired around related-party dealings, acquisition accounting and subsidiary transparency; the company disputes them, but the market's ~38% de-rating suggests the discount is being applied regardless.
  • VFS Global is larger and bids for the same mandates, with a fee that is fixed for the contract's whole term once won.

What to watch, in order

  • Applications processed in FY27. Does the 44.1 lakh figure grow, or does another year of fee-led growth arrive on flat volumes? This is the first thing to check in every result.
  • Net revenue per application. Whether ₹3,302 can hold once the easy premium-attach gains are banked.
  • The mandate ledger. Announced wins, announced renewals — and, critically, any corridor that quietly stops being mentioned.
  • Visa segment EBITDA margin. Whether 40.1% survives contact with newly-won mandates, which lose money before they make it.
  • Organic vs acquired growth in digital. Strip out consolidation effects; the underlying kiosk and correspondent business should be able to stand on its own.
  • Cash conversion and receivables. In a business with government counterparties across 80+ countries, the quality of the ₹1,434 Cr cash pile and the ageing of receivables is the most direct answer to the accounting questions.
The bottom line

BLS has built something unusual: a private Indian company holding the administrative trust of roughly 46 sovereign states, running counters in 80-plus countries, earning 40% margins on a business that requires almost no capital and pays almost no tax. It is also a company that does not own a single one of its customer relationships outright — each is a term contract that will one day return to the open market. That contradiction, not the growth rate, is what the market is arguing about at ~13× earnings. The next data point that will move the argument is specific and dated: FY27's applications-processed number, published alongside the first-quarter results. If it grows while ₹3,302 per application holds, the flat-volume year of FY26 was a pause. If it does not, BLS will have gone two consecutive years without adding a single net applicant to the counters it runs — and a services company that stops serving more people has to explain what, exactly, it is compounding.
