Jindal SAW, the pipes for gas and water

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Jindal SAW makes the big steel pipes that move fluids over distance. Its name comes from Submerged Arc Welding — the technique for rolling and welding thick steel plate into pipe up to roughly two metres wide. It is one of India's largest pipe makers, and it is genuinely three businesses under one roof: large-diameter line pipe (LSAW and HSAW) for cross-country oil-and-gas transmission; ductile-iron (DI) pipe for city water supply and sewage; and seamless tube for oil-well casing and industrial use. It backs all three with its own iron-ore pellet, coke and pig-iron capacity. Its customers are oil majors, gas utilities, state water boards and export buyers in the US and Gulf.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It earns like a contractor, not a commodity trader. A spot-price steelmaker rises and falls with the HR-coil ticker. Jindal SAW instead wins tenders, builds an order book, and executes it over 9–12 months. Its backlog hit a record ~$1.48 bn during FY26 — years of contracted work already sold. The whole business is best read through that backlog, not through the daily steel price.
  • Two very different fluids share one order book. The same company's pipe carries natural gas across countries and drinking water to villages. Energy-pipeline spending is lumpy, cyclical and geopolitics-sensitive; municipal-water spending is slow, steady and policy-driven. Bolting them together is a natural hedge that few single-product pipe makers can run.
  • FY26 was a hard year for the P&L but not for demand. Revenue fell ~14% to ~₹17,987 Cr and PAT dropped ~37% to ~₹925 Cr, hit by a MENA-region conflict that froze certain exports and a temporary API-monogram suspension on seamless pipe. EBITDA margin slipped from ~14.3% to ~10%. Yet the order book rose to a record across the same year. That divergence — weak print, full backlog — is the entire tension in the name.

In plain terms

Jindal SAW is a project supplier that happens to sell steel pipe. A developer building a gas grid or a state piping water to every home does not buy pipe off a shelf — it runs a tender, qualifies a mill, and awards a contract the mill then delivers over the better part of a year. So the market's habit of pricing this company against steel prices misses the point: the business actually runs on how many kilometres of pipe it has been contracted to deliver, and at what margin it converts them. Watch the backlog, not the coil ticker. The catch, and the reason FY26 stung, is that a full order book only becomes profit when the pipe is delivered and paid for — and that last mile can stall on an export freeze, a certification pause, or a slow-paying government buyer.

01

What a "SAW pipe" actually is

No metallurgy degree needed — just the handful of pipe types that drive the whole business.

Move oil, gas or water a few hundred kilometres and you need pipe that will not leak under pressure for decades. The bigger the diameter and the higher the pressure, the more the pipe stops being a commodity and becomes an engineered product with a paper trail. To follow the company, you need to tell four kinds of pipe apart — because they are, underneath, four different businesses.

The four pipe families you need to know
TypeWhat it is (and why it matters here)
LSAWLongitudinal SAW — a flat steel plate rolled into a cylinder and welded along one straight seam down its length. Thick wall, tight tolerances, heavy testing. The choice for the highest-pressure, large-diameter oil-and-gas trunk lines.
HSAWHelical (spiral) SAW — a continuous steel coil fed in at an angle and welded in a spiral. Cheaper per tonne for large diameters and long runs; favoured for water transmission and lower-pressure gas.
DI pipeDuctile iron — a different metallurgy entirely: molten iron spun in a mould (centrifugal casting) to make pipe that flexes rather than cracks. The workhorse of municipal water supply and sewerage.
SeamlessPierced and drawn from a solid steel billet with no weld seam at all. Essential where a weld would be a weak point — oil-well casing and tubing (OCTG), boilers, high-pressure industrial lines.
Why the welding method matters

A buried gas trunk line that fails is a catastrophe, so buyers specify pipe to API and equivalent standards and audit the mill before they will accept a bid. That certification is a moat: a new entrant cannot simply undercut on price, because the pipe has to be qualified before it can even be offered. It also cuts the other way — lose an API monogram, as Jindal SAW's seamless line briefly did in FY26, and a whole product's order flow pauses until it is restored. In this industry a certificate is not paperwork; it is a licence to bid.

So "pipe" spans three genuinely different economics under one roof: a heavy-engineering, project-tender business (line pipe); a steady municipal-infrastructure business (DI water pipe); and a specialised-metallurgy business (seamless). They share a customer instinct — someone building infrastructure — but little else about how they earn.

The value-add layer that is easy to miss

Line pipe is rarely sold bare. It is externally coated against corrosion, sometimes internally lined, and often supplied with bends, connectors and field-joint services. Each of those steps adds margin and stickiness — a buyer who has qualified a supplier's coated-pipe package does not lightly re-tender the whole scope. The more of a pipe's finished specification a mill can deliver in-house, the further it sits from bare tonnage and the closer it sits to being a project supplier. That is the direction Jindal SAW has spent two decades moving: from selling steel-shaped-as-pipe toward selling a qualified, coated, tested, delivered-to-site system.

Who buys pipe

  • Oil & gas transmission companies — building national gas grids, city-gas distribution networks and LNG-terminal connectivity. The traditional large-ticket buyer for LSAW/HSAW line pipe.
  • State water boards & municipal bodies — executing Jal Jeevan and AMRUT piped-water and sewerage schemes. The steady, policy-driven buyer for DI pipe.
  • Export buyers & drillers — US and Gulf pipeline projects for line pipe, and oil-and-gas producers for seamless OCTG. Higher-margin, qualification-gated demand that lifts a good year and, when it freezes, deepens a bad one.
02

Two fluids, one order book

The defining idea — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Here is the single sentence that captures Jindal SAW and describes almost no other listed company: the same firm's pipe carries natural gas across countries and drinking water to villages — and both jobs sit inside one backlog. Energy and water, the two fluids a developing economy must move at scale, run through the same order-book machine.

Energy pipe is lumpy and cyclical; water pipe is slow and structural. Both convert through the same backlog — a weak gas year can be partly cushioned by a strong water year, and the reverse.

That combination is strategically unusual. Energy-pipeline spending follows the oil-and-gas capex cycle, big one-off trunk-line awards, and geopolitics — it arrives in chunks and can freeze overnight. Municipal-water spending is the opposite: slower, steadier, policy-driven, and structurally rising as India tries to pipe treated water to every household. Running both inside one company means the order book is not hostage to a single end-market. Jindal SAW is, in effect, operating a natural hedge that most single-product pipe makers simply cannot.

The counter-intuitive part: the glamorous product — the giant oil-and-gas line pipe an export buyer flies in to inspect — is the volatile one. The unglamorous grey water pipe buried under an Indian street is the steadier growth engine. In a pipe company, boring is the feature.
The device that only fits Jindal SAW: two fluids, one backlog

Most infrastructure suppliers you can read through a single end-market. Jindal SAW needs two, and the interplay between them is the point. When you look at any quarter, the real question is not "what did revenue do?" but "how much of each fluid is the order book carrying this year, and at what margin does it convert?" A backlog leaning toward high-pressure export line pipe promises a very different margin than one loaded with domestic low-pressure pipe or slow-paying government water work. The two fluids are not a marketing line — they are the two dials you have to read together to understand the company at all.

Keep that frame for the rest of this primer. Every segment below is really a question of how much of each fluid the order book is carrying, and how cleanly it turns into cash.

03

The company, the group, and who controls it

The B.C. Jindal / P.R. Jindal side of the house — and why people confuse it.

The Jindal name spreads across several separately listed groups, and confusing them is the single most common mistake investors make here. Jindal SAW belongs to the B.C. Jindal Group, associated with Prithvi Raj (P.R.) Jindal — a different branch from the O.P. Jindal companies (JSW Steel, JSPL, Jindal Stainless) that dominate the headlines. Different family branch, different balance sheet, different fortunes.

Ownership & structure

04

Line pipe: the oil & gas engine

LSAW + HSAW — the lumpy, high-ticket business that gives the company its name.

Large-diameter SAW line pipe is the segment that gives Jindal SAW its name and its cyclicality. It is sold into big, discrete projects — a national gas-grid expansion, a city-gas distribution rollout, a cross-border trunk line, or an export order for a US or Gulf pipeline. When a large award lands, it fills the mill for months; when the gap between awards widens, capacity sits idle.

  • Domestic demand — India's push to raise gas in the energy mix drives the national gas grid, City Gas Distribution (CGD) networks and LNG-terminal connectivity, all of which need welded line pipe. Policy-backed and multi-year, but it arrives as chunky awards, not a smooth annuity.
  • Export demand — Jindal SAW is a qualified supplier into the US and Middle East pipeline markets. Exports lift margins and utilisation when they land — but they are exactly what geopolitics can freeze, as the FY26 MENA-conflict disruption showed.
  • Economics — the key input is HR coil / steel plate. Line pipe is a conversion business: the spread between steel cost and pipe price, plus how full the mill runs, sets the margin. Big orders fill the mill; gaps between orders hurt disproportionately.
Why this segment swings

A single large export or trunk-line award can move a year's line-pipe revenue meaningfully, and its absence can leave capacity idle. That is why the reported P&L can look volatile even when the long-run demand story — more gas pipeline, more CGD, more export qualification — is fully intact. The order book is the early-warning system for this segment: it tells you what the mill is contracted to roll long before the revenue shows up on the income statement.

FY26 is the case study. The underlying demand did not vanish, but the timing broke — export routes disrupted, some deliveries pushed — and line-pipe revenue and margin took the hit even as fresh orders kept filling the backlog for later years. A steelmaker with a bad year usually has weak demand; a line-pipe maker with a bad year can have a full order book and a bad delivery window.

Why exports matter out of all proportion to their tonnage

It is worth being concrete about why exports drive this segment's profitability. A domestic line-pipe tender is fought on price against Welspun, Man and others in the same home market; an export order to a US or Gulf pipeline is fought on qualification — the buyer needs a mill that has already cleared its stringent audits, and there are only a handful worldwide. Winning that order therefore tends to come at a better realisation and fills the mill with high-value work. This is why a single large export award can visibly lift a year, and why losing access to an export market — through a conflict, a sanction, or a trade barrier — hurts far more than the lost tonnage alone would suggest. The line-pipe segment's best years and worst years are usually written in the export column.

Ductile iron: the water engine

DI pipe — the quiet compounding core, and the real surprise of the company.

If line pipe is the volatile star, ductile-iron pipe is the quiet compounding core — and, per company commentary, DI has grown into the largest share of the order book. It is levered to a spending wave that is arguably the most durable in Indian infrastructure: getting treated, piped water to every home.

India's water programmes translate directly into kilometres of DI distribution pipe — a multi-year, policy-funded order stream that keeps the backlog full when the oil-and-gas cycle turns down.
  • Jal Jeevan Mission (JJM): the flagship "har ghar jal" programme to give every rural household a functional tap connection. Laying that much distribution pipe is a direct, multi-year DI-pipe order stream.
  • AMRUT: urban water supply, sewerage and septage management across hundreds of cities — the town-side complement to JJM.
  • Why DI, not plastic or steel: ductile iron flexes without cracking, handles pressure, and lasts decades underground — the default municipal specification for water mains at scale.
The genuine surprise

Most people picturing a "SAW pipe company" imagine oil-and-gas mega-projects and export inspectors on a factory floor. But the segment doing the steadiest heavy lifting is the humble grey water pipe buried under village and city streets. Water infrastructure — policy-funded, recession-resistant, and enormous in scope — is the ballast that keeps the order book full when the glamorous energy cycle turns down. The unglamorous product is the growth engine, and that inversion is the single most useful thing to internalise about the company.

The real caveat is payment. Government water contracts can be slow to pay, which loads working capital onto the balance sheet. Steady demand, sticky cash — that trade-off is the DI segment's signature, and it is a big part of why net debt matters so much in the thesis (§08–09).

Why scale narrows the field

Scale matters here in a way it does not for line pipe. A city water board placing a JJM or AMRUT order wants a supplier that can deliver tens of thousands of tonnes of certified DI pipe on a schedule, across a state, without the mill running dry mid-project. Few players can. That size requirement — plus the metallurgy know-how of centrifugal casting and the cement-mortar lining that water pipe needs — quietly narrows the field to a handful of credible bidders, which is why DI has held margin better than the more contested line-pipe segment. The demand is public and enormous; the qualified supply is not. For a country still connecting its last several crore rural households to piped water and rebuilding ageing urban mains, that supply-demand asymmetry is the DI segment's structural advantage — and the reason it, not the oil-and-gas headline product, is the part of the order book most worth compounding.

06

Seamless: OCTG & industrial

The specialised-metallurgy leg — smallest, but highest-quality when it runs clean.

Seamless pipe has no weld seam — it is pierced and drawn from a solid billet — which makes it the choice wherever a weld would be the weak point. The marquee use is OCTG: oil-country tubular goods, the casing and tubing that line and produce an oil or gas well. It also serves boilers and high-pressure industrial lines.

  • OCTG demand: tied to drilling and well activity at oil-and-gas producers — a different, more upstream driver than the mid-stream line-pipe business.
  • Certification-heavy: seamless OCTG runs on API qualifications. In FY26 a temporary API-monogram suspension paused part of this order flow — a sharp reminder that in this business a certificate is a licence to bid.
  • Industrial & boiler tube: a steadier baseline of demand from power, refining and general engineering that underpins the segment between drilling cycles.
The FY26 seamless disruption is instructive: nothing was wrong with the market for the pipe — the issue was a compliance/audit pause on the mill's certification. That is a self-inflicted, recoverable problem rather than a demand collapse, but it still dents a year's numbers, which is exactly why segment certification status is worth tracking alongside order intake.
Why seamless is the option value in the portfolio

Seamless is the smallest of the three legs but arguably the highest-quality one when it runs well. OCTG is a global, dollar-priced product with real technical barriers, so a qualified seamless mill can earn export-grade margins that neither commoditised line pipe nor tender-driven DI can match. The flip side is concentration risk: because it leans on oil-field drilling activity and a short list of certifications, a single audit pause or a downturn in well counts hits it disproportionately. Treat seamless as the small, high-beta option in the mix — capable of punching above its weight when certification is clean and drilling is strong, but never the leg to hang the whole thesis on.

Backward integration: pellets, coke, pig iron

Owning the inputs — a margin cushion that carries its own commodity wobble.

Jindal SAW does not only buy inputs — it makes some of them. Iron-ore pellets, metallurgical coke and pig iron feed its own DI-pipe and pipe operations and are also sold externally. The point is control: over cost, over quality, and over supply security for the metal that becomes pipe.

Integration is a margin lever that cuts both ways

When input prices spike, owning pellet and pig-iron capacity cushions the cost line and protects DI margins — the integration steadies the water business. But it also ties up capital in commodity-linked assets whose own selling prices swing, so the same integration that calms one part of the P&L adds a second, ore-price-linked wobble to another. And note the split: for the SAW line-pipe and seamless side, the binding input is still externally bought HR coil / steel plate, so pass-through of steel cost into pipe price remains the margin question there. Integration is a real advantage for water pipe and a partial one for the group — not a blanket shield across every segment.

08

The order-book machine

The single most important idea in the case — backlog → book-to-bill → revenue.

This is the section to read twice, because the order book is the one number that best predicts Jindal SAW's next few years. A pipe company's revenue is yesterday's order intake showing up as today's deliveries. Get comfortable with the backlog and its conversion rhythm, and the P&L stops looking random.

  • Book-to-bill (new orders won ÷ revenue billed). Above 1 means the backlog is growing faster than it is being consumed — the pipeline of future revenue is filling. Below 1 for several quarters is the early warning that a revenue air-pocket is coming.

The order book also tells you something a single revenue print cannot: the mix of future work. A backlog tilting toward exports and DI water pipe implies a different margin trajectory than one loading up on domestic low-pressure line pipe. So the backlog is not just a quantity of future revenue — it is a preview of future margin. Reading its composition each quarter is closer to reading a company's forward P&L than anything on the current income statement.

The non-transferable read: a full book meeting a slow year

In FY26 the order book reached a record while revenue fell ~14% and profit fell ~37%. That divergence is the entire investment question in one line: the demand (backlog) is there; the doubt is whether the company can convert it on time and at margin. A steelmaker's bad year usually means bad demand — a signal to sell the cycle. A pipe maker's bad year can mean a full order book and a bad delivery window — a very different thing to underwrite, and the reason you cannot value this company off the steel price alone.

Roughly four points of margin came off in a single year — not because demand fell, but because the highest-value work (exports, seamless) was throttled by a conflict and a certification pause. The question is whether that reverses as the shocks roll off.

The metric that settles the thesis

Not the P/E. The case for Jindal SAW is proven or broken by four linked numbers, read together each quarter: (1) order backlog and book-to-bill — is the pipeline still filling? (2) the DI / water share of the mix — is the steady engine growing? (3) EBITDA margin / conversion — is the full book turning into profit, or stalling on timing and payment lags? and (4) net-debt reduction — is the working-capital-heavy model deleveraging? If backlog stays full and margin recovers toward the mid-teens as FY26's shocks fade, the FY26 dip reads as an interruption. If the book converts slowly and margins stay near 10%, it reads as the new normal in earnings power.

10

The competition

Three businesses, three different rival sets — and no single peer proxies for the whole.

Because Jindal SAW is really three businesses, it faces three different competitive sets. Few rivals span all three the way it does — which is both its differentiator and the reason no single peer's numbers proxy for it.

The competitive landscape, segment by segment

The competitive shape to hold in mind

The strategic edge is the breadth itself: a buyer building both a gas grid and a water scheme can source across product lines from one qualified vendor, and the internal mix can tilt toward whichever fluid is spending that year. The strategic risk is the mirror image — in each individual segment there is a focused specialist (Welspun in line pipe, Maharashtra Seamless in seamless, Electrosteel and Tata Metaliks in DI) that competes hard on that turf alone. Breadth wins the group order and smooths the cycle; focus can win the single big tender on price. Jindal SAW's job is to make sure breadth does not become an excuse for being second-best at any one thing.

11

What can go wrong

The risks, stated plainly — FY26 was a live demonstration of most of them at once.

FY26 was a live demonstration of most of these at once — which makes it a useful year to underwrite against rather than to wave away. None of these risks is exotic; they are the ordinary hazards of a working-capital-heavy project business exposed to two cyclical end-markets and one policy-driven one.

  • Order-book lumpiness & execution. A full backlog does not guarantee smooth revenue. Project delays, export disruption (FY26's MENA freeze) and slow government-water payments can push deliveries and stretch working capital.
  • Certification risk. The FY26 API-monogram suspension on seamless showed how a compliance/audit pause — not a demand fall — can halt a product's order flow until it is restored.
  • Oil-&-gas capex dependence. The line-pipe engine rides a cyclical, geopolitics-sensitive spend. A slow global pipeline-capex year, or a sanctions/conflict shock on export routes, hits the highest-ticket segment first.
  • Input-cost squeeze. HR coil / steel plate is the key line-pipe input; if steel prices rise faster than pipe prices can be repriced, margin compresses. Pellet/pig-iron integration helps DI but adds ore-price exposure of its own.
  • Working capital & leverage. Tender-and-execute businesses tie up cash in inventory and slow-paying government receivables. Net debt of ~₹2,528 Cr (Mar '26) is manageable but must trend down for the model to de-risk.
  • Competition & pricing. Focused specialists in each segment can undercut on the single big tender, capping pricing power on any one product line.
The FY26 numbers already price a bad version of most of these in. Revenue down ~14%, margin cut from ~14.3% to ~10%, profit down ~37% — that is close to a stress case delivered by reality, not a model. The real question the year poses is narrow and answerable: was FY26 the trough, or the trend?
12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

Two of India's largest spending programmes, one order book — asked to convert on schedule.

Jindal SAW sits astride two of India's largest, most durable spending programmes at once — the gas-grid / city-gas buildout and the drive to pipe treated water to every home — plus an export line-pipe business into the US and Gulf. The demand runway is genuinely long. The open question is execution and margin, and FY26 put a number on how much those can hurt.

The bull case

  • Record backlog — a ~$1.48 bn order book even in a weak year means future revenue is contracted, not hoped for.
  • Water as ballast — DI / water is the largest, steadiest slice, riding Jal Jeevan and AMRUT for years regardless of the energy cycle.
  • Structural energy demand — gas-grid, CGD and export line-pipe demand rising, with export orders that lift realisation when they land.
  • Cushioned cost & moderate price — backward integration steadies DI margins; a ~16× multiple leaves room if margins normalise toward the mid-teens.

The bear case

  • Shock fragility — FY26 showed how fast an export freeze and a certification pause can gut a year's margin (~14% → ~10%).
  • Cash-conversion drag — working-capital-heavy and government-payment-exposed; reported profit can run ahead of the cash actually collected.
  • Cyclical high-ticket leg — line pipe is cyclical and geopolitics-sensitive; a soft capex year bites the highest-value segment first.
  • Segment specialists — focused rivals compete hard product-by-product, capping pricing power and keeping margins thin.
The bottom line

The way to hold both sides at once is to watch the backlog convert. If the order book stays full, the DI/water mix keeps rising, margins climb back toward the mid-teens as the MENA and API shocks roll off, and net debt trends down, then FY26 will look like the interruption it appears to be — and the moderate multiple will have been the entry point. If, instead, the book converts slowly and margins settle near 10% as the working-capital and payment-lag drag persists, then FY26 is not a dip but a reset in earnings power. The four linked numbers from §09 — backlog and book-to-bill, DI/water mix, margin/conversion, and net-debt reduction — settle it between those two readings.

The concrete milestone to circle is not a valuation level but a delivery event: the quarter when the record backlog starts flowing back into revenue at a recovered margin — the first clean quarter where a full order book and mid-teens EBITDA show up in the same P&L, with net debt lower than the year before. Until the pipe in the backlog becomes pipe in the ground at a healthy spread, everything else is a forecast. That quarter is when Jindal SAW's two-fluid demand story stops being a promise and becomes cash.
