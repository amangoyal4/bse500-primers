Gokaldas Exports, the world's clothes, sewn in India

A full walk through one of India's largest garment exporters — a B2B contract manufacturer that designs and stitches outerwear, activewear and everyday clothing for the labels you already own (Nike, Puma, Columbia, GAP, H&M), why a business this labour-intensive lives or dies on a few points of margin, and how the global scramble to source apparel anywhere but China and Bangladesh handed a mid-cap Bengaluru factory group its biggest opening in a generation.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Gokaldas Exports makes clothes for other companies' labels. It does not sell a single garment under its own brand; it is a contract manufacturer — a design-to-delivery export house that takes a design brief (or co-designs it), sources the fabric and trims, cuts and sews the garment across dozens of Indian factories, finishes and packs it, and ships it to a global brand or retailer. The tag inside says Columbia, GAP, Puma, H&M, Marks & Spencer or Carhartt; the factory that made it was very often Gokaldas. It is among India's largest apparel exporters, running 30-plus production units, ~30,000 sewing machines and a workforce above 54,000 — roughly three-quarters of them women — supplying customers in 50-plus countries.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It sells sewing capacity, not clothes. Revenue was ~₹4,065 Cr in FY26, but the product is really labour organised at scale: thousands of stitching operators turning cloth into finished, export-quality garments to a brand's exact specification. That makes Gokaldas a low-margin, high-volume, working-capital-heavy business where a two-point move in operating margin is the difference between a good year and a poor one — FY26 EBITDA margin was just ~10.7% and net profit only ~₹100 Cr.
  • Its tailwind is where the world sources. For a decade global brands have been diversifying apparel sourcing away from China; more recently, political turmoil in Bangladesh and rising costs in Vietnam have made India a bigger beneficiary of "China+1" than ever. Layer on the India-UK free-trade agreement (which zeroes UK apparel duties that once ran up to ~12%), and Gokaldas sits directly in the path of a structural shift in who stitches the West's wardrobe.
  • It just bought its way bigger. Two 2024 acquisitions — Atraco (a Dubai-based garment group with Middle East and East-Africa factories) and Matrix (an Indian outerwear/fabric maker) — vaulted FY25 revenue up ~63%. They added scale, geographies and outerwear capability, but also diluted margins and loaded on integration and working-capital risk. The stock trades near 55-57× earnings — pricing in a lot of the shift going right.
In plain terms

When a brand like Puma or Columbia decides to make a million jackets, it does not own the factory that sews them — it hands the order to a specialist contractor. Gokaldas is one of the largest such contractors in India: a company whose real asset is the ability to reliably produce complex garments, on time, to a Western retailer's quality bar, across tens of thousands of workers. The economics are humble — you earn a modest margin on each piece and win by doing enormous volume flawlessly — but the opportunity is not, because the biggest brands on earth are actively looking to move orders into India and out of China and Bangladesh. The whole investment question is whether Gokaldas can grow into that opening while lifting a stubbornly thin margin.

01

Garment export 101: how a contract stitcher earns its keep

No fashion knowledge needed — just how the money moves from a brand's order to a factory's margin.

A garment exporter sits in the middle of the apparel chain: below the fabric mills that weave and dye cloth, and above the brands and retailers that put a logo on the finished product and sell it. Its job is conversion — turning bolts of fabric into finished, packed, export-ready garments — and it is paid for that conversion. To read the business you need to understand who does what, and where the thin margin actually comes from.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
CMT"Cut, Make, Trim" — the factory is handed the fabric and just sews it. Low value-add, lowest margin, least risk. The bottom rung of garment work.
FOB / full-package"Free On Board" — the factory sources the fabric and trims itself, makes the garment, and delivers it ready to ship. It carries more cost and risk but earns a higher margin. Gokaldas mostly operates here, which is why it matters.
ODM / design-ledThe manufacturer also designs the garment, not just makes it. The top rung — highest value-add and stickiness with a customer. A key margin lever for Gokaldas.
OuterwearJackets, coats, performance and technical wear — complex, many-panel garments needing specialised machines and skilled operators. Higher barriers, higher margin than a basic tee.
SMV / line efficiency"Standard Minute Value" — how many minutes a garment should take to sew, versus how long it actually does. The single productivity number that quietly decides the margin.
The economics that decide everything: minutes on a sewing line

Strip away the glamour and a garment factory is a machine for selling operator-minutes. The price a brand pays is roughly fixed by the market; the profit is whatever is left after fabric, trims, labour and overhead. So the levers are brutally concrete: how fast and how accurately each operator sews (line efficiency), how full the factory runs (capacity utilisation), and how much of the work is complex, high-value garments rather than plain basics (product mix). A well-run outerwear line stitching to 60%+ efficiency at high utilisation can earn a double-digit margin; a half-full plant making commodity tees earns almost nothing. Everything Gokaldas does — automation, worker training, chasing outerwear orders — is aimed at those same few percentage points.

Who buys from a garment exporter

  • Global apparel brands — Nike, Puma, Adidas, Columbia, Carhartt and the like, who design and market clothing but outsource nearly all the sewing to contractors across Asia.
  • Mass-market retailers — GAP, Old Navy, Banana Republic, H&M, Marks & Spencer, Walmart, JC Penney — who place very large, repeat, price-sensitive orders and demand rigorous quality and compliance.
  • Sourcing agents & private labels — intermediaries and store-brand programmes that place orders on behalf of many smaller buyers, adding volume but squeezing price.
02

What Gokaldas actually makes: one factory network, many garments

The product mix and geography — read this once and the company's margin lever becomes obvious.

Gokaldas is a full-package (FOB) manufacturer that increasingly designs too. Its range spans outerwear and performance wear (its highest-value speciality), activewear and sportswear, formal and casual wear, and women's and kids' garments. The single most important thing to grasp is that these are not equally profitable — and the company is deliberately tilting toward the harder, richer end.

The product ladder, decoded

  • Outerwear & performance wear — jackets, technical shells, insulated and weatherproof garments. These are the hardest things a garment factory can make: many panels, tricky materials, specialised sewing and sealing machines, skilled operators. That difficulty is the moat — few Indian exporters can do it well, so it commands better pricing and stickier customers (think Columbia, Carhartt). Gokaldas has leaned into this for years and its Matrix acquisition deepened it.
  • Activewear, formal & casual — sportswear for the Nike/Puma/Adidas set, plus everyday shirts, trousers and casuals for the GAP/H&M/M&S retailers. Big, repeat volume; the backbone of utilisation; moderate margin.
  • Women's & kids' — a broadening of the range that widens the addressable order book and helps keep lines full across seasons, smoothing the natural lumpiness of any single category.
Why the mix matters more than the revenue line

Two exporters can report the same revenue and earn wildly different profits depending on what they sew. A plant full of plain cotton tees is running flat out for a razor-thin margin; a plant full of insulated technical jackets earns far more per operator-minute because the work is scarce and skilled. That is why the honest way to track Gokaldas is not the top line but the drift of its mix toward outerwear and design-led work — the quiet lever that can lift a 10-11% margin toward the low-to-mid teens without needing a single new customer. It is also the reason the Matrix deal, which bought outerwear and fabric capability, mattered strategically even though it looked small.

03

The company, the Blackstone decade, and who controls it now

A Bengaluru garment house, a famous private-equity misadventure, and a turnaround under new hands.

Gokaldas Exports has one of the more instructive ownership histories in Indian mid-caps — a rare case where a global private-equity giant bought control, held for a decade, and walked away with a loss, after which the company thrived under a smaller, hands-on owner. Understanding that arc explains both the culture and why the current management is trusted to execute.

Timeline

  • 1979–2000s
    Founded and built by the Hinduja family in Bengaluru into India's largest ready-made garment exporter, crossing the ~₹1,000 Cr sales mark — a first for an Indian apparel maker.
  • 2007
    Blackstone, the US private-equity firm, buys a controlling ~68% stake for ~₹676 Cr (about ₹275/share), betting on Indian apparel exports.
  • 2008–2016
    The lost decade. The end of textile quotas (the Multi-Fibre Agreement) unleashed low-cost Bangladesh, currencies moved against India, and profit swung from a ~₹70 Cr gain to heavy losses. Blackstone's bet soured.
  • 2017
    Blackstone exits with a ~79% haircut, selling ~40% to Clear Wealth Consultancy — a vehicle of ex-Blackstone India PE co-head Mathew Cyriac — at ~₹42/share. Sivaramakrishnan Ganapathi joins as Managing Director to run the turnaround.
  • FY19–FY22
    Operational repair: better line efficiency, new customers, more wallet-share with existing brands. The company returns to profit and rebuilds credibility.
  • 2024
    The inorganic leap: acquires Matrix (outerwear/fabric, EV ~₹489 Cr) and Atraco (Dubai-based, ~US$55m) — adding scale, geographies and capability. FY25 revenue jumps ~63%.
  • FY26
    Revenue ~₹4,065 Cr (+4%); absorbs a ~₹90 Cr discount to shield US customers from new reciprocal tariffs; margin held at 10.7%; Africa +17%.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter: Clear Wealth Consultancy (Mathew Cyriac's investment vehicle) is the promoter/controlling shareholder — a hands-on, India-focused owner rather than a global buyout fund. The balance is held by institutions and public shareholders; there is no large family promoter block in the classical sense.
  • Management: Sivaramakrishnan Ganapathi (Vice-Chairman & Managing Director), an IIM-Bangalore-trained operator, has led the business since 2017 and is closely identified with the turnaround — improving efficiency, deepening customer relationships and, latterly, driving the acquisition strategy.
  • Structure: the group now spans its core Indian factory network plus the acquired Atraco (Middle East / East-Africa manufacturing and customer relationships in the US and Europe) and Matrix / BTP (Indian outerwear and fabric capacity). Consolidated accounts therefore blend a high-margin domestic core with newer, lower-margin acquired units still being optimised.
A genuine surprise: Blackstone — one of the most sophisticated investors on earth — lost roughly four-fifths of its money owning Gokaldas for a decade, and the company's real turnaround only began after it left, under a far smaller owner. It is a useful antidote to the idea that a marquee sponsor guarantees success in a labour-intensive, low-margin export business: in apparel, execution on the factory floor beats balance-sheet firepower.
04

The sourcing shift: China+1, Bangladesh's stumble, and India's turn

The structural tailwind — why the world's apparel orders are looking for a new home.

The single biggest reason to care about Gokaldas has nothing to do with the company itself — it is where global brands choose to place their orders. For years that answer was overwhelmingly China, then increasingly Bangladesh and Vietnam. That map is being redrawn, and India — long a bit-player in the very largest apparel supply chains — is finally a serious alternative.

A near one-third drop in China's UK share in four years, driven by rising Chinese labour costs and buyer diversification. Every point China loses is a point that flows to India, Vietnam, Bangladesh and others — and India's slice is growing as the alternatives wobble.

Three forces are pushing orders toward India at once. China+1 — brands have spent years deliberately reducing dependence on Chinese factories, a trend that only hardened with geopolitics and rising Chinese wages. Bangladesh's disruption — the country that hoovered up low-cost basics for two decades has been rattled by political turmoil and labour unrest, making buyers nervous about concentrating there. And Vietnam's cost creep — the other big winner of the last cycle is no longer cheap. India, with a vast, young, low-cost and English-speaking workforce and a deep domestic textile base, is the natural third leg — and Gokaldas, as a scaled, compliant, brand-trusted exporter, is exactly the kind of supplier a nervous sourcing manager wants.

The insight that separates this from wishful thinking

India has been "about to win" global apparel sourcing for twenty years, and mostly hasn't — so scepticism is fair. What is different now is that the shift is being pulled by brands de-risking their own supply chains, not pushed by an Indian subsidy. When a Columbia or a GAP decides it cannot have too much of its production in China or Bangladesh, it goes looking for capacity elsewhere and is willing to pay to build a relationship — a far stickier reason to move orders than a temporary incentive. The catch is capacity: India can only capture the shift as fast as its exporters can add compliant, skilled sewing lines. That constraint — building factories and training operators quickly enough — is the real bottleneck, and it is precisely why Gokaldas's greenfield expansion and acquisitions are the whole growth story.

Why it also cuts both ways: the very same disruption that helps Gokaldas — India's move to curb some Bangladeshi garment imports, US tariff turbulence — is a reminder that this is a policy-sensitive tailwind. Trade winds that blow orders toward India today can shift with an election or a tariff notification. The structural direction is favourable; the quarter-to-quarter path is not a straight line.
05

Tariffs, FTAs and the trade chessboard

The bespoke crux — for an exporter, trade policy is the business, and right now it is moving in two directions at once.

A domestic company competes on product; an exporter competes on landed cost in a foreign market — the price of its garment after that country's import duty. Change the duty and you change the exporter's competitiveness overnight, without the factory doing anything. In 2025-26 two opposite tariff stories have been playing out for Gokaldas simultaneously, and holding both in view is the key to the company.

The two-front trade story
FrontWhat's happeningEffect on Gokaldas
US · reciprocal tariffsNew US "reciprocal" tariffs raised the cost of importing apparel into America, Gokaldas's single largest market.A headwind. In FY26 Gokaldas absorbed a net ~₹90 Cr discount to its US customers to help offset the tariff — eating margin to keep the orders.
UK · India-UK FTAThe free-trade agreement zeroes UK import duties on Indian apparel that previously ran up to ~12%.A tailwind. India instantly undercuts Bangladesh, Vietnam and China in the UK on duty. UK is ~5% of revenue today; management sees it potentially doubling.

The device that only fits Gokaldas: a supplier that paid its customer's tax

Here is a fact that captures this company's position better than any ratio. In FY26 Gokaldas handed its US customers a net discount of roughly ₹90 Cr — a real, margin-eroding cheque — to absorb the burden of a tariff that America levied on its own importers. Think about how unusual that is: a mid-cap Indian factory group effectively subsidised a US trade policy to keep its shelf space. That single line tells you exactly where the power sits in this relationship (with the big Western buyer, not the supplier), why the margin is thin and hard to lift, and why the UK/EU FTAs matter so much — they are the rare cases where trade policy pushes the other way and hands the exporter, not the buyer, the advantage. Watch which direction the next tariff or FTA moves; for Gokaldas, that is the weather.

The strategic response is geographic diversification of the customer book. The Atraco acquisition added Middle East and Africa exposure; the UK and EU are being courted hard on the back of the FTAs; and India's own domestic market is a growing outlet. The more balanced the destination mix, the less any single country's tariff politics can whipsaw the company — which is itself a reason the acquisitions were about more than just size.

The inorganic leap: Atraco and Matrix

How Gokaldas bought a step-change in scale, geography and capability — and what it cost.

For most of its listed life Gokaldas grew the slow way: adding lines, winning customers, improving efficiency. In 2024 it changed gears and grew by acquisition, doing two deals that between them re-based the company's size. This is the reason FY25 revenue leapt ~63% while FY26 grew only ~4% — the step came in a single jump, and the years since are digestion.

What each deal bought

  • Atraco (~US$55m; ~US$45m at close + ~US$10m deferred) — a Dubai-headquartered garment group with manufacturing in the Middle East and East Africa (including Kenya) and established customer relationships across the US and Europe. It brought Gokaldas new geographies — factories outside India, which diversify both the cost base and the tariff exposure — and a ready book of Western customers. Africa's ~17% growth in FY26 came largely from this footprint and helped offset the US tariff drag.
  • Matrix (Matrix Design & Industries; EV ~₹489 Cr, 100% acquired) — an Indian outerwear and fabric business. This deepened Gokaldas's most valuable capability (complex outerwear) and, via its BTP fabric operation (running at ~50 lakh metres/month and expanding), pushed the group a step upstream toward its own fabric — reducing dependence on outside mills for its highest-value garments.
The strategic logic — and the price of it

The two deals are best read as buying three things India's organic garment sector is short of: scale (to matter to the biggest brands you must be able to take very large orders), geographic spread (factories and customers outside a single country reduce tariff and concentration risk), and capability (outerwear and fabric are hard to build from scratch). The cost is equally real: acquired units run at lower margins than the polished domestic core, so the blended EBITDA margin fell from the mid-teens toward ~11% even as revenue soared; debt rose to fund the deals and their working capital; and management now has to prove it can lift those acquired operations to Gokaldas's own efficiency standard. The acquisitions bought a bigger company — whether they bought a better one is what the next two years of margin will reveal.

07

The thin-margin machine: where profit actually comes from

Why an apparel exporter's economics are humble — and the handful of levers that move them.

It is worth being blunt about the model: garment export is a low-margin, capital-and-labour-intensive grind. Fabric and labour eat most of the revenue; the profit that survives is a single-digit-to-low-double-digit slice. Gokaldas cannot change that reality, but it can push a few levers that decide whether it earns 8% or 13% — and those few points are the entire equity story.

The pre-acquisition Gokaldas earned a mid-teens EBITDA margin. Folding in lower-margin acquired units pulled the blended figure to ~11% — so holding 10.7% in FY26 while also absorbing a ~₹90 Cr tariff discount was, in context, a defensive win, not a decline of standards.

There are essentially four levers, and management pulls all of them:

  • Capacity utilisation. A garment factory's costs are largely fixed once built; profit comes from keeping every line full. A half-empty plant bleeds. Winning enough orders — across geographies and seasons — to run the whole ~30,000-machine base near capacity is the first and biggest lever.
  • Worker productivity (line efficiency). The same operator sewing faster and with fewer defects lifts output per rupee of wage. Training, incentives, better line balancing and selective automation are the slow, compounding grind that separates a good apparel manager from a mediocre one — and it is Gokaldas's stated core competence.
  • Product mix. Shifting the order book toward outerwear and design-led garments earns more per operator-minute than commodity basics. This is the highest-quality lever because it is defensible — few rivals can make complex outerwear well.
  • Vertical integration. Owning fabric (via Matrix/BTP) and sourcing smartly shaves input cost and speeds delivery on the highest-value work, capturing margin that would otherwise leak to outside mills.
Why the same revenue can hide two different companies

Because the margin is so thin, small operational choices swing the profit disproportionately. Consider FY26: revenue barely grew (+4%) and net profit actually fell to ~₹100 Cr — not because the business weakened, but because a ~₹90 Cr tariff discount and the drag of newly acquired lower-margin units flowed straight through a slim margin. The flip side is the upside: as acquired units are optimised, utilisation climbs and the mix tilts to outerwear, the same revenue base can throw off materially more profit. In a thin-margin manufacturer, operating leverage runs in both directions with unusual force — which is why the profit line here is far more volatile than the steady-looking revenue line suggests.

08

Customer concentration and the dollar

The two structural risks baked into any large garment exporter — and how they show up here.

Two features of the business model deserve their own section because they are always present, whatever the tailwind: the company depends on a handful of very large customers, and it earns most of its money in US dollars while spending rupees. Neither is a flaw unique to Gokaldas — they are the price of admission to global apparel supply — but both can hurt.

Customer concentration

Gokaldas serves a marquee roster — GAP, Banana Republic, Old Navy, Columbia, Carhartt, Puma, Adidas, H&M, Marks & Spencer, Walmart, JC Penney — but the reality of the industry is that a small number of these accounts typically drive a large share of revenue. That concentration is double-edged. A deep relationship with a giant brand brings large, repeat, planning-friendly volume and years of embedded trust. But it also hands the buyer pricing power (the FY26 tariff discount is concentration in action), and the loss or slowdown of one big account can dent a whole year. Diversifying — more customers, more geographies, more categories — is a permanent management priority precisely because concentration is a permanent risk.

Forex

As a dollar exporter with a rupee cost base, Gokaldas is structurally exposed to the USD/INR rate. A depreciating rupee is a quiet tailwind — the same dollar invoice converts to more rupees — while a sharp appreciation squeezes margins on orders priced months earlier. The company hedges a portion of its receivables to smooth this, but currency remains a swing factor on a thin margin, and one that can flatter or flatter-to-deceive a given quarter's numbers independent of how the factories actually ran.

The concentration nuance to watch: because a few US accounts anchor the book, Gokaldas's fortunes are unusually tied to American consumer demand and American trade policy at once. A US retail slowdown (inventory gluts, weak apparel spending) and a US tariff hike can arrive together and compound — as a softer FY26 partly showed. The UK/EU FTA push is, among other things, an attempt to reduce exactly this single-country dependence.

The industry and the rivals

A crowded field of Indian exporters — and why Gokaldas is not the same animal as a branded retailer.

India's garment-export sector is fragmented and competitive, spanning giant private houses, listed peers and countless smaller units. What matters for placing Gokaldas is understanding which kind of company it is — a pure garment-export manufacturer — and how that differs from the fabric makers below it and the brand owners above it, with whom it is often confused.

The distinction that trips up newcomers

It is easy to file Gokaldas alongside Arvind Fashions or Aditya Birla Fashion (ABFRL) because they all involve "clothing" — but they are opposite businesses. ABFRL and Arvind Fashions own brands (Louis Philippe, Allen Solly, Van Heusen and the like) and sell to consumers through shops; their game is marketing, retail footprint and consumer trends. Gokaldas owns no brand and no shop — it is the invisible factory behind other people's labels, and its game is manufacturing efficiency, customer relationships and trade access. Nor is it a fabric maker like the weaving mills that supply it. Placing it correctly — as a design-to-delivery export manufacturer — is the difference between valuing it on retail metrics and valuing it on what actually drives it: utilisation, mix and landed cost.

Return on equity sits in the mid-single digits, depressed by the enlarged equity base and the margin dilution — a figure that only improves if the acquired units are lifted toward the core's efficiency.

The one number that settles the thesis: blended EBITDA margin recovery

At ~56× earnings, Gokaldas is not priced as a cheap cyclical exporter; it is priced as a company that will both ride the sourcing shift and rebuild its margin. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the blended EBITDA margin working back up from ~11% toward the mid-teens — read together with capacity utilisation at the new greenfield plants, the outerwear / value-added share of the mix, and revenue diversification away from the US toward UK/EU/India. If margin climbs as the acquired units are optimised and the new Jharkhand and Karnataka plants fill, then the rich multiple is a growth-plus-mix story that can compound. If the margin stays stuck near 11% while revenue grinds higher, a 56× stock is paying a premium for volume that earns too little — and re-rates. The revenue line will look busy; the margin line is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A well-run exporter with a real opening — asked to convert a global tailwind into a thicker margin.

Gokaldas enters FY27 larger, more diversified and better-placed than at any point in its listed history: a credible management, a genuine China+1 tailwind, an FTA advantage in the UK, and fresh capacity coming. The debate is not whether the opportunity is real — it plainly is — but whether a thin-margin manufacturer can grow into it fast enough, and profitably enough, to justify a premium multiple.

The bull case

  • Structural sourcing shift — China+1, Bangladesh's turmoil and Vietnam's cost creep steer large, sticky orders toward scaled Indian exporters like Gokaldas.
  • FTA advantage — the India-UK deal zeroes UK apparel duty; an EU deal would repeat it across a far bigger market, handing India a landed-cost edge.
  • Margin recovery runway — optimising acquired units, filling new plants and tilting to outerwear can lift a diluted ~11% margin back toward the mid-teens.
  • Capability & scale — outerwear expertise, in-house fabric (Matrix/BTP) and 54,000-strong capacity make it a supplier the biggest brands can rely on.
  • Proven operator — a management team that already turned this company around once, now with a diversified, multi-geography footprint.

The bear case

  • Thin, hard-to-lift margin — a ~10.7% EBITDA margin leaves little cushion; a tariff shock or cost spike flows straight to a slim bottom line.
  • Rich valuation — ~56× earnings prices in both the tailwind and a margin recovery; any stumble on either can trigger a sharp de-rating.
  • US & concentration risk — heavy reliance on a few large US customers and on American demand/tariff policy, as the FY26 ₹90 Cr discount showed.
  • Integration & debt — acquired units run at lower margins and must be optimised; higher debt and working capital raise the stakes if execution slips.
  • Trade-policy whiplash — the same tariffs and quotas that help can reverse; forex and cotton-price swings add further volatility on a thin margin.
The bottom line

Gokaldas is a well-run factory business handed a genuinely large opening: the West is actively looking to sew more of its clothing somewhere other than China and Bangladesh, and Gokaldas is exactly the scaled, trusted, outerwear-capable Indian supplier those buyers want. It has spent the last two years buying the scale and geography to seize that — and paid for it in a diluted margin and a heavier balance sheet. What remains unproven is the harder second act: turning acquired, lower-margin capacity and new greenfield lines into the mid-teens margin its pre-acquisition core once earned, while spreading its customer book beyond a tariff-exposed America. The next few quarters answer it in one checkable figure — whether the blended EBITDA margin starts climbing back off ~11% as the Jharkhand and Karnataka plants fill and the outerwear mix thickens. Track that margin each quarter; it, not the headline revenue, tells you whether the sourcing shift is reaching the bottom line.
