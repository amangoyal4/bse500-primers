00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Gland Pharma is a Hyderabad-based maker of sterile injectable generic medicines — the drugs that come in vials, ampoules and pre-filled syringes and are injected or infused rather than swallowed. What makes it unusual is who it sells to: Gland runs a mostly business-to-business ("B2B") model, developing and manufacturing injectables and selling them to other pharma companies and distributors, who then market the drugs under their own brand names. It is one of the world's largest pure-play generic-injectables companies, supplying nearly 90 countries, with the bulk of revenue from the United States.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A high-barrier manufacturing business, not a branded one. Sterile injectables are hard to make — aseptic manufacturing, heavy regulation, and repeated USFDA plant inspections mean far fewer credible competitors than in ordinary oral generics. That scarcity is Gland's moat. FY26 revenue was ₹6,431 Cr (+14%), with an adjusted EBITDA margin of ~26% and adjusted profit (PAT) up 50% to ₹1,045 Cr.
  • A Chinese parent is the defining wildcard. Gland has been majority-owned by China's Fosun Pharma since 2017 (~51.8% today) — the first Chinese-controlled Indian pharma company. That brought capital and China-market access, but it also created a geopolitical overhang: India-China tensions, scrutiny of Chinese ownership, and US-market sensitivity. Fosun has been trying to exit since 2020, and a change of control is a perennial live question.
  • Earnings have been volatile — and are now recovering. US injectable price erosion, dependence on a few large products, and a loss-making European acquisition (Cenexi, France) whipsawed profits for years. FY26 is a clear normalisation: the base business is steady, Cenexi has turned EBITDA-positive, and a fast-growing CDMO / GLP-1 / biosimilar pipeline is the new optionality on a debt-light balance sheet trading at a de-rated ~39× earnings.
In plain terms

Think of Gland as a giant, ultra-clean contract kitchen for injectable medicines. It doesn't run the restaurant — it doesn't put its own brand in front of doctors and patients the way Sun or Cipla do. Instead, big generic and specialty drug companies come to Gland saying "make us a sterile, FDA-approved version of this injectable," and Gland develops it, manufactures it in sterile plants, and ships it for the customer to sell under their label. Because injections must be perfectly sterile and are heavily policed by regulators, very few companies can do this reliably at scale — which is exactly why Gland can earn good margins doing the unglamorous work of making other people's drugs.

01

Injectables 101: why "sterile" changes everything

No pharma degree required — just why an injection is a far harder product than a pill.

Most generic-drug companies you've heard of make oral solids — tablets and capsules you swallow. Gland lives in a different, harder corner of the industry: sterile injectables. Understanding why that distinction matters is the whole key to the business.

What an injectable actually is

  • The format. Injectables are liquids (or freeze-dried powders re-dissolved into liquids) delivered directly into the body — into a vein (IV), a muscle, or under the skin. They come as vials, ampoules, pre-filled syringes, and infusion bags. Gland's core products include heparin and enoxaparin (blood thinners), rocuronium (an anaesthetic muscle relaxant), and daptomycin (an antibiotic), among many others.
  • Where they're used. Overwhelmingly in hospitals and clinics — emergency rooms, operating theatres, oncology wards, ICUs — where a drug has to act fast, or where a patient can't take anything by mouth.
Why "sterile" is the whole ballgame

A tablet passes through your stomach, where acid kills most stray microbes. An injectable bypasses all of that and goes straight into your bloodstream or tissue — so a single bacterial contaminant can be lethal. That means every injectable must be manufactured under aseptic ("sterile") conditions: cleanrooms, filtered air, gowned operators, sterilised equipment, and constant testing. The tolerance for error is essentially zero, and regulators inspect these plants relentlessly. This is why injectable manufacturing is a high-barrier business: it takes years and heavy capital to build a compliant sterile plant, and one serious regulatory finding can shut a line down. Fewer players can clear that bar — so competition is thinner and margins are more durable than in the brutal, crowded world of oral generics.

02

The B2B model: making other companies' medicines

The single most distinctive thing about Gland — read this once and the whole company clicks.

Almost every famous pharma company sells to the market — its own brands, its own sales reps calling on doctors. Gland is deliberately built the other way round. Its dominant model is B2B: it sells to other pharma companies, who put their own name on the box. Understanding the three ways Gland actually earns is essential.

Most of Gland's business is B2B (Models A & B): it does the hard, regulated manufacturing and lets partners handle branding, marketing and distribution. That's why you've likely never seen "Gland" on a medicine — even though its factories may have made it.

Why choose B2B at all?

  • No sales-and-marketing army to fund. Branded generics require thousands of medical reps and marketing spend. Gland skips almost all of that and pours its money into R&D, plants and regulatory filings — the things it's actually good at.
  • Play to a manufacturing strength. Gland's edge is process development and sterile manufacturing at scale, plus a deep regulatory track record. B2B monetises exactly that, without the messy, capital-hungry work of building consumer/prescriber brands in dozens of countries.
  • Stickier, capacity-filling relationships. Once a partner has Gland qualified in its regulatory filing for a given product, switching suppliers means re-filing and re-inspection — costly and slow. That "designed-in" quality tends to keep partners loyal and Gland's plants busy.
  • The trade-off. B2B means thinner margins per unit than a branded seller and less control — you're one link in someone else's chain, exposed to their volumes and to price pressure passed down the line. Gland accepts lower gross economics in exchange for lower cost, lower risk of consumer-brand failure, and scale.
The B2C piece — and why it's growing

Gland does also sell some products under its own label ("B2C") — most visibly in India, and increasingly in selected markets. This matters because own-label sales capture more of the value chain (Gland keeps the marketing margin instead of handing it to a partner). So the long-run mix-shift story is: keep the reliable B2B base, and gradually layer on higher-value own-label and full-service CDMO work on top. It's a move up the value chain without abandoning the manufacturing DNA.

03

The company and who controls it

From a 1978 contract manufacturer to a Chinese-owned, listed injectables giant.

Gland Pharma was founded in Hyderabad in 1978 by P.V.N. Raju as a small contract manufacturer of liquid parenteral (injectable) products. Over four decades it grew into one of the largest generic-injectables companies in the world — before passing, remarkably, into Chinese ownership and then to the public markets.

Timeline

  • 1999

  • 2013–14

  • Jan 2023
    Acquires French/Belgian CDMO Cenexi (enterprise value ~€230m; ~€120m equity) — a bet on European sterile fill-finish and CDMO (see §07).
  • 2024
    Fosun trims its stake by ~6% (to ~51.8%) via a bulk deal — the parent openly seeking to reduce or exit its holding.
  • FY26
    Record revenue ₹6,431 Cr; adjusted PAT +50%; Cenexi turns EBITDA-positive; CDMO/GLP-1 pipeline scales up.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter: Shanghai Fosun Pharma (via Fosun Pharma Industrial), holding ~51.8% — Chinese majority control. Promoter shares are declared unencumbered (no pledging) for FY26.
  • The rest: a public float held by domestic and foreign institutions and retail investors — roughly the remaining ~48%.
  • Management: a professional, India-based leadership team runs day-to-day operations from Hyderabad; the Fosun connection provides board oversight, capital, and access to China. The original founding family is no longer the controlling owner.
  • Balance sheet: essentially debt-light — total borrowings ~₹284 Cr against ~₹10,300 Cr of reserves — funding growth (and the Cenexi turnaround) internally rather than via leverage.
04

Fosun and the China overhang — the defining wildcard

The one force an analyst leads with for Gland: what does it mean that a Chinese company owns it?

Gland's single most distinctive feature isn't a product or a plant — it's the identity of its owner. Since 2017, Gland has been majority-controlled by China's Fosun Pharma, making it the first Chinese-controlled Indian pharmaceutical company. That fact cuts both ways, and it hangs over the stock permanently.

The upside Fosun brought

  • Capital & ambition. Fosun's deep pockets underwrote a decade of expansion — new capacity, the Cenexi acquisition, and the push into CDMO and biologics.
  • China-market access. A Chinese parent is, in theory, a bridge into the world's second-largest pharma market — hard for an independent Indian firm to crack alone.
  • Biosimilar / CDMO optionality. Fosun has its own biologics and biosimilar ambitions; the relationship opens doors to higher-value contract work and technology.

The overhang it created

  • Geopolitics. After the 2020 India-China border tensions, Chinese ownership of a strategic Indian asset became politically sensitive. India tightened scrutiny of Chinese investment, and questions have circled around Fosun's control ever since.
  • US-market sensitivity. Gland's biggest customer is the United States — a market increasingly wary of Chinese involvement in its drug supply chain. A Chinese-controlled supplier of hospital injectables is a lightning rod in a world of "de-risking from China."
  • The perennial "will Fosun sell?" question. Fosun has sought to exit since ~2020; global PE names (Blackstone, Brookfield, Warburg Pincus and others) have repeatedly been reported circling a majority stake. A change of control could remove the overhang and re-rate the stock — or introduce fresh uncertainty. Either way it's a live, unresolved swing factor.
The "China+1" irony: the global pharma trend is "China+1" — Western buyers deliberately diversifying their supply chains away from China by sourcing from places like India. Gland is an Indian manufacturer perfectly placed to win that shift... yet it is owned by a Chinese company. So the very tailwind that should benefit an India-based injectables maker is partly blunted by who sits at the top of Gland's cap table. Resolving that contradiction — most cleanly via a Fosun exit to a non-Chinese owner — is arguably the biggest single re-rating lever the stock has.
Why this dominates the investment debate

For most companies, "who owns it" is a footnote. For Gland it's front and centre, because the ownership question touches demand (will US and Western customers keep leaning in, or hedge away from a China-linked supplier?), regulation (Indian and foreign scrutiny), and the share price (a control change is a genuine catalyst). An investor in Gland is, unavoidably, also taking a view on India-China relations and on what Fosun does next.

Why the US weight matters both ways

The US is the most attractive place on earth to sell injectables — huge volumes, structured reimbursement, and (crucially) recurring drug shortages that reward reliable manufacturers. But leaning ~53% on one country means Gland's fortunes ride on US pricing dynamics, US regulatory inspections of its plants, and US-market sentiment toward a China-linked supplier. When US injectable prices erode (as they periodically do), it hits Gland hard; when shortages appear and prices firm, Gland benefits fast. That single-market sensitivity is why the US price cycle (§08) is such a big part of the story.

06

CDMO, GLP-1 and biologics — the growth re-rating

The optionality that could turn a cyclical generics maker into a structural growth story.

If the China overhang is the risk that defines Gland, the CDMO opportunity is the upside that could re-rate it. "CDMO" — Contract Development & Manufacturing Organisation — means Gland acts as a full-service manufacturing partner for other drug companies, including innovators, not just supplying finished generics. Three threads make this the exciting part of the story.

1 · CDMO is scaling fast

  • The CDMO business contributed roughly 23% of base-business revenue in FY26 and grew about 33% — well ahead of the rest of the company.
  • Why it matters: CDMO revenue is typically stickier and higher-value than commodity generics. Once an innovator qualifies Gland to make its product, that relationship can run for years — and it's not exposed to the same brutal generic price erosion.

2 · The GLP-1 wildcard

  • GLP-1s are the blockbuster weight-loss and diabetes injectables (the class that includes semaglutide, liraglutide and tirzepatide). Demand is exploding globally, and much of it is injectable — squarely Gland's wheelhouse.
  • Gland is positioning as a CDMO/fill-finish partner for GLP-1s, reportedly with around eight GLP-1 customers approved and several more in the pipeline. As patents expire and generic/biosimilar GLP-1s roll out across markets, a trusted sterile manufacturer stands to win large, long-dated volumes.
  • The honest caveat: management has deliberately excluded GLP-1 upside from its guidance — it's early, approvals and volumes are uncertain, and the timing is hard to call. So GLP-1 is best viewed as free optionality, not a base-case number.

3 · Biosimilars & biologics

  • Gland is pushing to double the share of complex products in its US business — led by respiratory, complex injectables, and biosimilars (near-copies of biologic drugs). Its biosimilar ranibizumab (an eye-disease drug) has secured EU approval, an early proof point.
  • Biologics and biosimilars are far harder to make than small-molecule injectables — which is precisely why a manufacturer that can master them earns better economics and faces fewer rivals. It's the natural "up the complexity curve" extension of Gland's core skill.
Why this is the re-rating lever

For years the market treated Gland as a cyclical US-generics injectables play — good margins, but hostage to price erosion and one big geography. The CDMO / GLP-1 / biosimilar push is an attempt to change what kind of company it is: from a commodity generics supplier toward a higher-value, longer-contract, harder-to-displace manufacturing partner. If that shift takes hold, the earnings become steadier and the multiple can expand. GLP-1 in particular is a genuinely large "call option": if even a few of those customer programmes scale, the volumes could be material. But it is optionality, not a promise — which is why the bull case rests on execution, and the guidance still leans on the boring base business.

07

Cenexi and the European bet

The acquisition that dragged on earnings for years — and is finally turning.

In January 2023 Gland made its biggest strategic move since the IPO: it acquired Cenexi, a French/Belgian sterile fill-finish and CDMO company, for an enterprise value of about €230 million (~€120m of equity). The logic was sound; the execution has been hard — and Cenexi's swing from drag to profit is a big part of the FY26 recovery.

The rationale — why buy Cenexi at all

  • A European manufacturing footprint. Cenexi runs four sites (three in France, one in Belgium) with sterile liquid and lyophilised (freeze-dried) fill-finish capability — including oncology and complex products. That gave Gland an instant EU CDMO base and local, regulated-market capacity.
  • Customer & capability diversification. It widened Gland's customer base beyond its US-centric generics book and deepened its complex-injectable and CDMO skills — feeding directly into the growth strategy in §06.
  • De-risking the geography. Adding Europe reduced the single-market weight on the US, at least on paper.

Why it was a drag — and the turnaround

  • Integration pain. Cenexi came with legacy cost structures, and for several quarters it operated at a loss — an EBITDA drag that muddied Gland's consolidated numbers and frustrated investors.
  • The fix. Gland renegotiated contracts and pricing, cut costs, and grew volumes. The payoff showed in FY26: Cenexi revenue reached ~€182m (₹1,869 Cr), up ~25%, and it turned EBITDA-positive — from a meaningful loss a year earlier. That inflection is one of the clearest signals in the FY26 results.
The honest read: Cenexi is best understood as a strategically-sensible but operationally-difficult bet. It bought Gland European capacity and complex-manufacturing depth it couldn't easily build, but it took roughly two-to-three years, real management effort and margin dilution to make it work. The turnaround to EBITDA-positive is encouraging, but Cenexi's margins still lag the high-quality base business — so its continued improvement is a swing factor for group profitability, not a settled win.

What "normalisation" means here

FY26 doesn't just show growth — it shows the drivers of past pain reversing at once. The base business grew steadily, Cenexi flipped from loss to EBITDA-positive, new product launches added volume, and margins expanded (Q4 adjusted EBITDA margin ~30%). Stack those together and profit jumps far faster than revenue — the mirror image of the years when several things went wrong simultaneously. The question for investors is durability: is this a genuine structural step-up (helped by CDMO/GLP-1 and a fixed Cenexi), or a favourable point in the US injectable price cycle that will eventually turn again? The truth is probably "some of both" — which is exactly why the stock trades at a discount to India's branded-pharma darlings.

The industry and the rivals

A niche with high barriers — and a crowded roster of both Indian and global competitors.

Gland competes in generic sterile injectables for regulated markets — a niche that is smaller and harder-to-enter than oral generics, but far from empty. Its rivals fall into three camps: Indian majors with injectable arms, global specialty-injectable players, and a growing set of CDMO peers.

The competitive tension to watch

Two dynamics matter. First, in commodity injectables, the barrier is manufacturing and regulatory reliability, not brand — so competition is a rotating cast of who has approval and capacity for a given molecule at a given time. When a rival wins re-entry on a big product, pricing suffers; when a competitor's plant hits regulatory trouble and creates a shortage, the survivors (Gland among them) profit. Second, in the CDMO/GLP-1 arena, the game is different: it's about being a trusted, high-quality partner for innovators — a race Gland's decades of sterile-manufacturing credibility position it well for, but where global CDMOs and other Indian entrants are all scrambling for the same GLP-1 wave.

The regulatory backdrop: everything hinges on plant compliance. Gland's facilities are inspected by the USFDA and other regulators; a clean record is a competitive asset, and an adverse inspection is one of the fastest ways to lose revenue and sentiment. This is a permanent, non-negotiable feature of the injectables business — and a risk to keep on the radar every quarter.

How to think about the ~39× multiple

At roughly ₹40,600 Cr on ~₹1,027 Cr of FY26 profit, Gland trades near 39× trailing earnings — a full multiple, but a discount to India's high-quality branded-pharma names (which often fetch 40–60×+). That gap is the market pricing in Gland's specific risks: US-price cyclicality, single-product dependence, the still-improving Cenexi, and above all the China/Fosun overhang. The bull view: those are de-rating factors that can reverse — a durable CDMO/GLP-1 shift steadies earnings, a Fosun exit removes the discount, and a debt-light, high-ROCE injectables leader deserves better. The bear view: injectable pricing is inherently cyclical, GLP-1 is unproven, and the ownership question could just as easily inject fresh uncertainty. The multiple sits where it does because both stories are credible.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-barrier manufacturer with real optionality — and an unusually large "who owns it" question.

Gland is a genuinely differentiated asset: one of the world's leading pure-play generic-injectables makers, debt-light, high-return, and now layering CDMO, GLP-1 and biosimilar optionality on top of a recovering base. The investment debate turns on two axes — the durability of the recovery and the resolution of the China overhang.

The bull case

  • High-barrier moat — sterile injectable manufacturing at scale, with a long USFDA track record few can match; thinner competition than oral generics.
  • Recovery under way — FY26 adjusted PAT +50%, Cenexi turned EBITDA-positive, margins expanding — the past drags reversing at once.
  • CDMO / GLP-1 / biosimilar optionality — a potential shift from cyclical generics to higher-value, stickier partnering; GLP-1 is a large free call option.
  • Debt-light, cash-rich — self-funds growth; high ROCE; room to invest through cycles.
  • China+1 tailwind — an India-based injectables supplier well placed as the West de-risks its drug supply chain.
  • Overhang can reverse — a Fosun exit to a non-Chinese owner could remove the discount and re-rate the stock.

The bear case

  • Earnings cyclicality — US injectable price erosion and key-product dependence make profits inherently lumpy.
  • US concentration — ~53% of revenue from one market exposes it to US pricing, plants and sentiment.
  • The China overhang — geopolitics, Western wariness of a China-linked supplier, and an unresolved Fosun-exit question hang over demand and the multiple.
  • GLP-1 is unproven — genuine upside, but excluded from guidance for good reason; timing and volumes are uncertain.
  • Cenexi still lags — turned positive, but margins trail the base; continued improvement isn't guaranteed.
  • Regulatory tail risk — a single adverse USFDA finding can disrupt supply and sentiment overnight.
The bottom line

Gland is a structurally advantaged manufacturer with a real recovery and genuine optionality — wrapped in an unusually large ownership question. The business risk (can it keep making sterile injectables reliably and profitably?) looks well-covered; the debate is about durability and discount. Watch three things each quarter: US base-business pricing (is the price cycle firm or eroding?), Cenexi margins & CDMO/GLP-1 traction (is the mix genuinely improving?), and anything on Fosun's stake (a control change is the single biggest potential catalyst). Get those right and a de-rated, debt-light injectables leader can re-rate; get them wrong and the discount is deserved.
