Pricol, the maker behind your dashboard

A full walk through the Coimbatore company that puts the instrument cluster — the speedo, fuel gauge and glowing screen — into most of India's two-wheelers and trucks. How it turned a humble dial into a rising-value electronic display, why "content per vehicle" matters more than units sold, how it survived a violent labour past and a botched overseas expansion, and why it is about to split itself in two.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Pricol makes the instrument cluster — the panel of dials and screens a rider or driver stares at all day: speed, fuel, engine warnings, and increasingly a full colour display with maps and calls. It is India's market leader in these "driver-information systems," and by volume one of the two largest cluster makers in the world. Beyond the dashboard, it also makes a spread of humble but essential parts — oil and coolant pumps, sensors, wiping systems, disc brakes, cab-tilt mechanisms — and, since 2025, a large plastic-injection-moulding business. Its customers are the vehicle makers themselves: TVS, Bajaj, Hero, Royal Enfield, Tata Motors, Ashok Leyland and, increasingly, exporters like Caterpillar.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It sells a part whose price keeps rising even when volumes don't. The dashboard has quietly become one of the most valuable pieces of electronics on a mass-market vehicle. A basic analog speedometer might fetch a few hundred rupees; a modern TFT colour cluster can command several thousand. Because Pricol is the dominant supplier as India's two-wheelers and trucks trade up from needles to screens, its revenue per vehicle climbs even if the number of vehicles sold barely moves. That single dynamic — premiumisation — drives the whole equity story.
  • FY26 was a step-change year, part organic and part bought. Consolidated revenue jumped ~51% to ₹3,964 Cr and net profit rose ~50% to ₹251 Cr. Roughly half that growth was real underlying demand and premiumisation; a big chunk came from consolidating the injection-moulding business acquired from a TVS-group company, which added a full year of ~₹900 Cr-plus revenue. Understanding what is organic and what is bought is the first job in reading the numbers.
  • It is about to split into two listed companies. In June 2026 the board approved carving the crown-jewel electronics arm out into a separately listed entity, Pricol Autotech, on a 1:1 share basis. A shareholder today owns one stock that quietly becomes two — one a focused dashboard-electronics pure-play, the other the mechanical, fluid and polymer businesses. That restructuring, still in progress, hangs over how the company should be valued.
In plain terms

Pricol earns its living on a part almost no buyer thinks about — until it changes. For decades the instrument cluster was a mechanical afterthought: a needle, a cable, a bulb. Emissions rules and the smartphone generation turned it into a screen, and a screen is worth many times a needle. Pricol's bet is simple: it already supplies more of India's two-wheeler and truck dashboards than anyone, so as those dashboards go digital it captures most of the upgrade — richer product, fatter margin, same customer. The risks are equally simple: it leans heavily on two-wheeler makers, a handful of them at that, and its own history shows how quickly a well-run supplier can trip over labour trouble and ill-judged overseas deals.

01

The dashboard business: how a cluster earns its money

No engineering background needed — just what the part is, and why it is worth more every year.

An instrument cluster is the module directly in front of the rider or driver that reports what the vehicle is doing. In its oldest form it was purely mechanical: a spinning cable drove a needle for speed, a float in the tank moved a fuel gauge, a few warning bulbs blinked. Today the same box is an electronic display — often a colour TFT screen — fed by sensors and software, showing not just speed and fuel but trip data, service alerts, navigation, call and music controls, and on an electric vehicle the battery state and range. Pricol designs, engineers and manufactures these clusters and sells them to the vehicle maker (the OEM), who fits them on the assembly line.

To follow the business, five terms carry most of the weight.

Why leadership on a small part is powerful

A cluster is a modest slice of a vehicle's cost, yet it is unusually sticky. It has to talk to the vehicle's electronics, pass safety and emissions certification, survive heat, rain and vibration for a decade, and match the exact look the OEM wants. Once a supplier is designed in to a model and its software is validated, swapping it out mid-life is expensive and risky for the OEM — so Pricol tends to keep a platform for that model's full run, often five to eight years. Multiply that stickiness across most of India's two-wheeler and truck models and you have a business that does not have to re-win its customers every year; it has to keep winning the next model.

Who buys from Pricol

  • Two-wheeler makers — the core. TVS, Bajaj, Hero, Honda's Indian arm, Royal Enfield and Suzuki all fit Pricol clusters across scooters and motorcycles. India makes and sells more two-wheelers than any country on earth, which is why this is the anchor customer class.
  • Commercial-vehicle makers — Tata Motors, Ashok Leyland, VE Commercial Vehicles and others, where Pricol's share is even higher. Trucks and buses carry more sensors and gauges per vehicle, so content per unit is richer.
  • Passenger-car & off-highway / export customers — a smaller but faster-growing slice, including LCD and TFT clusters for Tata Motors' cars and TFT units for electric commercial vehicles, plus off-highway export work for global names like Caterpillar.

Three businesses under one supplier

Read this once and the whole company clicks into place — because Pricol is really three companies stapled together.

Pricol is not just a dashboard maker. It runs three distinct segments with very different economics, and the mix shifted sharply in FY26 after a big acquisition. Knowing which is which is essential — especially because one of them is about to be spun off.

The three segments, decoded

  • DICVS — Driver Information & Connected Vehicle Solutions — the electronics heart of the company: analog, digital and TFT instrument clusters, telematics boxes, "e-cockpit" display systems, and battery-management electronics for EVs. This is where Pricol's design skill, share leadership and premiumisation all concentrate, and where margins are richest. It is also the segment being demerged into Pricol Autotech.
  • ACFMS — Actuation, Control & Fluid Management Systems — the mechanical portfolio: oil and coolant pumps, fuel and speed sensors, wiping systems, cab-tilt mechanisms, chain tensioners and a new disc-brake line. Less glamorous than a screen, but it deepens content per vehicle and — crucially — carries a real export book, supplying off-highway and industrial customers in the US and Europe, including Caterpillar.
  • P3L — the polymer / precision-plastics business — acquired in January 2025 from Sundaram Auto Components (a TVS-group entity) for ~₹215 Cr, this is a large plastic injection-moulding operation that added roughly ₹900 Cr of annual revenue. It runs at a low single-to-high-single-digit margin: a volume-and-scale business, not a premium one, and the main reason FY26 revenue grew far faster than profit.
Why the segment mix matters more than the headline growth

The ~51% FY26 revenue jump looks spectacular, but a reader has to unbundle it. A large part is simply P3L's ₹900-odd Cr of newly consolidated, thin-margin revenue — real turnover, but it barely lifts the profit margin and can even dilute it. The quality growth is inside DICVS, where a richer analog-to-TFT mix raises both the price of each cluster and the margin on it. So the right way to read Pricol is not "how fast did revenue grow?" but "how fast is DICVS growing, and is its margin holding as volumes scale?" That is the number that compounds shareholder value; the P3L revenue is scale, not richness.

03

The Mohan family, and a genuinely hard history

A half-century-old Coimbatore house — with a past far more dramatic than its steady numbers suggest.

Pricol was founded in 1974 in Coimbatore as Premier Instruments & Controls Ltd, and has been controlled by the Mohan family ever since. It is one of the anchors of the Coimbatore engineering cluster — a region famous for pumps, motors and precision manufacturing. But the company's path to today's steady compounding ran through two serious near-death chapters: a violent labour dispute, and a run of loss-making overseas acquisitions. Both are worth knowing, because both shaped the disciplined, domestically-focused company you see now.

Timeline

  • 1990s–2000s
    Becomes the dominant domestic instrument-cluster supplier to two-wheeler and commercial-vehicle makers; expands into pumps, sensors and other components.
  • 2007
    Opens an overseas subsidiary in Indonesia (PT Pricol Surya) — the start of an international push that would prove costly.
  • 2009
    A violent labour dispute at the Coimbatore plant turns fatal — a senior HR executive is killed during unrest. A dark chapter that forced a deep reassessment of the company's labour and operating model.
  • 2015
    Acquires Melling do Brasil (renamed Pricol do Brasil) for a nominal sum — an overseas bet that, alongside other foreign units, kept bleeding losses.
  • 2016–2017
    Corporate restructuring & relisting: the operating business is reorganised into the present listed entity (BSE 540293 / NSE PRICOLLTD); loss-making overseas and non-core units are exited; the company refocuses on India.
  • 2017
    Acquires PMP Auto Components' wiping-systems business, broadening the mechanical (ACFMS) portfolio.
  • Jan 2025
    Buys Sundaram Auto Components' injection-moulding business for ~₹215 Cr (the P3L segment), adding ~₹900 Cr of annual revenue.
  • May 2026
    Vikram Mohan becomes Chairman, succeeding Vanitha Mohan, who steps back to focus on social work — a generational handover.
  • Jun 2026
    Board approves the demerger of DICVS into a new listed company, Pricol Autotech, on a 1:1 share basis.

Ownership & control

  • Promoters: the Mohan family (Vijay Mohan as founder; Vanitha Mohan and now Vikram Mohan leading the board, with a third generation entering) hold roughly 38–39% — a controlling but not overwhelming stake, meaning institutional investors have a real voice.
  • Institutional weight: foreign investors hold ~14% and domestic institutions ~12% as of mid-2026 — a combined institutional book of over a quarter of the company, a sign the market takes the turnaround seriously.
  • Leadership handover: the 2026 shift from Vanitha to Vikram Mohan as Chairman is a genuine succession event, not a title change — worth watching for continuity of strategy through the demerger.
Why the hard history still matters: the 2009 factory tragedy and the loss-making foreign ventures are not just old news — they are the reason today's Pricol is deliberately India-centric, more conservatively run, and quicker to exit anything non-core. An investor should read the current discipline as a response to those episodes, and should also treat "will management stay disciplined?" as a live question, given the family's earlier taste for overseas expansion.
04

Analog → digital → TFT: the premiumisation engine

The single biggest driver of the whole investment case — and it works even if India sells no more vehicles.

For most of Pricol's life the instrument cluster was a low-value mechanical part. Two forces changed that. First, the 2020 shift to BS-VI emission norms pushed vehicles toward electronic engine management, which needs a digital dashboard to talk to. Second, buyers — raised on smartphones — now expect a screen on their scooter or truck the way they expect one in their pocket. The result is a steady migration up the value ladder: from needles, to a digital LCD readout, to a full-colour TFT graphical display. Each rung is worth several times the one below.

Illustrative bands, not Pricol's disclosed prices — the point is the ratio. Moving one cluster from analog to TFT can multiply Pricol's revenue on that vehicle several-fold, with no extra units sold. Digital and TFT clusters already make up the large majority of DICVS revenue.

This is why "content per vehicle" is the phrase to hold onto. India's two-wheeler market grows only a few percent a year in good years and can shrink in bad ones — a thin tailwind on its own. But if the average cluster Pricol ships keeps climbing from analog to digital to TFT, its revenue per vehicle can grow at a double-digit pace regardless of whether India buys more bikes. Layer on the extra ACFMS parts — a pump here, a sensor there, a disc brake — and the rupee value of Pricol on a single vehicle compounds from two directions at once.

The device that only fits Pricol: the same bike, more Pricol inside

Picture one specific scooter that sold last year and sells again this year — same model, same buyer, same showroom. On paper, that is zero unit growth for Pricol. Yet if last year it carried a ₹500 analog cluster and this year the facelift carries a ₹4,000 TFT screen, Pricol's revenue from that one unchanged scooter has grown roughly eight-fold. Now add a Pricol fuel-pump module and a disc brake the regulator newly mandates, and the content climbs again. Pricol's growth engine is not "more vehicles"; it is "more Pricol per vehicle" — and because it already supplies most of the dashboards, it captures most of that upgrade automatically. This is the mechanism that lets a supplier to a flat end-market grow revenue and profit for years.

A genuine surprise: electrification, which threatens many engine-part suppliers, is a tailwind for Pricol. An EV has no exhaust and fewer moving parts, but it needs a rich digital dashboard, a battery-management display and more connected electronics than a petrol bike — exactly Pricol's DICVS wheelhouse. The company is, unusually for an auto-component maker, close to powertrain-agnostic: whether India rides petrol or electric, the dashboard still gets more valuable, and Pricol still supplies it.
05

Where the moat actually lives

Why a part that looks copyable is hard to take away — the core of the bull case.

A skeptic's first reaction to Pricol is fair: a dashboard screen sounds like a commodity, and the world is full of electronics contract manufacturers. So why does a Coimbatore firm hold 55–75% of a market and earn a 20%-plus return on capital? The answer is that the moat is not in the screen itself — it is in three things that surround it: getting designed in, building locally, and being trusted with a part that cannot fail.

1 · Design-in and the platform lock

A cluster is not bought off a shelf; it is co-engineered into a specific vehicle model years before that model launches. Pricol's engineers work with the OEM's team to match the graphics, the warning logic, the communication protocol and the durability spec. Once that work is done and the model is in production, the OEM has every reason to keep Pricol for the model's full life — re-validating a new supplier mid-cycle risks recalls, delays and re-certification. Win the design-in, and you win five-to-eight years of supply. Pricol's incumbency across most Indian platforms means it is defending, not chasing, the next model each time.

2 · Localisation as a cost-and-speed weapon

Pricol builds its clusters and much of its electronics in India, close to its customers. For an OEM racing to launch a facelift, a local supplier that can iterate fast, hold inventory and avoid import lead-times and currency risk is worth paying for. As India pushes to localise electronics and reduce import dependence, a domestic designer-and-maker of dashboard electronics is exactly the kind of supplier OEMs are being nudged toward. That is a structural advantage a pure importer cannot match.

3 · The trust of a part that cannot fail

The cluster is a safety and compliance-critical part: it shows the warnings that keep a vehicle road-legal and a rider safe, and it must survive a decade of heat, monsoon and vibration. An OEM does not hand that to the cheapest bidder; it hands it to a supplier with a track record of not failing at scale. Decades of shipping tens of millions of clusters without a reputation-wrecking recall is the asset — one a new entrant cannot buy, only build over years.

How to test whether the moat is real

The honest test is not market share — it is whether Pricol keeps winning the next generation of clusters, not just holding the last one. Watch three tells: are marquee new launches (a flagship TFT bike, an EV cockpit, a new truck platform) shipping with Pricol inside? Is the DICVS margin holding or rising as TFT scales, rather than eroding under price pressure? And is content per vehicle climbing faster than unit volumes? If all three hold, the moat is doing its job. If Pricol starts winning volume only by cutting price — share up, margin down — the moat is leaking, and the premium multiple is the first thing that should worry an owner.

06

The Autotech demerger: splitting the jewel out

The live corporate event that changes how the whole company should be valued.

In June 2026 Pricol's board approved a plan to demerge the DICVS electronics business into a separate listed company, Pricol Autotech Limited, giving existing shareholders one Autotech share for each Pricol share they hold. Instead of one stock that mixes high-margin electronics with lower-margin mechanicals and polymers, investors will end up owning two distinct listed companies. This is the single most important corporate development in the current story, and it is still working its way through approvals.

What each side will hold

  • Pricol Autotech (the electronics pure-play): the DICVS crown jewel — instrument clusters, TFT displays, telematics, e-cockpits and battery-management electronics. The high-margin, high-growth, premiumisation-driven engine, now standing alone where the market can value it as a focused technology-and-electronics supplier.
  • Pricol (the mechanical & polymer base): the ACFMS pumps-sensors-wiping-brakes portfolio with its export book, plus the P3L injection-moulding scale business. A more industrial, more diversified, lower-multiple profile with its own growth levers in exports and new product lines like disc brakes.
Why a company splits itself in two

The logic of a demerger like this is that the market struggles to price two very different businesses stapled together — a 20%-margin electronics grower and a high-single-digit-margin polymer moulder end up sharing one blended multiple that flatters neither. Separating them lets each attract its natural investor: growth funds that want the dashboard-electronics story can own Autotech cleanly, while value and industrial investors can own the mechanical base. It also sharpens management focus and gives each entity its own currency for future deals or fundraising. The counterpoint is that a split adds cost and complexity, removes the cushion of diversification, and only creates value if each half is genuinely worth more apart than together — which the market, not the announcement, decides.

The nuance to hold: until the demerger completes, be careful comparing Pricol's valuation to peers — you are pricing a bundle that is about to become two. The key question for a prospective owner is which of the two future companies they actually want to hold, because after the split they will own both, in different proportions of value. Track the scheme's progress through board, creditor, shareholder and tribunal approvals; the record date and listing of Autotech are the milestones that turn this from a plan into a fact.
07

The second engine: ACFMS, exports and disc brakes

The less-glamorous half that diversifies the base and reaches beyond India.

If DICVS is the star, the ACFMS segment is the workhorse that keeps Pricol from being a one-product company. It gathers the mechanical and fluid-handling parts — oil and coolant pumps, fuel and speed sensors, wiping systems, cab-tilt mechanisms, chain tensioners — and increasingly a new disc-brake line. Two things make it more interesting than "spare bits": it carries a real export business, and it is winning new content on the back of regulation.

The export angle is the surprise inside ACFMS. Pricol supplies pumps and components to global off-highway and industrial customers — Caterpillar among them — with the United States accounting for the bulk of its export revenue in this division. That gives Pricol a foothold in demanding, higher-spec international markets and a source of revenue not tied to the Indian two-wheeler cycle. In FY26 the segment grew strongly, helped by US and European off-highway customers moving into mass production.

The disc-brake opportunity is a neat example of regulation creating content. As braking-safety norms tighten for two-wheelers, disc brakes shift from a premium option toward a wider requirement — and Pricol has geared up to supply a large two-wheeler OEM, with mass production beginning around FY27. It is a classic Pricol move: use its incumbency with the same customers who already buy its clusters to sell them a newly-mandated part, adding rupees of content to vehicles it already touches.

Why the workhorse matters to the story

Diversification is not free — ACFMS and P3L run at lower margins than DICVS, so leaning into them dilutes the blended profitability that makes Pricol look premium. But they buy something valuable: resilience. A pure dashboard-electronics company lives and dies by the Indian two-wheeler cycle and by winning each cluster platform. Bolt on an export book, a regulated new product and a scale polymer business, and a bad year in domestic two-wheelers no longer sinks the whole ship. After the Autotech demerger, this resilient-but-lower-margin base becomes the entire remaining Pricol — which is exactly why an investor needs to judge it on its own merits, not as a footnote to the electronics arm.

08

From drama to discipline: the turnaround

How a company with a troubled past became a debt-light, high-return compounder.

The Pricol of a decade ago was a different animal: burdened by loss-making foreign subsidiaries, carrying debt, and still living down a violent labour episode. The company that reports today has quietly become one of the better-run mid-cap auto-component names — margins up, returns up, debt manageable, and a clean domestic focus. The repair is the least-discussed but most important part of why the stock re-rated.

Three things drove the repair. Pricol exited the loss-making overseas ventures and non-core units, stopping the bleed and freeing management to focus on India. It rode the premiumisation mix up, so each rupee of revenue carried a better margin. And it kept the balance sheet in check — funding the P3L acquisition without over-levering, and carrying only modest net debt (a few hundred crore) against a business now throwing off healthy cash and a mid-20s return on capital.

Why returns, not revenue, tell the real story

Any company can grow revenue by buying it — P3L proves that. What separates a genuine turnaround from a debt-fuelled sugar high is whether returns on capital rise as the business grows. Pricol's did: ROCE roughly doubled to the mid-20s even while it was acquiring and investing. That combination — growth plus rising returns plus contained debt — is what re-rated the stock from a forgotten mid-cap to a ~35× earnings name. It also sets the bar: to keep the premium, Pricol has to keep returns high through the demerger and the P3L margin drag, not just keep growing the top line.

09

Customers, concentration and the EV question

The dependencies that could bite — and the technology shift that, unusually, helps.

Pricol's strength — dominating the dashboards of India's two-wheeler and truck makers — is also its concentration risk. A supplier that leans on a handful of large OEMs, in one or two vehicle segments, is exposed to their fortunes and their bargaining power. Set against that is a technology shift, electrification, that for once runs in Pricol's favour.

The EV point deserves emphasis because it inverts the usual auto-component fear. When a market electrifies, suppliers of engine parts — pistons, fuel systems, exhaust components — face shrinking content. Pricol is largely on the other side of that trade: an electric two-wheeler still needs a dashboard, and typically a more sophisticated one, plus battery-management electronics that a petrol bike never had. So the shift that threatens much of the components industry tends to deepen Pricol's content per vehicle. The mechanical ACFMS parts are more powertrain-sensitive, but the DICVS heart of the company is well placed whichever way India's fleet turns.

The concentration to watch, stated plainly

The uncomfortable truth is that a few big two-wheeler makers can move Pricol's numbers. If one major customer in-sources its clusters, switches to a rival, or simply has a bad couple of years, Pricol feels it directly — there is no consumer brand or pricing power to cushion the blow, only the strength of the design-in relationship. The mitigants are real: deep incumbency, a broadening product list that makes Pricol harder to drop, and diversification into exports and CVs. But an investor should size the position knowing that this is a business whose fortunes are tied, more than most, to the health and loyalty of a short list of powerful buyers.

10

Financials & valuation

A real quality business — priced as one, with a demerger in the mix.

FY26 was Pricol's biggest year: revenue up ~51% to ₹3,964 Cr, net profit up ~50% to ₹251 Cr, and returns in the low-to-mid 20s. The market has rewarded the turnaround with a premium multiple — around 35× earnings — which prices Pricol as a quality compounder rather than a cyclical component maker. The debate is whether the underlying, margin-rich growth keeps pace with that expectation, especially after the P3L revenue is stripped out.

The reported profitability needs one clarification. An EBITDA margin of ~12.4% is respectable for an auto-component maker, but it is a blended figure held down by the low-margin P3L polymer and ACFMS mechanical businesses; the DICVS electronics arm earns materially more. So the headline margin understates the profitability of the crown jewel — which is precisely why separating it out (the Autotech demerger) could let the market see, and price, that richer margin on its own.

The one number that settles the thesis: DICVS margin and content per vehicle

At ~35× earnings, Pricol is priced as a premiumisation compounder, not a bulk parts vendor — so the metric that proves or breaks the case is the growth and margin of the DICVS electronics business, read through content per vehicle and the digital-and-TFT share of cluster revenue. If DICVS growth slows, or Pricol is forced to buy volume by cutting cluster prices so the margin slips, then the blended P3L-and-ACFMS mix drags the whole business toward an ordinary component-maker profile, and a premium multiple has little to hold onto. The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A quality supplier riding a genuine content-per-vehicle wave — asked to keep the electronics margin as it splits itself apart.

Pricol enters this phase in the strongest shape of its listed life: market leadership in a part that keeps getting more valuable, a fixed balance sheet, mid-20s returns, a diversifying export and product base, and a value-unlocking demerger in motion. The debate is no longer whether the business is good — it is whether the premium electronics engine keeps compounding, and stays high-margin, as the company restructures and leans on lower-margin scale acquisitions.

The bull case

  • Premiumisation runway — the analog→digital→TFT shift lifts content per vehicle for years, largely independent of how many vehicles India sells.
  • Dominant, sticky share — 55–75% of clusters, design-in lock-ins and decades of trust make Pricol hard to displace on the next platform.
  • EV-agnostic, even EV-positive — electrification deepens dashboard content rather than removing it; a rare component maker on the right side of the transition.
  • Value-unlocking demerger — a separately listed Autotech could let the market price the high-margin electronics arm on its own merits.
  • Quality economics — mid-20s ROCE, low debt, strong cash generation and a diversifying export/disc-brake pipeline.

The bear case

  • Customer & segment concentration — heavy reliance on a few large two-wheeler and CV makers, who hold real pricing power and could in-source.
  • Margin dilution from scale deals — P3L and parts of ACFMS run at thin margins; leaning on them can drag blended profitability even as revenue grows.
  • Rich valuation — ~35× earnings prices in continued premium growth; any DICVS slowdown or margin slip can de-rate the stock sharply.
  • Execution & complexity of the demerger — the split adds cost, needs multiple approvals, and only creates value if each half is truly worth more apart.
  • History of missteps — a past of loss-making overseas ventures and a violent labour episode is a reminder that discipline, once lost, was costly to rebuild.
The bottom line

Pricol has turned a part most people never notice into one of the better content-per-vehicle stories in Indian autos: it owns the dashboards of the country's two-wheelers and trucks just as those dashboards graduate from needles to colour screens, and it does so with high returns and a clean balance sheet. What it must now prove is that the electronics engine stays both fast and rich — growing DICVS revenue per vehicle while defending its margin against tough OEM buyers and its own lower-margin acquisitions — through the disruption of splitting into two listed companies. The next concrete milestones are checkable: the record date and separate listing of Pricol Autotech, the disc-brake ramp into mass production, and quarter after quarter of DICVS margin holding as TFT scales. Watch the digital-cluster mix and the electronics margin; they will tell you whether the premium the market is paying was earned or merely hoped for.
