Electronics Mart India, Hyderabad's appliance kingdom

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Electronics Mart India Limited (EMIL) sells the televisions, refrigerators, air-conditioners, washing machines, phones and kitchen gadgets that fill an Indian home — not online, but through a chain of large, brightly-lit superstores you walk into, mostly under the trusted "Bajaj Electronics" banner. It runs roughly 150 stores across around 50 cities and is India's fourth-largest consumer-electronics and durables retailer — and, more to the point, the undisputed leader in South India, with a stronghold so deep in Telangana and Andhra Pradesh that in Hyderabad it is simply where people buy a fridge. It listed on the exchanges in a heavily-subscribed October 2022 IPO. The family behind it, the Bajajs of Hyderabad (no relation to the auto group), still control it.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a high-volume, thin-margin machine — not a high-margin one. EMIL turned ~₹6,965 Cr of FY25 revenue into a gross margin of only about 14–15% and an operating (EBITDA) margin near 6–7%, leaving a net margin barely above 2%. A consumer-electronics retailer does not get rich on any single sale; it gets rich on turning inventory over — buying a container of ACs, selling it fast, and doing that again and again. Scale, store density and inventory turns are the business, not sticker mark-up.
  • Its moat is geographic density, not a national footprint. The overwhelming majority of revenue still comes from the Telangana–Andhra Pradesh cluster, where four decades of "Bajaj Electronics" stores give EMIL better vendor terms, better brand recall and a defensive wall against Reliance and Croma. Owning one region block-by-block is worth more than a thin banner spread across twenty. The risk sits in the opposite move.
  • The whole equity story is now the march North. EMIL has pushed into Delhi-NCR and Kolkata to reduce its dependence on the Hyderabad economy — but a brand-new store in Gurugram, where nobody has heard of Bajaj Electronics, opens with high rent, low footfall and negative economics, and takes a couple of years to mature. That deliberate drag is exactly why FY25 profit dipped even as revenue grew — and why the stock trades on a demanding multiple, betting the new stores eventually earn like the old ones.
In plain terms

Picture a very large, very busy electronics showroom on a Hyderabad main road — rows of TVs on a wall, a fridge aisle, a phone counter, a summer stack of air-conditioners by the door — and then picture a hundred and fifty of them. EMIL's genius is not what it sells (everyone sells the same Samsung TV and LG fridge) but where and how densely it sells: so many stores packed into two southern states that it buys cheaper than rivals, is the default name locals trust, and squeezes a real profit out of a business where each sale earns almost nothing. The open question is whether it can copy that density in the north, where it starts as an unknown, before the cost of trying eats the profit the south throws off. FY25 was the year the northern bill showed up in the numbers; the summer of 2026 was the year the model reminded everyone how well it works at home.

01

Big-box electronics 101: how a razor-thin business makes money

No retail degree needed — just footfall, mark-up, and the speed at which stock turns.

A consumer-electronics retailer looks simple and is anything but. It buys branded goods from manufacturers, marks them up a little, and sells them from a shop. The trap is the "a little": on a ₹40,000 refrigerator, a chain like EMIL might keep ₹5,000–6,000 of gross margin, and after rent, salaries, electricity and financing, the net profit on that fridge is a few hundred rupees. There is no room to be sloppy. The entire craft of the business is doing five things well at once.

The five levers of a durables retailer
LeverWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Footfall × conversionHow many people walk in, and how many walk out having bought. A durables purchase is considered and assisted — a salesperson demonstrates the TV, explains the fridge — so store location, layout and staff quality drive the number directly.
ASP (average selling price)The average ticket per sale. Premiumisation — a customer trading up from a 43" to a 65" TV, or to an inverter AC — lifts ASP and rupee margin without adding a single extra footfall. Rising Indian incomes make this the quiet tailwind of the sector.
Gross marginThe mark-up kept after paying the brand. Thin (~14–15%) and largely set by the manufacturer, but nudged up by vendor schemes, volume rebates and mix (appliances margin better than phones).
Inventory turnsHow many times a year the shop sells and replaces its stock. This is the hidden engine: at a 2% net margin, turning inventory 6× a year earns far more on the same capital than turning it 4×. Speed is profit.
Attach & servicesThe extras bolted onto the box — extended warranty, consumer finance/EMI, installation, delivery. High-margin, capital-light, and where a chunk of the real profit hides (section 06).

Why "low margin" is not the same as "bad business"

A 2% net margin sounds fragile, and on any one sale it is. But retail earns its returns on capital velocity, not on margin. If a store buys a fridge for ₹34,000, sells it in two months, and does that repeatedly, the same ₹34,000 of shelf capital earns its 2% margin several times a year — so a thin margin compounds into a respectable return on capital provided the stock keeps moving. The danger is the mirror image: a store full of last season's unsold TVs is capital frozen at a 2% margin, quietly bleeding rent and interest. That is why for EMIL the numbers that matter are never just sales and profit, but sales per square foot and inventory days — the pulse of how fast the machine is turning.

02

The economics of a single store

The company is really just this one unit, repeated ~150 times — so understand the unit.

A retail chain is only as good as its typical store, because the whole business is that store copied and pasted across a map. EMIL runs large-format outlets — often 8,000 to 20,000+ square feet, averaging roughly 8,000 sq ft across a ~12 lakh sq ft estate — carrying 6,000+ SKUs from 70-plus brands. Divide the company by its stores and a clean picture appears: around ₹45–50 Cr of annual revenue per store, though that average hides an enormous gap between a mature southern flagship and a year-old northern opening.

Divide the company by its stores and the productivity metrics analysts actually track come into focus: roughly ₹46 Cr of revenue per store, about ₹6.7 Cr of gross profit per store (at ~14.5%), and on the order of ₹55,000–60,000 of sales per square foot per year across the ~12 lakh sq ft estate. None of these is a headline number the company trumpets, but together they are the pulse of the business — and every new store opening dilutes them until it matures, which is exactly why the blended figures move around.

Owned vs leased — a balance-sheet choice hiding in plain sight

EMIL runs two store models. Under the ownership model it buys the land and building; under the more common lease model it signs long-term rentals. Owning removes rent and gives control of prime locations, but ties up capital and shows up as property on the balance sheet; leasing is capital-light but loads the P&L with rent and creates lease liabilities. The mix matters because rent is one of the few large fixed costs a retailer carries — and a fixed cost is exactly what turns operating leverage vicious in a bad quarter and beautiful in a good one.

The device that decides this company: the store maturity curve

Every EMIL store lives on a curve. A brand-new outlet opens with the full cost — rent, staff, fit-out, interest — but only a fraction of the eventual footfall, so it loses money or barely breaks even in year one. Over two to three years, as locals learn the store exists and traffic builds, sales climb toward the mature level while costs stay roughly flat — and profit appears, then compounds. So EMIL's reported margin at any moment is a blend of mature cash-cows and immature cash-drains, and it moves not with how good the business is but with how many young stores are in the mix. Open a burst of new stores — as EMIL did pushing north — and group margin dips even though nothing is wrong; pause openings and let the base age, and margin springs back. This single mechanic explains the FY25 profit dip and the Q1-FY27 rebound better than any macro story. The same 8,000 sq ft is a cash machine in Secunderabad and a cash drain in Gurugram — for now.

03

The South India moat: why density beats footprint

The single most important idea in the case — and it is specific to a regional-cluster retailer.

Ask why a customer picks EMIL over Reliance Digital across the road, and the honest answer in Hyderabad is: habit and trust built over forty years. That is not a soft advantage — it is the entire moat, and it is made of density. EMIL has so many "Bajaj Electronics" stores packed into Telangana and Andhra Pradesh that it dominates those two states the way no national chain dominates anywhere. Cluster dominance, not geographic spread, is what compounds.

Why does packing stores into one region matter so much? Because retail density feeds on itself through three reinforcing loops:

  • Supplier terms. A brand like Samsung or LG sells more units through EMIL in Telangana than through any rival, so EMIL negotiates better prices, priority stock, richer margin schemes and exclusive launches. Cheaper buying on the same shelf price is pure margin — and it is available only to the dominant local player.
  • Brand recall & trust. Four decades of "Bajaj Electronics" storefronts, local advertising and after-sales presence make it the default — the name a Hyderabad family thinks of first for a wedding-season fridge. Trust in a big-ticket, once-in-five-years purchase is worth real money and takes real time to build.
  • Logistics & service density. Many stores in one geography share warehouses, delivery fleets and service technicians efficiently. The cost to serve per store falls as density rises — fast delivery and quick installation become cheap to offer, and those are exactly what beat an online order on a heavy appliance.
The surprise buried in the moat: this beats online precisely on the heavy stuff

It is tempting to assume Amazon and Flipkart must be steamrolling a bricks-and-mortar electronics chain. On phones and small gadgets, they genuinely pressure it. But on large appliances — the fridge, the washing machine, the split AC — physical density quietly wins, and that is EMIL's core. A customer buying a ₹60,000 air-conditioner in peak summer wants to see it, wants same-day delivery and next-day installation by a technician who will come back if it fails, and wants the reassurance of a shop they can walk into if something breaks. A dense local chain delivers all of that at a cost an online seller shipping from a distant warehouse cannot match. The heavier and more service-hungry the product, the stronger EMIL's hand — which is why its mix leans so deliberately toward big appliances rather than phones.

The two-edged nature of the moat: the same concentration that gives EMIL its pricing power and trust in the south also makes it a bet on two states' economies. A slowdown in the Hyderabad IT-driven economy, an adverse festive season in the Telugu belt, or a determined price war by Reliance in that specific geography hits EMIL harder than a nationally-spread rival. The moat is real; so is the concentration risk sitting right beside it. Reducing that dependence is precisely why the company is spending to go north (section 07).
04

What's on the shelf: the category mix and the AC season

A basket tilted toward big appliances — and a calendar ruled by the Indian summer.

EMIL sells across the full spread of a modern home, but not evenly. Its revenue leans heavily toward large appliances — air-conditioners, televisions, refrigerators and washing machines — with mobile phones a large second bucket and small appliances, IT and accessories making up the remainder. That tilt is a choice, and a smart one: big appliances carry better margins, demand in-store demonstration and after-sales service, and are hardest for online sellers to steal.

The tyranny (and gift) of the summer quarter

Because air-conditioners and refrigerators are such a large slice of the basket, EMIL's year is not evenly spread — it is ruled by the weather. The April–June quarter (Q1 of the Indian fiscal year) is peak cooling season, when a hot summer sends AC and fridge sales surging; the festive October–December quarter adds a second, smaller peak around Diwali and weddings. A blazing summer can make the year; a mild or delayed one leaves premium inventory sitting through the hottest weeks. The strong Q1 FY27 (revenue +39%, record quarterly profit) was in large part a good summer meeting a maturing store base.

Why premiumisation is the tailwind under the whole basket

Across every category, the same slow force is lifting the average ticket: Indian buyers are trading up. A household that once bought a 32-inch TV now buys a 55-inch; a window AC becomes an inverter split; a single-door fridge becomes a frost-free double-door. Each trade-up puts more rupees of margin through the same footfall and the same square foot, without EMIL having to win a single new customer. This is the quiet compounding beneath consumer-durables retail — rising incomes and aspiration doing the work — and it favours a chain that specialises in assisted, high-touch selling of exactly these considered, premium purchases. The risk on the other side is that discretionary trade-up is the first thing a nervous consumer postpones when the economy wobbles.

05

The Bajaj family, the brands, and the float

A four-decade Hyderabad family business that put a slice on the market in 2022.

Electronics Mart is a family-built retail house, not a private-equity roll-up. It traces to 1980, when the Bajaj family of Hyderabad (unrelated to the Pune auto and finance Bajajs) opened its first electronics store; the "Bajaj Electronics" name became a southern institution over the following decades, and the family — led today by the second generation — still runs and controls the company. Understanding the promoter and the brand architecture explains both the culture and the governance.

The brand architecture

  • Bajaj Electronics — the flagship multi-brand banner, and the powerhouse in Telangana and Andhra. This is the trusted, four-decade name that is the moat in the south.
  • Electronics Mart — the banner used for the newer markets, notably Delhi-NCR, where the Bajaj Electronics name carries no local history. The listed company itself is Electronics Mart India Ltd.
  • iQ, Kitchen Stories, Audio & Beyond — specialist exclusive-brand formats: iQ is an authorised Apple reseller; Kitchen Stories focuses on premium built-in kitchen appliances; Audio & Beyond on home audio. These are format brands, not house product labels (an important distinction — see section 06).
The governance texture to watch: as a promoter-controlled family retailer that came to market only in 2022, EMIL carries the usual family-business questions — related-party property and lease arrangements, succession, and the discipline of a founding family now answerable to public minorities. The flip side is genuine skin in the game and deep operational knowledge of a hard, low-margin trade. Investors should read the shareholding pattern, related-party disclosures and lease structures in the latest annual report rather than rely on any single summarised figure here.
06

The counter-intuitive edge: EMIL sells no house brands at all

Where the profit really comes from when you refuse to sell your own cheaper goods.

Here is the fact that surprises almost everyone who assumes a retailer's margin edge comes from private label: Electronics Mart does no private labelling or white-labelling whatsoever. It deliberately stocks only the top 70-plus national and global brands — no "EMIL-brand" budget TV, no house-label mixer sitting at a fat margin beside the Samsungs and LGs. Almost every retailer its size leans on private label to lift margin; EMIL has consciously chosen not to. So where does a thin-margin retailer that won't sell its own goods actually make its money?

The box itself earns little. The real economics come from buying better at scale, bolting high-margin services onto each sale, and steering the mix toward premium — none of which requires a house brand.

The most important of these is the attach pool. When a salesperson sells a ₹55,000 TV and then adds a three-year extended warranty and a no-cost EMI financing plan, those add-ons carry margins far above the box itself and tie up almost no inventory. Multiply a small high-margin attach across millions of transactions and it becomes a meaningful, capital-light slice of profit that is invisible in the headline "electronics retailer" label. Consumer financing in particular — the EMI schemes that let a middle-class family buy a premium appliance now and pay monthly — both lifts EMIL's ASP (people buy bigger on EMI) and earns fee income on the arrangement.

Why refusing private label is a considered bet, not a missed trick

The logic runs against retail convention, and it is deliberate. In big-ticket consumer durables, the brand is the reassurance: a family spending two months' income on a refrigerator wants a Samsung or an LG, not a store label, and will not gamble a five-year purchase to save a little. Stocking only trusted brands protects the one thing EMIL sells that online cannot easily replicate — confidence at the point of a large, infrequent purchase. It also keeps the powerful national brands as partners rather than rivals, which is what unlocks the vendor terms and priority stock in Pool 2. EMIL's answer to the margin question is therefore not "sell our own cheaper box" but "be the dominant, trusted seller of their box, and monetise the scale, the service and the finance around it." Whether that keeps margins competitive against private-label-heavy rivals is a fair debate — but it is a strategy, not an oversight.

07

The march North: the expansion that decides everything

The costly, deliberate bet to turn a two-state champion into a national retailer.

If the south is the cash engine, the north and east are the growth thesis — and the risk. EMIL has spent to enter Delhi-NCR and, more recently, Kolkata, investing on the order of ₹120 Cr+ to reduce its heavy dependence on the Hyderabad-driven southern economy. The strategic logic is sound: a company concentrated in two states is fragile, and India's durables market is nationwide. But the execution runs straight into the very moat mechanics that make EMIL strong at home.

In NCR, EMIL is not "Bajaj Electronics, the name grandad trusted." It is a newcomer under an unfamiliar banner, opening large stores at metro-Delhi rents, against entrenched rivals (Reliance Digital, Croma, Vijay Sales) who already have the density, the vendor terms and the brand recall that took EMIL forty years to build in the south. A new northern store therefore opens on the wrong end of the maturity curve from section 02: full fixed cost, thin traffic, negative-to-breakeven economics — and it stays there for a year or two while the market learns it exists.

The uncomfortable question at the heart of the stock

Density made EMIL what it is — so the real challenge is whether density can be rebuilt in a hostile new market, or whether it only ever formed once, by accident of a forty-year head start at home. Cluster dominance is a chicken-and-egg problem: you need many stores to earn the vendor terms and recall that make each store profitable, but you need each store profitable to justify opening many. In the south, EMIL solved that loop over decades, unopposed. In NCR it must solve it fast, against rivals who have already solved it there. If it can — if the northern stores climb the maturity ladder and a second cluster forms — EMIL becomes a genuinely national retailer and the current profit drag reads as investment. If the northern stores stall as perpetual also-rans in someone else's territory, the expansion is a slow leak on the cash the south generates. There is no third outcome that matters.

This is why the NCR/east build-out is not a footnote but the whole equity question. It is also why management pace matters: expand too slowly and the concentration risk lingers; expand too fast and the drag of immature stores overwhelms the mature base's profit. Reading EMIL over the next few years is largely reading how well it walks that line.

08

Inventory and the balance sheet: the retailer's real risk

Where a thin-margin chain quietly wins or loses — in working capital, not the P&L.

For a durables retailer, the balance sheet is where the danger lives. EMIL must buy and hold a deep inventory — 6,000+ SKUs across 150 stores, stocked ahead of the summer and festive peaks — and it partly funds that stock with debt. At a 2% net margin, a working-capital mistake is expensive: unsold premium inventory is capital frozen at a wafer-thin return, financed by interest that does not pause.

Inventory and borrowings both dwarf annual EBITDA — the signature of a capital-intensive retailer, and the reason the health question here is never the size of the debt but whether inventory keeps turning fast enough to service it. The metric that captures this is inventory days — how long, on average, a product sits before it sells. Low and stable is healthy: stock moving briskly, capital recycling, debt comfortably serviced. Rising is the warning light: goods lingering, cash tied up, a possible glut that will need discounting (and discounting a 14% gross margin is painful). Because EMIL stocks up ahead of the AC season, its inventory naturally swells before summer and drains after — the thing to watch is whether it drains on schedule. A cool summer that leaves ACs unsold is not just a revenue miss; it is a balance-sheet event.

Why growth and debt rise together here — and why that's normal, up to a point

An expanding store chain consumes cash as it grows: every new store needs fit-out capital and an opening stock of inventory before it earns a rupee, and those are funded partly by borrowing. So EMIL can grow revenue, open stores, and see its debt climb all at once — and none of that is inherently alarming. The line between healthy and worrying is whether the new capital earns its keep: are the new stores maturing into the returns that justify the debt raised to build them, or is borrowing funding stores that stay sub-scale? Read the debt not in isolation but against store maturity and inventory turns. Rising debt behind a maturing, fast-turning store base is investment; rising debt behind a stalling one is a problem. That distinction is the whole difference, and it points straight back to the northern expansion.

09

The rivals — on the street and on the screen

A two-front war: national big-box chains offline, and the marketplaces online.

EMIL competes in two arenas at once. Offline, it faces deep-pocketed national chains and strong regional players; online, it faces the marketplaces that reset consumer price expectations. It is the fourth-largest durables retailer nationally but the clear leader in its home region — a reminder that in this business, where you are large matters as much as how large.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two truths sit side by side. First, on phones and small gadgets, where the product is light, standardised and needs no service, online marketplaces set the price and EMIL is largely a price-taker fighting for footfall — a genuine, permanent margin pressure. Second, on large appliances, where demonstration, financing, fast delivery and local service decide the sale, a dense regional chain still holds a real, defensible edge that neither online nor a thinly-spread national rival easily takes. EMIL's whole strategy — big-appliance tilt, service and finance attach, cluster density — is an attempt to fight the battle it can win and minimise the one it cannot. The threat that would genuinely hurt is Reliance deciding to buy market share in Telangana and Andhra with sustained below-cost pricing; deep pockets versus local density is the one matchup where EMIL's moat is truly tested.

10

Financials & valuation

Steady double-digit revenue growth, a wobble in margin, and a multiple that already assumes the north works.

EMIL's top line has compounded reliably — roughly 10–11% revenue growth into FY25's ~₹6,965 Cr and FY26's ~₹7,191 Cr — but its profit has been choppier, dipping in FY25 as new-store costs bit before the strong summer of Q1 FY27 snapped it back. The stock does not look like a cheap value play: it trades on a demanding earnings multiple (well into the 30s at times), which means the market is already paying for the northern expansion to succeed. This is the crux of the valuation debate.

The one number that settles the thesis: new-region same-store sales, read through blended EBITDA margin

At a mid-30s multiple, EMIL is not priced as a sleepy regional retailer — it is priced as a national chain in the making. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is same-store sales growth in the NCR and eastern cohort, watched quarter by quarter, and its footprint in the blended EBITDA margin. The logic is direct: if the northern stores are climbing the maturity curve, their same-store sales will grow briskly off a low base and the group's EBITDA margin will drift back up toward the mature-south level (mid-single to high-single digits) as the young-store drag fades — the Q1-FY27 margin snap-back (near 10%) is the first hint of exactly this. If northern same-store growth stalls and blended margin stays stuck below the southern norm, the stores are not maturing, density is not forming, and the multiple has no support. Not headline revenue, which grows simply by opening doors — but same-store growth outside the home cluster, and the margin it drags behind it. That, quarter after quarter, is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A southern cash machine buying its way to national scale — asked whether the density travels.

Electronics Mart enters the next phase with a strong hand at home — the leading durables chain in a large, growing southern market, riding premiumisation and a favourable summer — and an unresolved question away from it. What no regional retailer can promise is the one thing the price now assumes: that the moat which took four decades to build in Telangana can be rebuilt, faster and against entrenched rivals, in Delhi and Kolkata. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Unassailable southern moat — four-decade "Bajaj Electronics" density in Telangana/AP delivers vendor terms, trust and service reach rivals cannot quickly match.
  • Premiumisation tailwind — rising incomes push customers to bigger TVs, inverter ACs and premium appliances, lifting ASP and rupee margin through the same stores.
  • Big-appliance & service tilt — the mix leans to exactly the products online struggles with; warranty, finance and installation attach add capital-light, high-margin profit.
  • Optionality of the north — if the NCR/east cohort matures like the south did, today's margin drag flips to growth and a second cluster forms.
  • Proven operators with skin in the game — a founding family that knows a hard, thin-margin trade intimately and controls the company.

The bear case

  • Expansion may not travel — density formed once, unopposed, over decades; rebuilding it in hostile metros against Reliance and Croma is unproven and cash-hungry.
  • Wafer-thin margins — a ~2% net margin leaves no cushion; a cool summer, a demand wobble or a discount war compounds straight to the bottom line.
  • Concentration risk — heavy dependence on two states' economies means a southern slowdown or targeted price war hits harder than for a national rival.
  • Online pressure on phones/small goods — a permanent margin drag on a large, low-differentiation slice of the basket.
  • Full valuation — a mid-30s P/E already prices success; any stumble in northern maturation or margin recovery has little support beneath it.
The bottom line

Electronics Mart is a genuinely good business doing a genuinely hard thing — running a two-state electronics empire on razor-thin margins better than almost anyone, and throwing off the cash to attempt something harder still. The southern engine is real and defensible; the premiumisation tailwind is real; the big-appliance-plus-service strategy is smartly matched to the one battle online cannot easily win. But the price has moved ahead of the proof, and the proof is specific and datable: whether the Delhi-NCR and Kolkata stores post accelerating same-store sales and drag the group's blended EBITDA margin back toward its southern norm over the next several summer-and-festive cycles. Show that, and a two-state champion is becoming a national one and the multiple is earned; fail to, and EMIL settles back into what it undeniably already is — the best electronics retailer in South India, worth a good deal less than a national chain. The next few Q1 summers, read store-cohort by store-cohort, will tell which.
