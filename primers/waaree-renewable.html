00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Waaree Renewable Technologies is a solar contractor. When a factory, a utility or a power developer wants a solar plant built, Waaree Renewable is the firm that designs it, buys the panels and inverters, and constructs the plant — a business the industry calls EPC (engineering, procurement and construction). It also keeps some plants running under long-term operations-and-maintenance (O&M) contracts, and it owns and operates a modest pool of its own solar assets that sell power (an IPP, or independent power producer). Crucially, it is the build-and-operate arm of the Waaree group — the same group whose separately listed flagship, Waaree Energies, is India's largest maker of solar modules. Waaree Renewable does not make the panel; it builds the plant that the panel goes into.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is an order-book business, and the book is full. Waaree Renewable ended FY26 with an unexecuted solar-EPC order book of roughly 2.9 GWp — worth over ₹5,300 Cr, or about 12–15 months of forward revenue already signed. An EPC company's value is essentially this backlog multiplied by the margin it earns executing it and how fast it recycles its cash. Winning work has not been the constraint; building fast and getting paid is the game.
  • Revenue and profit just doubled. FY26 revenue rose about 108% to ~₹3,331 Cr and net profit rose about 109% to ~₹479 Cr, riding India's solar build-out and a flood of commercial-and-industrial (C&I) demand. This is not a slow compounder; it is a small-cap that is scaling violently, off a small base, in a hot market.
  • It sits inside India's biggest solar group. Waaree Energies owns roughly 74% of the company and supplies modules from the group's ~12 GW+ of panel capacity. That in-house supply is a genuine execution and cost edge for an EPC contractor — but it also means the parent, the supplier and the largest shareholder are all the same house, which is a relationship an investor has to weigh, not just admire.
In plain terms

Waaree Renewable is a construction and services firm for solar power — closer to an infrastructure contractor than to a factory. It earns a margin building plants for other people (EPC), a thin recurring fee keeping some of them running (O&M), and a bit of power-sale income from the few it owns (IPP). The reason it is interesting — and the reason it re-rated so hard — is the address: it is the plant-builder attached to India's largest panel-maker, so it can source modules in-house and lean on the Waaree brand to win work. The catch is baked into the same fact. An EPC business is only as good as its next order and its ability to build without choking on working capital; and a company 74%-owned by its own supplier is a story about a group as much as about a stock.

01

Solar EPC 101: three ways to make money from the sun

No engineering degree needed — just the difference between building, maintaining and owning.

A solar power plant is simple to picture: rows of panels convert sunlight into electricity, inverters turn it into grid-usable current, and cables carry it away to be sold or consumed. But there are three completely different ways a company can earn money around that plant, and Waaree Renewable does all three. Understanding how they differ — in risk, in capital, and in how the cash arrives — is most of understanding the company.

The three ways to earn from a solar plant
ModelWhat the company doesHow it gets paid (and the catch)
EPCEngineering, Procurement & Construction — you build the plant for a customer. Design the layout, buy the panels/inverters/structures, pour the foundations, wire it up and hand over a working plant.A one-time project margin on the build. Capex-light (you don't own the plant) but working-capital-heavy — you buy materials and build for months before the customer pays in full. This is Waaree Renewable's core.
O&MOperations & Maintenance — once a plant is running, you keep it healthy: clean the panels, replace parts, maximise output.A small, recurring annuity fee per plant, year after year. Low margin per unit, but steady and it grows with every plant added.
IPPIndependent Power Producer — you own a solar plant and sell the electricity it makes, usually under a long-term power-purchase agreement (PPA).Decades of power-sale revenue, but you must fund the whole plant up front. Capital-heavy — a different animal entirely from the asset-light EPC business.
Why an EPC business is fundamentally an order-book game

An EPC contractor does not own a factory churning out product, and it does not own the plants it builds. Its entire economic engine is: win an order → build it at a margin → get paid → recycle the cash into the next order. So three numbers, multiplied together, describe the whole business — the size of the order book (how much work is signed), the execution margin (what percentage you keep after buying materials and building), and the working-capital cycle (how many months your money is tied up in half-built plants before the customer pays). A fat order book at a thin margin is mediocre; a smaller book at a fat margin that turns over quickly can be excellent. Waaree Renewable's re-rating is a bet that it can keep all three favourable at once — win the orders, hold the margin, and not drown in receivables.

Who buys solar EPC

  • C&I (Commercial & Industrial) customers — factories, cement and textile mills, warehouses and data centres putting solar on their roofs or on nearby land to cut their power bills and green their operations. This is the fastest-growing buyer class and a Waaree Renewable specialism.
  • Utility-scale developers & IPPs — companies building large solar parks to sell power in bulk, who hire an EPC firm to construct at scale and on schedule.
  • Government & PSU tenders — state and central agencies procuring solar and, increasingly, rooftop and agri-feeder projects. Bigger tickets, but slower-paying and price-competitive.
02

What Waaree Renewable actually sells: one job, three revenue lines

The value chain — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Waaree Renewable's pitch to a customer is that it can take a solar project from a blank patch of land or an empty rooftop all the way to a plant feeding power — and then keep it running. That single relationship splits into distinct revenue streams with very different economics. The heavy lifting, and the vast majority of revenue, is EPC. O&M is the quiet tail. IPP is the small, capital-hungry sideline.

The three revenue lines, decoded

  • Solar EPC — the engine. Waaree Renewable builds ground-mounted utility plants, rooftop systems for factories, floating solar, and offers flexible commercial structures: a straightforward capex build (customer pays and owns), or a RESCO / opex model where the plant is financed and the customer simply buys the power. It has executed well over 1.8 GW of solar and, at recent count, had another ~1.7 GW under construction. This is where the doubling revenue comes from.
  • O&M service — the long tail. Once plants are built, Waaree Renewable is paid an annual fee to keep them producing at peak — cleaning, monitoring, part replacement. Small per plant, but it recurs and compounds as the installed base under management grows. It is the steadiest, least cyclical slice.
  • IPP / own-and-operate — the small, capital-heavy corner. Waaree Renewable develops, finances, owns and operates a modest portfolio of its own solar assets, earning power-sale revenue over the life of the PPA. It is a different economic model — you tie up capital for a decade-plus — and deliberately a minor part of the mix, not the growth story.
Why the split between "build" and "own" matters so much

The single most important thing to hold onto about Waaree Renewable is that it is overwhelmingly a builder, not an owner. That is a good thing for a fast-growing small-cap: building plants for others is capital-light, so the company can scale revenue quickly without pouring its own balance sheet into every megawatt it touches. A pure IPP has to raise huge capital to grow one plant at a time; an EPC firm can multiply its output as fast as it can win and execute orders. The price of that asset-light growth is fragility — an EPC firm has no owned power plants throwing off cash if the order flow dries up. Its revenue is only ever as durable as its next order and its ability to build the current one without a working-capital crunch. Growth is fast and the model is light; the flip side is that there is little annuity to fall back on if the building stops.

03

The company, the group, and who controls it

A former stock-market shell, reborn as the Waaree group's EPC vehicle.

Waaree Renewable's back-story is genuinely unusual, and it explains both the opportunity and the caution. This is not a company the Waaree group built from scratch — it is one it adopted. The listed shell that is now Waaree Renewable Technologies began life in 1999 as a small advisory outfit called Sangam Advisors, later Sangam Renewables. In 2019 Waaree Energies took control, and in July 2021 it was renamed and turned into the group's dedicated solar-EPC platform.

Ownership & structure

  • The Waaree map — keep it straight (this is the whole point): Waaree Energies (NSE: WAAREEENER) is the separately listed group flagship — India's largest solar-module manufacturer, ~12 GW+ of panel/cell capacity, a much larger company (FY26 revenue several times WRTL's). The history is the caution and the appeal at once: a former advisory shell, taken over and rebranded into a group's EPC vehicle, backed by a powerful parent and a hot end-market, can scale astonishingly fast — which is exactly what the last few years look like. But a company built by adoption rather than a long operating record earns less of the benefit of the doubt on governance, related-party dealing and the durability of margins. The Waaree name is doing real work here; an investor should be clear about how much of the story is the group and how much is a standalone franchise.
04

The in-group module edge — and its shadow

The single feature that most distinguishes Waaree Renewable from any other solar EPC firm.

Most solar-EPC contractors buy their panels on the open market, at whatever price and lead-time the module makers offer. Waaree Renewable's defining feature is that it does not have to: its 74% parent, Waaree Energies, is India's largest solar-module manufacturer. For an EPC firm, where modules are the single biggest cost line of any project, having the country's biggest panel factory inside the family is a structural advantage — and one that no independent EPC rival can copy.

Why does in-group module supply matter so much? Three reasons, all of which go straight to an EPC firm's two hardest problems — cost and schedule:

  • Cost of the biggest input. Panels are typically the largest slice of a solar project's cost. Sourcing them from within the group can mean better pricing, priority and coordination than a third-party EPC firm bidding for the same panels on the open market — a direct lever on the execution margin.
  • Supply certainty and schedule. An EPC firm lives or dies on hitting build timelines. Module shortages, import delays or price spikes can wreck a project's economics. A captive, domestic supplier de-risks the single most schedule-critical input — you are far less likely to have a plant sitting half-built waiting for panels.
  • Brand, bankability and trust. The Waaree name is one of the most recognised in Indian solar. For a C&I customer or a lender deciding who to trust with a multi-year build, being "the Waaree EPC company" is a real door-opener — a marketing and bankability edge a no-name contractor lacks.
The edge that only Waaree Renewable has: the plant-builder next to the panel-factory

Here is the framing that fits this company and no other in its space. A solar EPC firm's profit is order book × execution margin × working-capital turns — and the biggest swing factor in execution margin is what you pay for modules and whether they show up on time. Waaree Renewable is the only listed pure-play EPC contractor that can answer both of those from inside its own group, because its controlling shareholder runs the country's largest panel line. That is a moat an ordinary EPC bidder cannot buy. It is also the cleanest way, on the market, to own the Waaree group's building story separately from its manufacturing story — because the manufacturing is in the parent that owns 74% of this company.

A genuine surprise — the edge has a shadow: the same relationship that supplies the modules also concentrates the risk. Waaree Energies is simultaneously the majority owner, a key supplier, and increasingly a potential competitor — in early 2026 the parent group registered its own independent-power-producer vehicle, moving downstream into owning renewable assets. So the parent could, in principle, capture the most attractive projects for itself, set intra-group module pricing in ways that flatter or squeeze the EPC arm, or reshape the relationship. The in-house supply is a real advantage; but an investor is buying a business whose supplier, owner and possible rival are all the same house — and that is a governance question to weigh, not just a synergy to celebrate.

05

The order book and the EPC engine

The single most important mechanism in the whole investment case.

Everything about Waaree Renewable as an investment comes back to one machine: win an order, build it at a margin, get paid, recycle the cash. An EPC firm has no factory and no owned power plants to fall back on — its revenue is nothing more or less than the backlog it has signed and its ability to execute it. So the order book is not a footnote; it is the fuel gauge. Watch how it stacks up.

The ~2.9 GWp unexecuted book — worth over ₹5,300 Cr — is roughly 12–15 months of forward revenue already signed. For an EPC firm this backlog is the visibility; there is no annuity underneath it to smooth a gap in order flow.

A signed order is a promise; revenue only arrives as the plant is physically built and milestones are hit. That is why an EPC firm's cash is perpetually tied up in work-in-progress — modules and steel bought, plants half-constructed — for months before the customer pays in full. This is the quiet, unglamorous heart of the business: working capital. Buy materials too early, or let a customer pay too slowly, and even a profitable order book can starve the company of the cash it needs to start the next project. Manage it tightly, and the same capital builds two or three projects a year instead of one.

The three numbers that are the whole thesis: book × margin × turns

You can hold Waaree Renewable in your head with a single equation. Order book tells you how much work is coming (~2.9 GWp / ₹5,300 Cr — full). Execution margin tells you how much of each rupee of that work the company keeps (recently ~19% at the EBITDA line — high). And working-capital turns tell you how many times a year it can recycle its cash through that book (the hidden governor on growth). Multiply them and you have the business. The bull case is that a full book, a fat margin and disciplined working capital compound into rapid, cash-generative growth. The bear case is that any one of the three can crack — orders slow in a solar-capex lull, margins normalise from an unusually high level, or receivables balloon as the company chases scale — and because there is no owned-asset annuity beneath it, the earnings have nothing to lean on when they do.

The demand behind the book is real and broad. The genuinely cautious read is that an order book is a snapshot: it can be refilled quarter after quarter, or it can quietly stop growing, and the difference decides whether FY26's doubling was a step-change or a peak.

06

The margin question: why ~19% may not last

The most important thing management itself is telling you — and it's a warning.

There is one number in Waaree Renewable's FY26 results that deserves more attention than the headline doubling of revenue: the EBITDA margin of about 19%. That looks wonderful — but the striking thing is that management's own guided sustainable margin band is materially lower, around 14–16%. In other words, the company is currently earning well above the margin it tells investors to expect over time. That gap is the single most under-appreciated risk in the story.

FY26 EBITDA margin (~19%) sits several points above management's guided sustainable band (~14–16%). A high margin today that the company itself expects to fade is a very different thing from a durable one.

Why might solar-EPC margins be elevated right now, and why might they fade? EPC pricing is competitive and cyclical. In a hot market with tight module supply and a rush of C&I demand, a well-positioned contractor — especially one with in-group panel supply — can command strong pricing and pick its projects. As more capacity chases the same tenders, as module prices fall, and as the mix shifts toward larger, keener-priced utility work, project margins tend to compress. Management guiding to 14–16% is not pessimism; it is a realistic read of where EPC margins settle once the current tightness eases.

Why this is the number that quietly settles the case

Revenue can keep doubling and the stock can still disappoint, if margins mean-revert faster than volumes grow. Picture the arithmetic: if the EBITDA margin drifts from ~19% back toward ~15%, roughly a fifth of the profit on each rupee of revenue simply evaporates — so revenue has to grow materially just to stand still on profit. That is why the thesis-settling metric here is not the order book in isolation but execution margin held against the company's own 14–16% guidance, read alongside how fast the ~₹5,300 Cr book converts to revenue and whether working capital stays disciplined. If margins hold near 19% and the book keeps refilling, the business is exceptional. If margins slide to guidance while competition intensifies, a fast-growing small-cap can grow its revenue and shrink its profit at the same time — the outcome that catches solar-EPC investors out most often.

07

India's solar build-out: the wave underneath

The structural tailwind that turned a small contractor into a doubling one.

Waaree Renewable's numbers do not happen in a vacuum. They sit on top of one of the largest infrastructure build-outs anywhere in the world: India's push to install vast amounts of solar capacity this decade. For an EPC contractor, that national target is not an abstraction — it is the direct source of the tenders and orders that fill the book.

The tailwind is real — and it cuts both ways

The bullish case writes itself: a national build-out of this size gives a well-positioned, group-backed EPC contractor years of work to chase, which is exactly what turned Waaree Renewable from a small contractor into a doubling one. But the same wave that lifts the company also defines its ceiling and its cyclicality. Solar EPC is a capex-cycle business — it booms when developers and businesses are commissioning capacity and slows when a tender cycle pauses, financing tightens, or land and grid-connection bottlenecks stall projects. The demand is structural, but it is not smooth; order flow can be lumpy quarter to quarter, and an EPC firm with no owned-asset annuity feels those lulls immediately. Riding a wave is wonderful while it is cresting.

08

Widening the footprint: BESS, T&D, data centres, exports

A small-cap deliberately broadening beyond pure solar EPC.

A pure solar-EPC contractor is exposed to a single technology and a single capex cycle. Through FY26, Waaree Renewable made a series of moves to widen that base — pushing into adjacent infrastructure and services, and beginning to look beyond India. Some of these are genuinely strategic; all of them raise the question of whether the company is compounding its edge or stretching it.

  • Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS). As grids add more solar, they need storage to shift daytime power into the evening. Moving into BESS lets Waaree Renewable bid for the storage layer that increasingly rides alongside solar tenders — a natural extension of the EPC skill set into the fastest-growing new segment of the renewable build.
  • Transmission & Distribution (T&D). In June 2026 the company acquired a 55% stake in Associated Power Structures for about ₹1,225 Cr, giving it a foothold in T&D infrastructure — the wires and substations that carry power. It is a deliberate widening beyond generation into the grid side, but it is also a sizeable acquisition for a company of this size, and integration and returns on that ₹1,225 Cr are now something to watch.
  • Data centres. Management has flagged data centres as an emerging opportunity — power-hungry facilities that are natural customers for captive renewable power and the infrastructure around it.
  • Exports. The company has begun stepping outside India — including signing an early-contractor-involvement agreement for a solar-PV-plus-storage project in New Zealand — an early move to take the EPC franchise into the Australia/New Zealand market.
The diversification is a double-edged sword: broadening into BESS, T&D, data centres and exports genuinely reduces reliance on a single solar-capex cycle and lengthens the runway — a sensible response to being a one-technology contractor. But each new line is a new set of execution risks, a new competitive field, and (in the case of the ₹1,225 Cr Associated Power deal) real capital and integration risk for a company still young in its current form. The right lens is not "more markets, therefore better," but "is management widening from a position of strength, or chasing growth beyond where its edge actually travels?" The answer will show up in the returns on that acquisition and whether the new segments earn margins like the core.
09

The industry and the rivals

A competitive, fragmenting EPC market where the group backing is the differentiator.

Solar EPC is a more contestable business than solar-module manufacturing. Building a plant does not need a billion-rupee factory; it needs engineering capability, project-execution muscle, a balance sheet strong enough to carry working capital, and the trust to win tenders. That means more players can compete — from large listed EPC specialists to the in-house EPC teams of big developers. Waaree Renewable's edge in that crowd is less about proprietary technology than about group backing, brand and in-house modules.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out about this market. First, it is contestable and price-competitive — because building a plant has a lower barrier to entry than making a panel, EPC margins are inherently more exposed to competition than manufacturing margins, which is a big part of why management guides margins down toward 14–16%. Second, Waaree Renewable's real moat is relational, not technological: the in-house module supply, the Waaree brand and the group balance sheet are what let it out-compete a no-name contractor and win up-market C&I and utility work. That is a genuine edge — but it is an edge borrowed from the parent, which is why the group relationship sits at the centre of both the bull case and the bear case.

Financials & valuation

A violent scale-up on a small base — priced for the growth to continue.

FY26 was a step-change: revenue and profit both roughly doubled, margins ran ahead of guidance, and the order book stayed full. The market has rewarded that with a premium rating. The question, as always with a fast-growing small-cap, is whether the trajectory that justifies the price can actually continue — or whether FY26 was the peak of an unusually favourable window.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A group-backed contractor riding a real wave — asked to prove the margins and the growth can both last.

Waaree Renewable enters FY27 as one of the market's best-known small-cap solar names: a full order book, roughly-doubled earnings behind it, an in-group module edge no rival can copy, and a widening footprint into storage and grid infrastructure. The real debate is not whether the business works today — it plainly does — but whether the two things that made FY26 so good, rapid order growth and a ~19% margin, can hold together as the market matures and competition sharpens.

The bull case

  • Structural solar wave — India's build-out and booming C&I demand give a group-backed EPC firm years of work to chase; the order book is full and the end-market is growing.
  • In-group module edge — ~74% parent Waaree Energies is India's largest panel-maker, giving cost, supply-certainty and brand advantages no independent EPC rival can match.
  • Asset-light, high-return growth — building for others is capital-light, letting revenue scale fast; FY26 revenue and PAT both roughly doubled.
  • Clean balance sheet, no pledge — modest debt, working-capital-led, and promoter shares confirmed unencumbered.
  • Widening footprint — moves into BESS, T&D (Associated Power), data centres and exports lengthen the runway beyond a single solar-capex cycle.

The bear case

  • Margin normalisation — ~19% EBITDA margin sits above management's own 14–16% guide; if it mean-reverts as competition intensifies, profit can stall even as revenue grows.
  • Order-book dependence — no owned-asset annuity underneath; revenue is only as durable as the next order, and solar EPC is a lumpy, cyclical, capex-driven business.
  • Working-capital / execution risk — scaling EPC ties up cash in receivables and work-in-progress; slow-paying customers or a stretched cycle can choke growth.
  • Related-party concentration — supplier, majority owner and possible future competitor (parent's new IPP vehicle) are all the same group.
  • Premium valuation on decelerating growth — a rich multiple, with Q1 FY27 growth (~53%) already cooling from FY26's doubling; little cushion if any lever slips.
The bottom line

Waaree Renewable is a former shell turned into the build-and-operate arm of India's largest solar-module group — and for a few years that has been a spectacular place to stand, with revenue and profit doubling on the back of the country's solar build-out and a module edge no rival can copy. What is unproven is durability: whether an EPC firm with no owned-asset annuity can keep its order book refilling and defend a ~19% margin that management itself expects to fade toward 14–16%. The next few quarters answer it in two checkable prints — the EBITDA margin each quarter, measured against that 14–16% guide, and whether the ~₹5,300 Cr book keeps getting replenished rather than merely worked down. Hold the margin and refill the book, and the doubling was a step-change. Let either slip while the growth cools, and a premium-rated contractor re-rates on the arithmetic — because in EPC, revenue is the story you hear, but margin held is the story that pays.
