Sandur Manganese, the mine that makes metal

A complete walk through a 70-year-old Karnataka miner that owns its own iron-ore and manganese-ore leases and has spent a decade converting that dirt-cheap raw material upward — into ferroalloys, coke, power and now special steel. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

The Sandur Manganese & Iron Ores Limited (SMIORE) is, at its root, a mining company that refuses to stop at the mine gate. From leases it holds in the hills around Sandur, in Ballari district, Karnataka, it digs two ores — iron ore and manganese ore — and it is the country's largest private-sector manganese-ore miner and a sizeable iron-ore producer. But raw ore is only the start. Over the past decade Sandur has built downward-of-the-mine, adding ferroalloys (silico-manganese and ferro-manganese), a coke-oven plant, captive power from waste heat, and — the newest and biggest bet — a growing special-steel business. The company's whole edge is that it feeds these plants with ore it dug itself, at cost, rather than buying it on the open market.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The ore is the moat, not a commodity input. In ferroalloys and steel, the single biggest cost is the ore you feed the furnace. A rival ferroalloy maker buys that manganese ore at whatever the market charges; Sandur owns the mine and pays only its own extraction cost. So it captures the mining margin and the conversion margin on the same tonne — and on the day ore prices spike, the input cost that crushes everyone else is a windfall for Sandur's mining arm. The standalone mining-and-ferroalloy business earned a ~27% net margin in FY26; that is a mining number, not a steel number.
  • FY26 was a record year on almost every line. Consolidated total income rose ~61% to ₹5,163 Cr, EBITDA ~49% to ₹1,284 Cr, and net profit ~40% to ₹658 Cr. Iron-ore output hit a record ~4.35 Mt and manganese ore ~0.59 Mt. The company also turned net-debt-free, prepaying ₹423 Cr of non-convertible debentures out of its own cash.
  • The name is now half the story. "Manganese & Iron Ores" describes where Sandur came from. Where it is going is steel: in November 2024 it consolidated Arjas Steel, a maker of Special Bar Quality (SBQ) steel with plants in Andhra Pradesh and Punjab, and it has a proposed 1.0 MTPA integrated steel plant on the drawing board in Karnataka. The stock — a market cap near ₹10,350 Cr, roughly 16× earnings — is priced as a cyclical miner, not a growth story, even as the business quietly re-shapes into an integrated metals producer.
In plain terms

Start with a hill in Karnataka that happens to contain both iron ore and manganese ore, and a family company that has held the mining rights to it for seventy years. That ore, dug at cost, is worth a few thousand rupees a tonne raw. But run it through a furnace with coke and power — which Sandur also makes — and it comes out as ferroalloys or steel worth ten to twenty times as much. Sandur's entire strategy is to keep climbing that ladder: sell less ore raw, convert more of it in-house, and pocket the value added at every rung. The reward is unusually fat margins and a natural hedge against its own raw-material cost. The catch is that everything above the mine — ferroalloys, coke, steel — swings with brutal global commodity cycles, and the big steel ambitions will swallow serious capital before they pay.

01

Metals & mining 101: why the ore is everything

No metallurgy degree needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

To understand Sandur you need to understand one chain: how a lump of ore in the ground becomes a bar of steel, and where the money is made along the way. Ore is a rock rich in a useful metal. Iron ore is the feedstock for steel; manganese ore is the feedstock for the ferroalloys that steelmakers add to their molten steel to make it stronger and cleaner. Almost no steel is made without manganese — it is the fourth-most-used element in steelmaking — which is why a manganese-ore miner sits quietly upstream of the entire steel industry.

The one economic fact that decides everything: ore is the biggest cost

In a ferroalloy or a steel plant, the raw ore is not a minor line item — it is typically the largest single cost of the finished product, ahead of power, coke and labour. That is the pivot the whole company turns on. If you must buy your ore, your margin is a thin sliver left over after the ore seller has taken his cut. If you own the ore, that seller's margin is yours too. Sandur has chosen to own the ore and then convert it — which is why its profitability looks nothing like a plain steel mill's, and why an ore-price spike that terrifies its competitors is, for Sandur, partly a gift.

Who buys what Sandur makes

  • Steel mills — the buyers of both its manganese ore and its ferroalloys. Every integrated steelmaker needs manganese units; Sandur supplies the ore and the alloy.
  • Industrial and auto customers — the buyers of Arjas SBQ steel: forging shops, bearing makers, gear and axle manufacturers, who need clean, consistent, high-grade bar.
  • Iron-ore buyers & exporters — Sandur still sells a large share of its iron ore raw, chiefly to sponge-iron and steel units in the region, with pricing tied to national and global iron-ore benchmarks.
02

What Sandur actually owns: mine, furnace, oven, mill

The asset base — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Sandur is easiest to grasp as a stack of assets, each one sitting on top of the one below it. At the bottom are the mines that produce ore for almost nothing but the cost of digging. On top of them sit the plants that convert that ore into progressively more valuable products. The genius — and the risk — is that the same tonne of ore can travel all the way up the stack inside one company.

The pieces, decoded

  • The mines (Ballari district, Karnataka) — the foundation. Sandur holds captive leases yielding both iron ore (permissible capacity ~4.45 MTPA) and manganese ore (~0.60 MTPA). These are the lowest-cost, highest-margin part of the business and the source of everything above.
  • Ferroalloys, coke & power (Vyasanakere, near Hospet) — the first conversion step. Sandur smelts manganese ore into silico-manganese and ferro-manganese (expanded capacities of ~95,000 / ~1,25,000 TPA), runs a 0.5 MTPA coke-oven plant (two of four batteries currently firing), and generates its own electricity from a waste-heat recovery boiler (~32 MW). Coke and power in-house mean the alloy plant isn't at the mercy of outside fuel and tariff markets.
  • Special steel (Arjas Steel, Andhra Pradesh & Punjab) — the newest layer, consolidated from November 2024. Arjas makes Special Bar Quality steel — the clean, high-grade long product used in autos and engineering — with combined capacity around 0.585 MTPA, and an expansion underway to lift the Andhra unit from ~0.30 to ~0.40 MTPA.
Why "integrated" is worth spelling out here

Most metals companies own one link of this chain: a pure miner sells ore and stops; a pure ferroalloy maker buys ore and smelts; a pure steel mill buys everything and rolls. Sandur owns the mine, the coke oven, the power plant, the alloy furnace and (now) a steel mill. Each link it owns is a link where a middleman's margin becomes Sandur's instead. The flip side is that Sandur also carries every link's risk and capital cost — a soft steel market, a coke-battery outage, or a mining regulator's ruling all land on the same balance sheet, with no supplier or customer to share the pain. Integration concentrates both the reward and the exposure into one company.

03

The captive-ore moat: paid twice on the same tonne

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — and it is specific to Sandur.

Here is the device that makes Sandur unlike almost any other name in Indian metals. Picture two companies both selling a tonne of silico-manganese. The first is a standalone ferroalloy maker: it buys manganese ore on the open market, smelts it, and keeps whatever thin margin survives after paying the ore seller. The second is Sandur: it digs the same ore from its own lease at extraction cost, smelts it, and keeps both the margin the ore seller would have taken and the conversion margin. Same finished product, two very different profit pools — and the difference is simply who owns the mine.

Every tonne Sandur converts in-house rather than selling raw is value it keeps instead of handing to a downstream buyer.

This "paid twice" structure does two things at once. First, it produces mining-grade margins on a metals business: Sandur's standalone operations (the mines plus ferroalloys, before Arjas) earned roughly ₹543 Cr of net profit on ₹2,011 Cr of revenue in FY26 — a ~27% net margin, a level a standalone steel mill can only dream of, because so much of that profit is really the mining rent flowing through. Second, it creates a natural hedge. When global manganese-ore prices spike, standalone ferroalloy makers get squeezed as their biggest input cost jumps; Sandur's alloy plant feels the same pinch, but its mining arm is now selling ore into a hot market and coining money. The two halves lean against each other.

The framing that only fits Sandur: it sits on both sides of the ore price

For nearly every metals company on the exchange, a rising ore price is unambiguously bad news — it is a cost going up. Sandur is one of the very few for which a rising ore price is partly good news, because it is simultaneously a buyer (at its furnaces) and a producer (at its mines) of that same ore. It has, in effect, built its own internal supplier and its own internal customer. That is why the number that really matters for Sandur is not the ore price or the steel price on their own, but the spread — the gap between what it costs to dig a tonne of ore and what the converted product sells for. Owning the mine widens that spread and steadies it, in a way a buyer of ore can never replicate.

A genuine surprise: for a company whose name leads with "Manganese," iron ore is by far the bigger tonnage — roughly 4.35 Mt of iron ore versus 0.59 Mt of manganese ore in FY26, about seven to one. And neither raw ore is really where the future profit is meant to come from; that is special steel, made hundreds of kilometres away in Andhra Pradesh. The three words in the company's name describe its past far better than its plan.
04

The integration ladder: ore → ferroalloys → coke → steel

Why each rung was added, and why each one adds both value and stability.

Sandur did not become integrated overnight. It climbed the ladder deliberately, one rung at a time, over more than a decade — and the logic of each step is the same: take a product you were selling raw and capture the next slice of value yourself. Understanding the sequence explains both why the margins are so high and why the capex never quite stops.

The forward-integration ladder
RungWhat it doesWhy Sandur added it
Mining (base)Extracts iron ore and manganese ore from captive leases.The foundation — lowest cost, highest margin. Everything above is fed from here.
FerroalloysSmelts manganese ore into SiMn/FeMn.Turns a low-value raw ore into a higher-value alloy — capturing the smelter's margin on Sandur's own ore.
Coke ovenBakes coal into metallurgical coke.Coke is essential fuel for smelting. Making it in-house removes reliance on volatile coke imports and adds a saleable by-product.

Steel (newest)Arjas SBQ steel; a proposed integrated plant.The largest value jump — from selling ore/alloy to selling finished special steel to auto and engineering buyers.

Notice how the rungs reinforce one another. The coke oven exists because ferroalloy smelting needs coke; the power plant exists because it can harvest free electricity from the coke oven's waste heat; the ferroalloy plant exists because it monetises the manganese ore the mine already produces. Each new asset lowers the running cost of the assets around it. That is why an integrated operator's margins compound: the plants are not a collection of separate businesses but a single, tightly plumbed system where one unit's by-product is the next unit's input.

Where the ladder pays off — and where it strains

The payoff is visible in the numbers: coke and power in-house are the reason Sandur's ferroalloy conversion is among the lower-cost in the country, and captive ore is the reason the alloy carries a mining margin on top. The strain is capital. Each rung — a new coke battery, a bigger furnace, a steel plant — costs hundreds of crore and takes years to build and ramp. Sandur's coke plant, for instance, still runs only two of its four batteries; lighting the other two is future output and future spending. The ladder is a wonderful engine once built, but building the next rung always competes with paying it back.

05

The steel pivot: Arjas and the special-steel bet

The newest, boldest rung — and the reason the company is quietly changing shape.

For most of its life Sandur was a miner with a ferroalloy plant bolted on. In November 2024 it changed category. By consolidating Arjas Steel Private Limited — a maker of Special Bar Quality steel with plants in Andhra Pradesh and Punjab — Sandur added a whole steel business at a stroke, and turned a two-ore mining company into an integrated metals producer. Arjas is now openly described by the company as its "new growth engine," and in FY26 it did most of the heavy lifting on revenue.

The strategic logic is the value ladder taken to its top. SBQ is not cheap construction steel; it is the clean, closely-specified bar that goes into gears, axles, bearings and forgings — the guts of vehicles and machinery. It sells at a premium to ordinary long steel, and its buyers are demanding, sticky industrial customers rather than spot traders. In Q4 FY26 Arjas realised roughly ₹70,361 per tonne on steel sales — a figure that dwarfs raw-ore realisations and shows exactly why moving up into finished steel is worth the trouble.

The honest tension in the steel bet

Steel is where the growth is and where the margin isn't. Sandur's mining-and-alloy core throws off roughly a 27% net margin; bolt a steel business on top and the blended margin falls, because steelmaking is a scale-and-conversion game with thinner spreads than sitting on your own ore. The prize is that special steel gives Sandur a much larger addressable market and a use for its own iron ore, coke and power further up the chain — the same integration logic, one rung higher. The danger is that a mining company with fat margins is deliberately pouring capital into a lower-margin, more cyclical, more competitive business, and the market will judge it on whether that trade actually lifts total profit rather than just total revenue.

Beyond Arjas, Sandur has proposed a much larger 1.0 MTPA integrated steel plant in Karnataka — a full works with sinter plant, blast furnace, coke oven, rolling mill, oxygen plant and waste-heat power — which would, if built, close the loop by turning its own iron ore into finished steel on home ground. That is a multi-year, capital-heavy ambition rather than a near-term earnings driver, and it is exactly the kind of project a cautious investor watches closely: it could either compound the integration advantage or absorb years of cash flow before it earns its keep.

06

The company, the Ghorpades, and who controls it

A seventy-year-old family miner, now a listed integrated-metals house.

Sandur is a genuinely old company. It traces back to the princely legacy of the Ghorpade family, who have controlled the mining leases around Sandur for generations, and it remains a promoter-led business today — the family and associated entities hold roughly 74% of the shares, a high-conviction stake that keeps management and owners tightly aligned. This is not a professionally-managed float with a scattered register; it is a family enterprise that has run these same hills for seven decades and is now, in its own words, transitioning into an integrated metals and mining company.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Ghorpade family and associated entities hold roughly 74% (approx.) — a controlling, long-term stake. A promoter this concentrated means decisions are made with a family's own capital on the line, but it also means minority investors are along for whatever ride the family chooses, including a capital-heavy steel expansion.
  • Group map: the listed entity SMIORE (NSE: SANDUMA / BSE: 504918) holds the mines, ferroalloys, coke and power directly (the "standalone" business), and consolidates Arjas Steel as a subsidiary (the bulk of the difference between standalone and consolidated revenue). Why the family control matters here: the strategic pivot into steel — buying Arjas, contemplating a 1 MTPA plant — is a promoter's bet, not a hired manager's. A ~74% owner reshaping a cash-rich, high-margin miner into a bigger, lower-margin integrated producer is expressing real conviction; the same concentration means external shareholders have limited say if that bet ties up cash or the steel cycle turns. Track the promoter stake and any pledge levels directly in the shareholding filings.
07

The commodity cycle: ore, alloy spreads, steel prices

The tide every part of the business floats on — and how the pieces offset.

However clever the integration, Sandur is still a commodity company, and commodity prices move in cycles it cannot control. Three prices matter: the iron-ore price, the manganese-ore / ferroalloy price, and the steel price. Each has its own global drivers — Chinese steel demand, seaborne ore supply, alloy inventories, domestic steel spreads — and each swings hard. The saving grace of Sandur's structure is that these swings do not all hit the same way at the same time.

The key nuance is the offsets. A jump in manganese-ore prices lifts Sandur's mining revenue even as it pressures the ferroalloy plant's input cost — the two lean against each other. Cheap iron ore hurts the mining line but helps whenever Sandur is feeding that ore into its own steelmaking. A strong steel cycle lifts Arjas but also lifts demand for the manganese ore and ferroalloys that go into everyone's steel. Because Sandur owns pieces at several points of the chain, no single price move is purely good or purely bad — which softens the cycle without removing it.

The insight that separates Sandur from a pure miner

A pure iron-ore miner is a leveraged bet on one price: ore up, it wins; ore down, it bleeds. Sandur has deliberately spread itself across the chain so that it is never a one-way bet on any single commodity. That diversification is real and valuable — but it comes at a cost the bulls sometimes gloss over: it also means Sandur never gets the full upside of a raging bull market in any one product, because some other part of its own business is being squeezed at the same time. It has traded peak upside for steadier through-cycle earnings. Whether that is the right trade depends on what kind of investor you are — but it is a conscious choice, not an accident.

Because iron-ore output is already near its permitted ceiling, the phrase to listen for on results calls is "capacity enhancement" — Sandur has been seeking approval to raise its iron-ore mining limit. Whether and when that approval comes is a real swing factor for volumes, and it sits with regulators, not with management.
08

The mines, the leases, and the regulatory stack

The moat is a licence — and licences are granted, renewed, and constrained by the state.

Everything in the previous sections rests on one thing: Sandur's right to mine the ore. That right is not owned outright the way a factory is; it is a lease granted by the state, with production capped by environmental clearances, forest approvals and mining regulations. This is the part of the story a first-time reader most often overlooks, and it is precisely where a mining company's fortunes can be made or unmade — Karnataka's iron-ore sector spent years under court-ordered production caps in the last decade, a reminder that the tap can be turned down by forces outside the company.

The regulatory realities to keep in view

  • Lease tenure & renewal. Mining leases run for fixed terms and must be renewed. A well-run incumbent with a long, clean history — as Sandur is — is well placed, but renewal is never a pure formality; it is a periodic dependence on the state.
  • Capacity caps. How much ore Sandur may extract is set by its environmental clearance, not by its own appetite. With iron-ore output already near the ceiling, growth requires regulatory approval to expand — approvals that can take years and are not guaranteed.
  • Environmental & forest clearances. Mining in and around forest and eco-sensitive land in the Western Ghats belt carries heavy scrutiny. New pits, dumps, conveyors and plants each need their own clearances, and conditions can be tightened.
  • Royalties, levies & auction dynamics. Miners pay royalties and contribute to district mineral funds; policy on mineral royalties and the auctioning of future blocks shapes long-run economics for the whole sector.
Why the licence is the real asset — and the real risk

Sandur's captive ore is what makes its margins extraordinary, but that ore is only Sandur's for as long as the leases and clearances hold. The great strength is that Sandur has mined these hills responsibly for seventy years and is an established, low-controversy operator — exactly the profile a regulator prefers to keep in business. The great vulnerability is that the single best thing about the company is granted, capped and periodically reviewed by the state, and a mining company cannot conjure new ore if the tap is turned down. The downhill pipe conveyor Sandur is commissioning — a cleaner way to move ore than trucks — is the kind of investment that both improves economics and signals the good-operator standing that keeps clearances flowing.

09

The balance sheet — and the capex wall ahead

Net-debt-free today; a self-funded appetite for steel tomorrow.

One of the quieter but most important FY26 facts is that Sandur turned net-debt-free, prepaying and redeeming ₹423 Cr of non-convertible debentures ahead of schedule out of its own cash. For a company about to lean into a capital-heavy steel expansion, a clean balance sheet is not a luxury — it is the fuel that lets it build without begging lenders.

The reason a clean balance sheet matters so much here is the shape of the spending ahead. Sandur is expanding Arjas (the Andhra unit from ~0.30 to ~0.40 MTPA, plus renewable power), commissioning a downhill pipe conveyor for cleaner ore transport, has headroom to fire its remaining two coke batteries, and is contemplating a much larger 1.0 MTPA integrated steel plant. Each of these swallows capital before it returns any. A miner that has just gone net-debt-free and generates ₹1,200 Cr-plus of annual EBITDA can fund a meaningful chunk of that from its own cash flow — which is exactly why deleveraging first, then building, is the sensible sequence.

The permission slip — and the temptation

Being net-debt-free gives Sandur the freedom to build its next rungs on its own terms, without a lender dictating the pace or a rights issue diluting holders at a bad moment. That is the permission slip. The temptation is the mirror image: a cash-rich, family-controlled company with a big steel ambition can talk itself into a project that ties up years of cash flow at thin steel-cycle margins. The discipline to spend only where the integration genuinely adds value — and to let some ore keep flowing out as high-margin raw sales rather than force everything downstream — is what will separate a good capital allocator from an empire-builder. The next few years of capex decisions are the real test.

10

Financials & valuation

A record year, mining-grade margins, and a cyclical's modest multiple.

FY26 was, on almost every measure, the best year in Sandur's listed history — driven by record ore volumes, the first full contribution from Arjas, and firm realisations. Yet the stock trades at a multiple that treats it as an ordinary cyclical miner rather than an integrating growth company. Whether that gap closes depends on one thing: proving that the downstream build genuinely adds profit, not just revenue.

The one number that settles the thesis: the converted-tonne spread

At roughly 16× earnings, Sandur is priced as a commodity miner that had a good year, not as a company remaking itself into integrated steel — a modest multiple that already discounts the cyclicality. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is not the ore price or the steel price alone, but the realisation spread on a converted tonne: what it costs Sandur to dig and process ore versus what the finished ferroalloy or steel fetches, tracked alongside captive-ore volumes (are the mines still near their ceiling, and does the capacity cap lift?) and the downstream ramp (coke-battery utilisation, ferroalloy output, Arjas SBQ volumes and margin). If those spreads hold and the steel business scales into profit rather than just revenue, a 16× miner re-rates toward an integrated producer. If steel dilutes returns and the ore cap stays put, the multiple is telling the truth. Watch the spread; everything else is weather.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-margin miner betting its cash on becoming an integrated metals house.

Sandur enters FY27 in its strongest-ever financial position: record earnings, net-debt-free, iron-ore output near its permitted ceiling, a national lead in manganese ore, and a special-steel business scaling under it. The genuine debate is not whether the mining core is good — it plainly is — but whether spending that mining cash to build steel makes shareholders richer or simply bigger.

The bull case

  • Captive-ore margins — a ~27% standalone net margin is a structural, hard-to-replicate advantage that most metals companies cannot touch.
  • Natural hedge — owning both the mine and the furnace softens commodity swings; ore-price spikes that hurt rivals partly help Sandur.
  • Net-debt-free with real cash flow — can self-fund the coke, conveyor, Arjas and steel expansions without dilution or heavy leverage.
  • Integration runway — remaining coke batteries, Arjas ramp, and a proposed 1 MTPA plant give years of internal growth using its own ore.
  • Modest multiple — ~16× earnings for a record-profit, net-cash, integrating producer leaves room if the steel build works.

The bear case

  • Commodity cyclicality — ore, ferroalloy and steel prices all swing hard; a downturn compresses every rung at once despite the internal offsets.
  • Regulatory & lease risk — the moat is a state-granted licence; capacity caps, clearances and renewals sit outside management's control, as Karnataka's mining history shows.
  • Steel dilutes margins — the growth engine earns far less per tonne than mining; scaling it lowers the blended return unless executed superbly.
  • Capex execution — a 1 MTPA integrated plant and multiple expansions are capital-heavy, multi-year, and can absorb cash long before they earn.
  • Concentration & control — a single family owns ~74% and is steering the strategy; minorities have limited say if the steel bet ties up cash.
The bottom line

Sandur is a rare thing on the Indian exchange: a company that sits on both sides of the ore price, digging its raw material at cost and converting it upward into ferroalloys and steel, so that its metals business carries a miner's margin. FY26 proved the core works — record ore, record profit, net-debt-free. The open question is the next chapter it has chosen to write for itself: turning a fat-margin miner into a bigger, thinner-margin integrated steelmaker. The milestones that will answer it are concrete and datable — the Arjas Andhra unit crossing into its 0.40 MTPA ramp, the remaining two coke batteries firing, the downhill conveyor commissioning in H1 FY27, and — the big one — whether the proposed 1.0 MTPA Karnataka steel plant ever breaks ground and at what return. Watch those, and watch the converted-tonne spread beneath them; together they will show whether Sandur's climb up the value ladder keeps compounding the mining advantage or slowly spends it away.
