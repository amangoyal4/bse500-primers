00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

IndiaMART is a website where businesses find other businesses. A garment factory that needs zippers, a restaurant that needs a bulk supplier of cooking oil, a builder who needs steel — they type what they want into IndiaMART and get back a long list of manufacturers, wholesalers and traders who sell it, with phone numbers and price quotes. It is, in one line, India's largest online B2B (business-to-business) marketplace — a giant, searchable directory-plus-marketplace connecting buyers with suppliers across essentially every product category a business could want, from industrial pumps to packaging to plastic granules.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Only the sellers pay — and almost none of them do. Around 41 million buyers use IndiaMART for free. The money comes entirely from ~220,000 paying suppliers who buy a subscription (branded Silver / Gold / Platinum "listing" packages) to appear prominently and receive the buyer enquiries, called leads. That is roughly half a percent of the platform's participants funding one hundred percent of its revenue — a lopsided, one-sided monetisation that is the first thing to understand about the business.
  • The whole company is a subscriber count times a price. FY26 revenue was ~₹1,569 Cr, up ~13%. Strip it back and IndiaMART's revenue is simply paying suppliers × ARPU (average revenue per supplier). In FY26, ARPU rose ~8% to ~₹67k while the supplier count stayed stuck at ~220K — meaning almost all the growth came from charging existing sellers more, not from adding new ones. That single sentence is the tension in the stock.
  • It is a cash machine that just posted a shrinking profit. The model is beautifully asset-light: no factories, no inventory, ~34% operating margins, and a treasury pile of ~₹3,280 Cr in cash and investments (effectively debt-free). Yet FY26 consolidated net profit fell to ~₹474.7 Cr, and Q4 profit dropped ~72% — largely a mark-down on that very treasury, not a hole in the core business. The disconnect is worth understanding early.
In plain terms

Picture the classified ads at the back of an old trade magazine — "manufacturers of ball bearings, call this number" — and now imagine that whole thing moved online, made searchable, and scaled to millions of products. The readers (buyers) flip through for free; the advertisers (suppliers) pay to have a bigger, better-placed ad and to get the phone calls it generates. IndiaMART is that magazine for the entire Indian small-business economy. It earns nothing from the buyer who found a supplier and everything from the supplier who wants to be found. So the business is only ever as healthy as its count of paying advertisers — and the uncomfortable truth of the last three years is that this count has barely grown.

01

B2B marketplace 101: how a directory becomes a business

The handful of ideas that make the whole model click.

To follow IndiaMART you need to understand what a two-sided marketplace is and why a plain directory is worth real money. On one side sit buyers — any business that needs to purchase something. On the other sit suppliers — manufacturers, wholesalers and traders who want customers. IndiaMART's entire job is to stand in the middle and make the two find each other faster than they could anywhere else. It never takes possession of a single product, never touches inventory, never ships a box. It sells discovery and connection.

Why a free directory is worth paying to be in

A supplier does not pay IndiaMART for a listing; it pays for attention it cannot buy anywhere cheaper. A small pump manufacturer in Coimbatore has no way to reach thousands of factories across India that might need pumps — no sales force, no ad budget, no way to be found on Google above larger rivals. IndiaMART hands it a stream of pre-qualified enquiries from buyers who are actively searching right now for exactly what it makes. For a lead that might become a ₹5-lakh order, an annual fee of a few tens of thousands of rupees is trivial. That is the value exchange the whole company rests on: cheap, high-intent demand delivered to small suppliers who have no other affordable way to get it.

Who the players are

  • The buyers — overwhelmingly India's small and mid-sized businesses (SMEs): the shops, factories, workshops, traders and contractors who buy inputs and goods. They use IndiaMART the way a consumer uses a search engine, and they pay nothing.
  • The suppliers — also mostly SMEs: small manufacturers, distributors and traders. The vast majority list for free; a minority upgrade to a paid subscription because the leads justify it. Converting free suppliers to paid — and keeping them paid — is the entire commercial game.
  • IndiaMART — the matchmaker in the middle, running the search, the storefronts, the lead-routing, a light CRM to help suppliers manage enquiries, and cloud-telephony and payment tools that make it stickier. It monetises the supplier side only.
02

How IndiaMART actually makes money: one side pays, one side doesn't

The subscription-and-leads engine — read this once and the economics fall into place.

IndiaMART runs a subscription business dressed as a marketplace. A supplier picks a paid plan — the tiers run from an entry "Star/Silver" package up through Gold and Platinum — and pays for a defined period (often a year or more, billed up front). In return it gets prominence in search results, a larger catalogue, priority in the lead flow, and tools to chase those leads. The buyer pays nothing, ever. So every rupee of revenue flows from one side of the network to a company that never touches a transaction.

The revenue lines, decoded

  • Supplier subscriptions — the overwhelming bulk of revenue. A paying supplier commits to a plan (monthly, annual, or multi-year) and pays for placement plus a slice of the enquiry flow. Higher tiers cost more and deliver more leads. This is a classic recurring, renew-every-period software-like revenue stream.
  • Lead / enquiry monetisation — inside the subscription, IndiaMART meters value by how many quality buyer enquiries a supplier receives. It also sells "BuyLead" credits — pay-as-you-go access to specific buyer requirements — which lets it charge for demand more granularly and nudge free suppliers toward paying.
  • Value-added SME tools — a growing tail: a lightweight CRM to manage enquiries, cloud telephony, payment collection, and — via its subsidiary Busy — accounting software. These deepen the relationship and, crucially, make a paying supplier harder to dislodge.
The genuine surprise: the whole business monetises one side

It is easy to assume a "marketplace" earns a commission on what changes hands — the way a food-delivery app or a stockbroker does. IndiaMART earns nothing on the actual trade between buyer and seller. A buyer could find a supplier on IndiaMART and place a ₹1-crore order, and IndiaMART's cut of that order is zero. It only ever earns the supplier's subscription. That design has a startling consequence: the company's fortunes are almost completely detached from the value of commerce flowing across it, and tied instead to a single, countable number — how many small suppliers decide the leads are worth paying for this year. It is a demand-aggregation toll booth where only one lane has a toll, and even that lane is narrow.

03

The company, the founder, and who controls it

A founder-run, Noida-based internet original that survived the dot-com bust.

IndiaMART is unusually old for an internet company — it predates almost every consumer name in Indian tech. It was founded in 1996 by Dinesh Agarwal (still Managing Director & CEO), later joined by co-founder Brijesh Agrawal, as a web-directory business in the earliest days of the Indian internet. It is headquartered in Noida, near Delhi, and listed on the NSE and BSE only in 2019 — more than two decades after it began.

Ownership & structure

This is a genuine founder-led company — Dinesh Agarwal has run it for its entire ~30-year life. Track the exact promoter stake and any pledge in the quarterly shareholding filings.
  • Group map: IndiaMART InterMESH Ltd (INDIAMART) is the marketplace — the company in this primer. Its most important subsidiary is Busy Infotech (Busy accounting software), majority-owned. The flip side of a mature, founder-run cash cow is that the easy growth is behind it: the platform already has most of the SME suppliers worth having, which is precisely why the supplier count has plateaued and the debate has shifted to churn and pricing.
04

The engine: paying suppliers × ARPU − churn

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — and it is specific to this company.

IndiaMART's revenue is not complicated, and that simplicity is the point. It reduces almost perfectly to one identity: revenue = paying suppliers × ARPU. Two levers, nothing else. The number of sellers who pay, and the average price each pays. Every quarterly result, every management commentary, every analyst debate is really an argument about which of those two levers is moving, in which direction, and why. And lurking behind both is a third force — churn — that quietly drains the first lever every single quarter.

Nearly all of FY26's growth was price, not volume. ARPU rose ~8% to ~₹67k; the paying-supplier base ended flat at ~220K after losing ~1,200 in the March quarter. A subscriber business growing on price alone is a very different animal from one growing on new subscribers.

Hold that decomposition in mind, because it reframes the entire company. For years the bullish story was that IndiaMART would keep adding paying suppliers as India's tens of millions of SMEs came online — a volume story with a huge runway. What FY26 shows is a business that has quietly become a pricing story: the same ~220,000 sellers, charged more each year. Pricing power is real and valuable, but it has a ceiling that volume growth does not — push ARPU too hard and you accelerate the very churn that is already holding the base flat.

The device that fits only IndiaMART: two levers and a leak

Model IndiaMART as a bucket you are trying to fill. Each year you pour in new paying suppliers at the top; each year some leak out the bottom through churn; the water that stays gets charged a slightly higher ARPU. In a good year, the pour-in beats the leak and the price rises, so the bucket both fills and gets more valuable per litre. In FY26 the pour-in and the leak roughly cancelled — the bucket's level held at ~220K — and the only thing lifting revenue was the rising price on the water already in it. That is why you cannot judge this company on revenue growth alone: a healthy +13% can hide a base that isn't actually growing in bodies. The three numbers to read together, every quarter, are net supplier additions (is the bucket filling?), ARPU (is the price rising?), and the gap between gross adds and net adds (how fast is it leaking?).

05

The leaky bucket: why churn is the recurring villain

The core weakness of the model, stated plainly — small suppliers sign up, then leave.

IndiaMART's greatest vulnerability is baked into who its customers are. Its paying suppliers are, overwhelmingly, very small businesses — a two-person trading firm, a single-workshop manufacturer, a home-run wholesaler. Small businesses are fragile and impatient: they sign up for a subscription, and if the leads don't convert into orders fast enough, or cash gets tight, or the founder gets busy, they simply don't renew. The result is a structurally high churn rate at the bottom of the customer pyramid that the company has never fully solved.

The mechanics are unforgiving. To grow the paying base net, IndiaMART must add enough new subscribers to cover everyone who leaves and then some. When churn among small suppliers runs hot, gross additions can look healthy while net additions flatline — exactly the pattern of the last three years, and exactly what produced a net loss of ~1,200 paying suppliers in Q4 FY26. The company is running up a down escalator: sprinting to stand still on the metric that matters most.

Why the churn is a feature of the market, not a bug in the product

It would be comforting to think IndiaMART could fix churn with a better product or a smarter sales team, and it has tried — tiering plans, tightening onboarding, pushing annual and multi-year contracts to lock suppliers in. But the deeper cause is the mortality of Indian micro-businesses themselves. A meaningful share of the tiny firms that subscribe simply don't survive, or don't grow, or never had the volume to justify the spend in the first place. That churn floor is set by the health and turnover of the SME economy, which IndiaMART does not control. It can improve retention at the margin; it cannot make its smallest customers permanent. This is the single most important reason the paying base has plateaued — and why the company has leaned so hard on ARPU instead.

The uncomfortable feedback loop: raising ARPU and churning small suppliers are linked. Push prices up to grow revenue, and the marginal small supplier — the one for whom the maths barely worked — is the first to drop off. So the two levers partly fight each other: the harder IndiaMART pulls the price lever, the more it strains the volume lever. Sustainable growth needs the paying base to resume net additions, not just endure another round of price hikes.
06

The collections float: cash before revenue

A quiet feature that makes the model a cash machine — and a leading indicator hidden in plain sight.

Here is a mechanic that makes IndiaMART financially lovely and, for an analyst, unusually informative. Because subscriptions are billed and paid up front — often for a full year or more — the company collects cash before it has delivered the service. That upfront cash sits on the balance sheet as deferred revenue (money received but not yet earned), and it is released into the income statement month by month as the subscription is served. Two numbers fall out of this that lead reported revenue: collections (cash actually taken in) and deferred revenue (the stock of pre-paid subscriptions waiting to be recognised).

Why the float is both a gift and a warning light

The gift: IndiaMART's customers fund its working capital for it. It never has to borrow to bridge the gap between spending and earning, because the cash lands before the cost of serving it is incurred. That is why the balance sheet holds ~₹3,280 Cr of investments and no real debt — the float compounds into a treasury. The warning light: because collections and deferred revenue lead recognised revenue, they are the early tremor. If small suppliers start balking at renewals, you will see it in collections growth slowing quarters before it shows up in the reported top line. When collections growth (~10%) runs below revenue growth (~13%), as it did in FY26, it is a subtle flag that the pipeline is filling a little more slowly than the P&L is emptying it. The float is the most honest number the company reports.

This is the metric set a careful reader watches instead of the headline: not "did revenue grow?" (it almost always will, thanks to the float) but "did collections and deferred revenue grow faster or slower than revenue?" That comparison tells you whether the water going into the bucket is speeding up or slowing down, well before the reported number reacts.

07

The moat: the largest two-sided SME network in India

Why a plateauing platform can still be extremely hard to displace.

For all the worry about churn, IndiaMART sits on a genuine, durable competitive advantage — a classic network effect. The value of the platform to any one participant rises with the number on the other side. More buyers searching makes a listing more valuable, which attracts more suppliers, which makes the search results richer, which attracts more buyers. IndiaMART has spent 30 years accumulating both sides of that network, and it now has the largest pool of Indian SME buyers and suppliers in one place. That is not easy to rebuild.

The moat shows up as three reinforcing barriers. First, scale of demand: a new entrant can build a slick website in months, but it cannot conjure 41 million active buyers — and without the buyers, no supplier will pay to list. Second, data and category depth: three decades of listings, categories, and enquiry history make IndiaMART's search useful across an enormous breadth of niche industrial products a generalist could never match. Third, switching inertia: a supplier that has built up reviews, a storefront, and a steady lead flow on IndiaMART loses all of it by leaving — and the value-added CRM, telephony and payments tools raise the cost of walking away.

The subtle point: the moat protects share, not necessarily growth

It is important to separate two questions that get conflated. Can a rival take IndiaMART's position as India's dominant horizontal B2B marketplace? Very hard — the network effect makes that position sticky, and no direct challenger has come close. Can IndiaMART keep growing its paying base inside that dominant position? That is a completely different question, and the churn plateau suggests the answer is "only slowly." A moat can be wide and deep and still surround a business whose growth has matured. IndiaMART's moat is real; what it guarantees is durability and pricing power, not re-acceleration. Confusing the two is the most common mistake in reading this stock.

The horizontal-vs-vertical tension: IndiaMART's breadth — every category under one roof — is its moat, but breadth is also its exposure. A vertical B2B specialist (steel, chemicals, textiles) can go deeper in one category, with better pricing data and logistics, and peel off the highest-value suppliers where the money is. IndiaMART is the general store; the risk is a thousand specialist boutiques nibbling at its best aisles.
08

Busy and the SME software stack: the second act

The adjacency bet — from getting suppliers found to running their whole business.

If the core marketplace has matured, IndiaMART's answer is to sell its suppliers more — to move from being the place a small business gets discovered to being part of the software that runs it. The centrepiece is Busy (Busy Infotech), a widely used SME accounting and GST-billing software that IndiaMART bought a majority stake in around 2021. Around it sits a portfolio of minority stakes in SME-focused SaaS and fintech startups — invoicing, logistics, HR, payments.

The logic is a land-grab on the Indian small business's digital workflow. A supplier already pays IndiaMART for leads; if it also keeps its books, files its GST, sends invoices and collects payments through IndiaMART-owned tools, the relationship deepens from one annual subscription to an embedded operating system. That does three things: it lifts revenue per customer, it raises switching costs (churn's antidote), and it gives IndiaMART data on the supplier's actual business — a foundation for future fintech, like lending against verified sales.

Why the adjacency is the answer to churn — if it works

Every problem in the core business points to the same fix. Churn is high because small suppliers have a shallow relationship with IndiaMART — a listing they can drop. Give that supplier its accounting software, its billing, its payments, and dropping IndiaMART now means re-plumbing its whole back office. That is why Busy matters out of proportion to its current revenue: it is the mechanism by which a leaky, low-commitment marketplace subscription could become a sticky, multi-product SME platform. The execution risk is real — building or buying and integrating SaaS and fintech is a different muscle from running a directory — but the strategic direction squarely targets the model's central weakness.

09

The rivals — and the AI-discovery question

A dominant platform with a fragmented competitive set and one genuinely new threat.

IndiaMART has no single equal-sized rival — its dominance in horizontal B2B classifieds is real. But it is squeezed from several directions at once, and one of those directions, the way buyers discover suppliers, is changing in a way that could matter more than any named competitor.

The threat that isn't a company: how buyers discover suppliers

IndiaMART's product is discovery — helping a buyer find a supplier. Its risk is that discovery is being rewired by AI. If a factory owner can ask an AI assistant "find me three verified suppliers of stainless-steel fasteners near Pune with prices," and get a useful answer without ever visiting IndiaMART, then the paid listing that sits at the heart of the model loses its reason to exist. This is not hypothetical erosion at the edges; it is a change to the fundamental behaviour IndiaMART monetises. The counter-argument is that IndiaMART owns something the AI needs — the actual database of verified Indian suppliers, their catalogues and their enquiry history — and could itself become the trusted supply-side layer that AI discovery draws on. Which way that resolves is the most important unknown in the long-term thesis, and it is genuinely open.

If net adds turn convincingly positive for a few quarters, the plateau story dies and the multiple has room to expand on a dominant SME platform with a growing software attach. If the base keeps grinding sideways while ARPU does all the lifting, IndiaMART is a fine cash cow that the market will keep valuing like one. Supplier additions are the scoreboard; everything else is weather.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A dominant, cash-generative platform asked to prove its subscriber base can grow again.

IndiaMART enters FY27 as what it has become: the unchallenged leader of Indian horizontal B2B discovery, a cash machine with a treasury larger than most rivals' entire market value, and a founder-run franchise 30 years in the making. The open question is no longer whether the business is good — it plainly is — but whether its central engine, the count of small suppliers willing to pay, can climb again, or whether the company is now a mature utility dressed as a growth stock.

The bull case

  • Dominant network moat — the largest two-sided pool of Indian SME buyers and suppliers; no direct rival is close, and the position is very hard to rebuild.
  • Asset-light cash machine — ~34% margins, negative working capital via the collections float, ~₹3,280 Cr treasury, and dividends to prove it.
  • Pricing power intact — ARPU still rising ~8%; a long runway to charge more as suppliers extract more value from leads and tools.
  • Software attach via Busy — accounting and SaaS growing ~40%+, deepening the relationship and directly attacking churn.
  • Cheaper than it was — a de-rated multiple on a category-defining franchise; re-acceleration in net adds is not priced in.

The bear case

  • Supplier plateau & churn — the core metric has stalled near 220K and went negative in Q4; the model can't grow bodies faster than its tiny customers leave.
  • Growth is price, not volume — leaning on ARPU has a ceiling and feeds the very churn it's trying to outrun.
  • AI-discovery shift — if buyers find suppliers via AI, general search or WhatsApp, the value of a paid listing erodes at the root.
  • SME cyclicality — subscription spend is discretionary and tied to fragile small-business health; a slowdown hits renewals fast.
  • Adjacency execution & treasury noise — SaaS/fintech is a new muscle, and a huge investment book makes reported profit lumpy, as Q4 FY26 showed.
The bottom line

IndiaMART is a rare thing — a genuinely dominant, genuinely profitable Indian internet platform that funds its own growth and returns cash. Nothing about its moat, margins or balance sheet is in doubt. What is in doubt is arithmetic: a company whose revenue is paying suppliers times price cannot outrun the fact that, for three years, the first term has barely moved. The next act rests on two concrete, checkable things — whether Busy and the SME software stack lock suppliers in tightly enough to turn the churn tide, and whether AI-driven discovery becomes IndiaMART's replacement or its raw material. Watch one figure above all others each quarter: the net change in paying suppliers. The quarter that number breaks convincingly back above its old peak is the quarter IndiaMART stops being a cash cow the market prices for stagnation and starts, once more, being a growth story.
