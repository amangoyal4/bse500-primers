00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

City Union Bank (CUB) is a 121-year-old private-sector bank from Kumbakonam, a temple-and-trade town in Tamil Nadu. It lends mainly to small businesses (MSMEs), traders, farmers and households across South India, almost always against collateral, in small amounts. It belongs to a distinct family of Indian lenders — the "old private-sector banks" — long-lived, regionally rooted, professionally run, with no controlling owner. As of FY26 (year ended March 2026) it runs roughly 932 branches and carries about ₹1.45 lakh crore of total business (deposits plus advances).

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • Its defining trait is caution, not growth. CUB is India's oldest private-sector bank, incorporated in 1904, and it has never reported a full-year loss in its entire history. It got there by lending small, secured, and slow — refusing the big-ticket corporate loans that repeatedly wrecked flashier peers. For two decades it aimed to grow only two to three percentage points faster than the banking system, no more. That discipline is the whole personality of the bank.
  • The book is granular and collateral-heavy. Roughly two-thirds of its loans go to MSMEs, traders and agriculture, much of it backed by property or by household gold. Average loan sizes are small, so no single default can dent the bank. That design kept credit losses low through the dotcom bust, 2008, COVID and every rate cycle in between — and it is why CUB has long earned a return on assets around 1.5%, elite for a bank its size.
  • The live question is whether the tortoise can accelerate. FY26 was a break from habit: advances jumped ~26% — the fastest in years — deposits rose 23%, and bad loans fell to an 11-year low of 1.91%. But return on assets dipped to 1.36% as the bank spent on branches, technology and a new AI-assisted underwriting engine, and a brand-new chief executive took charge in May 2026. Can CUB grow faster and still lend as safely as it always has? That is the entire debate.
In plain terms

Picture the careful shopkeeper's bank of small-town Tamil Nadu — the one your grandfather's textile unit borrowed from, that took a pledge of gold or a mortgage on the godown before lending a rupee, and never once lent so much to one big customer that its failure could hurt. That caution is CUB's brand and its moat: 120 years, no losing year, steady mid-teens returns. The catch is the flip side — it grew slowly while glossier banks sprinted, and its heartland is almost entirely one state. Now, with a faster growth push and a new boss at the top, the bank is testing whether it can pick up the pace without giving up the very carefulness that made it worth owning.

01

What is an "old private-sector bank"?

The category CUB belongs to — and why it behaves the way it does.

Indian banks sort into a few distinct breeds, and knowing which one CUB is explains most of its instincts. There are the giant government-owned banks (SBI, PNB, Bank of Baroda); the polished new private banks licensed from the 1990s onward (HDFC Bank, ICICI, Axis, Kotak); the small finance banks that grew out of microfinance in the 2010s; and then the oldest survivors of all — the "old private-sector banks."

These are institutions that were already private and operating before the government nationalised most large banks in 1969, and simply were too small, too regional or too well-run to be swept in. A handful still trade today — CUB, Karur Vysya, Federal Bank, South Indian Bank, Tamilnad Mercantile, CSB, Dhanlaxmi, RBL. They share a recognisable character:

  • A hometown, not a headquarters. Each was founded a century or so ago to bank a specific community — CUB for the traders and cultivators around Kumbakonam; Federal Bank in Kerala; Tamilnad Mercantile for the Nadar merchant community. Their trust, deposits and branch density are still concentrated where they were born, which is both a moat and a ceiling.
  • Nobody in charge but the board. Unlike the new private banks (which sprang from a founding sponsor) or the state banks (owned by government), a classic old private bank has no promoter and no single controlling shareholder. Ownership is scattered across the public, mutual funds and foreign investors; a professional management team runs it. CUB fits this pattern exactly — it has no promoter holding at all.
  • Grown on trade, land and gold. They built their books financing local shops, small manufacturers, farm cycles and gold-pledge loans — sticky, relationship-driven, small-ticket lending rather than headline corporate deals. It is unglamorous banking, and deliberately so.
Why the category matters to an investor

An old private bank lives in the gap between the giants and the upstarts. It can never match an HDFC or SBI on scale, brand or the cost of raising deposits, so it cannot win the cheapest corporate business. But it is usually sharper, better capitalised and more profitable than a state-owned bank, and its local roots are a genuine advantage on its home turf. So the investment question for every one of them is identical: can a small, regional, owner-less bank keep bad loans low and returns high without a controlling promoter driving it? CUB has answered "yes" for longer than almost any of them — the interesting part is how, and whether the answer survives a faster gear and a new driver.

02

How a bank actually earns its keep

No finance degree needed — five ideas explain almost everything a bank does.

Strip a bank to its engine and it is a spread machine: it takes in money cheaply as deposits, lends it out dearer as loans, and keeps the gap — minus whatever loans go sour. Learn five terms and you can read any bank's results, CUB's included.

The banker's five-word vocabulary
TermWhat it means (and CUB's FY26 reading)
NII & NIMNet Interest Income = interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits. Measured against the assets that produced it, that is the Net Interest Margin (NIM) — the bank's core "gross margin." CUB's NIM was about 3.74% in FY26 (Q4 ~3.87%). Wider = more profit per rupee lent.
CASACurrent + Savings Account money — balances sitting in cheque and savings accounts that pay little or no interest. The larger the CASA share, the cheaper the bank's funding. CUB's CASA is only ~28% — modest, and one of its structural weak spots.
Fee incomeEarnings that do not come from interest: processing charges, transaction and card fees, forex, commissions on insurance and investments. It lifts profit without tying up capital, and a steady fee stream is a sign of a well-run bank.
Asset qualityHow much of the loan book is going bad. Gross NPA is the share of loans in default; Net NPA is what remains after money set aside; PCR (provision coverage) is how much of the bad loans is already funded; credit cost is the yearly profit hit from loan losses; slippage is fresh loans turning bad. CUB FY26: GNPA 1.91%, NNPA 0.68%, PCR ~84%.
Capital (CRAR)The cushion of shareholders' own money held against risky loans, as a percentage of risk-weighted assets. Regulators set a floor (~11.5%); more is safer but can dilute returns. CUB's CRAR is ~18.1% — very comfortably capitalised.
The one equation to carry around

A bank's profit boils down to: (interest earned − interest paid) + fees − running costs − loan losses − tax. Two levers dominate the result. The first is the spread (NIM), which widens with cheap CASA deposits on one side and higher-yielding loans — small business, gold — on the other. The second is loan losses: a bank can look brilliantly profitable for years, then surrender it all in one bad-loan season. Most banking disasters are a story of the second lever. CUB's entire reputation is built on rarely losing on that lever — which is what the rest of this primer unpacks.

Why RoA is the single number analysts watch

A bank sits on a thin sliver of its own capital beneath a mountain of deposits and loans, so its leverage is enormous. To cut through that, analysts collapse spread, fees, costs and loan losses into one figure: Return on Assets (RoA) — profit as a percentage of total assets. It is the cleanest single measure of how efficiently a bank turns its balance sheet into earnings. In Indian banking, an RoA above ~1.5% is considered strong; only the very best sustain it. CUB has done so for most of the past decade — which, for a bank this small and this cautious, is the quiet marvel at the centre of its story.

03

The company and who runs it

From a 1904 town bank in Kumbakonam to a widely-held lender under new leadership.

City Union Bank was incorporated on 31 October 1904 as The Kumbakonam Bank Limited, in the Cauvery-delta temple town of Kumbakonam, to serve the local trading and farming community. It is the oldest private-sector bank in India. Over the decades it absorbed several small neighbouring banks, gradually widened beyond its home district, and in 1987 took its present name, City Union Bank — the "Union" a nod to the small lenders it had merged in. It has paid dividends across generations and, remarkably, has never closed a financial year in loss.

Ownership & management

  • No promoter, widely held. Like its old-private-bank cousins, CUB has no promoter and no controlling shareholder. The register is spread across foreign investors, domestic mutual funds, insurers and a large retail public. The board and management — not a founding family — set the strategy, which is precisely why professional continuity at the top matters so much.
  • A 15-year era just ended. Dr. N. Kamakodi led CUB as MD & CEO from 2011 until April 2026, building the modern MSME-and-gold franchise while guarding the bank's caution. His departure is genuinely significant: for a bank whose whole edge is disciplined underwriting, a change of hand on the tiller is not a footnote.
  • The new driver is a growth banker. R. Vijay Anandh, MD & CEO from 1 May 2026 for a three-year term, brings ~28 years in banking, most recently running retail-asset and collections businesses at RBL Bank; he joined CUB in 2023 and moved onto the board in 2024. His retail-lending pedigree is a deliberate signal — the board wants faster, more diversified growth, layered onto the old caution.
  • Fortress balance sheet. With CRAR ~18.1% (CET-1 ~15.7%) and a ₹500 Cr QIP capital raise in train, CUB has ample room to fund years of loan growth without straining its cushion.
04

The conservatism — CUB's whole identity

Why the most interesting thing about this bank is what it refuses to do.

Most bank stories are about ambition. CUB's is about restraint. Its defining feature is a set of things it deliberately won't do: it won't make big-ticket corporate loans, won't chase a growth rate far above the system, won't lend unsecured at scale, and won't stretch the value it lends against gold even when the metal is soaring. That discipline is not a marketing line — it is measurable in the one fact that matters most: in 121 years, across every crisis India has thrown at it, CUB has never reported an annual loss.

Four rules the bank lives by

  • Small tickets, spread wide. CUB's book is built from many small, secured loans rather than a few large ones. When no single borrower is big enough to matter, one default cannot blow a hole in the balance sheet — the arithmetic of low loan losses starts here, before any clever risk model.
  • Secured, or not at all. The overwhelming majority of lending is backed by collateral — property, machinery, receivables or physical gold. Unsecured consumer credit, the fastest-growing and riskiest corner of Indian banking, is a segment CUB has stayed almost entirely out of.
  • No overexposure to any one sector. Management explicitly caps its appetite in any single industry, a habit hardened by watching the dotcom bust, the 2008 crisis and successive rate reversals punish banks that concentrated. The book is a mosaic of trades, not a bet on a theme.
  • Grow a little faster than the system, no more. For two decades CUB targeted loan growth just 2–3 percentage points above the banking industry's — fast enough to compound, slow enough to underwrite every rupee properly. Restraint, codified as a growth policy.
The counter-intuitive payoff

Here is what surprises people: CUB earns one of the better long-run returns on assets in Indian banking because it refuses to grow fast, not despite it. Chasing size forces a bank to loosen underwriting, buy risky loans and pay up for deposits — all of which eventually surface as bad debt. By staying small-ticket and secured, CUB kept its credit costs low year after year, and low credit costs are what preserve a high RoA. The bank's caution is not a drag on its returns; for most of its history it has been the source of them.

A bank that has never lost money in 121 years is not lucky. It is a bank that decided, again and again, to leave the exciting loans for someone else.
The clearest illustration: when gold prices spiked in FY26, CUB refused to raise the amount it would lend per gram of pledged gold beyond about ₹10,300 — well below the ruling market rate. Rivals lend closer to the peak price to win more business; CUB deliberately left money on the table to keep a fat safety cushion, so that even a sharp fall in gold would not push its loans underwater. That single per-gram number is the bank's whole philosophy made literal.

The loan book — MSME, traders and the gold engine

What CUB actually lends against — a granular, secured, South-Indian book.

CUB's advances reached about ₹66,699 crore in FY26. The shape of that book is the strategy in one picture: roughly two-thirds sits in MSME, wholesale/retail trade and agriculture, most of it secured, with large-corporate lending deliberately kept to a modest slice. Why gold lending is the quiet workhorse

A meaningful chunk of CUB's book is gold (jewel) lending — a South-Indian staple the bank has practised for over a century. It runs quietly under everything else, and it matters for three reasons:

  • It is about the safest loan there is. The borrower hands over physical gold jewellery worth more than the loan (a conservative loan-to-value), and if they default the bank simply sells the gold. Losses are minimal — which is a big part of why CUB's credit costs stay so low.
  • It is fast and well-priced. Gold loans can be disbursed within minutes against a pledge, carry attractive yields, and bring customers back repeatedly. Good for margins, good for stickiness in CUB's semi-urban and rural heartland.
  • It fits the culture perfectly. Lending against family gold is woven into how South India finances small business and farming. For a Kumbakonam bank, it is not a product line so much as a native language.

The catch hidden inside the strength

Small, secured, high-yield lending is exactly what makes CUB safe and profitable — but it carries two exposures. MSME and trade are cyclical: small businesses are the first to stumble when the economy or a monsoon turns, so this book can throw up bad loans faster in a downturn than a book full of salaried mortgages would. And a gold-backed book is tied to the gold price: if gold fell hard and fast, loan-to-value ratios would jump and collateral cushions would thin — which is precisely why CUB keeps its per-gram lending rate so far below the market. The model is a fair-weather superpower engineered, unusually carefully, to survive foul weather too.

06

Deposits, CASA and the margin

The funding side — solid on size, thin on cheap money.

A bank's profitability begins with how cheaply it can gather deposits. CUB's total deposits reached about ₹78,308 crore in FY26, up a brisk 23%. But its CASA ratio — the share of near-free current and savings balances — is only ~28%, and its cost of deposits sits around 5.70%. That low CASA is the obvious soft spot in an otherwise sturdy story, and it puts more of CUB's funding into pricier term deposits than a big brand-name bank would carry.

Rates are illustrative. The more of a bank's deposits are CASA (near-free), the lower its overall cost of funds and the wider its margin. CUB's ~28% CASA trails the best large private banks (40%+), so it leans more on costlier term money.

The NIM picture

  • NIM ~3.74% in FY26 (Q4 ~3.87%) — a healthy core margin, propped up by the higher-yielding MSME, trade and gold assets on the lending side. It comfortably beats most state-owned banks.
  • But margin is hard-won. With CASA stuck near 28% and deposit competition fierce, CUB is more exposed than deposit-rich rivals to rising funding costs. Every basis point of NIM here is earned on the asset side — through yield — rather than handed over by cheap deposits.
  • Fee income helps at the margin. Processing charges, transaction and card fees, third-party commissions and treasury gains supplement the spread and lift RoA without consuming capital — though CUB's fee engine is smaller than the big private banks'.
The CASA problem, plainly: gathering low-cost deposits is the single hardest thing for a regional bank, because it is exactly where the giants win — with scale, salary accounts and brand. Lifting CASA (and holding NIM as rate cycles turn) is one of the two or three swing factors that decide whether CUB's high RoA proves durable through a faster-growth phase.
07

Can the tortoise speed up?

The crux — accelerating growth without surrendering the discipline that is the whole point.

Caution made CUB safe, but it also made it slow. For years its loan growth lagged the faster private banks, and the market rewarded the safety with a premium multiple while quietly discounting the pace. The defining question of the current era is whether CUB can grow materially faster — through better technology, new products and partnerships — without letting its famously low credit costs creep up. FY26 is the first real data point, and it cuts both ways.

What is powering the acceleration

  • AI-assisted underwriting. Working with outside consultants, CUB rebuilt its credit process so that a loan approval that once took hours can now be turned around in a fraction of the time. Faster, data-driven underwriting lets the bank grow the MSME and retail book without hiring an army of credit officers — and, if it works as intended, without loosening standards.
  • A revamped loan-origination engine. Digitised origination and scoring pull the historically manual, relationship-heavy underwriting into a system that can scale — the plumbing a slow bank needs before it can safely run faster.
  • New products, slowly. Retail housing and vehicle finance are being introduced deliberately, targeted to reach only single-digit percentages of the book over five-to-seven years — growth diversification without abandoning the secured, small-ticket DNA.
  • Co-lending partnerships. Tie-ups with NBFCs extend CUB's reach into vehicle, gold and housing finance, letting it grow assets and share risk rather than carrying every rupee alone.
  • A growth-minded chief executive. The appointment of a retail-lending specialist as MD & CEO is itself a strategy statement: the board chose acceleration, and gave it a leader whose career was built on exactly the consumer-and-MSME engines CUB now wants to run harder.
The tension, made concrete

FY26 shows the trade-off in real numbers. Advances jumped ~26% and bad loans fell to an 11-year low — the acceleration and the discipline held together. But RoA slipped from ~1.55% in FY25 to 1.36%, because faster growth meant spending on branches, technology and people, plus fresh equity from a QIP that enlarges the capital base. Management is openly guiding RoA back toward 1.65–1.66% as that investment matures. So the picture is not "safe but slow" any more; it is "faster, but temporarily earning a little less while it retools." Whether the RoA recovers while growth stays high and credit costs stay low is the entire thesis.

Read this once, honestly: speeding up is where cautious banks historically get hurt. Rapid MSME growth flatters today's book — a loan made this year has not had time to go bad — so the true test of the new engine is not FY26's growth rate but the slippage on those loans two and three years out. Optimists see a well-capitalised bank finally monetising a century of underwriting skill with modern tools; sceptics note that every bank that ever blew up was, at some point, "growing fast with pristine early asset quality."

How to read the valuation

For a bank, the primary lens is price-to-book (P/B) — market value against accounting net worth — read alongside RoE, because a bank earning a higher return on equity deserves a higher multiple of its book. As of mid-July 2026 CUB traded around ₹229 a share, ~₹22,700 crore market cap, ~2.1× book value and ~16–17× earnings (a 1:3 bonus in June 2026 having adjusted the per-share figures).

  • A safety premium, historically. CUB has usually traded at a richer P/B than most old private banks — the market paying up for a bank that never loses money and earns ~1.5% RoA through the cycle. That premium is a verdict on durability, not on growth.
  • A growth discount, periodically. When the market fixates on CUB's slower loan growth relative to sprinting private peers, the multiple de-rates — the price you pay for a tortoise, however reliable. The stock has oscillated between these two framings for years.
  • The debate right now. At ~2.1× book on an RoA that just dipped to 1.36%, bulls are underwriting a re-rating: if CUB proves it can grow ~18–20% and pull RoA back to ~1.65% while credit costs stay low, both the earnings and the multiple can expand together. Bears counter that you are paying a safety premium for a bank mid-way through an unproven acceleration under a brand-new CEO, with returns temporarily going the wrong way.
The one metric that settles the thesis

Ignore the noise and watch a single combination: can CUB keep advances growing at ~18–20% while return on assets climbs back toward ~1.65% and gross NPA / credit cost stay low? If growth, returns and asset quality move up together, the tortoise really has learned to run, and ~2× book is fair-to-cheap. If growth comes only at the price of a stubbornly sub-1.5% RoA or rising slippages from the newly-grown book, then the safety premium is being paid for a bank that is no longer quite as safe. Everything about CUB's near-term case lives inside that one sentence.

Squeezed between bigger private banks above and small finance banks below.

Where CUB's edge really lies

  • Underwriting reputation and stickiness. A century of relationships with the same trading communities is hard for a new entrant to replicate — CUB knows its borrowers, and its borrowers keep coming back. That trust is the moat.
  • Through-cycle discipline. Where several rivals have needed dramatic turnarounds or capital repairs, CUB simply never broke. Consistency, not spectacle, is its differentiator.
  • The ceiling is geography and scale. With the vast majority of branches in Tamil Nadu, CUB cannot out-grow its home state easily, and it lacks the deposit franchise to win the cheapest business. Its advantage is depth in one region, not breadth across many.
The competitive squeeze: CUB's core products — secured MSME and gold loans — are precisely the ones small finance banks and gold-loan NBFCs are chasing hardest, often at higher yields and faster turnaround. CUB's answer is not to out-run them on price but to out-last them on credit quality; whether that holds as competition intensifies is a real, open question.
11

The risks — read before you fall in love

A safe bank is not a risk-free stock. Here is what can go wrong.

  • The acceleration doesn't stick — or costs too much. The entire bull case rests on CUB growing faster while staying safe. If the ~26% FY26 advance growth proves a one-off, or if it seeds higher slippages two-to-three years out, the re-rating thesis unwinds and CUB reverts to "reliable but slow."
  • A new hand on a discipline-dependent tiller. CUB's edge is its underwriting culture, and it just changed chief executives for the first time in 15 years. A growth-oriented, externally-hired MD is the right choice if he preserves the caution — and a real risk if faster growth quietly loosens standards.
  • Geographic concentration. The overwhelming majority of branches are in Tamil Nadu. A regional shock — a bad monsoon, a state-level industrial or political disruption — hits CUB harder than a nationally diversified bank.
  • Gold-price sensitivity. A large, secured-on-gold book is safe while gold holds or rises, but a sharp fall would thin collateral cushions and dampen fresh gold-loan demand. CUB's conservative per-gram cap mitigates this, but does not remove it.
  • The CASA and NIM squeeze. With CASA near 28% and cost of deposits around 5.7%, CUB is more exposed than deposit-rich rivals to funding-cost pressure. If it must grow deposits faster to fund faster lending, margins can compress.
  • Small size against encroaching rivals. Small finance banks and gold-loan NBFCs are attacking CUB's exact niche with higher yields and slicker technology, while the big private banks out-scale it. CUB's moat is credit quality, not cost or reach — a defensible position, but not an expanding one by default.
  • Cyclical asset quality. Today's 11-year-low NPAs are partly the weather. In a genuine MSME downturn, small-business and trade loans sour faster than salaried mortgages, and CUB's book tilts toward exactly that.
12

The future — bull, bear, and the number to watch

Where this goes, and how you'll know which way it's going.

CUB enters its next chapter with a rare combination: a 121-year record of never losing money, a fortress balance sheet, its fastest growth in years — and, for the first time in a generation, a new chief executive hired precisely to push the pace. The next few years will reveal whether India's most cautious bank can add speed without subtracting safety.

The bull case

  • A century of underwriting skill is finally being paired with modern, AI-assisted origination — letting CUB grow the MSME and retail book far faster than its manual past allowed.
  • FY26 already showed acceleration (advances +26%) alongside record-low bad loans — proof the two can coexist, at least for now.
  • A well-capitalised balance sheet (~18% CRAR) plus fresh QIP equity funds years of growth without strain.
  • A retail-lending CEO and new products (housing, vehicle, co-lending) diversify a book that was arguably too narrow.
  • If RoA climbs back to ~1.65% while growth holds, both earnings and the multiple can re-rate together.

The bear case

  • Fast growth in cautious banks is exactly where discipline erodes — the newest loans haven't been cycle-tested, and early asset quality always looks pristine.
  • RoA already slipped to 1.36%; if the "investment year" doesn't reverse, the safety premium looks stretched.
  • A brand-new CEO changes the very culture that is the bank's moat — execution risk sits right at the top.
  • Tamil-Nadu concentration, low CASA and gold-price exposure are structural, not cyclical, vulnerabilities.
  • SFBs and gold-loan NBFCs are squeezing CUB's niche from below while giants out-fund it from above.
The bottom line

City Union Bank is the tortoise of Indian banking — a lender that got rich by refusing to hurry, and that has now, deliberately, decided to move faster. Its next several years turn on a test it has never had to pass before: growing at ~18–20% a year and pulling return on assets back toward ~1.65% and keeping gross NPA and credit costs at today's lows — all three at once, under a chief executive it has only just met. The single milestone to circle is the first FY (FY27–FY28) in which advances, RoA and asset quality all improve together. Manage that, and the 122nd year without a loss will look like the start of a faster bank, not merely a safer one. Miss it, and CUB stays what it has always reliably been — the careful shopkeeper's bank of Kumbakonam, worth exactly what a tortoise is worth.
