Shakti Pumps, the farmer's pump, the state's cheque

A full walk through India's biggest solar-agricultural-pump maker — how a 43-year-old stainless-steel pump business from Pithampur became one of the largest suppliers to a government subsidy scheme, why a company that grew revenue to a record in FY26 still saw profit fall by more than a third, and what it means that Shakti's real customer is not a farmer but a state tender committee.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Shakti Pumps makes water pumps and the electric motors that drive them — submersible pumps that sit down a borewell, surface pumps, and increasingly the star product: complete solar-powered agricultural pumping systems, where a panel array, a controller and a specially wound motor let a farmer irrigate with sunlight instead of diesel or an unreliable grid. It is an old, unglamorous engineering company from Pithampur near Indore in Madhya Pradesh — and over the last three years it turned into one of the fastest-growing industrial stocks in India, because the government decided to pay farmers to buy exactly what Shakti sells.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A subsidy scheme, not a sales team, drove the boom. The central government's PM-KUSUM programme subsidises solar irrigation pumps for farmers — the farmer pays a fraction, the Centre and states pay the rest. Shakti became one of the largest approved suppliers, and revenue roughly doubled from ~₹1,371 Cr in FY24 to ~₹2,516 Cr in FY25, with profit tripling. In FY26 it installed 86,086 solar pumps. This is the whole re-rating in one sentence: a mid-cap pump-maker rode a policy wave.
  • Record revenue, falling profit — in the same year. FY26 revenue hit an all-time high of ~₹2,698 Cr, yet net profit fell ~37% to ~₹258 Cr and the EBITDA margin dropped from ~24% to 15.6%. The reason is the crux of this company: when your customer is a state tender committee bidding suppliers down to the lowest price, growth and margin can move in opposite directions. Shakti grew into a lower-margin book.
  • It is spending heavily to stop being a pure pump-and-policy play. A phased ~₹1,700 Cr capex is doubling pump/motor/drive capacity and — the strategic bet — building its own 2.2 GW solar cell & module plant, plus a separate push into EV motors and controllers and rooftop solar. The aim: make the electronics and cells it currently buys, widen exports (now 100+ countries), and lean less on one government scheme.
In plain terms

Shakti sells the machine that lifts water out of the ground. For most of its life it sold that machine to dealers, farmers and exporters at a price it set. Then PM-KUSUM arrived, and the buyer changed: now a state agency runs a tender, dozens of pump-makers bid, and the lowest responsible price wins a block of tens of thousands of pumps. That transformed the top line — suddenly there were enormous, government-funded orders — but it also handed pricing power to the tender room. The investment question is whether Shakti can keep that volume and claw margin back by making its own cells, motors and controllers, while building a second engine (exports, EVs, rooftop) that doesn't depend on a subsidy line item surviving the next Union Budget.

01

Solar pumping 101: why a farmer buys sunlight

No engineering needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

An irrigation pump is a motor and an impeller that lift groundwater up a borewell to the surface so a farmer can water a field. Traditionally that motor ran on diesel (expensive fuel, every day) or the grid (cheap but erratic — many farm feeders get only a few hours of supply, often at night). A solar pump swaps the fuel bill for a panel: a rooftop-sized array of solar panels feeds a controller, which drives a specially built motor, which runs the pump whenever the sun is up. The fuel is free and the supply is reliable in daylight. To follow Shakti, you need five terms.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Submersible pumpA pump-and-motor unit that sits down inside the borewell, underwater, pushing water up. Shakti's heritage product — it was India's first maker of stainless-steel submersibles, more durable than the old cast-iron ones.
HP / kWHorsepower (or kilowatts) — the size of a pump/motor. Farm solar pumps are typically 3 HP, 5 HP, 7.5 HP. Tenders are specified and priced by HP band, so the mix of sizes drives revenue per unit.
VFD / controller"Variable Frequency Drive" — the electronics box that converts the panel's DC power and controls motor speed. It is the brain of a solar pump; making it in-house (rather than buying it) is a margin lever for Shakti.
PM-KUSUMThe central government scheme (Pradhan Mantri Kisan Urja Suraksha evam Utthaan Mahabhiyan) that subsidises solar pumps for farmers. It is the demand engine behind Shakti's boom — read §04.
DCR cell / module"Domestic Content Requirement" — solar cells and panels made in India, mandatory for government solar schemes. Shakti is building its own DCR cell & module plant so it can supply the panel too, not just the pump (§07).
The economics that decide everything: the fuel bill that disappears

A diesel pump can cost a smallholder tens of thousands of rupees a year in fuel; a grid connection to a remote farm may simply not exist, or deliver power only at 2am. A solar pump's appeal is that after the up-front cost, the running cost is close to zero for 20-plus years. The problem was always that up-front cost — too high for a farmer to fund alone. PM-KUSUM solves precisely that by having the government pay most of it. So the entire solar-pump industry is, in effect, a business that only exists at scale because a subsidy bridges the gap between what a solar pump costs and what a farmer can pay. Understand that and you understand both the opportunity and the fragility.

Who ultimately buys the pump

  • The state, on the farmer's behalf — under PM-KUSUM a state nodal agency (e.g. a renewable-energy development corporation) floats a tender, selects suppliers, and pays them; the farmer contributes only their small share. This is now the dominant channel and the source of the margin story.
  • Farmers & dealers directly (cash sales) — the traditional retail market for submersibles and surface pumps, sold through a dealer network at Shakti's own pricing. Higher-margin, but a smaller slice today.
  • Exporters & overseas distributors — Shakti ships pumps and motors to 100+ countries; export revenue was ~₹411 Cr in FY26. A steadier, non-subsidy leg the company wants to grow.
02

What Shakti actually makes: from a pump to a power system

The product ladder — and why the company keeps climbing up it.

Shakti started as a maker of one thing — a stainless-steel submersible pump — and has spent four decades adding rungs to a ladder that runs from a simple pump all the way to the solar panel and the electronics that drive it. The pattern is consistent: make more of what goes into a solar pumping system yourself, so that when a big tender lands, more of the value stays in-house.

The product lines, decoded

  • Pumps & motors (the core) — submersible pumps (the borewell workhorse), surface and monoblock pumps, pressure boosters, wastewater and open-well pumps, plus the induction and submersible motors that power them. Shakti's original claim to fame was making these in stainless steel when the Indian market ran on cast iron — more corrosion-resistant, longer-lived, and the basis of its export credibility.
  • Solar pumping systems (the growth engine) — the packaged product for PM-KUSUM: a purpose-built solar motor, the pump, the VFD/controller, and the mounting structure, sized to a panel array. Shakti doesn't just ship a box; under most tenders it installs and commissions the system in the field and warrants it. This is where the volume and the tender economics live.
  • Drives, electronics & the new frontiers — VFDs/controllers (increasingly in-house), and two forward bets: solar cells & modules (so Shakti supplies the panel, not just the pump) and EV motors, controllers and chargers through its Shakti EV Mobility arm — a bid to redeploy its motor-winding expertise into electric two- and three-wheelers.
Why the ladder matters more than any single product

A solar pump is really an assembly of three costly things: a motor (Shakti's), a controller (partly Shakti's), and a solar panel (until now, someone else's). In a government tender, the winning bid is thin, so every rupee of a component that Shakti buys from a third party is a rupee of margin it hands away. By marching up the ladder — winding its own motors, building its own drives, and now planning to fabricate its own cells and modules — Shakti is trying to convert a low-margin assembly job into an integrated one where it owns most of the bill of materials. That, not the pump itself, is the real story of the next three years.

03

The family, the company, and who controls it

A Patidar-family engineering house from Madhya Pradesh, listed since 1996.

Shakti Pumps is a promoter-run, family-controlled business built by the Patidar family of Madhya Pradesh. It was founded in 1982 as a small-scale unit in the Pithampur industrial belt near Indore, and made its name as the first Indian manufacturer of modern stainless-steel pump-sets — a genuine product innovation in a cast-iron market. The founder's son, Dinesh Patidar (Chairman & Managing Director), expanded the range into solar and drove the export push; the next generation of the family is now active in the business.

Timeline

  • 1982
    The Patidar family sets up a pump-manufacturing unit in Pithampur, Madhya Pradesh — the first in India to make modern stainless-steel submersible pump-sets, moving the market off cast iron.
  • 1996
    Listed on the BSE. Through the 2000s the company builds a stainless-steel pump and motor franchise and a growing export book across 100+ countries.
  • 2010s
    Adds solar pumping systems and energy-efficient motors; wins early state solar-pump orders — laying the groundwork for the PM-KUSUM years, but still a modest mid-cap.
  • FY24
    The inflection begins: revenue jumps ~42% to ~₹1,371 Cr as PM-KUSUM order flow scales; the solar-agri-pump business becomes the growth centre of gravity.
  • FY25
    The breakout year — revenue nearly doubles to ~₹2,516 Cr, PAT triples to ~₹408 Cr, EBITDA margin peaks near 24%. The stock re-rates hard; the company raises fresh equity (incl. a ~US$34m raise) to fund expansion.
  • FY26
    Record revenue ~₹2,698 Cr but PAT falls ~37% to ~₹258 Cr as tender realisations and input costs squeeze margins; 86,086 solar pumps installed; ₹1,700 Cr capex into cells, modules, capacity and EV motors kicks off.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Patidar family holds the controlling stake and runs the company operationally (Dinesh Patidar as CMD, with family members in senior roles). Track the exact promoter percentage and any pledge in the latest shareholding filing; a bonus issue and equity raises in the boom years have moved the share count.
  • Group entities: the listed company is Shakti Pumps (India) Ltd (SHAKTIPUMP). New ventures sit in subsidiaries — notably Shakti EV Mobility (EV motors/controllers/chargers) and the entity building the solar DCR cell & module plant. These are the vehicles for the diversification capex.
  • Manufacturing base: concentrated in Madhya Pradesh (Pithampur), with pump capacity of roughly 5 lakh pumps a year and an integrated set-up (foundry, motor winding, assembly) that underpins the export-quality and cost story.
Why the family-and-heritage detail matters: Shakti is not a subsidy start-up that appeared with PM-KUSUM. It is a four-decade engineering business with real stainless-steel pump and motor competence and a genuine export franchise — which is exactly what let it scale into government tenders fast and credibly. The risk is the mirror image: a conservative, promoter-run pump-maker is now running a large, working-capital-heavy, policy-linked order book and a multi-hundred-crore foray into solar-cell fabrication — a very different kind of company to manage than the one the family built.
04

PM-KUSUM: the scheme that remade the company

The single biggest demand driver — and the source of both the boom and the risk.

You cannot understand Shakti's last three years without understanding PM-KUSUM. It is a central government scheme designed to put solar power into Indian agriculture — cutting diesel use, easing the grid's farm-power subsidy burden, and giving farmers reliable daytime irrigation. For a maker of solar pumps, it is the difference between a small niche and a multi-thousand-crore market. Here is how the money actually flows.

How a PM-KUSUM solar pump gets bought — and who pays
Step 1 · Demand
Farmer applies
A farmer registers for a subsidised standalone solar pump under the state's KUSUM allocation.
→
Step 2 · Tender
State agency bids it out
A nodal agency floats a tender by HP band; approved suppliers compete on price (L1 wins).
→
Step 3 · Supply
Shakti installs
The winning supplier makes, delivers, installs & commissions the pump in the field.
→
Step 4 · Payment
Subsidy pays the bill
Centre + state subsidy (~60%) + farmer share (~10%) + financing (~30%) settle the invoice.
Broadly, the farmer pays only ~10% up front; the Centre and state jointly fund ~60% as subsidy, with the balance often bank-financed. The supplier's cheque comes largely from government — which is why who pays, and when, matters as much as the pump itself.

The shape of that split holds across states: the farmer's ~10% is the smallest cheque, the Centre and state jointly fund the majority as subsidy, and bank finance often bridges the rest. For a supplier it means huge addressable demand — but a customer whose price is set by a tender and whose payment can lag by quarters.

PM-KUSUM has three parts, and Shakti's bread-and-butter is Component B — standalone off-grid solar pumps for individual farmers — with Component C (solarising existing grid-connected pumps and feeders) a growing adjacency. States run their own branded versions on top: Maharashtra's Magel Tyala Saur Krishi Pump, plus large programmes in Karnataka, Madhya Pradesh, Haryana and elsewhere. Shakti has won sizeable blocks under several — for example a ~₹524 Cr Maharashtra Magel Tyala order and a ~₹476 Cr Karnataka (KREDL) order — the kind of single tenders that used to exceed a whole year's revenue not long ago.

The demand picture, and why the tap can be turned

The bullish read is enormous and real: India has well over 10 million diesel and grid pumps that policymakers want to convert to solar, and PM-KUSUM has been repeatedly extended and expanded, with a "KUSUM 2.0" push widening targets. That is years of demand for a top-tier approved supplier. The sobering read is that this demand is a line item in government budgets and state programmes. Allocations can be underfunded, tenders can be delayed or repriced, and a change in subsidy design can slow the flow overnight — the way the shift to auctions once froze a different renewable industry. Shakti's order book is huge because a policy is generous; it is exposed because that generosity is discretionary.

05

Same pump, half the margin: the tender paradox

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is unique to this company.

Most companies you can size up by watching revenue: more sales, more profit. Shakti breaks that rule, and understanding why is the whole game. In FY26 it sold a record amount — revenue rose to ~₹2,698 Cr — and yet profit fell ~37%, from ~₹408 Cr to ~₹258 Cr. The pump coming off the line was essentially the same product it sold at a fat margin a year earlier. What changed was not the machine. It was who set the price.

FY26 revenue was the highest ever, yet profit fell back below the FY25 level. The scale kept growing; the profitability of that scale did not. That divergence is the entire debate about this stock.

The mechanism is specific and worth naming. Under PM-KUSUM, a state agency doesn't negotiate with Shakti; it runs a lowest-price (L1) tender among many approved pump-makers. When several states tender at once and every supplier wants the volume, bids compress — and some state schemes (notably Maharashtra's Magel Tyala in FY26) were awarded at lower realisations per pump than the earlier cohort. Layer on higher raw-material costs (steel, copper, panels) and heavier logistics of installing pumps across scattered villages, and the margin gives way even as unit volumes climb.

The device that only fits Shakti: the margin isn't lost in the factory, it's bid away in a tender room

Here is the sentence that could describe no other pump-maker cleanly. Shakti sold more pumps in FY26 than in any year of its 43-year history, from the same factories, using the same stainless-steel and motor-winding skills — and roughly ₹150 Cr of profit vanished versus the year before. That money did not disappear on the shop floor; it was competed away in state tender committees and eaten by input costs the company couldn't pass on, because a government-set L1 price has no clause for steel going up. When a business's selling price is fixed by an auction it doesn't control and its cost base is exposed to commodities, the swing factor isn't demand or even efficiency — it's the gap between the winning bid and the bill of materials on the day of delivery.

06

The hidden tax of government orders: receivables

The less-visible half of the KUSUM boom — cash tied up waiting to be paid.

There is a second, quieter cost to living off government tenders, and it doesn't show up in the margin line at all: you get paid slowly. Selling to a state agency means delivering and installing tens of thousands of pumps, then waiting — sometimes several quarters — for subsidy claims to be processed and released. Meanwhile Shakti has already paid for the steel, the panels and the labour. The result is a balance sheet where an enormous amount of cash is frozen as trade receivables.

Receivables of ~₹1,276 Cr at March 2026 are still large against ~₹2,698 Cr of revenue — roughly six months of sales locked up. But the sharp fall from ~₹1,697 Cr a quarter earlier, and a jump in cash to ~₹439 Cr, show FY26 was also a year of collecting old government dues.

The number to sit with: receivables of ~₹1,276 Cr against revenue of ~₹2,698 Cr means well over five months of sales are outstanding at any moment. That is the structural signature of a government-order business — and it explains why Shakti has raised equity and carries working-capital borrowings even while it is profitable. To fund the next tender, it must buy inputs now for cash it will collect later. Operating cash flow did improve markedly in FY26 (to ~₹124 Cr from ~₹20 Cr) as it chased collections, and cash rose to ~₹439 Cr — genuine progress — but short-term borrowings also rose, a reminder that the working-capital engine still needs feeding.

Why receivables are the real cyclicality here

A conventional pump-maker's risk is demand — will farmers buy this season? Shakti's most acute risk is collection: it can win a huge tender, deliver flawlessly, and still be squeezed if the state's subsidy disbursement lags. Cash tied up in receivables can't fund the cell-and-module plant or the EV push; it has to be borrowed or raised. So the metric that quietly governs how fast Shakti can grow isn't the order book — it's receivable days. A quarter where receivables fall while revenue rises (as in the March 2026 quarter) is, for this company, as important as an earnings beat. Watch it like the margin.

07

Backward integration: from pumps into solar cells

The ~₹1,700 Cr bet to own the panel, not just buy it.

If the tender room sets Shakti's selling price, the only way to defend margin is to own more of the cost. That is the logic behind the boldest part of the strategy: a phased ~₹1,700 Cr capital-expenditure programme that both doubles pump/motor/drive capacity and, more radically, builds Shakti's own solar cell and module manufacturing in Pithampur. Today, the solar panel in a KUSUM pump is bought from a third party; tomorrow, Shakti wants to make it — and pocket that margin.

The centrepiece is a 2.2 GW integrated DCR solar cell-and-module facility (targeted around FY28), with a smaller ~0.5 GW module line arriving first (around Sep 2026). "DCR" = Domestic Content Requirement — the India-made cells/panels that government solar schemes mandate.

The strategic prize is twofold. First, margin: management estimates in-house cells and modules could add roughly 3 percentage points to EBITDA margin once the plants run, by internalising a component it currently buys. Second — and subtler — eligibility: government solar schemes increasingly demand domestically made cells and modules (DCR / ALMM rules). By fabricating its own DCR cells, Shakti secures compliant supply for its KUSUM pumps rather than competing for scarce third-party panels, and it can sell surplus modules into the booming rooftop and utility solar market.

The catch worth naming

Backward integration is the right idea and a demanding one. Making solar cells is a different industry from making pumps — capital-intensive, technology-fast-moving, and already crowded with large, well-funded Indian players building gigawatts of capacity. Shakti is a first-time cell manufacturer stepping into a field where scale and yield decide who survives, funding it while its core business is working-capital-hungry and its margins have just been squeezed. The 3-point margin uplift is a promise contingent on commissioning these plants on time, running them at good yields, and doing so before a wave of new domestic cell capacity compresses panel prices. It is the highest-reward, highest-execution-risk line in the whole story.

The tell in the capex plan: a pump company doesn't build a 2.2 GW solar-cell plant unless it believes the solar-pump demand cycle has years to run. The capex is management voting with the balance sheet that PM-KUSUM (and rooftop) demand is structural, not a one-off — and simultaneously insuring itself against being a price-taker on the one component it doesn't yet make.
08

The second engine: exports, EV motors and rooftop

The push to earn money that doesn't depend on a subsidy line.

Management is candid that leaning ~77% on government tenders is a concentration it wants to reduce. So alongside the KUSUM machine, Shakti is nurturing three businesses whose common feature is that none of them needs a subsidy budget to exist: exports, EV motors, and rooftop/commercial solar. If these scale, the revenue mix — and the quality of earnings — changes.

The mix tells the story bluntly: roughly three-quarters of FY26 revenue came from government solar programmes, exports were ~₹411 Cr, and the traditional cash/retail pump business made up much of the balance. Shifting that weight toward exports, EV and rooftop is the stated multi-year goal — and the measure by which the diversification should be judged.

The three non-subsidy bets

  • Exports (the proven leg) — Shakti already ships pumps and motors to 100+ countries, with FY26 export revenue of ~₹411 Cr. This is its oldest diversified income and the one most ready to scale: stainless-steel quality gives it credibility in agricultural and industrial markets abroad, and export margins aren't set by an Indian state tender.
  • EV motors & controllers (the option) — through Shakti EV Mobility, the company is redeploying its core skill (winding efficient motors and building drives) into electric-vehicle traction motors, controllers and chargers. It has committed roughly ₹114 Cr of capex (much already deployed) and says motors and controllers are in vehicle-level trials with auto OEMs — early-stage, but a credible adjacency because the underlying engineering is the same.
  • Rooftop & commercial solar (the pull-through) — once Shakti makes its own modules, it can sell into rooftop and C&I solar directly, not just consume panels internally. This turns the cell/module plant from a cost-saver into a revenue line and rides India's separate rooftop-solar boom.
Why the second engine is the real long-term question

The bull and bear cases on Shakti often argue about the wrong thing — whether KUSUM tenders are good or bad this quarter. The more consequential question is whether the exports-EV-rooftop cluster can grow from a supporting act into a genuine second engine before the government-scheme cycle inevitably wobbles. If it does, Shakti becomes a diversified motors-and-solar company that happens to have a large government pump business. If it doesn't, it remains a well-run but policy-tethered contractor whose earnings swing with tender pricing and subsidy budgets. Management's ~₹5,000 Cr FY29 revenue ambition explicitly rests on all four legs firing — not KUSUM alone.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded pump market — but a narrower field for scaled solar-pump tenders.

India's pump industry is large and competitive, spanning listed majors and thousands of unorganised makers. But the game that matters for Shakti — winning big, approved solar-pump tenders at scale — is played by a smaller set of qualified suppliers, because it needs manufacturing depth, motor competence, installation reach and the balance sheet to carry government receivables.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two dynamics matter. First, the solar-pump tender field is concentrated but contestable — a handful of qualified suppliers (Shakti, Oswal, CRI, the big pump majors) chase the same state blocks, and because selection is largely on price, no one owns pricing power for long. That is precisely why FY26 margins fell. Second, the rivals are integrating in the same direction Shakti is: Oswal in particular is building its own solar and motor capabilities, so the backward-integration edge Shakti is spending ₹1,700 Cr to build is not a moat it will hold alone. The differentiator over time will be who executes the cell/module and cost integration best, and who can fund the receivables to keep bidding — an engineering-and-balance-sheet race more than a branding one.

10

Financials & valuation

A genuine growth story with a genuinely volatile margin — priced accordingly.

The financial arc is dramatic: revenue up ~2× in two years, a profit peak in FY25, then a margin give-back in FY26 even as the top line hit a record. The market has ridden all of it — the stock re-rated hard on the way up and remains valued as a growth compounder, not a sleepy pump-maker. The debate is whether margins normalise back up as integration kicks in, or whether tender pricing keeps them capped.

The one number that settles the thesis: EBITDA margin per government pump — the realisation gap

At ~32× earnings, Shakti is priced for margins to recover toward the mid-20s, not to stay stuck near 15%. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is EBITDA margin on the government solar-pump book — specifically the spread between the tender price it wins and the bill of materials it can build to. Read it alongside three supports: the government-vs-non-government revenue mix (is the higher-margin exports/retail share rising?), receivable days (is government cash coming in faster?), and the cell/module plant's on-time commissioning (does the promised ~3-point integration uplift actually land?). If tender realisations firm and integration adds margin while exports grow, FY25's profitability was a preview, not a peak. If L1 bidding keeps compressing prices faster than Shakti can cut costs, the record revenue keeps coming with thin profit — and a 32× multiple has to justify itself on volume alone.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A real engineering franchise riding — and hostage to — a government scheme.

Shakti enters FY27 as a genuinely bigger, more ambitious company than the mid-cap pump-maker of three years ago: a record revenue base, a multi-year solar-pump demand runway, and a credible plan to integrate backward into cells and forward into EV motors. The fair debate is not whether the business is real — it plainly is — but whether it can rebuild margin against tender pricing and turn its diversification bets into a second engine before subsidy cyclicality tests it again.

The bull case

  • Structural KUSUM runway — millions of diesel/grid pumps still to convert; PM-KUSUM extended and expanded ("2.0"); Shakti a top approved supplier with real scale.
  • Margin self-help — backward integration into DCR cells/modules and in-house VFDs targets a ~3-point EBITDA uplift, plus a richer mix as exports/retail grow.
  • Proven export franchise — 100+ countries and ~₹411 Cr of non-subsidy revenue, built on genuine stainless-steel pump quality.
  • Optionality in EV motors — the same coil-winding skill repurposed for EV traction motors/controllers, now in OEM trials — a call on a large adjacent market.
  • Improving cash discipline — receivables cut sharply and cash rebuilt in FY26; operating cash flow up several-fold.

The bear case

  • Policy dependence — ~77% of revenue rides on government subsidy budgets and tender cadence; an allocation squeeze or scheme redesign can stall the order flow.
  • Tender-driven margins — L1 price selection plus commodity costs can compress profit even as volume rises, exactly as FY26 showed.
  • Working-capital intensity — five-months-plus of sales locked in government receivables; growth must be funded by borrowing or fresh equity.
  • Execution risk in cells — first-time solar-cell manufacturing in a capital-heavy, crowded field; the margin uplift is contingent on timely, high-yield commissioning.
  • Competition integrating too — Oswal, CRI and the pump majors chase the same tenders and are integrating similarly; no durable pricing moat.
The bottom line

Shakti Pumps spent four decades becoming a quietly excellent stainless-steel pump and motor maker, then a government subsidy scheme turned it into one of the country's largest solar-pump suppliers almost overnight — with all the reward and all the fragility that implies. FY26 laid the tension bare in a single fact: its best-ever sales year was also a year its profit fell by more than a third, because a tender committee, not Shakti, held the pen on price. Everything the company is now spending on — its own solar cells, its own controllers, more exports, EV motors — is an attempt to take that pen back. The next stretch turns on one checkable figure each quarter: the EBITDA margin it earns on the government solar-pump book, and whether its own cells and a growing export mix start lifting it off the FY26 floor. Watch that margin against the tender prices it keeps winning; it will tell you whether Shakti is a policy trade that already had its run, or an engineering company still early in owning its own value chain.
