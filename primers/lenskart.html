Lenskart, the eyewear factory that owns its own shops

A full walk through Lenskart Solutions Ltd — the company that designs, manufactures and sells its own spectacles across ~3,300 stores plus the app. How owning the entire chain — from an automated mega-factory in Rajasthan to the shelf in a mall — creates a hard-to-copy moat in a huge, largely-unorganised eyewear market; why the international arm (led by the Japanese chain OWNDAYS) is now ~40% of sales; and why a business that only just turned genuinely profitable trades at ~185× earnings.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Lenskart Solutions Ltd is India's largest branded eyewear company — the business behind the ubiquitous Lenskart stores in every mall and high street, plus the app most Indians now open to buy a ₹1,500 pair of prescription glasses, ₹2,000 sunglasses or a box of contact lenses. What makes it unusual is that it is not just a retailer: Lenskart designs its own frames, manufactures a large share of its own glasses in its own automated factories, sells them under its own brands, and delivers them through its own stores and website — it owns the whole chain from the lathe to the shelf. It listed on the NSE and BSE on 10 November 2025 in one of India's biggest consumer-internet IPOs (issue price ₹402; NSE: LENSKART · BSE: 544600).

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is an omnichannel, vertically-integrated eyewear company — not a pure e-tailer. In FY26 (year to March 2026) it did ₹8,814 Cr of revenue from operations (+32%), sold ~35 million eyewear units (+25%), and ran 3,327 stores across India and abroad. Roughly ~60% of revenue is India, ~40% international — the overseas leg is anchored by the Japanese chain OWNDAYS, which Lenskart bought in 2022.
  • The moat is owning the stack in a fragmented, largely-unorganised market. India's eyewear market is worth roughly $9 bn and still ~77% unorganised — a sea of small opticians. Because Lenskart manufactures most of its own glasses (a giant automated plant in Bhiwadi, Rajasthan can make ~50 million pairs a year), it strips out layers of middleman cost, sells branded product cheaper, and earns ~64% product margins that a mom-and-pop optician selling third-party frames cannot match. That's the whole story.
  • It just turned genuinely profitable — and trades like it will keep compounding. FY26 adjusted PAT was ₹530 Cr (reported ₹501 Cr), up ~148%, and EBITDA margin reached ~19.9% — but as recently as FY25 the core eyewear business still lost money, flattered to a reported profit only by non-operating income (see §07). The stock trades on a ~185× P/E — an extraordinarily rich multiple that prices in years of margin expansion and store growth, not today's earnings.
In plain terms

Think of Lenskart as "the Zara of glasses" fused with its own lens factory. A traditional optician buys frames and lenses from distributors, marks them up, and sells you a pair — with several middlemen taking a cut along the way, which is why glasses in India were historically expensive and opaque. Lenskart collapsed that chain: it designs the frame, makes the frame and lens in its own robot-run factory, brands it (Lenskart, John Jacobs, Vincent Chase), and sells it directly to you through its own store or app — with a 3D try-on, a free eye test, and quick delivery. Owning every link means it can sell fashionable, quality glasses at prices small opticians can't beat, in a country where hundreds of millions of people need vision correction and most don't yet own a proper pair. The catch: it has spent a decade and a fortune building all that, is only now clearly profitable, and the market is already paying an eye-watering multiple for the growth still to come.

01

Eyewear retail 101: the numbers that run the business

No finance degree required — just the handful of terms that decide whether an eyewear company makes money.

Eyewear is a wonderful business if you can control the supply chain, because a pair of glasses is really two things bundled together — a fashion accessory (the frame) and a medical device (the prescription lens) — sold at a high mark-up to a customer who replaces it every couple of years. To judge a company like Lenskart, you look less at "revenue" first and more at the unit economics of a pair of glasses and how many pairs flow through how many stores. A few terms tell you almost the whole story.

The only vocabulary you need

Why "who makes the glasses" is the first thing to get right

Most retailers are resellers: they buy a product wholesale and mark it up. Their margin is capped by what the manufacturer charges them. Lenskart broke that ceiling by becoming the manufacturer. When a company controls design and production and the store, three good things happen at once: the cost of goods is lower (no distributor markup), the product is exclusive (you can't buy a Lenskart frame anywhere else), and pricing is fully in its own hands. That is why a ₹1,500 pair at Lenskart can be better value than a ₹1,500 pair at a small optician while also being more profitable to the seller. The whole investment case rests on this one structural fact — so it's worth sitting with before the rest.

Online, offline — one customer, many touchpoints

  • Offline (stores) — the large majority of India revenue. Glasses are a considered, try-before-you-buy purchase needing an eye test and a fitting, so physical stores matter far more here than in most e-commerce. Stores also host the eye test that starts the funnel.
  • Online (app + web) — where Lenskart began, and still a big discovery and repeat-purchase channel, powered by 3D virtual try-on. Online and offline are stitched together: try on the app, test in the store, reorder online — one omnichannel customer, not two.
02

The vertically-integrated, omnichannel model

The first and most important force — the reason Lenskart is a different animal to a normal optician or e-tailer.

If you understand only one thing about Lenskart, understand this: it owns the entire chain from design to your face. A conventional eyewear seller sits at the end of a long chain — a designer somewhere, a frame factory in China, an importer, a distributor, a wholesaler, then the shop. Every link adds cost and margin. Lenskart deliberately collapsed that chain into one company: it designs the frames, manufactures a large share of the frames and lenses in its own plants, sells them under its own brands, and reaches customers through its own stores and app. This is what "vertical integration" means — and in a fragmented eyewear market, it is the whole moat.

Why owning the whole stack is the moat

  • It strips out middleman cost. By making its own glasses, Lenskart avoids the distributor and wholesaler markups baked into a traditional optician's cost. It reckons in-house production saves roughly 35–40% versus sourcing the same product — savings it can pass to the customer as lower prices, keep as margin, or split between the two.
  • It creates exclusive, branded product. A Vincent Chase or John Jacobs frame exists only at Lenskart. That turns a commodity (a pair of glasses) into branded product with pricing power, repeat pull and a fashion cycle Lenskart controls — the opposite of a corner shop selling the same third-party frames as everyone else.
  • It makes the omnichannel funnel cheap to run. Because Lenskart owns the store, the app, the eye test and the factory, it can offer a free eye test to create demand, a 3D try-on to convert it, and fast delivery to fulfil it — all as one integrated system. A fragmented optician can't fund a national app, a robot factory and a virtual try-on; Lenskart's scale lets it spread those investments over ~35 million pairs a year.
  • It is genuinely hard to replicate. To copy Lenskart you would need to build a design house, a mega-factory, a national store network, an app with try-on tech, and a trusted brand — simultaneously, at scale, and survive years of losses doing it. That combination is the barrier to entry; no Indian rival has assembled all of it.
A small optician sells you someone else's glasses. Lenskart sells you its own — designed, made, branded and fitted under one roof. That is the difference between a shopkeeper and a supply chain.
The subtlety: vertical integration is powerful and capital-hungry

Owning the whole chain is a double-edged choice. On the upside it delivers the cost advantage, exclusivity and control described above — a real, durable moat. On the downside, you have to build and pay for every link yourself: the factory, the stores, the tech, the brand marketing. That is enormously capital-intensive and is exactly why Lenskart burned cash for years before reaching profitability. The pay-off arrives only at scale, when the fixed costs of the factory and the brand are spread over enough volume to earn a return. Lenskart has now reached the point where that leverage is finally showing up in the margins (§07) — which is precisely what the valuation is betting continues.

03

The company and who controls it

From a 2010 online frame store to a listed, founder-led eyewear giant.

Lenskart is a first-generation, founder-led start-up success. It was founded in 2010 by Peyush Bansal — later a household name as a judge on Shark Tank India — together with co-founders Amit Chaudhary and Sumeet Kapahi. It began as an online store for spectacles, then made two decisive bets that define it today: it went omnichannel (opening physical stores) and vertically integrated (building its own manufacturing). Backed by a who's-who of global investors, it became one of India's most valuable consumer start-ups and listed in November 2025.

Ownership & management

  • Founder-promoters — Peyush Bansal (CEO, ~10.3%) leads a promoter group holding roughly ~20% post-listing (including Neha Bansal, Amit Chaudhary and Sumeet Kapahi). Notably, Bansal bought back ~2.5% from early investors at a steep discount just before the IPO — a governance point critics flagged, but one that raised founder skin-in-the-game to ~10%.
  • Marquee global backers — SoftBank (the largest institutional holder, ~15% pre-IPO via its Vision Fund), Premji Invest (~5%), Temasek (~4.9%), plus TPG, Kedaara, Chiratae, Alpha Wave and others. Deep, patient capital that funded the long build-out — but also a future supply overhang as lock-ups roll off.
  • Balance sheet — well-funded post-IPO, with the ₹2,150 Cr fresh issue earmarked for new stores, a new manufacturing plant, technology and marketing; borrowings are modest (~₹220 Cr) and the model generates cash (operating cash flow ~₹887 Cr, ~91% of pre-lease EBITDA in FY26).
04

The factory — where the moat is actually manufactured

The second defining force — the automated plant that turns "we own the chain" from a slogan into a cost advantage.

The single most physical expression of Lenskart's strategy is its manufacturing. Most eyewear "brands" are really marketing companies that outsource production; Lenskart chose the opposite. Its flagship is an automated eyewear plant in Bhiwadi, Rajasthan — which the company describes as among the world's largest and most automated for prescription eyewear — capable of producing on the order of 50 million pairs of glasses a year. This is the engine room of the whole business, and understanding it is understanding why Lenskart's margins can go where a normal retailer's can't.

What the automation actually does

  • Robotic lens edging at scale. The plant uses robotic systems that grind and fit prescription lenses to micron-level precision — processing many eyeglasses per minute, day and night, with consistency a manual lab can't match. That precision reduces remakes and returns, which is itself a cost saving.
  • Frames and lenses under one roof. The facility houses both a lens laboratory and frame manufacturing, including complex progressive and bifocal lenses — the higher-value products where margins are richest and quality matters most.
  • Volume that only a scaled player can fill. A 50-million-pair factory is a huge fixed cost. It only pays off if you can sell that many glasses — which Lenskart, with 3,300 stores and an app, can. This is the chicken-and-egg that keeps rivals out: you need the retail scale to justify the factory, and the factory to make the retail cheap. Lenskart built both.
  • A launchpad for more capacity. Part of the IPO proceeds funds a new plant, and Lenskart plans to shift more of its South-East Asia production into India — deepening the cost edge as international volume grows.
Why the factory is the difference between a fad and a moat

Plenty of eyewear brands have come and gone selling cheap frames online. What makes Lenskart durable is that its advantage is manufactured, not marketed. Marketing advantages are copyable — a rival can outspend you. A world-scale, automated, vertically-integrated factory that took years and huge capital to build, feeding a national store network that took another decade — that combination cannot be replicated by a start-up or a small optician chain in any reasonable time. Every pair that comes off the Bhiwadi line at a lower cost than a competitor can buy one is the moat working. And because the factory's cost is largely fixed, every extra million pairs sold makes each one cheaper — the classic manufacturing operating leverage that improves as Lenskart grows.

The nuance on margins: in FY26 India product margins held around 64% despite a weaker rupee raising the cost of imported inputs — the vertical-integration gains offset the currency headwind. That's the moat doing its job: it gives Lenskart a cost buffer that a pure importer-retailer simply doesn't have.
05

The under-penetrated eyewear market & premiumisation

The third force — the structural runway that justifies owning the stock for the long run.

Under everything sits a powerful, multi-decade tailwind: hundreds of millions of Indians need vision correction and most don't yet own proper glasses — and the market they'd buy in is still overwhelmingly run by small, unorganised opticians that Lenskart is steadily displacing. This is the single best reason to own the business for the long haul: the addressable market is not just large, it is expanding and formalising at the same time.

Three structural forces stack on top of each other, and each one is a growth lever for Lenskart:

  • Rising need. Refractive error (the technical term for needing glasses) has climbed from ~43% of Indians in FY20 to ~53% in FY25, heading toward ~62% by FY30 — driven by screens, education and ageing. More eyes need correcting every year.
  • Low penetration, huge under-served base. A very large share of people who need vision correction don't own an adequate pair — either they've never had an eye test, or they make do with an old, wrong prescription. Every free eye test Lenskart runs (23.8 million in FY26) converts some of that latent need into a first purchase. It is, quite literally, creating the market as it grows.
  • Formalisation + premiumisation. Only ~23% of India's eyewear market is organised today; the organised sector is projected to grow ~1.6× faster than the unorganised one, reaching ~31% by FY30. As shoppers shift from a nameless corner optician to a branded chain, and trade up from a bare pair to fashion frames, better lenses (blue-cut, progressives) and multiple pairs, average spend per customer rises — Lenskart captures both the share shift and the trade-up.
Why glasses are a lovely category to own — the replacement cycle

Eyewear has a quietly attractive economic feature: it is a recurring, replacement purchase, not a one-off. Prescriptions change, frames break or date, styles move, and many people now own several pairs (a work pair, sunglasses, a spare). Indian consumers replace eyewear roughly every couple of years, and as the market premiumises they buy more often and spend more each time. That gives Lenskart a base of tens of millions of customers who come back — cheaper to serve than new ones — layered on top of a stream of first-time buyers created by the eye-test funnel. A growing need, a formalising market, a trade-up in spend, and a built-in repeat cycle: that is about as good a demand backdrop as a consumer business can ask for. The debate is never whether the market grows — it's how much of it Lenskart keeps, and at what margin.

A figure to hold loosely: Lenskart pegs India's eyewear market near $9 bn, doubling toward ~$17 bn by FY30; Titan (via Titan Eye+) counts only the organised slice and cites a far smaller ~$3–3.5 bn. Both can be "right" — they're measuring different things. The direction (large, under-penetrated, formalising) is not in dispute; the exact size is a definitional argument, so treat headline TAM numbers as illustrative, not precise.
06

The international leg and OWNDAYS

The fourth force — how Lenskart quietly became a pan-Asian eyewear company, not just an Indian one.

A fact that surprises many first-time observers: roughly 40% of Lenskart's revenue now comes from outside India. The company is not simply an Indian retailer with a few overseas outlets — it is a genuine pan-Asian eyewear group, and the anchor of that story is OWNDAYS, the 30-year-old Japanese eyewear chain Lenskart acquired a majority of in 2022 for about $400 million (it has since raised its stake toward full ownership).

Why the OWNDAYS deal was so strategic

  • An instant, profitable international platform. Rather than build store-by-store across Asia, Lenskart bought a mature, established chain with hundreds of stores and a trusted brand in Japan and South-East Asia. OWNDAYS came with real revenue and, importantly, positive economics — a rare thing to bolt on.
  • Geographic diversification. OWNDAYS extends Lenskart across ~13 markets — Japan, Singapore, Thailand, Taiwan, the Philippines, Indonesia, Malaysia and more — reducing dependence on any single economy and giving the group exposure to more developed, higher-ASP eyewear markets.
  • Two-way capability transfer. Lenskart brings OWNDAYS its manufacturing scale and technology (and plans to shift more SE-Asia production into its Indian plants, lowering OWNDAYS' cost); OWNDAYS brings Lenskart operating know-how in mature retail markets and a premium brand. The Middle East and other new geographies extend the runway further.
  • A margin turnaround in flight. The international business was low-margin when acquired; it is now improving sharply — full-year international EBITDA margin rose to ~7% in FY26 (from ~3.6% in FY25), and hit ~9.2% in Q4. That is the integration and cost-transfer thesis starting to pay off.
In plain terms — why buy a Japanese chain at all?

It looks odd for an Indian company to own a big slice of Japanese eyewear — until you see the logic. Building a retail brand from scratch in a foreign country is slow, expensive and often fails. By buying OWNDAYS, Lenskart got an established, profitable, well-run chain overnight — and then set about making it more profitable by feeding it Lenskart's manufacturing scale and tech. The prize is twofold: a diversified revenue base that isn't hostage to the Indian market alone, and a second engine of growth in markets where people already spend more per pair of glasses. The risk is the mirror image: integrating a foreign business across currencies, cultures and regulations is hard, and the international margin — while improving — is still well below India's. Watch that international EBITDA margin each quarter: if it keeps climbing toward India's level, the OWNDAYS bet is compounding; if it stalls, the ~40% of revenue sitting overseas becomes a drag rather than a driver.

07

The path to profitability & unit economics

The fifth and central force — how a store-expanding eyewear maker scales toward real, sustainable profit, and the flags to watch.

For most of its life Lenskart lost money — deliberately, pouring cash into the factory, the stores, the brand and the tech that now constitute its moat. FY26 is the first year the picture looks unambiguously profitable, and the trajectory is steeply the right way. But there is an important accounting caveat: as recently as FY25, the core eyewear business still lost money on an operating basis, and only reported a profit because of non-operating income. Getting this right is the difference between understanding Lenskart's true turn and being misled by a headline.

The one flag to remember: Lenskart's FY25 reported profit (~₹297 Cr) was flattered by roughly ₹356 Cr of non-operating (other) income — the underlying eyewear operating business actually lost ~₹59 Cr that year. FY26 is the first year the operating business itself is clearly profitable. So when you see "Lenskart has been profitable for two years," read it carefully: the operating turn is genuinely recent. The reported-PAT figures also swing on other-income base effects (e.g. Q4 FY25 carried a large one-off other-income), which is why adjusted PAT (₹530 Cr FY26) and EBITDA margin are the cleaner gauges than headline net profit.

How the model gets to a sustainable ~25% margin — the levers

  • Store maturation (operating leverage). A new store opens with full fixed cost (rent, staff, fit-out) but light sales, so it drags margin at first, then improves as footfall builds. With 603 stores added in FY26, a big cohort is still ramping — as they mature, they lift margins with no extra capex. India store-level margins already expanded ~6pp in Q4.
  • Manufacturing scale. Every extra million pairs spreads the fixed cost of the Bhiwadi plant over more units, cutting cost per pair — the factory gets more profitable as volume grows (§04).
  • International margin catch-up. The overseas/OWNDAYS EBITDA margin (~7% in FY26) is well below India's; as it converges upward, group margin rises even without India improving (§06).
  • Premiumisation & ASP. More premium frames, better lenses and multiple-pair purchases lift revenue per customer and per store — margin expansion without needing more footfall (§05).
Why store expansion and profit can move together — the J-curve, quietly

It can look contradictory that Lenskart adds hundreds of loss-leading new stores and expands margins in the same year. The reconciliation is that its existing stores and factory are throwing off enough operating leverage to more than offset the drag from the new-store cohort. Each store follows a mini J-curve — a couple of years to fill up and hit its stride — so at any moment Lenskart is a blend of mature, high-margin stores subsidising a rolling wave of young, ramping ones. The bull case is that as the network matures and the factory fills, the blend keeps shifting toward the mature end, dragging group margin toward that ~25% target. The bear case is that aggressive expansion, currency swings, or a stumble in a new geography keeps the blend diluted and the target perpetually one more year away. The single cleanest thing to track is India same-store-sales growth alongside the EBITDA margin: if existing stores keep growing double-digits and margins keep climbing, the leverage is real.

08

Technology, AI and the try-on

Why an eyewear company keeps talking about robots, AI and smart glasses.

Lenskart positions itself as much a technology company as a retailer, and the tech is not decoration — it directly lowers cost, converts more shoppers and creates demand. Three pieces matter.

  • 3D virtual try-on. The app lets a shopper "wear" thousands of frames using their phone camera — Lenskart logged tens of millions of virtual try-ons in a year. It solves the biggest objection to buying glasses online ("how will they look on me?"), lifting online conversion and stitching the online-offline journey together.
  • AI-enabled and remote eye testing. Over 500 stores run AI-assisted remote optometry, with self-test kiosks and mobile solutions in development. This lets Lenskart offer eye tests in more places at lower cost — scaling the top of the demand funnel (23.8 million tests in FY26) without a qualified optometrist physically in every store. A geo-analytics tool (GeoIQ) uses AI to pick store locations with short payback.
  • Robotic manufacturing. Covered in §04 — precision robotics in the factory cut cost and remakes. This is the tech that most directly feeds the margin.
  • "B by Lenskart" AI smart glasses. In FY26 Lenskart launched in-house AI camera glasses (Qualcomm chip; UPI payments, health/food logging, photo/video, translation). It is early-stage and unproven — a moon-shot optionality bet on eyewear as a computing device, not a core earnings driver, and one that carries privacy questions.
The point of the tech, in one line

Strip away the buzzwords and the tech does three unglamorous jobs: it creates demand (free/cheap AI eye tests find people who need glasses), converts it (3D try-on turns browsers into buyers), and lowers cost (robotic manufacturing and smart store-siting). Smart glasses are a separate, speculative call option on top. For an investor, the first three are what compound the core business today; the fourth is a lottery ticket — nice if it hits, not part of the base case.

How to think about a ~185× P/E

At roughly ₹88,000 Cr market cap on ~₹500 Cr of reported FY26 profit, Lenskart trades near 185× trailing earnings — several times the multiple even of expensive Indian consumer names. A number like that cannot be justified by today's profit; it only makes sense if you believe earnings will grow very fast for a long time — margins climbing from ~20% toward ~25%, stores multiplying, international catching up, and premiumisation lifting ASP, all compounding for years. Bulls argue Lenskart is a rare, category-defining, vertically-integrated leader with a huge runway and a genuine moat, deserving a scarcity premium — and that on forward earnings two or three years out the multiple compresses fast if execution holds. Bears counter that ~185× prices in near-flawless execution with essentially no margin for error: any slowdown in store economics, a currency or integration stumble abroad, a competitive price war, or simply time passing without the profit catching up to the price, and a stock this richly valued can de-rate hard. Both can be right at different points — which is exactly why the operating metrics (SSSG, margin, international EBITDA) matter more than the share price each quarter.

The rivals

A fragmented field Lenskart dominates — plus a well-capitalised incumbent and the global giant.

Lenskart's genius was to organise a market almost everyone else left fragmented. Its main competition is less any single brand and more the vast unorganised base of small opticians — but there are organised rivals worth knowing, and a global gorilla in the background.

The competitive tension to watch

Two dynamics matter. First, the moat versus the incumbent: Titan is the one rival with the brand and balance sheet to invest seriously, and it is expanding Titan Eye+ — but it has not matched Lenskart's vertical-integration cost edge or store density, and its own market-size framing is more conservative. As long as Lenskart's manufactured cost advantage holds, it stays the price-and-value leader. Second, the giant and the pricing question: EssilorLuxottica dominates global eyewear and could press harder in India, and any price war among organised players would test Lenskart's margins. The strategic question for the next five years is whether Lenskart can keep converting the unorganised ~77% into its own branded customers faster than well-funded incumbents can move up-market or global players can localise — while defending the margins its rich valuation now demands.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely differentiated, moat-rich business — priced for near-flawless execution.

Lenskart is one of the cleaner structural stories in Indian consumer: the vertically-integrated, omnichannel leader of a huge, under-penetrated, formalising eyewear market, with a real manufacturing moat, a growing pan-Asian footprint, and a business that has just crossed into genuine operating profit. The investment debate is almost entirely about valuation and pace — not whether the business is good.

The bull case

  • Hard-to-copy moat — owns design → automated manufacturing → house brands → stores + app; a cost and exclusivity edge no Indian rival matches end-to-end.
  • Enormous structural runway — ~777 mn Indians with refractive error, low penetration, a market ~77% unorganised and formalising ~1.6× faster than the rest.
  • Margin expansion in flight — EBITDA margin up to ~20%, guided toward ~25%; store maturation, factory scale and international catch-up all still to come.
  • Diversified growth — ~40% international via OWNDAYS, with overseas margins improving; premiumisation and ASP lifting revenue per customer.
  • Founder-led, well-backed — Peyush Bansal at the helm; SoftBank, Premji Invest, Temasek behind it; cash-generative, lightly levered.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — ~185× earnings leaves essentially no margin for error; vulnerable to a sharp de-rating on any stumble or simply the passage of time.
  • Profit turn is recent — the operating eyewear business only just turned profitable; FY25's headline profit leaned on non-operating income.
  • Execution & expansion risk — 600+ new stores a year, a new plant, foreign integration and smart-glasses R&D all at once; currency swings hit imported inputs and OWNDAYS.
  • Competition & pricing — Titan investing behind Titan Eye+, EssilorLuxottica globally; a price war would pressure the very margins the multiple assumes expand.
  • Post-IPO overhang & governance flags — large VC stakes create future supply as lock-ups roll off; the pre-IPO founder buyback at a discount drew scrutiny.
The bottom line

Lenskart is a genuinely differentiated, moat-rich, category-defining business riding one of the best long-term consumer trends in India — a huge, under-served, formalising eyewear market it is uniquely built to capture. The business risk is modest: the vertical-integration moat is real, the demand runway is long, and the operating profit is finally here. The valuation and execution risk is where all the action is. The story over the next few years will be written in a handful of numbers each quarter — India same-store sales growth (are existing stores compounding?), EBITDA margin (is the path to ~25% on track?), and international/OWNDAYS margin (is the overseas 40% pulling its weight?). Get those right and today's ~185× multiple compresses into something defensible; stall them, and a stock priced for perfection has a long way to fall. It is, in the end, an exceptional business wearing a very expensive price tag.
