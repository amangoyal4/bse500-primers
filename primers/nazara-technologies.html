Nazara Technologies, a listed basket of gaming bets

India's only listed pure-play gaming company is not one product but a diversified portfolio assembled by acquisition — freemium mobile games, premium PC/console titles, gamified early-learning, ad-tech and esports. This primer walks through how each part makes money, how to judge a company that grows by buying other companies, where the 2025 real-money-gaming ban actually touches it, and the one number that settles the thesis.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're actually looking at, before we go deep.

Nazara Technologies is India's only listed pure-play gaming company — but that description hides the important truth. It is not one game or one studio; it is a holding company that owns majority (or large minority) stakes in a spread of gaming and interactive-entertainment businesses, each with its own product, audience and economics. Freemium mobile games (Fusebox, Animal Jam, Kiddopia), premium PC/console titles (Curve Games), ad-tech for gaming (Datawrkz), and — historically — esports and sports media (Nodwin, Sportskeeda). Founded in 1999 and listed in 2021, it was famously backed by the late ace investor Rakesh Jhunjhunwala, whose family estate still holds a stake.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • Record FY26 on profitability, not just revenue. Consolidated revenue rose ~12.6% to ~₹1,829 Cr, but the eye-catching move was EBITDA jumping 66% to ~₹255 Cr — the highest in the company's history — and PAT up ~61% to ~₹82 Cr. The company earned more, and earned it at a better margin.
  • The profit mix flipped hard toward core gaming. Gaming's share of segment EBITDA leapt from ~56% in FY25 to ~90% in FY26. Nazara spent the year deliberately narrowing from a sprawling holdco toward a focused global gaming platform — even deconsolidating its esports arm, Nodwin.
  • It sits inside a fast-moving regulatory storm — but mostly on the safe side of it. India banned online real-money games in 2025, on top of a 28% GST imposed in 2023. Nazara is largely not a real-money-gaming company; its direct exposure runs through a minority stake in one associate (PokerBaazi's parent), not its core.
In plain terms

Nazara is best read as a listed venture portfolio of gaming companies, not as a single studio you can rate hit-by-hit. Its founders spotted early that Indian public markets had no way to own "gaming," so they built one — buying majority stakes in promising game studios, learning apps, esports leagues and ad-tech, and stitching them into one balance sheet they call the "friends of Nazara" network. That means you don't judge it the way you'd judge a maker of a single blockbuster game. You judge it the way you'd judge a capital allocator: are the businesses it buys actually growing and turning profitable, or is it just buying revenue? FY26 gave the first genuinely encouraging answer to that question — profit rose far faster than revenue — which is exactly why the year matters.

01

A portfolio, not a game: how to read Nazara

No gaming knowledge required — just one idea that governs the whole company.

Most game companies live or die on a hit. That makes them terrifying to own: a single title can carry the whole business, and when it fades there may be nothing behind it. Nazara was built to escape that fate. Instead of betting everything on one game, it spreads across many — different genres, different revenue models, different geographies — so no single flop sinks the ship, and the winners can pay for the experiments. That is the entire logic of the company, and everything else is detail.

The "friends of Nazara" model, decoded

Nazara doesn't usually build games from a blank page. It acquires controlling or large stakes in existing gaming businesses that already have a product and an audience, then supports them with capital, shared technology, data, ad-monetisation and cross-promotion — a network it brands the "friends of Nazara." A hit early-learning app (Kiddopia), a TV-IP mobile-games studio (Fusebox), a British PC/console publisher (Curve Games), an esports league (Nodwin), a sports-content site (Sportskeeda) and a gaming ad-tech firm (Datawrkz) have almost nothing in common as products — but as a portfolio, they diversify each other's risk. The trade-off: you inherit whatever you buy, including its integration headaches and the price you paid for it.

Why one yardstick can't measure Nazara

Because the pieces earn money in genuinely different ways, you cannot price the whole thing on a single number. A subscription learning app, a one-time premium game sale and a low-margin ad-reselling business each deserve a different multiple. The professional approach is to think in parts — value each business on its own terms, then judge the group on how well capital is being moved between them.

03

The company and who owns it

A quarter-century-old gaming pioneer, a legendary backer, and a founder-family at the helm.

Nazara was founded in 1999 by Vikash Mittersain — long before "gaming" was an investable Indian theme — and grew up through the era of telco value-added services and early mobile games. His son Nitish Mittersain (Joint MD / CEO) drove the transformation into a diversified, acquisition-led gaming platform. In 2021 it became India's first, and still only, listed pure-play gaming company, in an IPO that was heavily oversubscribed.

Ownership & who's on the register

  • Promoter group ~34%. The Mittersain family (Vikash and Nitish, with associates) forms the promoter core. It is a relatively low promoter holding by Indian standards — a legacy of the many equity issuances used to fund acquisitions — which makes the outside investor register unusually influential.
  • The Jhunjhunwala estate. Following Rakesh Jhunjhunwala's death in 2022, Rekha Jhunjhunwala continues to hold a meaningful stake (around 7% as of early 2025) — a long-standing anchor investor whose presence has long been part of the Nazara story.
  • New marquee names. FY26's large block deal brought in Zerodha co-founder Nikhil Kamath and saw institutional holder Axana Estates add to a ~5%+ position — a sign that sophisticated capital is treating the post-pivot Nazara as an investable gaming proxy.
  • Institutions & funds. A rotating cast of mutual funds and FIIs hold the balance; with a modest promoter stake, Nazara trades more like a widely-held platform than a founder-locked company.
Why the ownership shape matters

Two things stand out. First, a ~34% promoter holding is low — Nazara has repeatedly issued equity to fund deals, so ownership is diffuse and the company answers to a broad base of institutions and marquee investors rather than a controlling family alone. Second, the calibre of the outside register — the Jhunjhunwala estate, Nikhil Kamath, large domestic funds — is itself a signal: these are patient, portfolio-minded investors who understand they are backing a capital allocator in a young industry, not a single-product bet. That patience is an asset when the payoff from acquisitions takes years to show.

04

Five ways Nazara makes money

The mechanics — because "gaming revenue" hides five very different machines.

To understand Nazara you have to understand that "a gaming company" can earn money in completely different ways, and Nazara does most of them at once. Each model has its own rhythm, its own margin and its own risk. Here is how each one actually works.

1 · Freemium mobile games — the volume engine

Games like Fusebox's interactive-story titles (built on TV franchises such as Love Island and Big Brother) and Animal Jam are free to download. The money comes from a small slice of players who buy in-game items, currency or premium content, plus advertising shown to the rest. It is a volume game: you need huge downloads and strong retention because only a few percent of players ever pay — but the payers can be very valuable, and "live-operations" (constant events and updates) is the craft that keeps them spending. Mobile gaming was Nazara's largest revenue vertical in FY26 at roughly ₹713 Cr.

2 · Gamified learning by subscription — the steady one

Kiddopia is an early-learning app for young children, sold on a recurring subscription — parents pay a monthly or annual fee. This is the most predictable model in the portfolio: revenue recurs, and the key metric is whether the cost of acquiring a subscriber is paid back before they churn. FY26 saw Kiddopia's unit economics improve — a quietly important swing, because a subscription business only compounds once each new customer is profitable.

3 · Premium PC & console publishing — the catalogue

Curve Games, a UK publisher Nazara bought in 2025, sells premium one-time-purchase titles on Steam, PlayStation and Xbox — most famously the physics-comedy hit Human Fall Flat, which has passed 58 million lifetime unit sales. This model is the opposite of freemium: fewer, higher-value transactions, and a back-catalogue that keeps selling for years. PC/console added roughly ₹261 Cr in FY26.

4 · Ad-tech — big revenue, thin margin

Datawrkz is a digital-advertising business: it buys, sells and optimises online ads (increasingly gaming-related). It is genuinely large — about ₹454 Cr of FY26 revenue — but it is a media pass-through, so most of that revenue is cost. It flatters the top line far more than the bottom line, which is exactly why revenue is a poor guide to Nazara (see §08).

5 · Esports & sports media — the audience business

Nodwin runs esports tournaments and youth-focused media; Sportskeeda is a large sports-content site. These monetise audience and sponsorship rather than gameplay. Crucially, in FY26 Nazara deconsolidated Nodwin (it stepped back from majority control while remaining the largest shareholder) — so esports now sits partly outside the reported accounts (see §06).

Why running all five at once is the strategy — and the burden

Owning five models means one weak year in freemium can be offset by a strong catalogue year in PC/console, or by a steadier subscription line. That diversification is the whole appeal. But it is also a management burden: each model needs different talent, different metrics and different capital, and a company that does all five well is doing something genuinely hard. FY26's message was that Nazara is finally choosing — leaning into the profitable gaming models and stepping back from the ones (esports, and possibly ad-tech) that dilute margin.

05

Growth by acquisition — the engine, and how to judge it

The single most important thing to get right about this company.

Nazara grows mainly by buying other gaming companies. That is a legitimate strategy — but it is also the strategy that most often destroys value, because it is easy to buy revenue and hard to buy profitable growth. So the central question for any Nazara investor is blunt: are the acquired businesses actually growing and earning, or is the company simply purchasing a bigger top line and paying for it with new shares?

The three tests that separate good deals from expensive ones

When a company grows by acquisition, watch three things. (1) Does revenue growth come with margin? Buying a low-margin business (like ad-tech) inflates revenue but not profit — impressive-looking, value-neutral. (2) Are the acquired businesses still growing after purchase, or did they peak on the way in? A studio bought at the top of a hit's popularity can shrink the moment it joins. (3) Is the group generating cash, not just accounting profit? Acquisition-led companies can look profitable while quietly consuming cash. FY26 scored surprisingly well on all three: EBITDA rose far faster than revenue, gaming momentum continued after the deals, and pre-tax operating cash flow jumped ~81% to ~₹213 Cr — an ~84% conversion from EBITDA to cash.

The goodwill question

Every acquisition above the target's book value creates goodwill on Nazara's balance sheet — an accounting entry that says "we paid a premium for future potential." That is fine as long as the potential shows up. The danger is a write-down: if an acquired studio underperforms, Nazara must mark that goodwill down, taking a one-off hit to profit and admitting it overpaid. For an acquisition machine, goodwill is the running scorecard of past capital allocation — a growing pile that never gets written down is a sign the deals are working; a big impairment is the market's proof that one didn't.

How to judge Nazara's dealmaking each year: not "did revenue grow?" (buying it is easy) but "did margin and cash grow, did the acquired businesses keep compounding, and did goodwill hold without impairment?" On that harder test, FY26 was the best year Nazara has shown.
06

The FY26 pivot: from sprawling holdco to focused gaming platform

The strategic story of the year — and why deconsolidating Nodwin was a statement.

For years the knock on Nazara was that it was too diffuse — a little of everything, master of nothing, hard to value. FY26 was the year management answered that criticism head-on: it began narrowing the portfolio toward core gaming, stepping back from businesses that added revenue but diluted focus and margin. The clearest signal was the treatment of its own esports arm.

What "deconsolidating Nodwin" actually means — and why it's a signal

In August 2025 Nazara gave up majority control of Nodwin (its esports and youth-media business) while remaining its largest shareholder (~47.66%). In accounting terms, once you no longer control a subsidiary you stop "consolidating" it — its revenue and costs drop out of your headline accounts, and you instead carry it as an investment. That is why reported esports revenue collapsed (down ~85% in Q4 FY26 versus the prior year): the business didn't vanish, it left the consolidation. The strategic message is bigger than the accounting: Nazara is signalling that esports — capital-hungry and structurally lower-margin — is better run at arm's length (Nodwin is even mooted for its own IPO), while Nazara concentrates its own balance sheet on the higher-margin gaming it actually wants to own.

Management has also signalled it may divest or separate the ad-tech (Datawrkz) and remaining esports assets as it sharpens into a global gaming platform. If that happens, Nazara's reported revenue could fall even as its margin and quality rise — a counter-intuitive but healthy outcome, and one more reason to watch profit and cash rather than the top line.

The surprise most people miss: Nazara's single best-selling title is not an Indian game at all — it's Human Fall Flat, a quirky British physics-comedy game (58m+ lifetime units) that came in with the UK Curve Games acquisition. India's only listed "gaming" champion is, at its most profitable, a publisher of global catalogue hits made elsewhere.

07

Regulation: 28% GST, the 2025 real-money-gaming ban, and Nazara's real exposure

The scariest headline for "Indian gaming" — and why it mostly isn't Nazara's headline.

If you have read anything alarming about "Indian online gaming," it was almost certainly about real-money gaming (RMG) — fantasy sports, online poker, rummy for stakes. That corner of the industry took two heavy blows: a 28% GST on the full face value of player deposits (effective October 2023) and then, in August 2025, the Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, which banned online real-money games outright — games of skill, chance, or any mix — along with their advertising and facilitation. Entire RMG companies faced closure. The crucial question for this primer: how much of that is actually Nazara's problem?

The distinction that changes the whole risk picture

Nazara's core is not real-money gaming. Freemium mobile games, a children's learning subscription, premium PC/console titles, ad-tech and esports do not involve players staking money for a monetary return — so the 2025 ban does not hit the businesses that generate the vast majority of Nazara's profit. Nazara's direct RMG exposure runs almost entirely through a minority associate stake in Moonshine Technology (the parent of PokerBaazi), a skill-based RMG operator — a stake carried as an investment, not a consolidated business. When the ban was introduced, Nazara's share price did fall sharply on the day, but that was largely sentiment — the market reflexively selling anything labelled "gaming" — rather than a hit to its operating engine.

The overhang that remains — and the twist that helps

  • The associate takes the real damage. A ban on RMG directly impairs the value of the PokerBaazi/Moonshine stake — the one place Nazara genuinely owned RMG economics. Expect that investment's contribution and carrying value to be questioned; it is the concrete, quantifiable exposure.
  • Sentiment is a recurring tax. Because Nazara is the only listed gaming pure-play, it is the market's default proxy for every gaming headline — good and bad. Regulatory noise around RMG will keep moving the stock even when it doesn't move the business. That mismatch is a feature of being the only ticker in the sector.
  • The twist: esports is promoted, not banned. The same 2025 Act that outlawed RMG explicitly encourages esports and social/educational games as legitimate, government-backed categories. Nazara's Nodwin (esports) and its casual/learning titles sit on the favoured side of the very law that spooked the sector — a genuinely under-appreciated silver lining.
The sober read: the regulatory storm is real and it does touch Nazara — through one associate stake and through relentless sentiment volatility — but it does not threaten the core gaming-profit engine, and parts of the new regime (esports promotion) actively help. The risk is a re-rating from headlines, not a collapse of the operating business.
08

The margin puzzle: why a ₹1,829 Cr company earns only ₹255 Cr of EBITDA

The one analytical trap that catches every first-time reader of Nazara.

On the surface Nazara looks like a low-margin business: ~₹1,829 Cr of revenue turned into ~₹255 Cr of EBITDA — a blended margin of only about 14%. For a "tech" or "gaming" company that sounds thin. But the blended number is misleading, and understanding why is the single most useful thing you can learn about this company.

The non-transferable tell: profit mix moves far more than the revenue line

Here is the fact that could only be written about Nazara. In FY26 revenue rose only ~13%, yet gaming's share of EBITDA leapt from ~56% to ~90% and blended margin jumped from ~9% to ~14% — because a low-margin ad-tech business can add ₹450 Cr of revenue at almost no profit while a single premium catalogue title (Human Fall Flat) or a maturing subscription (Kiddopia) quietly carries the earnings. Revenue is a decoy at Nazara; segment EBITDA is the tell. A year where reported revenue falls because ad-tech is trimmed, while margin and cash rise, would be a good year — the exact opposite of how most companies read. Miss this, and you will misjudge every Nazara result you ever look at.

This is also why the FY26 pivot (§06) matters financially, not just strategically. Every rupee of low-margin ad-tech revenue Nazara sheds raises the blended margin even if it lowers the top line. The company is deliberately trading revenue optics for profit quality — and the margin chart above is the scoreboard.

Financials & segment mix

A fast-growing top line, a lumpy but now-improving bottom line.

Nazara's revenue has more than tripled since FY22, powered by acquisitions and organic gaming growth. Profit has been lumpier — rising, dipping, then recovering — which is exactly what you'd expect from a business absorbing deals, integration costs and one-off items. FY26's combination of steady revenue growth and a sharp profit recovery is the healthiest shape the numbers have shown.

10

Valuation & the metric that settles the thesis

A young industry, the only listed proxy, and one number that decides the case.

Nazara is genuinely hard to value, for a reason that is itself a fact worth knowing: it has no clean listed peer in India. It is the only listed pure-play gaming company, so the market cannot simply "compare it to the sector" — there is no sector on the exchange. That scarcity cuts both ways: it earns Nazara a premium as the sole way to own the theme, and it makes the stock a lightning rod for every gaming headline, sober or not.

The one number that settles the case

For Nazara the thesis-settling metric is core gaming-segment revenue growth read together with consolidated EBITDA margin — because that pair captures the two things that actually matter: is the profitable part of the portfolio still compounding, and is the whole company converting its scale into real margin? A rising gaming top line paired with a climbing blended margin (say, toward the high teens) would prove the pivot is working and the acquisitions are paying back. Flat gaming growth with stalled margin would say Nazara is a collection of purchased revenue that never became a profitable platform. Everything else — the RMG headlines, the block deals, the quarterly PAT swings — is weather; this is the climate.

The valuation debate, then, isn't "is gaming a big market?" (it plainly is, and growing) but "has this particular basket earned the right to a premium?" FY26's answer was the most affirmative yet — EBITDA up 66%, margin up, cash conversion strong, mix improving. A market cap many times EBITDA is pricing several more years like that. The reader's job is to check, each result, whether the gaming line keeps growing and the margin keeps rising — because those two lines moving together are what justify the price, and either one stalling is what would undo it.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A maturing capital allocator in a young industry — priced for the pivot to keep paying off.

Nazara is at the most interesting moment of its life: it has spent a decade assembling a portfolio, and FY26 was the first year that portfolio behaved like a real profit engine rather than a costly experiment. The question ahead is whether the discipline holds — whether it keeps concentrating on what earns and pruning what doesn't, and whether the studios it bought keep compounding after their purchase prices.

The bull case

  • The pivot is working — FY26 EBITDA +66%, margin up, gaming now ~90% of segment profit, and ~84% EBITDA-to-cash conversion.
  • Only listed gaming proxy — scarcity value in a large, structurally growing Indian and global gaming market.
  • Diversified, hit-resilient portfolio — five models and a global catalogue (Human Fall Flat, Fusebox, Kiddopia) reduce single-title risk.
  • On the favoured side of regulation — core isn't RMG; esports and casual/learning are explicitly promoted by the 2025 Act.
  • Patient, high-calibre register — the Jhunjhunwala estate, Nikhil Kamath and large funds back a capital allocator for the long game.

The bear case

  • Hit-and-content risk — gaming profit still leans on a handful of titles; a fading catalogue or a missed release hurts fast.
  • Acquisition & goodwill risk — growth by buying companies can mask weak organic growth; an impairment would expose an overpaid deal.
  • Thin blended margin — ~14% leaves little cushion; low-margin ad-tech and integration costs can drag results.
  • Regulatory & sentiment overhang — the RMG associate is impaired by the ban, and the stock swings on every gaming headline.
  • Capital-allocation dependence & rich valuation — the case rests on management deploying capital well, at a market cap that already prices years of success.
The bottom line

Nazara is a maturing capital allocator in a young, volatile industry — a listed basket of gaming bets that, in FY26, finally showed it can turn scale into rising margin and real cash. The core engine is insulated from the regulation that scares the sector, and the strategic direction — concentrate on profitable gaming, hive off the rest — is the right one. The case is not about whether gaming grows; it is about whether this basket keeps earning its premium. The next chapters will be written in three concrete developments: whether Nodwin's mooted IPO and any ad-tech separation lift group margin as promised, whether the acquired studios — Curve, Fusebox, Kiddopia — keep compounding past the prices Nazara paid, and whether consolidated EBITDA margin climbs into the high teens. If those land, a scattered holdco has become a genuine gaming platform; if the studios stall or a goodwill impairment lands, the market will re-mark a premium it granted on faith.
