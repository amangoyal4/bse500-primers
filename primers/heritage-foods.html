Heritage Foods, a South-Indian dairy squeezed between the farmer and the fridge

A complete walk through one of India's larger private dairies — the company behind the green-and-white "Heritage" milk packet, curd tub and ghee jar across the South — how a dairy business actually earns its money, why almost all of its slim profit lives in the gap between what it pays a farmer for raw milk and what it can charge you for the packet, and why moving from plain milk into curd, paneer, ghee and ice cream is the single most important thing it is trying to do.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Heritage Foods Limited buys raw milk from farmers, chills it, packs it, and sells it back to households — chiefly as pouch milk, and increasingly as curd, buttermilk, lassi, paneer, ghee, flavoured milk and ice cream. It is one of India's larger private-sector dairies, strongest across the South — Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu — with a procurement web of roughly three lakh farmers and a distribution reach into around 13 states. It was founded in 1992 by N. Chandrababu Naidu and is run today by the Nara family, with N. Bhuvaneswari as Vice-Chairperson & Managing Director.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The profit is a thin spread between milk bought and milk sold. Heritage pays a farmer a procurement price for raw milk (about ₹43 a litre in the June-2026 quarter, and rising) and sells the finished product at a shelf price the market and its rivals hold down. What is left after processing, packing, cold-chain and distribution is a slim operating margin — ~5.9% at the EBITDA line in FY26. That gap is the business, and it is one of the tightest in consumer goods.
  • Value-added products are the whole strategy. Plain liquid milk earns a wafer-thin gross margin; curd, paneer, ghee, ice cream and flavoured milk earn several times more. So Heritage is deliberately steering its mix toward these value-added products (VAP) — VAP revenue reached ~₹1,468 Cr in FY26 (+12.6%) and now sits above a third of dairy sales. This shift is the clearest lever the company genuinely controls.
  • FY26 was a hard year, and the numbers say so plainly. Revenue grew ~9% to ~₹4,526 Cr, but net profit fell ~20% to ~₹150 Cr as milk-procurement inflation ran ahead of what could be passed on and a weak flush season kept raw-milk costs high. The stock trades on a mid-20s earnings multiple — a consumer rating, but for a business whose margins swing with the price of milk.
In plain terms

Picture a company that stands between two parties it does not control. On one side is the dairy farmer, whose raw-milk price rises with feed costs, weather and how much milk the region's buffaloes and cows are giving that season. On the other is the household, which treats a milk packet as an everyday essential and notices any price rise instantly — as, therefore, do competitors and state governments. Heritage buys from the first and sells to the second, and keeps only the slim margin in between. Its escape route from that squeeze is to turn more of its milk into curd, paneer, ghee and ice cream — products people will pay a real premium for — rather than selling it plain. FY26 is what the tight year looks like: costs up, prices sticky, profit down. The skill of the business is widening that spread faster than milk inflation can close it.

01

Dairy 101: what's actually in the packet

No jargon needed — just the milk, the products, and how they reach your fridge.

India is the world's largest milk producer, but most of that milk still passes through the unorganised channel — the local milkman and neighbourhood vendor — which handles well over half the marketable surplus. The organised dairies, cooperative and private, are the ones that chill, test, pack and brand milk to a standard. Heritage is one of the larger private members of that organised club. To follow it you need five terms.

The one dependency that shapes the sector: milk is bought fresh, daily, from millions of small farmers

Unlike a factory that stockpiles steel or grain, a dairy cannot inventory its main raw material — raw milk sours in hours, and it arrives in small quantities from a vast, scattered base of farmers, twice every day. That single fact explains almost everything about how a dairy is run: why procurement is a daily logistics feat, not a purchasing decision; why a dense chilling and collection network is a genuine barrier that takes decades to build; why raw-milk cost cannot be hedged the way a commodity buyer hedges; and why the seasons — flush and lean — move margins directly. A dairy's edge is not a secret recipe. It is the reliability and reach of the cold pipe that carries fresh milk from the village to the packet before it spoils.

Who buys, and how it reaches the fridge

  • The household — the end customer, buying a daily pouch of milk or a tub of curd from a local dealer, retail outlet or a Heritage parlour. Milk demand is steady and non-discretionary; VAP demand skews more urban and more discretionary, and grows with incomes.
  • The distribution channel — a web of distributors, agents, retail stores and franchised parlours across the South and beyond. Because the product is perishable and delivered twice daily, the channel is dense, local and hard to replicate.
  • The farmer — not a customer but the supplier, and the party whose price sets the company's largest cost. Heritage procures almost entirely direct from farmers, which gives it grip on quality and cost but also puts it in daily competition with every other buyer for the same milk.
02

The milk spread: how a dairy really makes money

The single most important structural fact about this company — understand it and the rest follows.

Most people track a dairy by the price of a milk packet. That is the wrong number. Heritage does not keep the price of the packet; it keeps the spread — what is left after it has paid the farmer for the raw milk and paid to chill, process, pack and deliver it. The whole company can be read through one relationship: the gap between the procurement price it pays and the realisation it earns, per litre. It is one of the tightest gaps in the consumer economy.

The mechanics are unforgiving. Raw milk is roughly 65–70% of a dairy's cost — the single dominant line. Heritage pays that cost every day, in cash, to farmers. It then adds processing, packaging, energy, cold-chain and freight, and sells at a shelf price that is sticky: households resist milk-price rises, rivals hold theirs down, and state governments watch the number closely. So when the procurement price climbs, the maker cannot simply pass it through overnight. The spread narrows first, and only later — if at all — does the shelf price catch up.

Now watch the spread move. In the June-2026 quarter, Heritage's average milk-procurement price rose 4.7% to about ₹43.3 a litre even as it grew procurement volumes ~10%. Shelf prices did not rise in step. The result across FY26 was revenue up ~9% but EBITDA margin down ~210 basis points to ~5.9%, and net profit down ~20%. The company sold more milk, to more households, at higher revenue — and made less money, because the cost of the raw material outran the price of the packet. That is the spread doing its work, and it is the lens to hold for the rest of this primer.

The device that only fits a fresh-dairy maker: procurement price vs sticky realisation

Forget "the price of milk went up." The number that runs Heritage Foods is realisation per litre − procurement price per litre − (processing + cold-chain + distribution). Both halves move on different clocks. The left half — what households pay — is held down by competition, habit and political sensitivity, and moves in slow, cautious steps. The right half — what farmers are paid — moves continuously with feed prices, cattle yields and the flush-versus-lean season. Heritage's profit is the gap between a price it can barely raise and a cost it must pay in full, in cash, every day. When the gap is comfortable, a good year happens. When milk inflation opens up before shelf prices can follow — as through FY26 — the gap tightens and profit falls even as sales rise. Two products can sell for the same shelf price in both worlds; only the spread tells you which one Heritage is living in.

03

What Heritage actually sells: the product mix and the plants

A milk backbone, a widening VAP basket, and a feed-and-energy tail.

Heritage is at heart a procure-process-and-market dairy: it collects raw milk, converts it at its processing plants, and sells the output through a dense distribution network under the Heritage brand. Revenue splits across a milk base and a value-added basket with very different economics, plus a small non-dairy tail from cattle feed and renewable energy.

The product lines

  • Liquid milk — the volume backbone and the daily habit, the largest single share of revenue. High-recognition and steady, but the thinnest-margin product in the basket. It is what gets the brand into the fridge; it is not where the money is made.
  • Value-added products (VAP) — the strategic growth engine: curd, buttermilk, lassi, paneer, ghee, table butter, flavoured milk, UHT milk, milk powder, sweets and a fast-growing ice-cream line. VAP revenue was ~₹1,468 Cr in FY26 (+12.6%) and now runs above a third of dairy sales; ice-cream volumes jumped ~26% in the March-2026 quarter. These products carry the higher, steadier margins that lift the whole mix.
  • Cattle feed (Heritage Nutrivet) — a wholly-owned subsidiary selling feed to the same farmers Heritage buys milk from. FY26 revenue rose ~33% to ~₹245 Cr with feed volumes up ~40% — small, but strategically neat: it deepens the farmer relationship and can improve milk yields at source.
  • Renewable energy — wind and solar capacity that supplies power to the dairy operations, a legacy of the company's second reported "division." Modest, but it trims the energy bill of a power-hungry cold chain.

The physical footprint

  • Reach — a distribution presence spanning around 13 states and, by the company's account, roughly 1.5 million households, served by a portfolio of 400-plus SKUs across milk and value-added lines.
Why the VAP tilt is the smartest thing management controls

Of the variables in this business, most are set outside the company — the procurement price by farmers and the season, the shelf price by competition and habit. Product mix is one Heritage genuinely owns. Plain liquid milk is a near-commodity whose thin margin the spread dictates; curd, paneer, ghee and ice cream are branded, formulated products where taste, quality and distribution earn a much richer, steadier margin. Pushing volume from the packet into the tub, the jar and the tub of ice cream is therefore the one strategic dial that lifts the quality of earnings without waiting on the weather or a rival's price list. It will not rescue a bad milk-cost year on its own, but it makes each litre of milk worth more, every year.

04

The company, the family, and who controls it

A Nara-family dairy founded in 1992, refocused after exiting retail.

Heritage Foods has an unusually visible promoter family. It was founded by N. Chandrababu Naidu, later a long-serving Chief Minister of Andhra Pradesh, and is controlled today by the Nara family. Understanding the ownership and the company's earlier detour into retail explains both its focus today and the governance questions a minority investor should keep in view.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter family: the Nara family holds roughly 41% (approx.) of the company. Within that, N. Bhuvaneswari — Vice-Chairperson & MD — is reported to hold about 24% (approx.). The balance is public float. Investors should confirm the exact promoter percentages in the latest shareholding filing.
  • The founder link, stated plainly: the company was founded by N. Chandrababu Naidu, who has served as Chief Minister of Andhra Pradesh. By the family's and company's account, Naidu himself holds no shares in Heritage Foods today; the promoter holding sits with other Nara-family members. The day-to-day business is run by the family's executives, with Nara Brahmani serving as an Executive Director.

Why the ownership matters, kept factual: a founder with a prominent public and political profile draws attention to an otherwise ordinary consumer company, and the shares are held by family members rather than the founder personally. For a minority investor the relevant points are the usual ones for any family-run business — the concentration of control, the quality and independence of the board, and that related-party dealings (for instance with the cattle-feed subsidiary or any group entity) stay at arm's length. These are governance questions to monitor in the filings, not commentary on the family.
05

The VAP lever: turning milk into margin

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a fresh dairy.

If the profit is a thin spread and raw milk is two-thirds of cost, then the most valuable thing a dairy can do is sell each litre of milk in a higher-value form. A litre sold as plain pouch milk earns a gross margin of roughly 8–12%; the same litre turned into curd, paneer, ghee, flavoured milk or ice cream can earn a gross margin north of 25%. That difference is the VAP lever, and it is the heart of Heritage's self-help strategy.

The mix is moving in the right direction, if not fast enough to offset FY26's cost shock. Ice cream is the sharpest edge of this push: volumes rose ~26% in Q4 FY26, and a dedicated ice-cream plant is being built at Shamirpet near Hyderabad to feed the growth.

Why the VAP shift is worth the effort even in a cheap-milk world

You might ask: if plain milk is a reliable daily habit, why chase the harder VAP categories at all? Because plain milk's margin is structurally capped by its own stickiness — the shelf price can barely move, so the spread is only ever as wide as the gap to procurement cost, and in a bad milk year that gap all but closes. VAP breaks that ceiling. A tub of curd, a jar of ghee or a cup of ice cream is bought for its brand, taste and convenience, not weighed against a state-set milk rate — so it carries a fatter, steadier margin and can absorb milk inflation without collapsing. Over a cycle, every point of mix moved into VAP raises the floor under the whole company's profitability. It is the difference between selling a raw material and selling a brand.

06

The procurement engine: three lakh farmers, twice a day

The operational reality — and the seasonal trap — that a first-timer never sees coming.

Here is the fact that surprises people looking at a dairy for the first time: the hardest, most defining part of the business is not making curd or selling ice cream — it is buying the milk in the first place. Heritage procures raw milk almost entirely direct from around three lakh farmers across roughly nine states, collected twice every single day and chilled fast before it can spoil. That daily feat of logistics, not any factory, is the real machine.

The scale is considerable. Heritage runs on the order of 201 bulk coolers and chilling points close to the farm gate, procures around 1.5 million litres of milk a day, and does so through thousands of village-level collection centres. In the June-2026 quarter procurement volumes grew ~10% to about 17.8 lakh litres a day. Building this pipe — the coolers, the tankers, the farmer relationships, the twice-daily rhythm — is what takes decades and keeps new entrants out. It is the company's most durable asset, and it does not appear as a brand on any packet.

The seasonal trap to sit with: the flush that failed

A dairy's year has a rhythm built into it. In the flush season — broadly the cooler months — cattle give more milk, raw-milk supply is plentiful, and procurement prices normally ease, handing the dairy a seasonal margin tailwind. A dairy quietly relies on that flush to rebuild its spread after the expensive lean summer. FY26's difficulty was, in large part, a weak flush: the seasonal easing in milk costs that the model counts on simply did not arrive at its usual strength. So Heritage entered the year already paying up for milk and never got the customary mid-year relief — which is why a 9% rise in sales still translated into a fall in profit. The lesson for a first-timer: a dairy's profit is hostage to a season it cannot control, and the flush is the single most important weather event in its calendar.

Why this reframes the whole company: Heritage's profitability is set less by how cleverly it markets ice cream and more by two things it does not author — the price farmers demand for milk and whether the flush season cooperates. Read the procurement price and the seasonal commentary alongside the revenue line; together they explain the profit number that the top line alone hides. A great distribution year can still be a poor profit year if the milk came in dear.
07

Going north: the fight to grow beyond the South

The one structural growth lever — widening the map and deepening the mix.

Heritage's strength — and its limit — is regional concentration. Its procurement heartland and brand strength sit in the South: Andhra Pradesh, Telangana, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu, extending into Maharashtra. That density is an advantage at home but a ceiling on growth, and it ties the company's fortunes to one region's milk economics and monsoon. The strategic answer is to push north and west while deepening the VAP mix everywhere.

The northern seed was planted in 2017, when Heritage acquired the dairy business of Reliance Retail and gained a foothold in markets such as Punjab, Uttarakhand and Rajasthan. Management has since signalled ambitions to roughly double milk-handling capacity toward ~2.8 million litres a day over about five years, with a stated aim that a meaningful share — of the order of a third — should eventually come from northern markets. Procurement today already reaches into a wider set of states — Gujarat, Rajasthan, Haryana and Punjab among them — beyond the southern core.

The strategy has three prongs working together. Deepen the mix — more VAP, more ice cream, the Shamirpet plant. Widen the map — grow procurement and distribution in the North and West so no single region's milk economics decides the year. And strengthen the farmer relationship — the Heritage Nutrivet cattle-feed arm binds farmers closer and can lift milk yields at source, improving both supply security and quality. A cold-chain and distribution build sits behind all of it, because every new geography and every new VAP line needs the refrigerated pipe extended to reach it.

Why geographic spread is worth the capex in a thin-margin business

Expanding a dairy is expensive and slow: every new region needs its own procurement network, chilling infrastructure, plants and distribution before it earns a rupee. So why bother, when margins are already tight? Because concentration is itself a risk. A single-region dairy lives and dies by that region's monsoon, its milk-cost cycle and its competitive intensity; one bad southern flush hits the whole company at once, as FY26 showed. Spreading procurement and sales across more geographies smooths those swings, opens a longer growth runway once the home market matures, and lets the brand and VAP portfolio work over a larger base. It will not lift margins by itself — but it makes the thin margin the company does earn steadier and more durable over a cycle.

08

The three forces — none of which Heritage fully controls

Milk-cost inflation, the shelf-price ceiling, and the season: the trio that writes the year.

Strip the business to its essentials and a fresh dairy's year is decided by three forces. Heritage manages around all three — through mix, scale and farmer ties — but authors none of them. This is the core of the risk profile, stated straight.

The plain summary a first-timer should internalise

Heritage's job is to run a tight, dense procure-process-distribute operation and then survive the milk cycle — the cost of raw milk, the ceiling on what it can charge, and the season that governs both. It can improve its odds: shift into VAP, add scale and geography, bind farmers closer with feed. What it cannot do is guarantee any single year, because the three biggest inputs to that year — the farmer's price, the household's price tolerance, and the weather over the milkshed — are set outside its walls. FY26 was a year when the cost force and the season turned against it at once, and the shelf-price ceiling stopped it recovering the difference. A disciplined investor prices the good years knowing the model does not control whether they repeat.

Notice how the forces interact. A rich VAP mix (the company's own lever) is worth far more when milk costs are behaving, and it is precisely what cushions the blow when they are not — a fatter VAP margin absorbs some of a procurement spike that plain milk cannot. The worst years, like FY26, are when a weak flush and milk inflation land together faster than the mix can shift and the shelf price can follow. The best are when a strong flush eases milk costs just as a growing VAP share lifts the blended spread.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded market led by a giant cooperative, with private players fighting on VAP.

Indian dairy is a large, fragmented and fiercely competitive market. The unorganised sector — local milkmen and vendors — still handles well over half of all marketable milk. Within the organised segment, cooperatives lead on volume and private processors increasingly compete on value-added products. Heritage is a solid regional player, but it is not the giant, and it sits among several sharper VAP specialists.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, this is a market where scale in procurement and richness of mix decide the winners, not shelf-price wars — milk prices are broadly held in line across players, so the dairy with the densest low-cost procurement network and the fattest VAP portfolio earns the widest spread on the same litre. That is why Milky Mist's VAP-only model runs the industry's best margins (in the low-to-mid teens at the EBITDA line), while liquid-milk-heavy players sit far lower. Second, Heritage competes most directly with Dodla and Hatsun on its own turf, and its task is to close the mix gap to the VAP leaders while defending its procurement heartland against the cooperatives and the unorganised trade. Cost position and mix, not price, are the battleground.

Financials & valuation

A weak profit year on a consumer multiple — the question is whether the spread recovers.

FY26 was, on the profit line, a difficult year: revenue grew but earnings fell, as milk-cost inflation and a weak flush overwhelmed a growing VAP mix. Yet the stock still carries a consumer-style multiple — a mid-20s price-to-earnings — because the market prices Heritage as a branded FMCG-adjacent business, not a pure commodity processor. The debate here is whether FY26's compressed spread is a passing cost shock or a sign of structurally tougher milk economics.

The one number that settles the thesis: EBITDA margin, read with the VAP share

At a mid-20s multiple, Heritage is priced as a branded consumer compounder, not a cheap cyclical — so the case rests on the spread recovering and the mix improving, not on a re-rating. The metric that proves or breaks it is EBITDA margin (the ~5.9% of FY26), tracked quarter by quarter, read alongside two companions: the milk-procurement price (is the ~₹43/litre cost easing as the flush normalises, or staying stubbornly high?) and the VAP share of revenue (is the mid-30s mix climbing toward the VAP leaders, lifting the blended margin?). If milk costs normalise and VAP keeps gaining share, FY26's margin looks like a trough and earnings recover toward the FY25 level and beyond. If procurement inflation persists while the shelf-price ceiling holds, the consumer multiple looks generous for the margins on offer. Margin times volume, richened by mix — that is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A branded regional dairy through a hard milk year — asked whether it can widen the spread.

Heritage Foods enters FY27 as a focused, cash-generative dairy with a growing VAP portfolio, a dense southern procurement moat, a northward runway and a supportive promoter family — but coming off a year in which milk costs beat it. What no dairy can promise is the one thing that decides everything: that the gap between what it pays for milk and what it can charge for the packet stays kind. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • VAP mix compounding — curd, paneer, ghee and fast-growing ice cream lift the blended margin above thin liquid milk, with a dedicated ice-cream plant coming on stream.
  • A deep procurement moat — ~3 lakh direct farmers, ~201 coolers and twice-daily collection are a barrier decades in the making that rivals cannot quickly copy.
  • Northward runway — the 2017 Reliance dairy foothold and a plan to roughly double handling capacity give growth beyond the maturing southern base.
  • Cost normalisation upside — if the flush normalises and milk inflation eases, FY26's compressed ~5.9% margin is a trough, and profit rebounds sharply on operating leverage.
  • Structural demand — dairy is a non-discretionary daily habit with rising VAP consumption as incomes grow; a large, durable market.

The bear case

  • Milk-cost inflation — procurement price is two-thirds of cost and outside the company's control; a weak flush or feed spike squeezes the spread fast, as FY26 proved.
  • The shelf-price ceiling — milk prices are sticky and politically watched, so cost rises cannot be passed on in full or on time; the margin is structurally thin.
  • Regional concentration — heavy southern reliance ties results to one region's monsoon and milk cycle; expansion north is slow and capital-hungry.
  • A rich multiple for the margins — a mid-20s P/E on a ~6% EBITDA-margin business leaves little cushion if profit does not recover promptly.
  • Sharp competition — Amul's scale, Hatsun's VAP strength and Dodla on the same turf press on both procurement and the value-added categories Heritage is chasing.
The bottom line

Heritage Foods is a well-run regional dairy turning a daily milk habit into a branded consumer business, coming off a hard year in which the cost of milk overran the price of the packet. The self-help is real — a richer VAP mix, a growing ice-cream line, a deep procurement network and a slow push north — and it genuinely widens and steadies the spread the company lives on. But the three forces that write each year, the milk cost, the shelf-price ceiling and the season, remain outside its gift, and a mid-20s multiple already assumes the spread mends. The next test is concrete and datable: whether EBITDA margin recovers off FY26's ~5.9% through the FY27 flush as milk-procurement inflation eases and the VAP share keeps climbing. Widen the spread through a normal season and the multiple is earned; leave it squeezed by persistent milk inflation, and FY26 will read as the shape of a structurally tighter dairy rather than a passing trough.
