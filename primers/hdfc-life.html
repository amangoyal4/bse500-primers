00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

HDFC Life Insurance is one of India's largest and most respected private life insurers — born in 2000 as a joint venture between the then-mighty mortgage lender HDFC Ltd and Britain's Standard Life, and today a subsidiary of HDFC Bank, the country's biggest private bank. It consistently ranks in the top three private insurers and is prized in the market for two things: a reputation for clean, innovative product design, and privileged access to HDFC Bank's enormous branch network — the distribution engine that puts its policies in front of tens of millions of the bank's customers.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It sells a lot, at a good margin — but FY26 was a soft year. In FY26 (year to March 2026) HDFC Life collected roughly ₹77,760 Cr of total premium (+~12%) and wrote about ₹16,670 Cr of APE (+8%) — "annualised premium equivalent", the industry's true sales gauge. The lifetime profit on that new business (the VNB) was ₹4,034 Cr at a 24.2% margin — a margin that would have been ~25.5% but for two regulatory blows (a GST change and tougher surrender rules). Growth cooled after several stellar years.
  • The scorecard is embedded value, not accounting profit. Reported net profit was a modest ₹1,910 Cr — a poor gauge for a life insurer, which front-loads its costs (see §01–02). The number analysts actually track — embedded value, the worth of the whole book of policies — stood at ₹62,139 Cr, growing at an operating return (RoEV) of 15.0%.
  • The market pays up for it. At a market cap around ₹1.29 lakh Cr, HDFC Life trades at about 2.1× embedded value — the richest multiple among large listed private life insurers (SBI Life ~1.3×, ICICI Pru ~1.5×). Bulls call it deserved quality; bears note the premium sits atop a year of near-flat VNB.
In plain terms

Think of HDFC Life as a factory for savings-and-protection promises, plugged into India's premier private bank. When you visit an HDFC Bank branch for a loan, a deposit or to invest a bonus, the same relationship can sell you a life-insurance-cum-savings plan — and a large chunk of HDFC Life's business comes that way. Because it borrows the bank's reach and trusted brand, it can distribute policies cheaply and at scale. It collects premiums up front and pays claims decades later, investing the pool of money in between. The debate on the stock isn't about business quality — it is one of the best-run insurers in the country — it's about price: the market already pays a premium multiple for that pedigree, so the question is whether HDFC Life can re-accelerate growth (after a regulation-hit FY26) enough to justify it.

01

Life-insurance economics 101: how an insurer earns

No actuarial degree required — just five ideas that drive everything, and one big reason accounting profit misleads.

A life insurer is, at heart, a promise-and-float business. It collects premiums today in exchange for a promise to pay much later — on death, or when a savings policy matures — and it earns in two ways: from the margin baked into each policy it sells, and from investing the giant pool of premiums ("the float") while it waits to pay claims. Ordinary "profit" barely captures this, because a policy sold today produces cash flows for 20–40 years. So the industry built its own vocabulary. Five terms unlock the whole thing — with HDFC Life's FY26 numbers alongside each.

Why the ₹1,910 Cr "profit" understates the business

Here is the single most important idea for reading any life insurer. When HDFC Life sells a 20-year policy, it books the cost of writing it — commission, medical checks, setup — almost entirely up front, but the profit dribbles in slowly over the next two decades as the customer keeps paying. Under normal accounting, a fast-growing insurer therefore looks less profitable the harder it sells, because this year's growth loads this year's costs while the reward is deferred. That's why HDFC Life's reported net profit (₹1,910 Cr) is small and a poor guide. The industry fixes this with two purpose-built numbers that value the future cash flows properly: VNB (the lifetime value created by this year's sales) and embedded value (the value of the whole book). You judge a life insurer on those — not on the accountant's bottom line.

The two profit engines, simply

(1) Underwriting / new-business margin. Every policy is priced to earn a spread — the premium charged exceeds the expected cost of claims plus expenses plus any returns guaranteed to the customer. Bundle a year's sales together and the lifetime profit is the VNB; express it as a percentage of APE and you get the VNB margin (24.2% for HDFC Life — i.e., each ₹100 of "annualised" new business creates ₹24.2 of lifetime value). (2) The float. Because claims are paid years later, the insurer invests the premium pool in the meantime and keeps a slice of the return. The genius of the model: you collect the money up front and pay the promise decades later, so a growing insurer is perpetually cash-generative. The art is selling more policies, at higher margins, that stay on the books longer — and doing it at the lowest cost. Every section that follows is really about one of those levers.

You don't value a life insurer on this year's profit. You value the whole stack of future promises at once — which is exactly what "embedded value" and "VNB" are built to do, and why the market prices HDFC Life on P/EV, not P/E.
02

Embedded value & VNB — the real scorecard, and why P/EV

This is how insurers are actually judged — and it's the number the market attaches HDFC Life's premium multiple to.

If you only watch reported net profit, you'll misread HDFC Life. The industry uses two purpose-built numbers that capture future cash flows: Embedded Value (EV) — the worth of the whole existing book — and Value of New Business (VNB) with its margin — the worth of this year's sales. Because a life insurer's value is a stack of decades-long promises, the market prices it on a multiple of embedded value (P/EV), not a price-to-earnings ratio. HDFC Life's premium P/EV is the whole valuation debate.

Embedded value & P/EV, explained without jargon

Imagine you could fast-forward and collect every future profit HDFC Life will earn from policies already sold, discount it back to today's money, and add the company's net assets. That total is the embedded value — ₹62,139 Cr. Each year it should grow by the unwind of that discount (time passing brings future profits closer, so they're worth more today), plus the VNB you bolt on by selling new policies (₹4,034 Cr this year), then move with markets and any change in the actuaries' assumptions. So the health check is simple: is EV compounding at a healthy double-digit rate (RoEV), and is each year's new business added at a fat margin? And because the value lives in future promises, the market can't sensibly use a P/E ratio — it uses price ÷ embedded value (P/EV). HDFC Life trades at ~2.1× EV. That is the market saying "I'll pay ₹2.10 for every ₹1 of embedded value" — the highest such price in the sector, a bet that HDFC Life will keep adding VNB at a premium for years.

The blemish in FY26 was on the margin and on VNB growth. VNB rose just ~2% and the margin fell to 24.2% from 25.6%. The culprit was not a weakening franchise but two regulatory shifts: a GST change (from September 2025 the government cut GST on individual life products to nil — good for customers and long-term volumes, but insurers lost the input-tax credit on their own expenses, mechanically raising costs) and tougher surrender-value norms (which force insurers to pay more to early-exiting customers). Management flagged that ex those two effects, the VNB margin would have been ~25.5% and VNB would have grown broadly in line with APE. In other words, the FY26 dip is a policy artefact; the bull case rests on FY27 "normalising" as the GST change is fully absorbed.

03

The company and who controls it

From an HDFC Ltd–Standard Life JV, to an HDFC Bank subsidiary — with a big southern acquisition folded in along the way.

HDFC Life began in 2000 as a joint venture: the storied mortgage lender HDFC Ltd contributed its brand, capital and financial-services reach; Britain's Standard Life (later Standard Life Aberdeen / abrdn) contributed insurance and actuarial expertise. It listed in 2017, acquired and merged in Exide Life to deepen its southern and agency footprint, and — following the landmark 2023 merger of HDFC Ltd into HDFC Bank — is today controlled by HDFC Bank, India's largest private-sector bank.

Timeline

  • 2000

  • 2000s–2010s

  • Nov 2017

  • 2022
    Acquires Exide Life for ₹6,687 Cr and completes the merger (Oct 2022) — the sector's first-ever M&A, adding southern reach and an agency force; Exide Industries takes a ~4% stake.
  • Jul 2023
    HDFC Ltd merges into HDFC Bank — the parent restructuring makes HDFC Bank the promoter of HDFC Life; abrdn continues trimming its holding.
  • FY24–FY25
    Absorbs a wave of IRDAI reforms — new surrender-value norms (Oct 2024), expense-of-management rules — while pushing higher-margin protection and non-par to defend the VNB margin.
  • FY26
    Total premium ~₹77,760 Cr (+~12%), EV ₹62,139 Cr at 15.0% RoEV; VNB margin dips to 24.2% under the GST + surrender hit; retail protection +43%; HDFC Bank injects ₹1,000 Cr of fresh equity (preferential issue) to bolster solvency.

Ownership & management

  • The controlling owner: HDFC Bank ≈ 50.2% (the "promoter"). This is what gives HDFC Life its bancassurance distribution moat — access to the bank's branch network — and links its fortunes to India's premier private bank.
  • The original foreign partner: abrdn (ex Standard Life Aberdeen) has steadily sold down and is no longer a meaningful holder — its early expertise shaped the business, but it exited over time.
  • Public & institutional float: the balance (~50%) is a large, liquid free float — roughly FIIs ~22.5%, domestic institutions ~17.3% and the public ~10% — plus Exide Industries' residual ~4% stake.
  • Balance-sheet strength: solvency ratio ~177% against a 150% regulatory minimum — comfortably capitalised, and topped up by HDFC Bank's ₹1,000 Cr FY26 equity injection. Death-claim settlement ratio ~99.8%.
Why the HDFC Bank parentage matters: unlike SBI Life (controlled by a state-owned bank, which drags a "PSU discount" on its multiple), HDFC Life's promoter is a private, blue-chip bank — a big reason the stock enjoys a premium rather than discounted rating. The flip-side is dependence: much of HDFC Life's distribution runs through HDFC Bank's counters, so the relationship is both the moat and a concentration risk (see §04).
04

THE moat: HDFC Bank bancassurance & multi-channel reach

Every insurer sells the same abstract promise. The winner is whoever can put it in front of the most people, most cheaply. HDFC Bank's network is HDFC Life's answer.

"Bancassurance" simply means selling insurance through bank branches. When a customer walks into a bank for a deposit, a loan or to park a bonus, the same staff can offer a life-insurance-cum-savings plan across the counter — the customer is already there, already trusts the bank, and already has money in motion. It is the cheapest, highest-conversion channel in the industry. HDFC Life's promoter, HDFC Bank, runs one of India's largest branch networks — well over 9,000 branches and a vast, affluent customer base. Privileged access to that network is HDFC Life's core distribution advantage — and, crucially, HDFC Life has built a genuinely multi-channel model on top of it, so it leans less on a single bank than SBI Life does on SBI.

Why cheap distribution is a durable edge

  • Someone else pays for the shop-front. HDFC Bank already staffs, rents and runs its 9,000+ branches for its banking business. HDFC Life piggybacks on that footprint, so the marginal cost of putting a policy in front of a customer is a fraction of what a standalone insurer — which must recruit, train and pay a whole agency force — would spend.
  • The bank's customer base is affluent and pre-qualified. HDFC Bank skews toward salaried, urban, higher-income customers — exactly the demographic that buys protection and non-par savings. The bank knows the customer's balances and income, making the sale warmer and better-targeted than a cold agency pitch.
  • Trust and reach are pre-built. "HDFC" is one of India's most trusted financial brands. Selling a 20-year promise is a trust business; borrowing the parent's reputation is worth more than any advertising budget.
  • But HDFC Life doesn't rely on it alone. Post the Exide Life merger it scaled a large agency force (strong in tier-2/3 and southern markets) and a wide web of non-bank alliances (other banks, brokers, fintech partners) and a growing direct/digital channel. That diversification is a strategic hedge against over-dependence on any one counter.
The moat, in one line

An insurance policy is a commodity — the same abstract promise everyone sells. The lasting advantage is who can distribute it most cheaply and to the right customers, and HDFC Life has both a top-tier bank's branch network and a deliberately diversified set of other channels. That combination is why it is a durable top-three private insurer with a peer-leading brand. The dependency to keep in mind: the bancassurance slice is rented — it relies on the HDFC Bank relationship and on a regulatory regime that lets a bank sell through it (see §08). But because HDFC Life is more multi-channel than SBI Life, a shock to any single channel hurts it less.

A subtlety worth knowing: HDFC Bank is a "corporate agent" that may distribute for up to three life insurers — and it does sell some products of other group/associate insurers too. So HDFC Life does not have exclusive run of the bank's counters. In practice HDFC Life is the dominant life partner, but the relationship is commercial, not captive — a reminder that the moat is a privileged partnership rather than an owned asset.
05

Product mix & the VNB-margin engine

The same premium can carry a wildly different margin depending on what kind of policy it is. Mix is the master dial — and HDFC Life's is unusually balanced.

A life insurer's VNB margin is driven, more than anything, by what it sells. The product families carry very different economics, from thin-margin market-linked plans to fat-margin pure-protection cover. HDFC Life is known for one of the most balanced product portfolios in the industry — no single product dominates — which is a deliberate strategy to keep the margin stable and hedge product-cycle swings.

The margin lever, explained

Here's the mechanism. ULIPs and par plans dominate volume (~44% and ~25% of individual APE) because bank customers, walking in to save, gravitate to market-linked and traditional savings products — and ULIPs in particular are low-margin for the insurer. If that were the whole story, HDFC Life's VNB margin would be thin. Instead it runs at ~24% because the company deliberately grows the high-margin slivers faster: retail protection APE rose ~43% in FY26 and annuity is expanding. Every percentage point that shifts from ULIP toward protection, non-par or annuity lifts the blended margin. So the two things to watch each quarter are (1) is protection growing (the fattest-margin product, and where India is most under-insured), and (2) is the mix staying balanced rather than drifting toward low-margin ULIP when equity markets are hot. HDFC Life's edge here is breadth: a balanced book is more resilient across product cycles than a ULIP-heavy or non-par-heavy peer.

The honest tension — interest-rate risk on guarantees: pushing non-par savings and annuity (both high-margin) means selling long-dated guaranteed returns. The insurer must then earn more than it promised over decades, and if interest rates fall it faces "reinvestment risk" — future premiums and maturing bonds get reinvested at lower yields than the guarantee assumes. HDFC Life manages this with hedging and careful asset-liability matching, but a sharp, sustained fall in rates is a real risk to a guarantee-heavy insurer. It's the price of chasing the fat margins that non-par offers.
06

Persistency & cost ratios — the quiet quality signals

Two unglamorous ratios decide whether a policy actually makes money. They are the plumbing behind the VNB margin.

You can sell a policy at a great headline margin and still lose money on it — if the customer stops paying after a year or two (a "lapse"), or if you spend too much acquiring and servicing it. Two ratios capture this: persistency (do customers keep paying?) and the cost / expense ratio (how much of each premium is eaten by expenses?). They are exactly where the bancassurance model and disciplined underwriting earn their keep.

Why these two ratios decide the profit

Persistency matters because an insurer pays most of a policy's acquisition cost up front (big first-year commission and setup) and only recoups it if the customer keeps paying for years. A lapse in year two can turn a profitable-looking sale into a loss — which is why the actuaries' VNB assumes a certain persistency, and why a beat or miss on it moves embedded value. The cost ratio is the other half: the lower the expense per rupee of premium, the more of each premium survives as profit. HDFC Life's costs are structurally moderate because of bancassurance — it doesn't carry the full cost of a standalone agency force — though the FY26 GST change (loss of input-tax credit) pushed cost ratios up across the industry. Strong persistency plus disciplined costs is the machinery that produces a healthy VNB margin. These aren't headline numbers, but they are where the franchise quality becomes money.

07

Market share, growth & the under-insurance opportunity

A top-three private insurer, in a market that is one of the most under-insured on earth.

HDFC Life is a top-three private life insurer by individual weighted-received-premium (WRP), holding around 15% of the private market and roughly 11% of the total market (which includes state-owned LIC). But the bigger story is the size of the runway: India is one of the most under-insured large economies in the world, so the pie itself is set to grow for decades — and HDFC Life's brand and distribution position it to capture more than its share.

The under-insurance opportunity

  • India is dramatically under-insured. Life-insurance penetration (premiums as a % of GDP) is only around 3% — well under half the ~6–7% global average — and density (premium per person) is a small fraction of the global figure. Most Indians own far too little life cover.
  • The protection gap is measured in trillions. Swiss Re has estimated India's mortality protection gap at over US$16 trillion — the shortfall between the cover families need and what they actually hold. Closing even a slice of that is decades of pure-protection premium growth — and protection is HDFC Life's fastest-growing, highest-margin line (+43% in FY26).
  • Financialisation of savings. As Indian households shift from gold and property toward financial assets — insurance, mutual funds, pensions — a rising share of a growing savings pool flows to insurers. HDFC Life sold to 46 million+ lives in FY26, ~70% of them first-time buyers, a sign it is expanding the market, not just fighting over it.
  • Regulator tailwind: "Insurance for All by 2047." IRDAI's stated mission is universal insurance by India's centenary. Whatever the exact pace, the policy direction is firmly toward more coverage, more distribution, and a bigger addressable market.
Why the opportunity favours HDFC Life specifically

Under-insurance is an industry-wide tailwind — every insurer benefits. What makes it HDFC Life's opportunity is the combination of brand, product breadth and multi-channel reach. The protection gap is largest among younger, salaried, urban Indians who are exactly HDFC Bank's customers — and HDFC Life leads in protection design and sales. Meanwhile the Exide Life agency force extends reach into tier-2/3 and southern markets where standalone insurers struggle. Combine a structurally growing pie with a premier brand, a balanced product suite and a diversified distribution machine, and you have the shape of a multi-decade compounding story — the reason the market awards HDFC Life its premium multiple.

08

IRDAI, regulation & the rules that shape the margin

A heavily-regulated business where a rule change can move margins, product mix — and the value of the bancassurance channel itself. FY26 was a case study.

Life insurance in India is governed by the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI), which sets the rules on product design, surrender values, expense caps, commissions and how insurers may distribute. Regulation isn't background noise — it is a permanent swing factor, and FY26 showed exactly how a policy change can knock a couple of points off the VNB margin.

The rule changes that matter now

  • Surrender-value norms (effective Oct 2024). IRDAI now requires insurers to pay a higher "special surrender value" to customers who exit early — even after just one year's premium. This reduces the profit an insurer keeps on lapses, pressuring margins on savings products. HDFC Life absorbed this and still held a ~24% VNB margin — a sign of the model's resilience.
  • The GST change (effective Sept 2025). The government cut GST on individual life products to nil — cheaper for customers and good for long-term demand, but insurers lost the input-tax credit on their own expenses, mechanically raising cost ratios. This is the main reason HDFC Life's FY26 margin dipped from ~25.6% to a reported 24.2% (~25.5% ex-GST). Management expects FY27 to "normalise" as the change is fully absorbed.
  • Tax on high-ticket policies. Earlier Budget moves removed the tax-free maturity benefit on high-premium ULIPs and non-par savings (premiums above certain thresholds), nudging affluent buyers and dampening the very high-ticket savings sales that once padded volumes — a structural headwind for the savings-heavy part of the book.
  • Bancassurance, "open architecture" & FDI. The rules let a bank act as a corporate agent for up to three life insurers, and periodic mis-selling concerns keep alive the debate over forcing full "open architecture" (compelling bank branches to sell rivals' products too). Meanwhile 2025 legislative moves aimed at modernising insurance law, raising the FDI limit and exploring composite licences point to an expansionary, competitive direction of travel.
Why regulation is the swing factor

Two opposite forces are at work. On product economics — surrender values, GST, tax on high-ticket policies — regulation has been a headwind, nibbling at margins; HDFC Life's ability to hold ~24% through it demonstrates the franchise's strength, and the expectation is that FY27 recovers as one-offs wash out. On the channel, the stakes are higher. A large slice of HDFC Life's business flows through HDFC Bank's counters; if IRDAI ever mandated true open architecture — forcing bank branches to offer competing insurers alongside HDFC Life — the bancassurance advantage would erode. For now the regulator has not done so, and the broader "Insurance for All" agenda favours expanding coverage. But any investor in HDFC Life is, in part, taking a view that the bancassurance arrangement and the current regulatory settlement stay broadly intact.

The rule to watch: the FY26 margin dip proves how sensitive a life insurer's economics are to policy. The single biggest structural threat, though, is any move toward mandatory open architecture in bancassurance — it is the risk that, if it materialised, would matter more than any one quarter's numbers.
09

Financials & valuation

A high-quality franchise trading at the sector's richest multiple — after a year of near-flat VNB. That's the whole debate.

FY26 was, on the numbers, a digestion year: premium and AUM grew double-digits, but VNB was near-flat (+2%) and the margin dipped as GST and surrender-value changes bit. The oddity in the valuation: HDFC Life — a top-tier franchise but with only mid-teens RoEV — trades at the highest price-to-embedded-value multiple in the group, a premium built on brand, pedigree and a private-bank parent.

How to think about the valuation

At roughly ₹1.29 lakh Cr market cap against ₹62,139 Cr of embedded value, HDFC Life trades at about 2.1× EV — the dearest among the large private insurers, even though it compounds its book (15% RoEV) more slowly than SBI Life (~19.7%). The market is, in effect, paying the most per rupee of embedded value for a franchise whose growth just slowed to a crawl. Bulls justify it: HDFC Life has the best brand and product-innovation reputation, the most balanced book, a premier private-bank parent (no "PSU discount"), and FY26's weakness was regulatory noise that should reverse in FY27. Bears counter that a ~2.1× multiple on ~15% RoEV and +2% VNB is a lot to pay for "quality" — that the stock is priced for a re-acceleration that must actually materialise. Several brokerages trimmed estimates after the soft Q4 but see an FY27 recovery. The debate is entirely about whether HDFC Life re-accelerates enough to grow into its premium price.

On dividends & capital: HDFC Life declared a ₹2.10/share dividend for FY26. Like most growing life insurers, the payout is deliberately modest — capital is retained to fund new-business growth and hold a strong solvency buffer. Indeed HDFC Bank injected ₹1,000 Cr of fresh equity in FY26 to bolster solvency. The return to shareholders is meant to come chiefly through embedded-value compounding, not yield.

The industry and the rivals

An under-penetrated market, a dominant state incumbent, and a handful of well-run private challengers each leaning on a bank.

Indian life insurance is a two-tier market: state-owned LIC still writes over half of all premium (dominant in group and rural agency business), while a cluster of well-run private insurers — nearly all built on bank distribution — compete hard for the higher-value individual customer. HDFC Life is one of the top three in that private pack, alongside SBI Life and ICICI Prudential. The contrast between them is instructive.

The two premier bancassurance insurers are near mirror images. SBI Life has the bigger, cheaper machine — SBI's ~22,000 branches, the lowest cost ratio, the highest RoEV (~19.7%) and the fattest VNB margin (~27.5%) — yet trades at the lowest multiple (~1.3× EV) under a state-owned-parent discount. 11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A blue-chip franchise priced for quality — after a year that tested the growth story.

HDFC Life is the rare company where the business quality is close to unarguable — best-in-class brand, the most balanced product book, a premier private-bank parent, and a huge under-insured market ahead. The debate is entirely about price: the stock already carries the sector's richest multiple, and FY26 delivered near-flat VNB. The investment question is whether growth re-accelerates enough in FY27 and beyond to grow into that premium.

The bull case

  • Best-in-class franchise — top brand, top product-innovation reputation, the most balanced book in the industry, and a blue-chip private-bank parent (no PSU discount).
  • The HDFC Bank moat — privileged access to 9,000+ branches and an affluent customer base, plus a genuinely diversified agency/alliance/direct mix that reduces single-channel risk.
  • Protection is roaring — retail protection +43% in FY26, the highest-margin, most under-penetrated line; mix-shift lifts the blended margin over time.
  • FY26 weakness is regulatory noise — the margin dip was GST + surrender-rule one-offs; management guides to FY27 normalisation as they wash out.
  • Huge runway — India ~3% penetration, a >$16tn protection gap, financialisation and "Insurance for All by 2047" — decades of expansion for a premier, multi-channel player.

The bear case

  • Priced for perfection — ~2.1× EV, the sector's dearest, on only mid-teens RoEV (~15%) and +2% VNB growth; a lot to pay if the re-acceleration disappoints.
  • Lower returns than the cheaper peer — SBI Life compounds EV faster (~19.7% RoEV) and at a fatter margin, yet trades at ~1.3×; HDFC Life must earn its premium.
  • Regulatory nibbling — surrender-value norms, the GST/input-credit change and tax on high-ticket policies have all pressured margins; more reform can follow.
  • Rented moat / open-architecture risk — a big slice of distribution runs through HDFC Bank; any move to mandatory open architecture would erode the bancassurance edge.
  • Guarantee & market risk — a growing non-par/annuity book carries interest-rate/reinvestment risk, and ULIP sales remain sensitive to equity-market sentiment.
The bottom line

HDFC Life is a blue-chip franchise wearing a premium price tag. The business questions are largely settled — it is, and will likely remain, a top-three private life insurer with the industry's best brand, the most balanced book, and a premier private-bank distribution partner. The investment question is narrower and sharper than SBI Life's: not "will the discount close?" but "is the premium deserved?" Watch three things each year — VNB growth & margin (does FY27 re-accelerate as GST washes out and protection keeps compounding back toward ~25%+?), RoEV (can it push mid-teens toward the high-teens of SBI Life?), and any change to bancassurance rules (does the moat stay intact?). Get those right and the premium multiple is justified; if growth stays stuck in single digits, a ~2.1× EV rating on mid-teens returns is the most vulnerable in the sector to a de-rating.
