00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Carborundum Universal — everyone calls it CUMI — makes the consumable tools that shape other things. Its grinding wheels, sandpapers and polishing products cut, grind, smooth and finish metal, glass, stone, wood and ceramics in virtually every factory, workshop, auto plant, foundry and steel mill. It also makes technical ceramics (wear-resistant, refractory and defence parts) and, crucially, the raw fused minerals — silicon carbide and fused alumina — that go into abrasives and ceramics. It is a Murugappa group company, headquartered in Chennai, and one of the largest players of its kind in India.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It sells "razor blades", not "razors". An abrasive is used up in the process — a grinding wheel wears down, sandpaper loads up and gets thrown away. So CUMI earns a recurring, low-ticket, repeat-purchase revenue stream tied to how busy Indian (and world) manufacturing is, rather than one-off capital-equipment sales. That gives it broad, diversified, industrial-activity-linked demand — but also cyclicality.
  • It is backward-integrated into its own raw material. CUMI doesn't just buy silicon carbide and alumina — it fuses them itself in electric-arc furnaces (a legacy of its founding). That in-house mineral supply is the cost-and-quality moat under the whole group, and it sells the surplus to other abrasive and refractory makers worldwide.
  • Two profit stories live in one company right now. In FY26 the India (standalone) business had a great year — PAT up ~29% to ₹416 Cr — while the consolidated group profit fell to ~₹168 Cr, dragged down by ~₹135 Cr of one-time charges from shutting/impairing overseas units and by its sanctions-hit Russian subsidiary. The gap between those two numbers is the overseas problem — and closing it is the whole near-term story.
In plain terms

If a factory anywhere makes a metal part, that part almost certainly gets ground, deburred, polished or sharpened at some stage — and the abrasive that does it wears away and has to be rebought. CUMI is the company that keeps selling those consumables, plus the industrial ceramics that line furnaces and armour vehicles, plus the fused minerals both are made from. It is unglamorous, diversified and defensively spread across dozens of end-industries — with one modern twist: the very mineral it has fused for grinding wheels for seventy years, silicon carbide, is now the hot material for electric-vehicle power chips. So a boring consumables maker is sitting on a genuinely interesting materials option.

01

The picks-and-shovels idea: what an abrasive actually is

No engineering degree required — just why "grit that wears out" is a good business.

Almost nothing comes out of a mould or a casting perfectly finished. Metal parts have rough edges, welds, burrs and surfaces that must be made smooth, flat, sharp or dimensionally exact. The tool that does this is an abrasive — hard mineral grains (grit) bonded onto a wheel, a belt, a disc or a paper. Press the grit against the workpiece, and it shaves material away. Every engine crankshaft, every steel sheet, every knife edge, every polished granite countertop, every semiconductor wafer passes an abrasive somewhere.

The commercial beauty is that the abrasive is a consumable. A grinding wheel grinds itself away; a sanding belt dulls and is replaced; a cut-off disc shrinks with every cut. The customer keeps coming back to rebuy — the classic "razor-and-blades" economics, except CUMI mostly just sells the blades. Demand therefore tracks the running of the economy, not the building of it: as long as factories, foundries, auto plants and construction sites are active, abrasives get consumed.

Why "consumable" changes the whole risk profile

A company that sells a ₹5-crore machine makes that sale once a decade; a company that sells the grinding wheels the machine chews through makes a sale every few weeks, to thousands of customers, across dozens of industries. No single customer or sector dominates, orders are small and frequent, and the product is genuinely necessary rather than discretionary. That is a structurally steadier, more diversified demand base than most capital-goods businesses — the trade-off being that it is still tied to overall factory utilisation, so a broad industrial slowdown quietly trims volumes across the board.

02

Three segments, one chain

Abrasives, Ceramics and Electrominerals — and the vertical integration that ties them together.

CUMI reports in three segments, and the single most important thing to grasp is that they are not three unrelated businesses stapled together — they are a supply chain the company owns end to end. Electrominerals make the raw grit; Abrasives and Ceramics turn that grit into finished products; and whatever grit isn't consumed internally is sold to the rest of the world.

CUMI starts with raw minerals (bauxite, petroleum coke, quartz), fuses them into abrasive grains in its own furnaces, then either uses those grains to make abrasives and ceramics — or sells them onward to rival manufacturers who lack their own mineral base.

Owning Stage 1 is the moat: CUMI controls the cost, quality and supply of the one input every downstream product depends on — and monetises the leftover externally instead of letting a competitor supply it.
Why owning the mineral matters more than it looks

Most abrasive makers buy their silicon carbide and alumina from someone else — often, historically, from CUMI itself or from a handful of global mineral producers. CUMI fuses its own. That does three things: it insulates the downstream businesses from grit price spikes and shortages; it lets the company tune grain quality to exactly what its wheels and ceramics need; and it turns a cost centre into a third profit centre by selling surplus minerals externally. The catch is that fusing minerals is power-hungry and cyclical — electrominerals margins swing with energy costs and with the global commodity price of SiC and alumina, which is why this segment is the most volatile of the three.

03

The company and who controls it

A 1954 three-way joint venture that the Murugappa group turned into a global materials firm.

CUMI's name is its history. It was born in 1954 as a tripartite joint venture between the Chennai-based Murugappa group, The Carborundum Company of the USA, and the Universal Grinding Wheel Company of the UK — hence "Carborundum Universal". ("Carborundum" is itself the old trade name for silicon carbide.) The foreign partners eventually exited over the decades; Murugappa consolidated control and built CUMI from an abrasives licensee into a vertically integrated, multinational materials group.

Timeline

  • 1954
    Incorporated in Madras as a JV of the Murugappa group + Carborundum Co. (USA) + Universal Grinding Wheel (UK) — an abrasives manufacturer under foreign licence.
  • 1960s–80s
    Builds backward integration: sets up its own electrominerals capacity (silicon carbide and fused alumina), so it makes the grit that goes into its wheels rather than importing it.
  • 1990s–2000s
    Murugappa steadily buys out the foreign partners and takes full control; CUMI diversifies from abrasives into industrial and technical ceramics, refractories and super-abrasives.
  • 2007
    Global push: acquires Volzhsky Abrasive Works in Russia (a large silicon-carbide and abrasives plant) and Foskor Zirconia in South Africa; also builds out China and other overseas operations. CUMI becomes a multinational.
  • 2010s
    Deepens technical ceramics, super-abrasives and electronics-grade minerals; positions the mineral business for higher-value, higher-purity products beyond commodity grit.
  • 2022
    Russia–Ukraine war and Western sanctions turn the Volzhsky subsidiary into a standing overhang — banking, logistics and currency friction, even though abrasives themselves aren't sanctioned goods.
  • FY26
    Cleans up the overseas portfolio — winds down Awuko Abrasives (Germany) and impairs Foskor Zirconia (South Africa), a ~₹135 Cr exceptional charge — while the India business posts record standalone profit. Lays out its "Aspiration 2030" plan, leaning into high-purity silicon carbide, graphene and nitrides.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter: the Murugappa group holds roughly ~39%, mainly through Ambadi Investments (~29%). Murugappa is one of India's oldest and most respected industrial houses (Tube Investments, Cholamandalam Finance, Coromandel International, EID Parry, Wendt India), known for conservative, long-horizon stewardship — a reassuring backer for a cyclical materials business.
  • Institutional base: domestic institutions (DIIs) hold a large slug (~29–30%) and foreign investors (~10–11%), reflecting CUMI's status as a quality mid-cap compounder that funds and index managers like to own.
  • Balance sheet: effectively net cash / low-debt at the group level (a modest ~₹400 Cr of gross debt against far larger reserves), which lets it self-fund capex and absorb overseas shocks without stress.
A useful lens on Murugappa ownership: the group's culture is famously patient and low-drama — it tends to hold assets for decades and reinvest steadily rather than chase quarters. For a business whose payoff (backward integration, then a high-purity-SiC option) plays out over many years, that ownership horizon is a genuine, if intangible, asset.
04

Segment one: Abrasives

The largest business, the broadest customer base, the truest "industrial-activity" proxy.

Abrasives are CUMI's biggest segment — roughly 43% of consolidated revenue — and the one closest to the "picks and shovels" idea. It spans the full range: bonded grinding wheels, coated sandpapers and belts, thin cut-off wheels, and the premium super-abrasives (diamond and CBN) for the hardest, most precise jobs. Brand names like Ajax grinding wheels and the coated-abrasives lines are fixtures on Indian shop floors.

Who buys abrasives

  • Auto & auto components — grinding crankshafts, camshafts, gears, bearings; deburring and finishing thousands of machined parts.
  • Steel & metals — conditioning slabs and billets, cutting and grinding in steel mills.
  • General engineering & fabrication — the long tail of workshops, tool rooms and job shops that cut and finish metal every day.
  • Construction & DIY / retail — cutting discs, hand sanding, stone and tile finishing.
  • Precision & electronics — super-abrasives for carbide tools, glass and high-tolerance components.

Because the customer base is so wide, abrasives revenue is one of the cleaner proxies for broad manufacturing activity in India. There is no dominant customer and no single cyclical end-market that can sink the segment on its own — but there is also no hiding from a genuine industrial downturn, when utilisation falls across the board and everyone consumes less grit.

The domestic tailwind: import substitution & "China+1"

Two structural forces work in CUMI's favour at home. First, India's manufacturing push (PLI schemes, capex revival, defence and rail localisation) lifts factory activity — and therefore abrasive consumption. Second, abrasives and technical minerals are exactly the kind of unglamorous industrial input that global buyers now want sourced outside China. CUMI, with its own mineral base and decades of process know-how, is well placed to win both the domestic replacement demand and a slice of that "China+1" reshuffling — a quieter, more durable tailwind than any single order win.

05

Segment two: Ceramics

The smallest segment by revenue — and, in a genuine twist, the biggest by profit.

CUMI's Ceramics segment is the most surprising of the three. It is the smallest by sales (~23%) but consistently the largest single contributor to segment profit — in recent years it has thrown off close to half of CUMI's total EBIT. This is not commodity crockery; it is high-value industrial and technical ceramics, where hardness, wear-resistance, heat-resistance and precision command premium prices.

What's inside "Ceramics"

  • Industrial ceramics — precision alumina and technical-ceramic components used in machinery, pumps, textiles and process industries where metal parts would wear out too fast.
  • Wear-resistant ceramics — linings and tiles that protect chutes, pipes, mills and hoppers handling abrasive materials (cement, mining, power) — they outlast steel many times over.
  • Refractories — heat-resistant linings for furnaces, kilns and metallurgical vessels; the ceramics that let steel and metal plants run at extreme temperatures.
  • Metallised / anti-ballistic & defence ceramics — high-hardness ceramics used in body and vehicle armour and specialised industrial applications — a small but high-value, strategically interesting niche.
Why ceramics carries the margin

Abrasives and raw minerals are, at their core, volume-and-cost businesses competing partly on price. Technical ceramics are a specification-and-engineering business: a customer designing a pump seal, a furnace lining or an armour tile qualifies a supplier over years, values reliability over price, and doesn't switch casually. That stickiness — plus genuine materials know-how — is why Ceramics earns fatter margins than its size implies, and why CUMI's push into higher-end technical and defence ceramics matters more to profit than to the top line.

06

Segment three: Electrominerals & the integration moat

The furnace business at the base of everything — and where the SiC story lives.

Electrominerals (~31% of revenue) is the least visible but most foundational segment: it is where CUMI makes the raw fused grit itself. Using enormous electric-arc furnaces, it fuses raw minerals — silica/quartz and petroleum coke into silicon carbide (SiC), and bauxite into fused / white aluminium oxide. These are the abrasive and refractory grains that feed the other two segments and that CUMI also sells to abrasive and refractory makers around the world.

The two workhorse minerals

What CUMI fuses, and what it's for
MineralMade fromUsed for
Silicon carbide (SiC)Silica/quartz + petroleum coke, fused at extreme heatVery hard abrasive grit; refractories; wear ceramics — and, increasingly, the base material for power semiconductors (see Part 07).
Fused / white aluminaBauxite (aluminium ore), electrically fusedThe workhorse grit in grinding wheels and sandpaper; refractory and technical-ceramic feedstock.

This is the segment that makes CUMI backward-integrated: it doesn't depend on outside suppliers for the one input every downstream product needs. That is a real cost-and-security advantage — but it comes with the segment's defining weakness. Fusing minerals consumes vast amounts of electricity, so profitability is highly sensitive to power costs and to raw-material prices (bauxite, petroleum coke). And because a large share of fused minerals trades as a global commodity, electrominerals earnings swing with world SiC/alumina prices — the most cyclical, least predictable line in the group.

One number that captures the integration logic

Roughly speaking, the grit CUMI fuses in Stage 1 becomes the abrasives and ceramics it sells in Stage 2, and whatever is left over is sold as merchant minerals in Stage 3. So a single furnace campaign can end up as a grinding wheel in a Pune auto plant, a furnace lining in a steel mill, and a bag of grit shipped to a rival abrasive maker in Europe — three different margins from one input. That is the practical meaning of vertical integration: the same tonne of silicon carbide gets three chances to earn.

07

The silicon-carbide call hidden inside a grinding-wheel company

The one part of this business a generalist should not skim — because it is where a "boring" stock owns a genuine EV-and-semiconductor option.

Here is the fact that should make you sit up. Silicon carbide — the same "carborundum" CUMI has fused for grinding wheels since the 1950s — has quietly become one of the most strategically prized materials in electronics. In its high-purity, crystalline form, SiC is the base for power semiconductors: the chips that manage high voltages in electric-vehicle inverters, on-board chargers, fast-charging stations, solar inverters and industrial power devices. SiC power chips switch faster, run hotter and waste less energy than ordinary silicon — which is exactly what EVs need to extend range and shrink charging time.

So a company most investors file under "abrasives" is one of the few Indian firms with decades of hands-on silicon-carbide manufacturing know-how and installed furnace capacity. The grade CUMI has historically made — metallurgical / abrasive-grade SiC — is not the same as the ultra-pure, single-crystal, semiconductor-grade material that goes into a wafer. The distance between the two is large and hard to cross. But the starting point — the process knowledge, the raw-material access, the furnaces — is something almost no other Indian company has.

What CUMI is actually doing about it

  • Under its "Aspiration 2030" plan, CUMI has explicitly flagged advanced materials — high-purity silicon carbide (HPSiC), graphene and nitrides — as strategic growth vectors, and talked about serving adjacent electronics areas such as wafer-fab consumables and electronic substrates.
  • It remains net-cash and free-cash-generative, which is what lets it fund long-gestation R&D and capacity (capex of ~₹309 Cr in FY26, ~₹400 Cr planned for FY27) without betting the balance sheet.
  • The prize is the difference between selling SiC as a commodity abrasive at commodity prices versus selling higher-purity SiC into electronics at a large multiple of that — a step up in both margin and strategic relevance.
Keep expectations honest. This is an option, not a shipping product line. Semiconductor-grade SiC wafers are a fiercely competitive global business dominated by large specialists, and full qualification is a multi-year, capital-intensive road with no guarantee CUMI reaches the highest-value tier. Treat it as embedded upside that costs little to hold — not as a number in this year's model.
The non-transferable way to frame it

Most "EV plays" are companies that would have to build a silicon-carbide capability from scratch to participate. CUMI is the rare case that would have to climb, not start — it already fuses the mineral, it already runs the furnaces, it already sells SiC globally; the task is moving up the purity curve from grinding-wheel grit toward electronics grade. That is a fundamentally different, and cheaper, starting position than a greenfield entrant — and it is precisely the sentence that could not be written about a Grindwell Norton or a 3M.

08

The overseas map — and the Russian anchor

The single biggest reason the India story and the group story diverged in FY26.

CUMI is not just an Indian company — it runs a global footprint, and that footprint is where its recent pain has come from. The standout asset (and liability) is Volzhsky Abrasive Works (VAW) in Russia, acquired in 2007 — a large silicon-carbide and abrasives plant, historically one of the more significant SiC producers globally, that exports a big share of its output to Europe. Around it sit smaller overseas units in South Africa, Germany, China, Australia, North America and elsewhere.

Since 2022, the Russia exposure has been a running overhang. The products themselves aren't sanctioned (abrasives and SiC aren't dual-use goods), so VAW keeps producing — but the banking, payment, shipping and reputational friction of running a large Russian subsidiary through a sanctions regime is real, and it comes bundled with ruble volatility and the risk that European customers de-source Russian-origin material. It is the definition of a discount-worthy asset: valuable, cash-generative, and permanently clouded.

The FY26 clean-up — and the wedge it created

In FY26 CUMI took the knife to the weaker overseas assets: it decided to wind down Awuko Abrasives (Germany) and wrote down Foskor Zirconia (South Africa), booking a combined ~₹135 Cr exceptional charge. Layer that on top of the Russia drag, and you get the year's defining oddity:

The wedge — the analytical device for this company

Look at the two profit numbers side by side. The India business earned ~₹416 Cr and grew ~29%; the whole group earned only ~₹168 Cr. That gap — roughly a quarter of a billion rupees of "missing" profit — is almost entirely the overseas portfolio: Russia's friction plus the one-time write-offs. Read the right way, that is oddly encouraging: it means the core Indian franchise is compounding underneath a foreign anchor, and the group's headline weakness is a clean-up cost, not a rot in the business you'd actually want to own. The bear reading is equally clean: those overseas assets were bought with shareholder money, and unwinding them is a real, recurring reminder that CUMI's global adventure has been a mixed bag.

What to watch: whether the FY26 clean-up is the end of the overseas drag or a stage in a longer de-risking. If Awuko and Foskor Zirconia are the last of it and Russia stabilises, the group and standalone profit lines should start converging again — and that convergence, more than any single order, is the near-term proof the strategy is working.
09

The industry and the rivals

A consolidated Indian abrasives market, global majors above it, and one very close listed peer.

The Indian abrasives market is reasonably consolidated at the top: CUMI and Grindwell Norton together dominate the organised space, with a long tail of smaller and unorganised players below. Above them sit the global majors — Saint-Gobain, 3M, Tyrolit and others — who compete in premium and specialised segments. On the electrominerals and SiC side, CUMI's competitors are global mineral and, increasingly, semiconductor-materials specialists.

CUMI vs Grindwell — the peer comparison that matters

The sharpest mirror for CUMI is Grindwell Norton, and the contrast is instructive. Grindwell is the cleaner business — tighter, more consistent margins, no Russia, no overseas write-offs, backed by Saint-Gobain's technology — and the market rewards it with steady, high-quality returns. CUMI is the broader and lumpier one: more segments, its own mineral base, a global footprint that has cost it lately, and the SiC option Grindwell doesn't have. Simplifying: Grindwell is the purer play on India's abrasives demand; CUMI is that plus a mineral-integration and semiconductor-materials angle, wrapped in more volatility. Which you prefer depends on whether you're paying for consistency or for optionality.

Financials & valuation

A quality compounder underneath — currently wearing a bruised headline.

CUMI's long-term record is that of a steady industrial compounder: consolidated revenue has grown at a low-double-digit CAGR over a decade, crossing ₹5,000 Cr in FY26. But FY26's profit line is the crux — it fell sharply at the group level even as India thrived, because of the overseas charges. Reading the two profit lines correctly is the whole valuation debate.

How to read the multiple — and the metric that settles it

On the depressed consolidated FY26 profit, CUMI screens at a very high P/E (in the high-70s to triple digits) — but that ratio is distorted, because the denominator was hit by one-time charges. On the standalone India earnings of ~₹416 Cr against a ~₹20,500 Cr market cap, the multiple is closer to the ~45–50× range — still a clear premium, the kind of price a market pays for a Murugappa-quality compounder with an embedded SiC option. The number that will actually settle the thesis is not the headline P/E but the reconvergence of consolidated PAT toward standalone PAT — i.e. the overseas book turning from a drag into a contributor — plus concrete progress on high-purity SiC and steady segment margins (especially Ceramics holding its EBIT lead). Get those, and the premium is earned by rising quality; miss them, and the "cheap standalone / expensive consolidated" gap simply persists.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A defensively diversified industrial franchise with one clean-up to finish and one option to prove.

Strip it back and CUMI is a high-quality, vertically integrated maker of the consumables and materials that Indian manufacturing runs on, controlled by a patient owner, with a genuine — if unproven — call on the silicon-carbide-for-electronics theme. The near-term debate is about the overseas clean-up and the premium price; the long-term debate is about how far up the value chain the SiC know-how can climb.

The bull case

  • Consumable, diversified demand — razor-blade economics tied to broad industrial activity, no single-customer risk, riding India's manufacturing and "China+1" tailwinds.
  • Vertical integration moat — owns its SiC/alumina mineral base; cost, quality and supply security no pure abrasive rival has.
  • Ceramics margin engine — the smallest segment earns the most profit, and the technical/defence ceramics mix is climbing.
  • The SiC option — decades of silicon-carbide know-how and furnaces as a low-cost starting point for high-purity, electronics-grade material.
  • Clean India business + strong owner — standalone PAT +29%, net-cash balance sheet, Murugappa stewardship.

The bear case

  • Cyclicality — abrasives and minerals volumes fall with any real industrial slowdown; electrominerals margins swing with power and commodity prices.
  • The Russia anchor — Volzhsky brings sanctions friction, ruble risk and reputational overhang that won't fully clear soon.
  • A patchy overseas record — the FY26 Awuko/Foskor write-offs are a reminder that CUMI's global acquisitions have underdelivered.
  • Premium valuation — even on clean India earnings the stock is richly priced; the SiC option is partly in the price but far from proven.
  • Cleaner alternative exists — Grindwell Norton offers the abrasives exposure with steadier margins and none of the overseas mess.
The bottom line

CUMI is two things at once wearing one ticker: a quietly excellent Indian materials compounder and a lumpy global portfolio it is still tidying up. The India franchise is doing the compounding; the overseas book is doing the drag; and underneath both sits a silicon-carbide capability that could, one day, matter far more than any grinding wheel. The next real milestones are concrete and namable: the full exit of the Awuko/Foskor loss-makers and stabilisation of Volzhsky (so group profit reconverges with India's), and the first credible qualification of high-purity, electronics-grade SiC. Until those land, you are buying a good business at a full price with a free option attached — and watching to see whether the grit that grinds India's factories can also end up inside its electric cars.
