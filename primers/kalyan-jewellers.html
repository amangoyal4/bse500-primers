00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Kalyan Jewellers sells gold, diamond and other precious jewellery through large branded showrooms — the kind of trusted, hallmarked, fixed-price store that Indian families increasingly prefer over the local family jeweller for a wedding purchase or a festival buy. It is one of India's largest organized jewellery retailers, with 507 showrooms as of March 2026 across India, the Middle East, the US and the UK, plus its online brand Candere. Only Titan's Tanishq is clearly larger among listed peers.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It rides the single biggest structural theme in Indian jewellery. India's ~US$90 billion jewellery market is still ~60–65% unorganized (thousands of local family jewellers), but hallmarking, GST and consumers' hunger for trust are steadily handing share to branded chains. Organized share has climbed from ~5% two decades ago to ~35–38% and is expected to reach ~60% by ~CY29 — a multi-decade tailwind that Kalyan is a prime beneficiary of.
  • The business just changed shape — and the market noticed. Kalyan shifted new-store expansion to an asset-light "FOCO" franchise model (the franchisee funds the store and stock; Kalyan runs it and takes a cut). By March 2026, 222 of its 342 India Kalyan showrooms ran on FOCO. This let it expand fast with far less of its own capital — lifting returns on capital and helping re-rate the stock.
  • Explosive but capital-light growth. FY26 revenue rose 43% to ₹35,743 Cr and net profit jumped 89% to ₹1,350 Cr, on strong same-store growth (Q1 FY26 SSSG ~18%), a richer studded (diamond) mix (~31%), and the franchise-fee stream. The debate is now about a high gold price, a still-thin margin, and a valuation (~29× earnings) that already prices in a lot of the runway.
In plain terms

Think of Kalyan as a trusted brand-name jewellery shop, scaled nationally. In India, gold jewellery is not a luxury impulse — it's a wedding necessity, a festival ritual and a store of family savings, and buying it is an act of trust (is the gold pure? is the weight honest? is the diamond real?). For decades that trust lived with the neighbourhood jeweller. Kalyan's bet is that families now want that trust branded, hallmarked and guaranteed — and it is building the showrooms, the buyback promises and the transparency to win them. The clever twist is who pays for the new stores: increasingly, a local franchisee funds the shop and the ₹40–50 crore of gold inside it, while Kalyan lends its brand, runs the store, and earns a margin — so it grows fast without tying up all its own cash in gold.

01

Jewellery-retail economics 101: how a jeweller earns

No trading desk required — just the handful of numbers that drive a gold shop.

A jewellery retailer looks like a shop but behaves like a working-capital machine wrapped around a commodity. Most of the ticket price a customer pays is simply the value of the gold — which the jeweller does not profit from. The real money is made on a few specific layers on top. Get these five ideas and the whole business model clicks.

The five levers of a jewellery P&L
LeverWhat it means (and why it matters)
Gold value (pass-through)The bulk of the bill. The jeweller buys gold, makes it into jewellery, and passes the metal cost straight to the customer. It is not profit — a rising gold price inflates revenue without adding much margin.
Making chargesA charge for craftsmanship — a % of the gold value or a per-gram rate. This is a core margin layer and the jeweller's pricing power in plain gold.
Studded (diamond) mixDiamond / gemstone jewellery carries far higher margins than plain gold. The share of studded in the sales mix (~31% for Kalyan) is the single biggest margin lever.
Same-store sales growth (SSSG)Growth from existing stores (not new openings). High SSSG (Kalyan ~18% in Q1 FY26) means the base is healthy and operating leverage is kicking in.
Working capital / inventoryEvery showroom must be stocked with crores of gold before it sells a gram. Jewellery retail is inventory-heavy; how that inventory is funded (own cash, gold loans, or a franchisee's cash) shapes the returns on capital.
Why "revenue up 43%" doesn't mean "43% richer"

Because gold is a pass-through, a jeweller's reported revenue is hostage to the gold price. When gold rallies hard (as it did into FY26), the rupee value of every sale rises, so headline revenue jumps even if the number of grams sold grows more modestly. That's why analysts watch volume growth, SSSG, and the studded mix — not just the top line. Kalyan's real story in FY26 was strong genuine demand (weddings, festivals) plus a gold-price tailwind on the reported figure. The margin, meanwhile, is thin — an EBITDA margin of only ~7% — because most of the "sale" is just gold changing hands. A jeweller makes its money on a small slice of a very large number.

The margin looks tiny — so where's the return?

A ~7% EBITDA margin and a ~3.8% net margin sound underwhelming until you remember the second engine: capital turns. If a store can sell its gold inventory two-to-three times a year and the making-charge-plus-studded margin drops to the bottom line each time, a low margin still compounds into a healthy return on capital employed — which is exactly why the way Kalyan funds its inventory (see §04–05) matters more than the raw margin number.

02

The formalization wave — unorganized to organized

The defining structural force for Kalyan — the tailwind an analyst leads with.

India buys more gold jewellery than almost anywhere on earth — but it has historically bought it from the corner family jeweller. The single most important thing to understand about Kalyan is that this is changing, and Kalyan is one of the biggest winners of the change. Share is migrating, slowly but relentlessly, from thousands of unorganized local shops to a handful of trusted, hallmarked, branded chains.

Even at ~35–38% today, the unorganized segment is still the majority of the market — meaning the branded chains are taking share from a pool larger than their own. That is the multi-year runway.

Why the shift is happening now — the four forces

  • Mandatory hallmarking. The government now requires gold jewellery to carry a hallmark and a unique HUID (a tamper-proof purity ID). Compliance is easy for organized chains (already >95%) but a real burden for small unorganized shops. It levels the "is this gold pure?" question in favour of brands. Over 40 crore items have been HUID-tagged across 361 districts.
  • GST & formal invoicing. Since GST (3% on gold, 5% on making charges) and PAN requirements on larger purchases, the informal cash economy that many small jewellers relied on has shrunk — pushing customers toward compliant branded stores that give a proper bill.
  • Trust & transparency. Younger, urban buyers want fixed prices, transparent making charges, certified diamonds, and a buyback / exchange guarantee — things a national brand can promise and honour at scale.
  • Weddings & the big-ticket nature of the purchase. A bridal set can cost several lakh rupees. For the biggest purchases, families increasingly want the reassurance of a brand — precisely where Kalyan positions itself.
Why formalization matters more than the gold price

A jeweller can't control the price of gold — but a branded jeweller can ride a structural transfer of market share. Even if the total jewellery market grew only with GDP, Kalyan (and Tanishq, Senco and others) could grow far faster simply by eating the unorganized segment's share. That's the crux of the bull case: the addressable pie for organized players roughly doubles as organized share moves from ~35% toward ~60%, independent of how many grams India buys in total. Formalization is a demand-side tailwind that lasts a decade or more, and it is why every branded chain is racing to plant showrooms in tier-2 and tier-3 towns before the local jeweller's grip loosens.

03

The company and who controls it

From one Thrissur store in 1993 to a pan-India, family-run chain.

Kalyan is a family-founded, family-run business. T. S. Kalyanaraman opened the first Kalyan Jewellers showroom in Thrissur, Kerala in 1993, building on the family's textile-retail roots. His sons Rajesh and Ramesh Kalyanaraman (both Executive Directors) run the business today. The company scaled across South India, then nationally, took private-equity backing, and listed in 2021.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Kalyanaraman family holds ~62.9% — a high, aligned promoter stake. Founder T. S. Kalyanaraman is Chairman & Managing Director; sons Rajesh and Ramesh Kalyanaraman are Executive Directors driving strategy and expansion.
  • Institutional pedigree: Warburg Pincus was a pre-IPO backer (and has been paring down post-listing); the float now carries a broad set of domestic and foreign institutions, reflecting the market's re-rating of the story.
  • Balance sheet: unlike a debt-free hospital chain, a jeweller needs financing for inventory. Kalyan carries borrowings of ~₹6,100 Cr (much of it gold-metal-loan and working-capital debt), and has been actively de-leveraging — targeting a non-gold-loan (non-GML) debt-free India business.
04

The FOCO model — the re-rating engine

The strategic shift that changed how Kalyan grows — and how the market values it.

If you only understand one company-specific thing about Kalyan, make it this. Around FY23–24 Kalyan changed the way it opens new showrooms — from funding them itself to a franchise structure called FOCO: Franchise-Owned, Company-Operated. This one change let it expand far faster on far less of its own capital, and is the biggest single reason the stock re-rated.

Why FOCO re-rated the stock

A jewellery chain's Achilles heel is capital intensity: every new store needs crores of gold sitting on the shelf, so growth normally eats cash and depresses returns on capital. FOCO breaks that link. By having franchisees fund the inventory, Kalyan can open dozens of stores a year without proportionally ballooning its own balance sheet — so revenue and profit grow while capital employed grows more slowly. That's the definition of rising return on capital, and it's what turned Kalyan from "a decent jeweller with heavy working capital" into "a capital-light, fast-compounding franchise" in the market's eyes. The company reported FY26 return on capital employed of ~28.8% on its own basis (broader screens put it nearer ~20%); either way, the trajectory is up, and FOCO is why.

The trade-off to watch: FOCO shares economics with the franchisee, so a FOCO rupee of sales earns Kalyan less than an owned-store rupee. The model boosts return on capital and rollout speed, but can dilute headline margin mix and adds dependence on franchisee relationships and financing. If franchisee funding or gold prices tighten, the expansion engine can slow. The Middle East already saw some FOCO stores temporarily converted back to company-operated (COCO) when investor arrangements needed reworking.
05

Gold, hedging & the working-capital machine

The one thing a good jeweller does NOT do: bet on the gold price.

Because gold is the dominant input, a naïve worry is that Kalyan is really a leveraged bet on the gold price. It is not — and understanding why is essential. A well-run jeweller hedges its gold, it does not speculate on it. The tool that makes this work is the Gold Metal Loan (GML).

How a Gold Metal Loan hedges the price

  • Borrow gold, not rupees. Instead of borrowing cash to buy gold (and then owning a pile of metal whose price swings daily), Kalyan borrows the physical gold itself from a bank under a GML — it takes delivery of, say, 100 kg of gold as a loan.
  • Sell it as jewellery, then repay in gold value. It crafts and sells that gold as jewellery. The purchase price is fixed to align with the sale, so the loan is effectively repaid at the gold value realised. If gold rises, both the cost owed and the revenue rise together; if gold falls, both fall together.
  • The result: the price risk cancels out. Kalyan is left earning its making charges and studded margin regardless of which way gold moved. It locks in a spread rather than betting on a direction.
Hedged, not exposed — the crucial distinction

Picture a baker who borrows flour, bakes bread, sells it, and repays the same weight of flour. Whether flour got dearer or cheaper in between doesn't matter to the baker's profit — the profit is the baking, not the flour. That's the GML logic: Kalyan's economics come from craftsmanship and mix, not from gold going up. So while a rising gold price flatters reported revenue (§01) and can dampen volumes if buyers balk at sticker shock, it does not make or break the actual margin. The genuine gold-related risks are narrower: the interest cost of the gold loan, availability of GML lines, and the working capital of unhedged inventory — not a directional bet on the metal.

The de-leveraging story

Kalyan has been deliberately reducing the debt attached to its India business. Through FY26 it cut India non-GML debt by ~₹360 Cr and reiterated a goal of making the India operation non-GML-debt-free — using gold loans for hedging while retiring the plain cash borrowing. Combined with FOCO (which shifts inventory funding to franchisees), the direction of travel is toward a lighter, higher-return balance sheet. Management has flagged that gold supply and GML interest rates are not a current constraint.

The advance-purchase schemes: Kalyan (like most Indian jewellers) runs gold-savings / advance-booking plans where customers pay in monthly instalments toward a future purchase, often with a bonus instalment. These are a source of customer stickiness and low-cost float (advances the jeweller holds before delivering jewellery) — a small but real working-capital and loyalty advantage that ties families to the brand ahead of a big wedding buy.

The margin levers — studded mix & store maturity

Where a thin 7% margin actually gets made — and how it can widen.

If gold is a pass-through and revenue is hostage to the metal price, what actually drives Kalyan's profitability? Two things above all: the share of studded (diamond) jewellery in the mix, and store maturity / operating leverage as new showrooms fill out. Both are moving in Kalyan's favour, which is why margins have been inching up.

Studded mix reported at ~31.2% in Q3 FY26 (vs ~29.5% a year earlier). Diamond jewellery carries materially higher gross margins than plain gold, so every point of studded-mix gain lifts blended margin. Candere is even more studded-heavy (>70% of its mix).

Why studded is the lever

  • Margin per rupee is far higher. Diamond and gemstone pieces command design value and higher making/margin content than commoditised plain gold, so shifting the mix toward studded widens the blended gross margin without needing more volume.
  • It's a mix, not a price hike. Kalyan doesn't have to charge more on the same product — it just sells a richer basket. Bridal and premium collections, marketed heavily, pull the mix up.
  • Regional nuance. Southern buyers skew toward heavier plain-gold traditional jewellery; the North and metros buy more studded — so Kalyan's non-South expansion (now ~51% of revenue) is itself a mix-and-margin tailwind.

Store maturity & operating leverage

A new showroom opens with full fixed costs (rent, staff, the flagship displays) but a customer base it must still build. As a store matures over 2–4 years, its sales rise on a broadly fixed cost base, so its contribution margin climbs — the same operating-leverage idea that governs any store-rollout retailer. With Kalyan opening ~100+ stores a year, a large slice of the network is always immature, which caps blended margin today but represents embedded margin upside as those stores season. Rising SSSG (~18% in Q1 FY26) is the signal that maturation and demand are both working.

The showroom footprint

A Southern heartland, a decisive Northern push, and a global diaspora business.

Kalyan's 507 showrooms (as of March 2026) are its distribution moat — but the shape of the footprint is shifting fast. Historically a Southern powerhouse, Kalyan now earns the majority of its revenue outside the South, and is planting stores in North and West India and tier-2/3 towns where organized penetration is lowest.

Why "the brand travels" is the whole game

A regional jeweller's biggest risk is that its trust and brand equity don't cross state lines — a name revered in Kerala may mean nothing in Uttar Pradesh. Kalyan's non-South revenue reaching ~51% is powerful evidence that the brand does travel, backed by heavy marketing (celebrity ambassadors, regional campaigns) and the FOCO model that lets a local franchisee bring local capital and market knowledge. The expansion is deliberately weighted to tier-2/3 towns, where the unorganized jeweller is still dominant and a trusted brand is most valued — the frontier of the formalization wave.

08

Candere and the Middle East

The digital growth option and the mature diaspora cash-cow.

Beyond the core Kalyan showrooms sit two distinct businesses that broaden the story: Candere, the online-led, studded-heavy brand aimed at younger digital buyers; and the Middle East operation serving the Indian diaspora. Each plays a different role in the portfolio.

Candere — the digital, studded-heavy option

  • What it is: an online-first jewellery brand (with a growing set of physical touchpoints, 124 by March 2026) targeting younger, urban, price-transparent buyers with lighter, more design-led, studded-heavy jewellery (studded is >70% of Candere's mix).
  • Why it matters: it gives Kalyan a foothold in the fast-growing online jewellery channel (where CaratLane, Titan's digital arm, is the reference point), a younger customer, and a structurally higher-margin mix. In FY26 Candere grew revenue ~160% and turned profitable in the second half — an inflection that turns a cash-consuming experiment into a genuine growth engine.
  • The role: optionality. Candere is small versus the ~₹35,000 Cr core (a few hundred crore of revenue), but it addresses a channel and a demographic the big showrooms don't, and its profitability inflection removes the "drag" objection.

The Middle East — mature diaspora business

  • What it is: 38 showrooms across the Gulf (UAE, Qatar, Oman, Kuwait), serving the large, affluent Indian expatriate community that buys gold for weddings and remittance-linked savings much as they would at home.
  • Why it matters: it's a relatively mature, cash-generative pillar that diversifies Kalyan beyond India and captures the diaspora's jewellery spend — plus new toeholds in the US (2) and UK (1) extend the same diaspora logic to Western markets.
  • The wrinkle: the Middle East has needed some restructuring — Kalyan temporarily converted certain FOCO stores back to company-operated (COCO) while reworking investor arrangements, a reminder that the franchise model depends on local partner economics holding up.
Three products, three jobs: the Kalyan showrooms drive scale and share gains; Candere buys a younger, digital, higher-margin future; the Gulf provides mature diaspora cash.
09

The industry and the rivals

A vast, fragmented market consolidating toward a few trusted brands.

India's ~US$90 billion jewellery market is huge, gold-centric, wedding- and festival-driven, and still deeply fragmented. The organized cohort taking share is led by Titan's Tanishq, with Kalyan a clear number two among listed players, followed by a set of regional and unlisted chains. The competitive question is not whether organized wins — it's which organized brands win the most share.

The competitive tension to watch

Two dynamics matter. First, Tanishq's lead: Titan enjoys a stronger brand, a richer studded mix and fatter margins, so Kalyan competes partly on footprint and value (making-charge offers, transparency, wider tier-2/3 reach) rather than premium positioning. Second, the land-grab: every organized player is racing to open stores before the unorganized jeweller's local grip weakens — which risks over-storing some markets and pressuring making charges. Kalyan's FOCO model is a weapon here (it can plant flags cheaply), but franchise-led growth in a competitive land-grab is exactly where discipline on store quality and franchisee economics is tested. The winners will be those who take unorganized share profitably, not merely fastest.

The regulatory & macro backdrop: jewellers sit at the intersection of gold import duty, GST, hallmarking rules and gold-price volatility. Policy that raises the cost of gold (higher duty) or squeezes the cash economy tends, on balance, to help organized players by hurting unorganized rivals more — but a sharp gold-price spike can also depress volumes as buyers wait for a dip. It's a sector where regulation is usually a tailwind for brands and a headwind for the corner shop.

How to think about the ~29× multiple

At ~₹39,400 Cr for ~₹1,350 Cr of FY26 profit, Kalyan trades around 29× trailing earnings — rich for a thin-margin, inventory-heavy, gold-linked business, and a level that only makes sense if you believe the growth and the returns-on-capital improvement persist. Bulls argue that a structural share-gainer with a re-rating capital-light model, rising studded mix and a long tier-2/3 runway deserves a premium and can compound earnings for years. Bears counter that (i) the reported growth is flattered by a one-off surge in the gold price, (ii) the margin is structurally thin and franchise-diluted, (iii) any gold-price correction could hit both volumes and sentiment, and (iv) 29× leaves little room for a stumble in the aggressive rollout. Both can be right at different points — which is why SSSG, studded mix and RoCE are the numbers to track each quarter.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine share-gainer — priced for it to keep executing.

Kalyan is one of the cleaner ways to own the formalization of Indian jewellery: a large, trusted, fast-growing brand with a newly capital-light model, running into a decade-long structural tailwind. The investment debate is mostly about valuation, gold-price optics and execution of an aggressive rollout — not whether the theme is real.

The bull case

  • Structural tailwind — organized share heading from ~35% toward ~60%; Kalyan is a prime beneficiary, especially in under-penetrated tier-2/3 towns.
  • Capital-light re-rating — FOCO lets it grow fast on franchisee capital, lifting RoCE and rollout speed.
  • Margin upside — rising studded mix (~31% and climbing) and maturing stores widen a thin margin over time.
  • Brand that travels — non-South revenue now >50%; first UK store; Candere profitable and growing ~160%.
  • Hedged, not exposed — GML hedging means profit is craftsmanship + mix, not a gold-price bet; de-leveraging under way.

The bear case

  • Full valuation — ~29× earnings for a thin-margin jeweller leaves little room for error.
  • Gold-price optics — a chunk of FY26's headline growth reflects a soaring gold price; a correction could hit volumes and sentiment.
  • Thin, franchise-diluted margin — ~7% EBITDA; FOCO trades margin for capital efficiency and adds franchisee dependence.
  • Execution & competition — an aggressive ~150-store-a-year land-grab against Tanishq and unlisted giants risks over-storing and making-charge pressure.
  • Balance-sheet & ME wrinkles — inventory-heavy model, ~₹6,100 Cr borrowings, and Middle-East FOCO restructuring show the model's fragilities.
The bottom line

Kalyan is a legitimate structural winner of Indian jewellery's shift from the corner shop to the trusted brand, and the FOCO model has genuinely improved how it grows — faster, on less of its own capital, with rising returns. The business risk is moderate and the runway is long. The valuation and gold-optics risk is the live question: much of the recent sparkle owes something to a high gold price, and a ~29× multiple assumes the compounding continues uninterrupted. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter — SSSG (is core demand real?), studded mix (is the margin widening?), and RoCE (is FOCO still lifting returns?). Get those right and the premium is earned; stumble, and a full multiple has room to fall.
