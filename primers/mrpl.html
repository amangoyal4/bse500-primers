Mangalore Refinery & Petrochemicals, the ONGC-owned barrel machine

A complete walk through India's largest single-location oil refinery — how a refinery actually earns money by turning crude oil into diesel, petrol and jet fuel, why the "gross refining margin" is the one number that decides everything, how ONGC's ownership frames the whole story, and why a company that made ₹51 crore one year and ₹1,931 crore the next is the textbook definition of a cyclical commodity processor.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Mangalore Refinery and Petrochemicals Limited (MRPL) runs one very large oil refinery on the Karnataka coast — a plant that buys crude oil, heats and chemically converts it, and sells the resulting fuels: diesel, petrol, jet fuel (ATF), LPG, plus petrochemical feedstocks and plastics. It is a subsidiary of ONGC, India's government-controlled oil major, and is India's largest single-location refinery at a 15 MMTPA (million-tonnes-a-year) nameplate capacity — in a good year it processes ~18 MMT by running above 100% utilisation.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a commodity processor, not a compounder. A refinery's profit is its gross refining margin (GRM) — the gap, per barrel, between the value of the products it sells and the cost of the crude it buys. That margin is set by global markets, not by MRPL, and it swings violently: MRPL's GRM was ~$10.4/bbl in FY24, collapsed to $4.45/bbl in FY25, then recovered to $9.22/bbl in FY26. Profit followed: PAT was ₹51 Cr in FY25 and ₹1,931 Cr in FY26 — same refinery, ~38× the profit, purely because margins moved.
  • ONGC controls it. ONGC directly owns 71.63% and its subsidiary HPCL another 16.96% — a combined 88.58% promoter stake, leaving a thin ~11% public float. That makes MRPL a piece of India's state oil apparatus, with a long-running (and long-delayed) question of whether it eventually gets merged into HPCL.
  • The balance sheet is deleveraging. A high-debt refiner in the FY25 downcycle, MRPL used the FY26 margin recovery to cut net debt from ~₹12,900 Cr (Mar 2025) toward ~₹9,300 Cr (Dec 2025), improving its debt-to-equity from ~0.99 to ~0.63.
In plain terms

Think of MRPL as a giant, expensive kitchen that buys one raw ingredient (crude oil) and cooks it into many finished dishes (fuels). It doesn't set the price of the crude it buys or the fuels it sells — both are global commodities. Its profit is simply the spread between the two, times the volume it can push through. When that spread ("the GRM") is fat, MRPL mints money; when it's thin, MRPL barely breaks even or loses money — even though the plant is running just as hard. So this is not a steady, quarter-after-quarter grower. It's a cyclical bet on refining margins and on India's ever-rising fuel demand, wrapped inside a government oil group.

01

Refining 101: how an oil refinery earns

No engineering degree required — just the one spread that drives the whole business.

A refinery is, financially, a spread business on a huge fixed asset. It buys a barrel of crude oil, spends energy and chemistry to break it into more valuable products, and sells those products. Almost the entire investment case reduces to one measured number: the gross refining margin (GRM).

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and MRPL's number)
Crude oilThe raw input — a barrel (~159 litres) of unrefined petroleum. Priced globally (Brent / Dubai benchmarks). MRPL imports most of its crude by sea into New Mangalore Port.
Refined productsWhat comes out: diesel (HSD), petrol (motor spirit), jet fuel/ATF, LPG, naphtha, kerosene, bitumen, pet-coke, plus petrochemical feedstock. Each has its own global price.
GRM ($/barrel)"Gross Refining Margin" — the value of products made from one barrel, minus the cost of that barrel of crude (and direct processing cost). MRPL's was $9.22/bbl in FY26, up from $4.45 in FY25. This is the number.
ThroughputHow many tonnes of crude it actually runs through per year. MRPL ran ~17.0 MMT in FY26 and a record ~18.2 MMT in FY25 — above its 15 MMTPA nameplate (utilisation >100%).
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Gross profit ≈ GRM × barrels processed. So a refiner makes more money in only two ways — a wider GRM (better product prices vs crude), or more throughput (running the plant harder). The brutal catch: the plant's costs — the debt on it, the staff, the maintenance, the depreciation — are almost all fixed. They don't shrink when margins collapse. So when GRM falls from $10 to $4.45, revenue barely moves (you still sell the same fuel volume at roughly crude-linked prices) but profit can nearly vanish, because the thin margin has to cover the same big fixed cost base. That is the single most important thing to understand about MRPL: high operating leverage on a margin it does not control.

Why a barrel is worth more split up

Crude oil is a jumble of hydrocarbon molecules of different sizes. Left whole it is nearly useless; separated and re-shaped, its pieces become high-value fuels. A refinery does two jobs:

  • Separation (distillation) — heat the crude in a tall column so the different fractions boil off at different heights: light gases and petrol at the top, then kerosene/jet, then diesel, then heavy residue at the bottom.
  • Conversion (cracking & treating) — take the low-value heavy residue and chemically "crack" it into more diesel and petrol, and clean out sulphur to meet fuel standards (BS-VI in India). This is where a complex refinery earns its keep: it can buy cheaper, heavier, dirtier crude and still squeeze out premium clean fuels.
02

The barrel: from crude to the pump

Follow one barrel through MRPL and the business model clicks.

MRPL's value chain is a physical journey: a barrel of imported crude enters at the port, is separated and converted inside the refinery, and leaves as a slate of fuels and chemicals sold to oil-marketing companies, airlines, industry and — increasingly — MRPL's own retail pumps.

The further right you go, the higher and (for petrochemicals) the steadier the value. MRPL's edge is Step 2 — a high-complexity plant that converts cheap heavy crude into premium clean fuels — plus the petrochemical upgrade that partly decouples it from raw fuel cracks.

What comes out of the barrel

MRPL produces almost the full petroleum slate. The mix matters because the middle of the barrel — diesel and jet fuel — is usually the most valuable and the biggest earner:

  • Diesel (High-Speed Diesel) — the workhorse of the Indian economy (trucks, farm equipment, gensets) and typically a refiner's single biggest and most valuable product stream.
  • Petrol (Motor Spirit) — for cars and two-wheelers; demand growing with vehicle ownership.
  • Jet fuel (ATF) — sold to airlines; MRPL markets it partly through Shell MRPL Aviation (a 50:50 JV with Shell).
  • LPG — cooking gas; a regulated, subsidy-linked product.
  • Naphtha, kerosene, bitumen, pet-coke, sulphur — the rest of the barrel, feeding petrochemicals (naphtha), roads (bitumen) and industry.
  • Petrochemicals — polypropylene (plastic) and aromatics (paraxylene, benzene), the value-added corner covered in section 06.
Who MRPL sells to — the marketing angle

Historically MRPL was almost purely a refiner-for-others: it sold bulk fuel to the big state oil-marketing companies (its own parent-group HPCL, plus IOCL and BPCL), which then sold it through their retail pumps. That makes MRPL a wholesaler exposed to margins but with little consumer brand. It is now building its own retail arm — 252 fuel outlets after adding 85 in FY26, plus a marketing terminal at Devangonthi (near Bengaluru) to push fuel inland. Forward integration into retail captures a bit of the marketing margin and reduces dependence on selling everything wholesale — but it is still small versus the refining engine.

03

The company and who controls it

A troubled birth, a state rescue, and life inside the ONGC group.

MRPL has an unusual history for an Indian PSU: it began as a joint venture with the private Aditya Birla group in 1988, hit deep financial trouble in the late 1990s, and was rescued and taken over by ONGC in 2003. Since then it has been the refining arm of India's largest oil-and-gas producer.

Timeline

  • 1988
    MRPL incorporated as a JV between the Aditya Birla group (Hindustan Petroleum's partner) and HPCL to build a coastal refinery at Mangalore.
  • 1996
    Refinery commissioned; Phase-II expansion follows, lifting capacity toward ~9 MMTPA.
  • 2003
    ONGC takes over MRPL, rescuing a debt-laden company — turning the upstream oil producer into a refiner and giving it a downstream foothold.
  • 2012–2014
    Phase-III upgrade lifts capacity to 15 MMTPA and adds high-complexity units (hydrocrackers, coker, aromatics) — turning MRPL into one of India's most complex refineries.
  • 2015
    Commissions its polypropylene plant (440 KTPA), entering petrochemicals; later integrates the aromatics complex (paraxylene, benzene).
  • 2018
    Government transfers HPCL to ONGC — bringing HPCL's 16.96% MRPL stake under the same parent and setting up the long-running merger question.
  • 2023
    After absorbing ONGC Mangalore Petrochemicals (OMPL), MRPL becomes India's largest single-location refinery and consolidates the group's Mangalore petrochemical assets.
  • FY25 → FY26
    A textbook cycle: GRM collapses to $4.45/bbl and PAT falls to ₹51 Cr in FY25, then GRM recovers to $9.22 and PAT jumps to ₹1,931 Cr in FY26; debt cut sharply.

Ownership & control

  • ONGC — 71.63%. The direct parent: Oil and Natural Gas Corporation, India's state-controlled upstream oil-and-gas giant. MRPL is its principal refining subsidiary.
  • HPCL — 16.96%. Hindustan Petroleum, itself now an ONGC subsidiary, holds a second block — so the promoter group is really "ONGC + its own HPCL".
  • Combined promoter stake — 88.58%, leaving only ~11.4% as public float (FIIs ~0.7%, DIIs ~2.7%, retail/others ~8%). A thin float means low liquidity and limited outside-shareholder influence.
  • Government at the top. Through ONGC, MRPL sits under the Ministry of Petroleum & Natural Gas — so national fuel policy, subsidy decisions and any windfall tax flow straight into its economics.
Why the ownership shapes the stock: a ~88.6% promoter holding makes MRPL less a free-standing company than a captive refining node of the ONGC group. The upside: crude-sourcing scale, group support in downcycles, and merger optionality. The downside: minority shareholders are along for the ride on decisions (fuel pricing, any HPCL merger swap ratio, dividend policy) taken with the group — and the government's — interests first.
04

The refinery and its complexity

One site, high complexity — and why "complexity" is the moat that money can't quickly copy.

Everything MRPL earns comes from one refinery complex at Mangalore. Two physical traits define its earning power: its scale (15 MMTPA nameplate, run above 100%) and its complexity (a Nelson Complexity Index of ~11.3, high for an Indian PSU refinery).

What "complexity" buys you

A refinery's Nelson Complexity Index measures how much upgrading kit it has bolted onto the basic distillation column — hydrocrackers, cokers, catalytic crackers, treaters. Higher complexity is a genuine competitive advantage for three reasons:

  • Cheaper crude diet. Simple refineries need light, "sweet" (low-sulphur) crude, which is expensive. A complex plant can digest heavy, sour (high-sulphur) crude that trades at a discount — and still make clean fuels. That discount is pure margin.
  • Richer product slate. Instead of leaving a lot of low-value heavy residue at the bottom of the barrel, conversion units turn it into more diesel and petrol — the high-value middle. More valuable output per barrel = higher GRM.
  • Fuel-standard compliance. The desulphurisation and hydro-processing units let MRPL meet India's BS-VI (ultra-low-sulphur) fuel norms and export-grade specs — a requirement, not an option, and one that keeps its products saleable at premium prices.
Why one site is both a strength and a risk

Concentrating everything at Mangalore gives MRPL scale economies and a deep-water port on its doorstep for cheap crude imports and product exports. But it also means single-site concentration risk: an unplanned shutdown, a fire, a cyclone on the Karnataka coast, or a long maintenance turnaround takes down the entire company's earning capacity at once. A multi-refinery peer like IOCL can lean on other plants; MRPL cannot. The plant's reliability — its on-stream days and utilisation — is therefore a first-order variable, not a footnote.

05

GRM, crack spreads and inventory swings

The single most volatile — and most important — driver, decoded.

If you remember one thing about MRPL, make it this: the GRM is set by global markets and swings wildly, and MRPL is a price-taker. It cannot decide to earn a good margin; it earns whatever the world's supply-and-demand for crude and fuels hands it that quarter.

A ~$6/bbl swing between FY25 and FY26 is enormous on ~120–130 million barrels of throughput — it is the whole difference between a ₹51 Cr year and a ₹1,931 Cr year.

What actually moves the GRM

  • "Crack spreads." A "crack" is the price gap between a specific product and crude — the diesel crack, the petrol crack, the jet crack. When global fuel demand is strong or refining capacity is tight (say, after refinery closures or during a supply shock), cracks widen and GRM rises. When new refining capacity floods the market or demand softens, cracks and GRM fall. MRPL benchmarks against the Singapore complex GRM, the reference for Asian refiners.
  • The crude slate & discounts. The cheaper the crude MRPL can source relative to the products it sells, the wider the margin. Discounted heavy/sour grades (and, in recent years, discounted Russian crude bought by Indian refiners) directly fatten GRM. MRPL has been diversifying its crude sourcing.
  • Inventory gains and losses. A refiner always holds a large stock of crude and products. When crude prices rise, the cheaper inventory it bought earlier is now worth more — it books an inventory gain (a paper profit). When crude prices fall, it books an inventory loss. These swings can add or wipe out hundreds of crores in a quarter and have nothing to do with how well the plant ran.
  • The rupee. Crude is bought in dollars and much product is priced off dollar benchmarks. A weaker rupee raises the rupee cost of crude and complicates the maths; currency moves add another layer of volatility.
Why "great years" and "loss years" both happen

Stack these together and you see why refining earnings are so jumpy. In a good year — wide cracks, cheap crude, rising oil prices booking inventory gains — all three tailwinds line up and profit explodes (FY24, FY26). In a bad year — cracks compress, crude prices fall (inventory losses), and the fixed-cost base still has to be paid — profit can collapse to near zero or turn into a loss (FY25; MRPL even posted a quarterly loss in Q1 FY26 when margins were weak). Nothing about the refinery changed. The market changed. That is the essence of a commodity-processing business, and it is why you must never value MRPL on a single peak year's earnings.

06

Petrochemicals, aromatics and marketing

The value-added corner that softens the pure-refining cyclicality.

MRPL is not only a fuel refinery. Bolted onto the plant is a petrochemical and aromatics business that takes some of the barrel further up the value chain — into plastics and chemical feedstocks that sell at higher, and somewhat steadier, margins than raw fuels.

Why petrochemicals matter to the investment case

Petrochemicals do two useful things. First, they lift the value of the barrel: turning naphtha and propylene into polypropylene or paraxylene earns more than selling them as plain fuel. Second, and more importantly, chemical margins don't move in perfect lockstep with fuel cracks — so a bigger petrochemical share partly diversifies the earnings away from pure refining volatility. This is the strategic logic behind MRPL's proposed PACE project (Petchem Addition and Capacity Expansion) — a large planned expansion to add more petrochemical capacity. The caveat: petrochemicals have their own cycle (a global glut of new plastic capacity, much of it in China and West Asia, has pressured chemical margins), so this is diversification, not a hedge that removes risk.

Marketing is real but small: the retail push (252 outlets) and the Shell aviation JV give MRPL a slice of the higher, steadier marketing margin — but the company's fortunes are still overwhelmingly decided by the refining GRM. Treat retail and petrochemicals as margin-smoothers and optionality, not as the main engine.
07

The refining cycle — living with volatility

The one chart that tells you what kind of business this really is.

The clearest way to understand MRPL is to put two adjacent years side by side. Same plant, same people, same 15 MMTPA of capacity — and a profit that moved by roughly 38×. That is the refining cycle in a single picture.

Notice revenue barely moved (down slightly, as fuel volumes are similar and prices track crude), while GRM roughly doubled and profit multiplied ~38×. Refining leverage runs through the margin, not the top line.

The mechanism is the fixed-cost leverage from section 01. Revenue is a poor guide to a refiner's health because it is dominated by the pass-through cost of crude — sell similar volumes of fuel priced off crude, and revenue looks stable even as profit swings from near-zero to strong. Watch GRM, throughput and EBITDA, not the headline sales number.

What a full cycle looks like

Refining margins move in multi-year waves driven by the balance between global refining capacity and fuel demand. When the world adds a lot of new refineries (or demand slumps, as in the pandemic), margins crater and refiners bleed. When capacity is tight (closures in Europe/US, sanctions reshuffling trade, or a demand surge), margins spike and refiners feast. India sits in a structurally favourable spot — fuel demand is still growing as incomes and vehicle ownership rise — which supports throughput. But the margin is a global variable, so even a growing-demand refiner like MRPL will keep having fat years and lean years. The skill in reading this stock is judging where in the cycle you are — and resisting the temptation to extrapolate a peak-margin year forever.

08

The ONGC-group integration story

A subsidiary of a subsidiary's cousin — and the merger that keeps being discussed.

MRPL cannot be understood in isolation from the restructuring of India's state oil sector. Sitting under ONGC alongside HPCL, it is entangled in a long-running plan to consolidate the group's downstream (refining + marketing) assets — a plan repeatedly floated and repeatedly delayed.

How the group fits together

  • ONGC — the upstream parent: it finds and produces crude oil and gas. It bought MRPL (2003) and later HPCL (2018) to build a downstream presence and become a more integrated oil company (from the well to the pump).
  • HPCL — a large refiner-and-marketer with a nationwide retail network, now an ONGC subsidiary; it also owns 16.96% of MRPL.
  • MRPL — the group's most complex standalone refinery, feeding fuel into the system (including to HPCL).

The strategic idea has been to merge MRPL into HPCL (after first consolidating the group's Mangalore assets — OMPL was folded into MRPL as a step toward this). A combined HPCL–MRPL would pair MRPL's refining with HPCL's marketing reach, creating scale and removing the awkward cross-holdings. The merger has been discussed since around 2018 and pushed back several times — snagged on share-swap ratios, valuation, and which group entity ends up owning what — and remains unresolved.

Why the merger question cuts both ways for MRPL shareholders

For a minority holder of MRPL's thin ~11% float, the integration story is genuine optionality — and genuine uncertainty. On the upside: an eventual merger into HPCL could offer a swap into a larger, more diversified, better-traded entity, potentially at a premium, and end the "orphan subsidiary" discount. On the downside: the terms are set by the parent group and the government, not by minorities — a swap ratio struck at an unfavourable point in MRPL's cycle could short-change them, and years of on-again-off-again talk create an overhang. Meanwhile, integration also means MRPL's crude sourcing, product offtake and even capex priorities are increasingly coordinated at the group level, further tying its fate to ONGC's strategy rather than its own.

Watch this space: any concrete move on an HPCL–MRPL merger (or a formal decision to keep MRPL listed and standalone) would be the single biggest corporate-action catalyst for the stock — more impactful in the short run than a quarter's GRM. As of now it remains an unconfirmed, long-pending possibility rather than a scheduled event.
09

Financials & valuation

Cheap-looking in good years, expensive-or-loss-making in bad ones.

MRPL's financials are the financials of a cyclical, leveraged commodity processor: enormous revenue (dominated by crude pass-through), thin and volatile margins, big swings in profit, and a debt load that rises in downcycles and gets paid down in upcycles. The valuation question is therefore not "what did it earn last year?" but "what does it earn across the cycle?"

How to value a cyclical refiner

The mistake beginners make is to put a P/E multiple on a single year. On FY26's ₹1,931 Cr of profit, MRPL trades around 17× earnings — but on FY25's ₹51 Cr it would look like a crazy ~600×, and in a loss year a P/E is undefined. That is why refiners are usually valued on price-to-book (P/B) and mid-cycle EV/EBITDA instead of trailing P/E. MRPL trades at roughly ~2× book — a modest premium that reflects both its high complexity and its high debt and cyclicality. The honest framing: MRPL looks cheap when you catch it on peak-margin earnings and expensive or loss-making at the trough. A sensible view normalises the GRM to a mid-cycle level, applies it to ~15–18 MMT of throughput, subtracts the fixed costs and interest, and asks whether the resulting mid-cycle profit justifies the ~₹32–33k Cr market cap. Debt reduction genuinely helps here — every rupee of debt paid down cuts interest and de-risks the next downcycle.

Figures to treat as approximate: market cap and share price move daily; the ~₹32–33k Cr / ~₹186 figure is a mid-2026 snapshot. Net-debt and D/E figures are the latest disclosed (Dec 2025) and will have moved with FY26 close. GRM, PAT, EBITDA and throughput are as reported for FY25/FY26. Verify against MRPL's filings and exchange disclosures before relying on any number.
10

The industry and the rivals

A state-dominated refining sector with one giant private outlier.

Indian refining is dominated by public-sector oil companies — IOCL, BPCL, HPCL and their refining arms — plus two large private/JV refiners (Reliance and Nayara). MRPL is a mid-size, high-complexity PSU refiner. Its closest structural twin is Chennai Petroleum (CPCL), the other listed standalone PSU refinery.

Where MRPL sits — and its structural disadvantage

MRPL's edge over some PSU peers is its high complexity and coastal location (cheap heavy crude in, easy product export out). Its structural disadvantage versus the integrated majors and Reliance is that it is largely a pure refiner — it lacks the big captive marketing network that lets IOCL/BPCL/HPCL earn a retail margin to cushion refining downturns, and it lacks Reliance's petrochemical scale and diversification. So when GRMs fall, MRPL has fewer places to hide. The offsets it is building — its own retail (252 pumps), petrochemicals, and group support — narrow but don't close that gap. Against CPCL, the two are close cousins; MRPL is larger and more complex, but both live or die by the same global refining margin.

The policy overhang — windfall tax & fuel pricing: Indian refiners and upstream producers were hit by a windfall tax on crude and fuel exports introduced in 2022 (since scrapped/adjusted repeatedly) — a reminder that when refining margins spike, the government can and does claw back part of the gains. Add regulated/subsidised products (LPG, at times), export duties, and the state's interest in keeping pump prices stable, and you get a permanent policy risk layered on top of the market cyclicality. For a government-owned refiner, that risk is doubly live.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap cyclical refining play with real optionality — and no control over its own margin.

MRPL is a genuine asset — a large, complex, deleveraging refinery riding India's still-growing fuel demand, with ONGC-group integration optionality on top. But it is, at its core, a price-taking commodity processor whose profits are decided by a global margin it cannot influence. The investment debate is entirely about that tension.

The bull case

  • India's fuel demand keeps growing — rising incomes and vehicle ownership underpin throughput; MRPL runs above nameplate.
  • High complexity = structural margin edge — can process cheap heavy/sour crude into premium clean fuels; coastal port advantage.
  • Deleveraging — debt cut from ~₹12,900 Cr toward ~₹9,300 Cr; lower interest and lower downcycle risk.
  • Cheap on good-year earnings — ~17× FY26 P/E, ~2× book; a leveraged play on the next up-leg in refining margins.
  • ONGC-group optionality — crude-sourcing scale, group support, and a possible HPCL merger that could re-rate the stock.
  • Petrochemical & retail expansion (PACE, 252 pumps) gradually diversifies away from pure fuel cracks.

The bear case

  • Zero margin control — GRM is set globally; MRPL is a price-taker. FY25 (₹51 Cr) shows how fast profit vanishes.
  • Intense cyclicality — great years and loss years alternate; peak earnings are a trap for valuation.
  • Single-site concentration — one shutdown, fire or cyclone takes down all earning capacity.
  • Policy & windfall-tax risk — a government-owned refiner exposed to export duties, subsidies and price-stability pressure.
  • Crude-price & rupee sensitivity — inventory losses and currency swings can erase a quarter's operating gains.
  • Thin float & parent control — ~88.6% promoter holding; minorities are along for the ride on any merger terms.
  • Structural energy-transition question — long-run EV adoption and decarbonisation cloud terminal demand for road fuels.
The bottom line

MRPL is a well-located, high-complexity, mid-size refinery that is a leveraged bet on refining margins and India's fuel demand — not a steady compounder. Bought at the right point in the cycle, with debt falling and a fat GRM, it can look cheap and reward handsomely; bought on peak-margin earnings, or caught at a margin trough, it can disappoint badly. The two numbers that decide each year are the GRM (is the global refining cycle with you or against you?) and throughput/reliability (is the single plant running hard?). Layer on the real-but-uncertain optionality of an ONGC-group re-organisation, and you have a stock that is best understood as a cyclical, policy-exposed, parent-controlled refining play — one to size and time with the cycle, not to hold blindly through it.
