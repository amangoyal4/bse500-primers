00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Alok Industries is a fully-integrated textile manufacturer — it takes raw fibre at one end and ships finished bed sheets, terry towels, fabric, yarn and garments out the other. It spins cotton and polyester into yarn, weaves and knits that yarn into fabric, dyes and finishes the cloth, and converts a good chunk of it into home textiles and apparel. In its size class it is one of the country's largest single-site integrated mills. But the reason Alok is a name every Indian investor half-recognises is not its cloth: it is that this company staged one of the largest corporate collapses in Indian textile history, defaulted on more than ₹30,000 crore of bank debt, and was pulled out of insolvency in 2020 by a consortium of Reliance Industries and JM Financial ARC. Reliance is now the controlling promoter. Everything about the stock flows from that fact.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a rescued bankrupt, not a healthy exporter. Do not read Alok the way you'd read Welspun Living or Indo Count — profitable, branded home-textile exporters. Alok went through the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), its old lenders wrote off roughly 83–84% of what they were owed, and the RIL–JM Financial consortium took control for a bid of about ₹5,000 Cr against debts north of ₹30,000 Cr. What trades today is the reorganised shell of a former giant.
  • The financials are still underwater. Six years after the rescue, Alok is still loss-making. FY26 revenue was roughly flat at ~₹3,715 Cr and the company posted a net loss of ~₹744 Cr (narrower than the prior year's ~₹816 Cr, but a loss all the same). It carries ~₹26,000 Cr of borrowings and a negative net worth of about −₹22,000 Cr — book value per share is negative. This is not a clean growth story; it is a wounded balance sheet being kept upright.
  • Reliance is both the owner and the crutch. RIL (~40%) and JM Financial ARC (~35%) together control ~75% of the equity. Crucially, RIL provides an unconditional corporate guarantee on Alok's entire term debt and has infused funds to keep it running. The polyester value chain — where Reliance is India's largest player — is the industrial logic for the turnaround. The equity is essentially a leveraged bet on how far, and how generously, Reliance chooses to rebuild this asset.
In plain terms

Alok is a giant textile factory that a decade ago borrowed far more than its business could ever repay, defaulted, and fell into bankruptcy court. Reliance and a distressed-asset firm bought the wreck cheaply, wiped out the old shareholders' value, and took over. Today the mills still run — spinning yarn, weaving cloth, making sheets and towels — but the company loses money every year and owes vastly more than it is worth. Reliance keeps it alive by guaranteeing its loans. Buying the shares is not buying a normal manufacturer; it is buying a thin, negative-equity claim that only has value if Reliance turns the plant around and the mountain of debt is eventually restructured in the equity's favour. That is a specific, high-risk wager, and this primer explains exactly what you are wagering on.

01

Integrated textiles 101: fibre to finished cloth

No industry knowledge assumed — just the handful of steps that turn raw fibre into a bed sheet.

A textile mill is a chain of physical transformations. You start with fibre — either natural (cotton) or man-made (polyester). You twist that fibre into yarn (spinning). You interlace yarn into fabric (weaving or knitting). You clean, dye and finish that fabric (processing). And finally you cut and stitch it into a product — a sheet, a towel, a shirt (made-ups and garments). A company can sit on just one of these steps, or — like Alok — own all of them. To follow the business, you need six terms.

The only terms you need

The further right you go, the more value is added per kilo of fibre. A company that only spins yarn is a price-taker on commodities; one that ships branded home textiles captures a real margin. Alok spans the whole chain — which is exactly why it needed so much capital to build, and why that capital sank it.
Why "integration" is a double-edged sword

Owning the whole chain means Alok captures value at every step and controls its own quality and supply — a genuine advantage when demand is strong and the plant runs at full tilt. But integration has a hidden cost: it is enormously capital-intensive. Spindles, looms, continuous-polymerisation lines and processing houses each cost hundreds of crores, and they only earn a return when they run near full capacity. Load an integrated mill up with debt and then run it at 70–90% utilisation, and the fixed costs and interest crush you before the operating margin can help. That is not a hypothetical for Alok — it is precisely the mechanism that turned a proud integrated giant into the biggest textile insolvency the country had seen.

The five divisions: what Alok actually makes

One integrated company, five product lines with very different economics.

Alok reports its business across five core divisions. They sit at different points on the fibre-to-fabric chain, sell to different customers, and carry different margins. Reading the mix tells you where the company is a commodity price-taker and where it captures real value.

The five lines, decoded

  • Polyester yarn — the biggest and most strategically important line. Alok is fully integrated in polyester: it runs continuous polymerisation, makes its own chips, and spins them into POY, FDY, DTY and PSF at roughly 1,250 tonnes/day of capacity (about 850 MT/day of POY and 210 MT/day of PSF). This is a petrochemical business as much as a textile one — and the reason Reliance, India's polyester king, wanted it.
  • Cotton yarn — spun on ~450,000 spindles, largely at Silvassa. A classic commodity-spinning business: margins swing with the cotton price and the yarn spread, and utilisation is everything. Alok's cotton capacity ran at ~88% in FY25 (down from 93%) after weather and power disruptions at Silvassa.
  • Apparel fabric — woven and knitted fabric sold to garment makers and brands, both domestic and export. A middle-of-the-chain product: more value-added than yarn, less than a finished garment.
  • Home textiles — the category that made peers like Welspun and Indo Count rich: bed sheets, terry towels and furnishings, heavily export-oriented (the US retail market is the big prize). This is where Alok could capture premium margins — but it is a smaller share of its mix than at the branded specialists.
  • Garments — the smallest line: finished apparel. The most value-added, most labour-intensive, and most competitive part of the chain, where Bangladesh and Vietnam set the global price.
The mix tells you Alok's real problem

Look at where the revenue sits: heavily in polyester and cotton yarn — the commodity, price-taking end of the chain — and comparatively light in branded home textiles, the value-added end. A healthy exporter tilts the other way, earning fat margins on US retail bedding. Alok's tilt toward commodity yarn means its operating margins are thin and cyclical, so even at full utilisation the business generates only modest EBITDA. That modest EBITDA is then asked to service a colossal debt load — which is why revenue can be broadly stable while the bottom line stays firmly in the red.

03

The polyester engine — and why Reliance wanted it

The strategic heart of the whole rescue: captive integration into India's largest polyester chain.

To understand why Reliance, of all buyers, took over a bankrupt textile mill, you have to look past the bed sheets and towels to the polyester. Reliance Industries is India's dominant producer of the petrochemical building blocks of polyester — PTA, MEG and polyester intermediates — and one of the largest polyester players in the world. Alok is a large downstream consumer and processor of exactly those inputs. Put the two together and you get a captive value chain running from Reliance's refineries all the way to finished fabric.

The value-chain logic

Alok's polyester division is not a bolt-on; it is a fully integrated line — continuous polymerisation → polyester chips → POY / FDY / DTY / PSF. That means it converts raw polymer into filament and fibre in-house. For a standalone company, the raw-material bill (PTA and MEG) is the single biggest cost and the biggest source of margin volatility. For a company sitting inside the Reliance ecosystem, those inputs can, in principle, be sourced captively and reliably, smoothing the single most dangerous variable in the business. Reliance gets a downstream outlet for its polyester chain; Alok gets a deep-pocketed, upstream-integrated parent. That is the industrial thesis for the whole deal.

The captive-synergy idea, and its limit

The appeal of Reliance ownership is that polyester's worst enemy — input-price swings — is partly neutralised when your parent makes those inputs. In a good scenario, Alok becomes a reliable, low-friction converter within Reliance's polyester value chain, running its lines hard and earning a steadier spread than an independent mill could. But the synergy has a ceiling: converting polymer into commodity yarn is still a thin-margin activity, whoever supplies the polymer. Captive inputs can stabilise the business; they cannot, on their own, generate the kind of profit that would fill a −₹22,000 Cr equity hole. The polyester logic explains why Reliance is here — not why the equity is cheap.

A genuine surprise: Reliance's interest in Alok was never really about towels and bed sheets. It was about securing a large, captive downstream polyester converter for its petrochemical chain — the textile brand and home-textiles business came along for the ride. That is why a company filed in most investors' minds under "bedsheet maker" is, in revenue terms, closer to a polyester-yarn business with a textile wrapper — and why its fortunes track polymer spreads as much as cotton and cushion covers.
04

How a textile giant went bankrupt

The debt-fuelled over-expansion that produced one of India's largest textile insolvencies.

Before the fall, Alok was a genuine industrial success story — a fast-growing, ambitious integrated mill expanding aggressively across the 2000s and early 2010s. The problem was how it grew: on borrowed money, faster than its cash flows could support, into a commodity business with thin margins. When the debt came due and the margins didn't, the whole edifice collapsed.

The anatomy of the collapse

  • Debt-funded expansion. Alok kept adding capacity across spinning, weaving, processing, polyester and even non-core ventures (including a much-criticised foray into real estate), financing it largely with bank loans. Capacity ballooned; so did interest costs.
  • Commodity margins couldn't service the debt. Yarn and fabric are low-margin, cyclical products. When cotton and polyester spreads compressed and demand softened, the EBITDA the plants threw off fell far short of the interest bill on a debt pile that had swelled past ₹20,000 Cr.
  • Working-capital strangulation. An integrated mill ties up huge sums in inventory and receivables. As liquidity tightened, Alok couldn't fund its own operations, utilisation slipped, and the losses compounded — a classic debt-death spiral.
  • Default and the "dirty dozen." Alok stopped servicing its loans. In 2017 the Reserve Bank of India named it among the first twelve large defaulters — the so-called "dirty dozen," together owing ~₹1.75 lakh crore — to be pushed into the new Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code process.

Why the collapse still matters to today's share price

The insolvency is not ancient history you can skip — it is the reason the current balance sheet looks the way it does. The IBC process cleaned up some debt, but Alok emerged still carrying very large borrowings and a shattered net worth, and it has kept losing money since. So when you look at today's ~₹26,000 Cr of debt and −₹22,000 Cr of equity, you are looking at the long tail of that over-expansion. The question the market is really asking is whether a second, quieter restructuring — this time in Reliance's hands — eventually repairs the equity, or whether the debt simply keeps the stub perpetually underwater.

05

The Reliance rescue and who controls it now

A Reliance–JM Financial consortium owns three-quarters of the equity — and guarantees the debt.

Out of the insolvency emerged a company with a completely new owner. The Reliance Industries–JM Financial Asset Reconstruction Company consortium implemented its resolution plan in September 2020, reconstituting the board and taking control. This is the single most important thing to understand about the equity: Alok is a Reliance-controlled company, and its survival to date has depended on Reliance's continued support.

The support that keeps the lights on

Ownership is only half the story. The more important support is financial. Credit-rating agencies note that Alok's ratings rest on an unconditional and irrevocable corporate guarantee from Reliance covering Alok's entire term debt, plus Reliance's willingness to infuse funds. In plain language: Alok's lenders are, in substance, lending against Reliance's credit, not Alok's own (barely-there) creditworthiness. That guarantee is why a company with negative net worth can keep borrowing and operating at all.

What "Reliance-backed" really buys the equity holder

Reliance's guarantee is a lifeline for Alok's lenders — it protects the debt. It does far less, directly, for the equity. A guarantee keeps the company solvent and operating; it does not, by itself, transfer profit to shareholders or shrink the accumulated deficit. So the public shareholder is relying on a two-step hope: first that Reliance keeps supporting the company (very likely, given its strategic stake), and second that Reliance eventually restructures the capital — converting or writing down debt, or injecting value — in a way that leaves something for the equity stub. The first step is well-established; the second is entirely at Reliance's discretion and on no announced timetable.

The 2026 wrinkle worth watching: reporting in 2026 indicated that Reliance has deconsolidated Alok from its own financial statements — accounting for it as an associate rather than folding its full results into RIL's. That is a nuance, not a divorce (RIL remains the controlling promoter and guarantor), but it is a reminder that the parent manages Alok as one strategic asset among many, on its own terms and timetable — not as a subsidiary it is racing to fix for minority shareholders' benefit.
06

The balance sheet that never healed

Negative net worth, ~₹26,000 Cr of borrowings, and a market cap that is a fraction of the debt.

Here is where the Alok story separates completely from a normal textile company — and where every prospective buyer of the shares must stare hard at the numbers. Six years after the rescue, Alok's net worth is still deeply negative, its debt still enormous, and its equity market value a small fraction of what it owes. This is the crux of the whole primer.

The debt is roughly 4.5× the entire equity market value, and the reported net worth is about −₹22,000 Cr. On a per-share basis, book value is negative (about −₹43). This is the shape of a distressed capital structure: the debt is the real claim on the assets; the equity is a thin residual.
The device that only fits Alok: a positive market cap on a negative book

Most companies you can sanity-check with one line: is the equity worth more than the net assets behind it? Alok breaks the exercise, because the net assets behind the equity are negative — book value is about −₹43 a share, yet the stock trades at a positive ~₹11–12 and the market assigns it ~₹5,700 Cr. That gap is the whole investment question expressed in one number. You are not paying for net assets (there are none for equity); you are paying for the option that Reliance eventually restructures ~₹26,000 Cr of debt in the equity's favour — a conversion, a write-down, a capital injection, or a slow grind back to positive net worth. The size of that option, and its timing, is undisclosed. When a stock's market cap exceeds its assets by more than its assets are worth, you are trading a restructuring outcome, not a business.

Why has the equity not simply healed on its own? Because the operating business, at current scale and margins, does not generate enough profit to climb out. The mills throw off only modest EBITDA in a good year, and against that sits an interest bill of roughly ₹600 Cr a year (about ₹150 Cr a quarter). When your interest cost is a large multiple of your operating profit, every year adds to the accumulated losses rather than subtracting from them — and the equity hole gets no shallower. This is the arithmetic that keeps net worth negative even as revenue holds steady.

07

The turnaround mechanics: what would actually have to work

Utilisation, spreads, working capital — the operating levers, and how far they can realistically go.

Set aside the balance sheet for a moment and look at the factory. For Alok's operations to turn — for the mills to earn enough to matter — a specific set of things has to go right. None of them is exotic; all of them are the same levers that break commodity textile companies. The turnaround is a story about running the plant harder, at better spreads, without choking on working capital.

The operating levers

  • Capacity utilisation. An integrated mill only makes money when it runs full — fixed costs are enormous. Alok's cotton utilisation slipped to ~88% in FY25 (from 93%) after power failures and a tornado disrupted the Silvassa plants. Every point of lost utilisation is margin that never appears. Getting the whole complex running consistently near capacity is job one.
  • Spreads. Profit in yarn and fabric is the gap between input cost (cotton, polymer/PTA-MEG) and selling price. These spreads are cyclical and outside the company's control — but captive polyester sourcing from Reliance can, in theory, steady the polymer leg.
  • Working capital. The very thing that strangled the old Alok. An integrated mill locks cash into inventory and receivables; with Reliance's backing, Alok can fund that cycle without the liquidity crises that idled it before.
  • Mix shift up the chain. Every rupee moved from commodity yarn toward branded home textiles and value-added fabric lifts blended margin. This is the slowest lever, and the one where Alok most lags the healthy exporters.

What a genuine turn would look like

Strip it to the mechanism: Alok turns operationally when its plants run near-full at healthy spreads, so that EBITDA comfortably exceeds the ~₹600 Cr interest bill and the company posts a real net profit — quarter after quarter, not once. Only then does the accumulated-deficit needle start moving the right way. The FY26 numbers show a company that has stabilised (steady revenue, narrowing loss) but has not yet crossed that line. Stabilisation is the prerequisite for a turnaround; it is not the turnaround itself.

08

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, cyclical textile sector — where Alok is the distressed exception, not the branded champion.

Indian textiles is a large, fragmented, fiercely competitive industry, and the listed space is full of far healthier names than Alok. The most important thing an investor can do is refuse to lump Alok in with the sector's profitable exporters. It competes in the same categories, but from a completely different financial position.

The comparison that matters most

Put Alok next to Welspun Living or Indo Count and the divergence is total. Those companies converted the same raw fibre into branded, high-margin home textiles, kept their balance sheets sane, and compounded shareholder value. Alok took the opposite path — it over-borrowed, tilted toward commodity yarn, and blew up. Today the profitable exporters trade on earnings and growth; Alok trades on a restructuring hope. They are in the same industry, but they are not the same kind of investment, and confusing the two is the single most common mistake made about this stock.

The industry backdrop cuts both ways for Alok. On the positive side, structural tailwinds — the "China+1" sourcing shift, government production-linked incentives for textiles, and a large domestic market — support demand for Indian mills broadly. On the negative side, the sector is brutally cyclical and price-competitive, with Bangladesh and Vietnam underpricing on garments and cotton/polymer swings whipping margins. For a company that needs every scrap of margin to service its debt, that competitive intensity is a headwind the healthy players can shrug off but Alok cannot.

09

Financials & valuation

A loss-making, negative-equity company whose share price is a claim on a future restructuring.

There is no conventional way to value Alok. You cannot use a normal P/E (there are no earnings), or price-to-book (book value is negative), or an EV/EBITDA that means anything (the EV is dominated by ~₹26,000 Cr of debt against thin EBITDA). What you can do is understand precisely what the market cap represents — and what would have to happen for it to be justified.

10

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A stabilised distressed asset in Reliance's hands — priced as a bet on what Reliance does next.

Alok Industries is a genuinely unusual security: a large, functioning industrial business wrapped inside a broken capital structure, held up by one of India's most powerful companies. The operational story has stabilised — revenue steady, losses narrowing, plants running. The financial story has not resolved — the debt is vast, the equity underwater, and the path to repair undisclosed. What you make of the stock depends entirely on how you weigh Reliance's intentions against the arithmetic.

The bull case

  • Reliance backing — a controlling promoter with a corporate guarantee on the entire term debt and the balance-sheet strength to fund the business indefinitely; solvency risk is largely off the table.
  • Captive polyester synergy — sitting inside India's largest polyester chain can steady input costs and lift utilisation of Alok's integrated lines.
  • Real, valuable assets — one of the country's largest integrated textile complexes (~450k spindles, ~1,250 T/day polyester), acquired for a fraction of replacement cost.
  • Stabilising operations — flat revenue and a narrowing loss suggest the bleeding has slowed; a cyclical upswing plus better utilisation could push toward operating breakeven.
  • Restructuring optionality — any Reliance-led recapitalisation (debt conversion or write-down) could transform the equity from a negative-book stub into a going concern with real value.

The bear case

  • Negative net worth — ~−₹22,000 Cr equity and ~₹26,000 Cr of debt; the equity has no net-asset backing and the market cap already exceeds the assets.
  • Still loss-making — six years post-rescue, EBITDA doesn't reliably cover the ~₹600 Cr interest bill; losses keep the equity hole from closing.
  • Commodity margins — a yarn-heavy mix in a brutally cyclical, price-competitive business limits how much the operation can ever earn.
  • Reliance discretion — the parent owes minority holders nothing; any restructuring happens on RIL's terms and timetable, and 2026 reporting of deconsolidation shows it manages Alok at arm's length.
  • Dilution / squeeze-out risk — a recapitalisation done at a low price, or an eventual delisting, could leave the ~25% public float with little.
The bottom line

Alok Industries is not a textile company you value on cloth. It is the reorganised remnant of India's biggest textile bankruptcy, kept alive by Reliance's guarantee, still losing money against a debt load many times its equity value. The mills work; the balance sheet does not. Owning the shares is a specific, speculative wager that Reliance eventually recapitalises the company in a way that leaves value for the equity stub — a plausible outcome, but one on no announced schedule and entirely in the parent's gift. The checkable milestone is simple and unglamorous: watch each quarter for the point at which Alok's operating EBITDA finally, durably clears its ~₹600 Cr interest bill and the net line turns black. Until that crossing prints — and Reliance signals its intentions for the capital structure — this remains a Reliance-backed restructuring bet dressed as a bedsheet maker, and it should be sized like one.
