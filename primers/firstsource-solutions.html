00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Firstsource Solutions is a business-process management (BPM) company — the modern name for what used to be called BPO, business-process outsourcing. In plain terms, other companies pay Firstsource to run chunks of their operations: answering customer calls, processing insurance claims, chasing overdue payments, underwriting and servicing mortgages, handling billing. Most of its clients are in the United States and the United Kingdom; most of the work is delivered from lower-cost hubs in India and the Philippines (plus onshore teams in the US and UK). It is majority-owned by the RP-Sanjiv Goenka Group (RPSG), the Kolkata conglomerate better known for power utility CESC, the FMCG brand Too Yumm!, and the IPL's Lucknow Super Giants.

Four facts frame everything that follows:

  • It just crossed the billion-dollar line. FY26 (year ended March 2026) revenue reached ~US$1,082 million (~₹9,556 Cr), up 19.7% as reported and ~13.6% in constant currency — one of the fastest growth rates among listed Indian BPMs. EBIT grew ~27% to ~₹1,122 Cr (11.7% margin); normalised profit after tax rose ~27% to ~₹754 Cr.
  • It is two big businesses in a trench-coat. Roughly a third of revenue is Healthcare (mostly US payers and providers), and another third is Banking & Financial Services — inside which sits a large and unusual US/UK mortgage-servicing franchise. Communications, Media & Technology and a fast-growing "Diverse Industries" basket make up the rest.
  • Its fate is unusually tied to US interest rates. Because mortgage volumes swing with rates, a slice of Firstsource's revenue rises and falls with the American refinance cycle — a link most Indian services firms simply do not have.
  • The whole thesis now turns on one question. Does generative AI destroy the seat-based work Firstsource sells, or does it let Firstsource do far more per person and take share? Management has gone all-in on the second answer.
In plain terms

Every large bank, insurer and hospital network has a mountain of repetitive, rules-based work — forms to key, calls to answer, claims to adjudicate, payments to collect. Doing it in-house is expensive and off-strategy. Firstsource is one of the specialists they hand it to. The pitch is simple: we will run this process cheaper, at higher quality, and at a scale you can flex up or down. The company earns a thin margin on an enormous volume of transactions, locked into multi-year contracts that are painful for a client to unwind. That is a sturdy, unglamorous model — and it now faces the most disruptive input technology of its life. This primer explains the machine, then the debate.

01

What "business-process management" actually is

No jargon — just the one idea that the whole industry rests on: someone else's work, run better and cheaper.

Start with the client, not with Firstsource. A US health insurer receives millions of claims a year. Each one has to be checked, priced, matched against a policy, queried if something is off, and paid or denied. This is labour-intensive, rules-heavy, and mission-critical but not differentiating — the insurer does not win customers by keying claims faster; it just cannot afford to key them wrong. That combination — essential, high-volume, low-strategic-value — is exactly the kind of work a company wants to hand to a specialist.

That is the BPM's entire reason to exist. Firstsource takes over the process end-to-end, staffs it with trained agents in lower-cost locations, wraps it in technology and quality controls, and runs it under a contract with agreed service levels. The client gets three things at once:

  • Lower cost — the same work done by well-trained people in India or the Philippines costs a fraction of a US or UK in-house team. This is the original engine of the industry, called labour arbitrage.
  • Flexibility — the client turns capacity up or down (seasonal claims, a product launch, a wave of collections) without hiring and firing its own staff.
  • Focus and quality — a specialist that does nothing but claims, or nothing but mortgage servicing, invests in tooling and domain depth that a generalist in-house team rarely matches.
BPM vs IT services — a distinction worth getting right

People lump Firstsource in with TCS and Infosys, but the businesses differ. An IT-services firm mostly builds and maintains software — it writes code, integrates systems, runs applications. A BPM firm mostly runs the operation itself — the actual claims desk, the actual collections calls, the actual mortgage-servicing queue. IT services sells engineering hours; BPM sells operational outcomes. The two increasingly blur (every BPM now layers software and automation over its people), but the DNA is different: BPM is operations-led, and its cost base is dominated by headcount, not licences. That single fact — people are the product — is why the GenAI debate lands so hard here.

How a BPM gets paid

Pricing sits on a spectrum, and where a contract sits on it matters enormously:

  • Per seat / per FTE — the client pays for a "full-time equivalent" agent, or a desk-seat, per month. The oldest model; it directly ties revenue to headcount.
  • Per transaction — pay per claim processed, per call handled, per loan boarded. Revenue tracks volume rather than bodies.
  • Outcome / gain-share — pay for a result: dollars collected, days of billing cycle removed, a quality threshold hit. The client shares the value created, not the hours worked.

A BPM that is paid per seat grows by adding people. A BPM that is paid per transaction or per outcome can grow by doing more with the same people. Hold that thought — in §06 and §07 it becomes the hinge of the entire Firstsource investment case.

03

The company and who controls it

From an ICICI back-office spin-out to an RPSG-controlled global operator.

Firstsource has an unusual pedigree. It was born inside ICICI Bank in 2001 as ICICI OneSource — the bank's captive back-office arm — then spun out, rebranded, went on an early acquisition spree in the US and UK, and listed in 2007. In 2012 the RP-Sanjiv Goenka Group bought control from ICICI, and it has been an RPSG company ever since. Today RPSG (through RPSG Ventures) holds roughly 54%; the rest is public and institutional float.

  • Promoter: the RP-Sanjiv Goenka Group, via RPSG Ventures, holds ~54%. Group chairman Sanjiv Goenka chairs Firstsource. RPSG's roots are in power (CESC), with interests spanning FMCG, retail, media, sports and now BPM — Firstsource is the group's largest global-services asset.

The surprise hiding in the ownership

A power-utility-and-FMCG house from Kolkata — the people behind CESC and an IPL franchise — controls one of the more significant processors of American and British home loans. Through the Sourcepoint franchise, Firstsource sits inside the plumbing of the US and UK mortgage markets: boarding loans, servicing payments, handling defaults and title work. It is a genuinely counter-intuitive fact — an Indian conglomerate's BPM arm quietly running a slice of Anglo-American housing finance — and it is exactly why this company's earnings move to a drumbeat set in Washington, not Mumbai.

04

Sourcepoint: the mortgage engine — and the rate beta

The most distinctive thing about Firstsource, and the reason a US central-bank decision shows up in a Mumbai-listed P&L.

Inside the Banking & Financial Services vertical sits Firstsource's most unusual asset: Sourcepoint, its US mortgage-operations business, complemented by a sizeable UK financial-services and collections book. This is not generic call-centre work. Sourcepoint does the real operational labour of a home loan — origination support, underwriting, loan boarding, servicing the monthly payment, escrow and taxes, default and foreclosure administration, title and due diligence. For a mortgage lender or servicer, this is heavy, regulated, cyclical work — precisely the kind you want a specialist to run.

The number that rules BFS: US mortgage volume

A mortgage processor earns per loan it touches — per origination, per file, per loan serviced. So its volume is set by how many Americans are buying homes and refinancing, and that is governed by interest rates. When rates fall, a wave of refinancing and new purchases floods through the system, and origination-linked processing work surges. When rates are high — as through much of 2023–24 — origination volumes collapse to multi-decade lows, and that part of the revenue thins out. Servicing (running loans that already exist) is steadier and even counter-cyclical on the default side, but the origination-linked piece gives Firstsource a genuine "rate beta" that no pure Indian IT or healthcare BPM has. A US rate-cut cycle is, quite directly, a tailwind for this company.

Why this cuts both ways

  • The upside: after a brutal high-rate stretch that crushed US origination volumes, any sustained easing lifts refinance and purchase activity — and Firstsource is geared to catch the rebound. A recovering mortgage cycle is one of the clearest, most mechanical growth levers in the whole story.
  • The downside: the same beta hurts on the way down. A renewed spike in rates, or a US housing slump, directly shrinks origination-linked work. Concentrate enough revenue in a few large mortgage clients, and a single client's volume decision moves a quarter.
  • The stabiliser: servicing and default administration are stickier and partly counter-cyclical (more distress means more default work), which softens the swing. Firstsource has deliberately broadened the BFS book — collections, lending operations, KYC/onboarding — so it is less purely a refinance play than it once was.
How to read BFS each quarter: watch the US mortgage origination environment and the direction of rates, not just Firstsource's headline BFS growth. A soft mortgage market with Firstsource still growing BFS means it is winning share and adding non-origination work; a strong mortgage market is a rising tide that should lift the whole vertical.

Healthcare, customer management & the rest

The steadier half — the ballast that offsets the mortgage cycle.

If BFS is the cyclical engine, Healthcare is the ballast. At roughly a third of revenue, it is Firstsource's largest single vertical and among its most defensive — Americans get sick, submit claims and receive bills in good times and bad. Firstsource serves both sides of the US system: payers (health insurers — claims processing, member services) and providers (hospitals and clinics — patient access, eligibility, and revenue-cycle management, or "RCM," the end-to-end process of getting a provider paid for care delivered).

Healthcare demand does not swing with interest rates, and RCM in particular is sticky: once a hospital network hands you its billing engine, ripping it out is disruptive and risky. Communications, Media & Technology (CMT)

CMT — about a fifth of revenue — is the customer-management heartland: contact-centre and customer-experience operations for telecom and media clients. This is the most commoditised corner of the business (many providers can staff a support line) and therefore the most exposed to price pressure and to automation. It is also, for the same reason, where AI-driven productivity can most visibly either erode a seat-based contract or, handled well, let Firstsource deliver a better experience at lower cost and defend the account.

Diverse Industries

The smallest bucket (~13%) but the fastest-growing (~45% constant currency in FY26): utilities, retail, public sector and other newer verticals. This is where the domain-led expansion is being seeded — new logos in industries where Firstsource had little presence a few years ago. Small today, but it is the clearest evidence that the company can plant a flag in a new domain and scale it.

06

Seats: how a BPM really makes money

The one unit of economics you have to internalise — because it is the unit AI is about to attack.

For most of the BPM industry's life, the fundamental unit of production has been the seat — a trained agent at a desk, handling a queue of work. Revenue was, at root, price per seat × number of seats. Costs were dominated by the wages of those seats. Margin was the arbitrage between a Western client's in-house cost per seat and Firstsource's delivered cost per seat, minus the slice competition hands back to the client. Growth meant one thing above all: more seats.

This produces a recognisable financial signature — and it explains almost every line of a BPM's accounts:

  • People are ~two-thirds of the cost base. Firstsource employs ~36,200 people; the bulk are front-line delivery agents. When wages rise, margin is squeezed directly — there is no software licence to renegotiate, only payroll.
  • Margins are thin-ish and hard-won. An EBIT margin around 11–12% is respectable for volume BPM, but it is a fraction of an elite IT-services firm's — because the work is people-heavy and price-competitive.
  • Attrition is a permanent tax. FY26 attrition was ~30% — normal for the industry, but it means Firstsource must recruit and train thousands every year just to stand still.
  • Contracts are sticky but re-priced. Multi-year deals give revenue visibility, yet clients push for annual "productivity" — a built-in expectation that the same work costs a little less each year.
Seats versus outcomes — the axis the whole company is trying to move along

Here is the frame that makes sense of Firstsource's strategy. A seat-based BPM is, financially, a labour re-seller: it grows by adding bodies, and its revenue is capped by how many people it can hire and how cheaply. An outcome-based BPM is something else — it is paid for a result (dollars collected, claims cleared, a billing cycle shortened), so it can grow by getting more result per person. Move a contract from "pay me per seat" to "pay me per outcome," and you break the link between revenue and headcount. That is the promised land: revenue that can rise while the human cost base flattens. The entire "domain-led, digital-first" pivot — and the entire GenAI bet in §07 — is, underneath, one manoeuvre: drag the business off the seat and onto the outcome.

Why does this matter so much right now? Because a technology has arrived that can automate the exact repetitive tasks a seat performs. If Firstsource is still selling seats when that happens, the technology is a threat — it shrinks what clients will pay for a body. If Firstsource is selling outcomes when it happens, the same technology is a gift — it lets fewer people deliver more. Same tool, opposite consequences, depending entirely on which side of this axis the contract sits. That is the subject of the next section.

07

The GenAI question: solvent or accelerant?

The single debate that will decide whether Firstsource compounds or corrodes.

No industry sits more squarely in generative AI's blast radius than BPM. The work Firstsource sells — reading documents, answering routine queries, summarising calls, keying and checking forms, drafting collections letters — is precisely what large language models do cheaply and tirelessly. So the bear's syllogism is brutal and clean: AI can do the seat's job; the seat is what you sell; therefore AI destroys your revenue. If a client can automate a support queue, why pay for 500 agents?

The bull's answer is not a denial — it is an inversion. It runs like this:

  • Automation without an operator is a project, not a result. A bank can buy an AI model; it still has to redesign the process around it, integrate it into legacy systems, put governance and compliance guardrails on it, monitor it, retrain it, and handle every exception the machine kicks out. That is operational work — and running operations is what Firstsource does.
  • AI lowers the cost of delivering an outcome — which expands the market for outcomes. Processes that were too expensive to outsource profitably become viable when a human-plus-AI team is dramatically more productive. The pie of "work worth handing to a specialist" grows.
  • Clients want an AI-savvy operator, not a science project. Enterprises are wary of deploying autonomous AI into regulated, customer-facing processes alone. A partner that designs, builds and runs AI-enabled operations inside the client's own systems — with accountability for the result — is exactly what a cautious CFO will pay for.
"Intelligence that operates" and the Kairos framework

Firstsource's chosen phrase for this is "Intelligence that operates" — the pitch that it doesn't sell AI as a product, it runs AI-enabled operations and stands behind the outcome. Its proprietary Kairos framework is described as roughly two-and-a-half decades of domain know-how encoded as executable rules, governance guardrails and continuous-learning loops — the accumulated "how a mortgage-default queue actually works" turned into software that AI agents can operate inside. A concrete FY26 proof point: a large US financial-services client deployed AI agents designed by Firstsource for its collections process. The strategic tell is that management is deliberately steering toward outcome-priced AI work — the very move off the seat that §06 described — rather than defending seat counts.

Notice what makes this genuinely two-sided rather than spin. The bull case requires Firstsource to willingly compress its own seat-based revenue — to cannibalise today's model before a competitor or the client does it. If the new AI-and-outcome work grows faster than the old seat work shrinks, the company re-rates into a higher-value operator. If the seat erosion runs ahead of the new work, revenue and margin fall while the transition is still half-built. There is no comfortable middle where nothing changes; the seat is being repriced by the technology either way. The only question is whether Firstsource is on the right side of the trade when it happens.

The technology most feared to end business-process outsourcing is the one Firstsource has bet its next decade on turning into growth.
The genuinely two-sided risk: unlike a normal competitive threat, this one can be an accelerant and a solvent at once — expanding the addressable market while compressing the price of the legacy unit. Firstsource can win the new work and still see reported revenue wobble if legacy seats deflate faster in a given year. Judge it over several years and by mix, not by any single quarter's headline.
08

The domain-led pivot and the deal machine

How Firstsource is trying to buy and build its way up the value chain — and why it carries debt to do it.

The strategy under the current management has a clear shape: stop being a generic cost-arbitrage vendor and become a domain-led, digital-first operator that wins on deep industry expertise and AI-enabled delivery. Two levers drive it — organic capability-building (the AI stack, the Kairos framework, new-vertical logos) and a steady cadence of bolt-on acquisitions that buy capability, clients and domain depth faster than they could be built.

Watch whether the acquired revenue sticks and whether the debt trends down as EBITDA grows.

The one metric that settles the thesis

Strip everything else away and Firstsource's case rests on a single, checkable question: can it keep organic constant-currency revenue growing inside its guided 10–13% band while EBIT margin climbs to 12.25–12.75% — and can it show the mix shifting toward AI-enabled, outcome-priced work? Growth landing in-band with margin expanding is the proof that AI is adding revenue faster than it compresses seats. Growth in-band but margin stalling, or growth slipping below-band, would signal the seat-deflation is winning and the pivot is running behind. That trio — constant-currency growth, margin trajectory, and mix — is the scoreboard. At a low-30s P/E, the market is already paying for the good version of it; the numbers each half-year will confirm or deny whether that's warranted.

Two supports and one caution on the valuation. Supports: growth is genuinely faster than most peers, and margins are moving the right way with explicit guidance to back it. Caution: this is a net-debt, people-heavy, US-demand-exposed operator being valued closer to a structural AI winner than to a commodity BPO — a re-rating that only holds if the growth-and-mix story keeps delivering.

10

The risks, squarely

A frank inventory — because the same forces that power the story can reverse it.

  • GenAI disruption (genuinely two-sided). The deepest risk and the deepest opportunity are the same thing. If seat-based revenue deflates faster than AI-and-outcome work replaces it, both revenue and margin come under pressure while the transition is unfinished. This is not a risk that resolves quickly — it plays out over years.
  • Client & vertical concentration. A meaningful share of revenue sits with a handful of large clients; a big account's volume decision, insourcing move, or AI project can swing a quarter. The mortgage-heavy BFS book concentrates that further.
  • US mortgage / rate cyclicality. The very "rate beta" that is a tailwind in an easing cycle is a headwind if US rates spike again or housing stalls — origination-linked volumes shrink directly.
  • Wage inflation, FX & margin. A ~two-thirds-people cost base means wage inflation bites straight into margin, and near-total dollar/pound revenue against a rupee cost base makes reported numbers sensitive to currency swings.
  • Commoditisation of arbitrage. The original labour-cost edge erodes over time as wages in delivery hubs rise and rivals match it; without the move up the value chain, the core model slowly loses its differentiation.
  • Execution: pivot & integration. The domain-led/AI strategy and the debt-funded acquisition run both demand flawless execution — retained clients, delivered synergies, and net debt trending down as EBITDA grows. Any of these slipping undercuts the re-rating.
  • Valuation. A low-30s P/E on a net-debt BPM already discounts the "AI-enabled share-gainer" outcome; the market is not paying commodity-BPO multiples, so it expects the optimistic path to materialise.

The competitive set is a useful reality check. Firstsource competes with global BPM majors — WNS, Genpact, ExlService, Concentrix and Teleperformance in customer management, and healthcare-focused operators such as Sagility and IKS Health — plus the BPM arms of TCS, Infosys and other IT majors. Several are larger and cash-rich. Firstsource's answer is not scale but focus: deep positions in mortgage, healthcare RCM and collections, and a willingness to lead with AI-enabled, outcome-priced delivery earlier and more aggressively than incumbents comfortable defending seat counts.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A fast-growing operator making the industry's boldest bet on the technology it is most exposed to.

Firstsource enters FY27 in its strongest shape in years — a billion-dollar top line, industry-leading growth, expanding margins, a record pipeline, and a clear strategic identity. It also faces the most consequential technological shift of its life, one it has chosen to run toward rather than away from. The investment question is not whether the business is sound today — it plainly is — but whether the seats-to-outcomes transition outpaces the seat erosion that the same AI will cause.

The bull case

  • Growth leader — ~13.6% constant-currency growth in FY26, faster than most listed BPM peers, with FY27 guided 10–13%.
  • Margin on the up — EBIT 11.7% and explicitly guided toward 12.25–12.75%, with ROCE improving to ~17.7%.
  • Mortgage-cycle optionality — a rare "rate beta": a sustained US easing cycle is a mechanical tailwind for the BFS book.
  • AI as accelerant — "Intelligence that operates" and Kairos aim to turn the feared technology into a share-gaining, outcome-priced growth engine.
  • Supportive owner — RPSG's ~54% backing has funded an aggressive capability-buying strategy at speed.

The bear case

  • AI as solvent — the same technology can deflate seat-based revenue faster than new AI work replaces it, mid-transition.
  • Concentration & cyclicality — large-client dependence plus a rate-sensitive mortgage book concentrate the risk in a few decisions and one macro variable.
  • People-cost squeeze — wage inflation and ~30% attrition press directly on a thin-ish margin; FX adds noise.
  • Net debt & integration — debt-funded M&A must be integrated cleanly, unlike cash-rich peers who carry no such load.
  • Priced for the good outcome — a low-30s P/E already assumes the AI-winner narrative plays out.
The bottom line

Firstsource is a well-run, fast-growing BPM that has made an unusually candid wager: it is trying to move itself off the seat and onto the outcome before generative AI forces the issue, and to be the operator clients trust to run AI inside their most sensitive processes. The next several results will show, in three plain numbers, whether the wager is landing — constant-currency growth staying inside the 10–13% band, EBIT margin reaching 12.25–12.75%, and the revenue mix visibly tilting toward AI-enabled, outcome-priced work — with a US rate-cut cycle as the swing factor on the mortgage book. If those move together, a Kolkata power group will have turned a labour-arbitrage back office into an AI-era operator worth its re-rating. If the seat deflates faster than the outcome grows, the market will re-mark a company it had begun to price as a winner. Either way, this is the rare Indian services name whose story is written half in Washington's rate decisions and half in the pace of its own reinvention.
