Steel Authority of India, the state's steel furnace, mid-cycle and mid-rebuild

A complete walk through India's largest state-owned steelmaker — how a tonne of steel is actually made and priced (the "spread" between the steel price and the cost of iron ore and coal), why this is one of the most violently cyclical businesses on earth, and the three forces that define SAIL above all: its fully captive iron ore but heavily imported coking coal, its legacy PSU cost gap against nimbler private rivals, and the ~₹1 lakh crore push to grow from ~20 to 35 million tonnes a year. Behind it: a Maharatna PSU that booked FY26 revenue of ₹1.11 lakh crore and profit of ₹3,233 crore — best-ever volumes, thin-but-recovering margins, and 65% owned by the Government of India.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Steel Authority of India Limited (SAIL) is India's largest state-owned steelmaker — a Maharatna public-sector giant that runs five big integrated steel plants, digs its own iron ore, employs a huge workforce, and supplies the steel that goes into India's railways, bridges, buildings and defence. It makes roughly 19–20 million tonnes of crude steel a year, and the Government of India owns about 65% of it.

Four facts frame everything:

  • It's a deeply cyclical commodity business. SAIL doesn't set the price of steel — the market does. Its profits swing violently with domestic steel prices, Chinese oversupply and import pressure. FY26 revenue was ₹1,10,810 Cr (best-ever), EBITDA ₹13,146 Cr (a thin ~11.9% margin), and net profit ₹3,233 Cr — up ~50% on a weak FY25, but still a fraction of the ~₹10,000 Cr it earned in the boom of FY22.
  • Captive iron ore is its cost trump card; imported coking coal is its Achilles heel. SAIL mines 100% of its own iron ore from ~15 captive mines (a genuine cost edge), but has to import roughly three-quarters of its coking coal — so its costs (and margins) lurch with global coal prices it can't control.
  • It carries a legacy PSU cost gap. Older plants, a large workforce (wages are ~a fifth of its cost) and lower productivity mean SAIL earns far less EBITDA per tonne than private rivals JSW and Tata Steel. Closing that gap — via modernisation, de-bottlenecking and cutting semi-finished (low-value) output — is the core operational story.
  • A ₹1 lakh-crore expansion is under way. SAIL is spending big to grow from ~20 MTPA today toward ~35 MTPA by 2030, brownfield-expanding all five plants. Execution and funding of that capex is the swing factor for the next decade.
In plain terms

Think of SAIL as a national steel furnace, half-owned by the taxpayer. It takes iron ore it digs itself and coal it mostly buys from abroad, cooks them into molten iron, turns that into steel, and sells it — heavily to the government's own infrastructure and railway machine. Because steel is a global commodity, SAIL is a price-taker: when steel prices are high (and coal cheap) it mints money; when China is dumping cheap steel and coal is expensive, its thin margins get squeezed to nothing. Its two lifelong challenges are a higher cost base than private rivals and the need to keep reinvesting to grow and stay competitive. Own SAIL and you are really making a bet on the steel cycle, on India's infrastructure spending, and on a state-owned giant learning to run leaner.

01

Integrated steel 101: how a tonne of steel is made and earns

No engineering degree required — just the recipe and the "spread" that drives every rupee of profit.

"Integrated" steelmaking means doing the whole thing from scratch — starting with rock dug out of the ground and finishing with finished steel — all under one roof. SAIL is an integrated steelmaker, so to understand its economics you need to understand the recipe. It has three main ingredients and one master number.

Roughly ~1.6 tonnes of iron ore and ~0.7–0.8 tonnes of coking coal go into every tonne of crude steel. This is why raw-material sourcing decides who makes money.
  • Iron ore — the rock that contains the iron. Melt it and refine it and you get steel. It's the metal itself.
  • Coking coal — a special high-grade coal baked into "coke", which does two jobs in a blast furnace: it burns to create the heat, and it chemically strips oxygen from the iron ore so pure iron drops out. There is no blast-furnace steel without it.
  • Hot metal — the molten iron that pours out of the blast furnace. It's then processed to burn off carbon and impurities, cast into slabs/billets/blooms, and rolled into finished shapes.
The one number that matters: the spread

A steelmaker's profit is essentially the "spread" — the gap between what a tonne of steel sells for (the realisation) and what it costs to make that tonne (mostly ore + coal + power + labour). SAIL sold steel at a net realisation of roughly ₹57,900 per tonne in a recent quarter; if it can make that tonne for, say, ₹49,000, the ~₹8,000–9,000 difference is its EBITDA per tonne. The whole game is: steel price up, or cost down, widens the spread; steel price down, or coal up, crushes it. Because both sides move on global markets SAIL can't control, the spread — and therefore the profit — is wildly volatile. That single sentence explains why a company with ₹1.1 lakh crore of sales can earn ₹10,000 crore one year and ₹2,000 crore the next.

02

The steel cycle: why this is a rollercoaster, not an elevator

The single most important idea for any steel investor — and SAIL sits right in the eye of it.

Steel is a global commodity: near-identical product, made by hundreds of producers worldwide, priced on a world market. That makes SAIL a price-taker — it can push volumes and control costs, but it cannot set the price of steel. And steel prices are famously cyclical: they boom when the world is building and bust when it isn't. Understanding this cycle explains almost every wild swing in SAIL's earnings.

What drives the cycle

  • Demand: steel demand tracks construction, infrastructure, autos, capital goods and railways. When India (and the world) is building, mills run hot and prices firm; in a slowdown, demand and prices sag.
  • China — the 800-tonne gorilla: China makes over half the world's steel. When its own economy (especially property) is weak, Chinese mills export their surplus cheaply, flooding markets like India with low-priced imports that drag down domestic prices. This is the single biggest external threat to Indian steel margins.
  • Raw-material costs: the cost side (iron ore, and especially coking coal) has its own cycle. A spike in imported coal — as SAIL saw — can gut margins even when steel prices hold.
  • Policy — the referee: when imports get too painful, New Delhi steps in with safeguard duties or minimum import prices to protect domestic mills. In 2025 India imposed a temporary safeguard duty on certain flat-steel imports — a direct positive for SAIL.

Why SAIL swings harder than a private mill

A cyclical business with a high fixed-cost base has extra "operating leverage" — its profits amplify the cycle in both directions. SAIL's costs (a big workforce, five sprawling plants, heavy depreciation) are largely fixed, so when the spread is fat the extra revenue drops almost straight to profit; when the spread is thin, those same fixed costs eat the profit alive. Because SAIL's baseline margin is thinner than a JSW's or Tata's (see §06), it has less cushion before a downturn pushes it toward break-even. That's why SAIL is one of the more geared ways to bet on the Indian steel cycle: it flies highest in the good years and falls hardest in the bad ones.

03

Captive iron ore vs imported coking coal — the raw-material split

SAIL's defining cost lever: it owns one half of its recipe and imports the other. This is essential to understanding its margins.

Every integrated steelmaker lives or dies on the cost of two rocks: iron ore and coking coal. SAIL's situation is unusual and important — it is fully self-sufficient in iron ore (a genuine, structural cost advantage) but is heavily dependent on imported coking coal (a genuine, recurring cost risk). This split is the key to reading SAIL's margins.

SAIL is India's third-largest iron-ore producer (~34 MT dug in FY25), covering 100% of its own steel plants' ore needs — but only about a quarter of its coking coal comes from domestic sources; the rest is imported at global prices.

The good half: captive iron ore

  • SAIL owns a network of ~15 iron-ore mines across Jharkhand, Odisha and Chhattisgarh, dug ~34 MT of ore in FY25, and meets 100% of its steel plants' iron-ore requirement in-house.
  • Because it mines its own ore, SAIL is insulated from the price of iron ore — when ore prices spike (as they periodically do), rivals who buy ore in the market (or from NMDC) get squeezed while SAIL barely notices. This captive ore is a real, permanent cost moat.
  • It's also a contrast with a pure ore miner like NMDC (which sells ore and profits when ore is dear) — SAIL consumes its ore and profits when steel is dear. Same rock, opposite bet.

The risky half: imported coking coal

  • India simply doesn't have enough good-quality coking coal, so SAIL — like all Indian integrated mills — imports roughly three-quarters of it (only ~24% is met domestically).
  • Coking coal is a huge chunk of the cash cost — close to 40% of production cost — and its price is set on volatile global seaborne markets. When coal spikes, SAIL's margin gets hit hard and fast, with a lag as inventory works through.
  • Because it buys ahead and holds inventory, coal swings also create accounting one-offs: in early FY26 SAIL took an inventory write-down of ~₹1,000 Cr as coal prices fell (you book a loss when the coal on your books is now worth less than you paid); later quarters saw small positive reversals as prices recovered.
Why this split is the margin story

Put the two halves together and you get SAIL's raw-material personality: the metal is cheap and stable (owned ore), but the fuel is expensive and volatile (imported coal). So while captive ore gives SAIL a structural leg-up over ore-buying rivals, the imported-coal dependence means its margins still swing with a global commodity it can't hedge away. This is also why SAIL is chasing more domestic coking-coal sourcing and coal-blending efficiency, and why analysts watch the global coal price almost as closely as the steel price when forecasting SAIL's next quarter.

The auditor's jab: India's national auditor (CAG) has publicly criticised SAIL for leaning so heavily on imported coking coal despite owning coal blocks of its own — a reminder that raising domestic coal use is both a cost opportunity and a long-standing execution challenge for the company.
04

The company and who controls it

A Nehru-era nation-building project that became a listed Maharatna — still 65% owned by the state.

SAIL is one of independent India's founding industrial institutions. Its plants were built in the 1950s–60s as flagship projects of India's early five-year plans — often with Soviet, German and British technical help — to give a newly independent nation its own steel. That heritage explains both its scale and its old-economy cost structure. Today it is a listed company, but the Government of India still owns ~65% and controls it.

Timeline

  • 1950s–60s
    India builds its first public-sector integrated steel plants — Bhilai (Soviet help), Rourkela (German), Durgapur (British) — as nation-building projects under the early five-year plans.
  • 1973
    SAIL is formed as a holding company to bring the government's steel plants under one roof.
  • 1978–onwards
    Plants restructured directly under SAIL; Bokaro and later IISCO/Burnpur come into the fold — five integrated plants plus the Salem special-steel plant.
  • May 2010
    Granted Maharatna status — the top tier of PSU autonomy, letting it take large investment decisions without case-by-case government sign-off.
  • 2010s
    Completes a major modernisation & expansion programme lifting crude-steel capacity from ~12.8 to ~21.4 MTPA — but the ramp-up is slow and coincides with a weak steel cycle.
  • FY26
    Best-ever crude-steel production (19.43 MT) and sales (19.93 MT); PAT up ~50% to ₹3,233 Cr; net debt cut ~₹8,148 Cr; unveils the ~₹1 lakh-crore drive toward 35 MTPA by 2030.

Ownership & character

  • Owner: the President of India / Government of India ~65%. That makes SAIL a state-controlled enterprise — the government appoints the board, and steel policy, dividends and disinvestment plans sit with the Ministry of Steel.
  • Maharatna PSU: the highest grade of operational and financial autonomy given to a public-sector company — SAIL can commit large capex and form JVs on its own, but strategic control stays with the state.
  • What state ownership means for you: pros — implicit sovereign backing, priority in government/railway orders, and captive resource allocations. Cons — a social/employment mandate that keeps costs high, slower decision-making than a private mill, and the ever-present overhang of government disinvestment and policy interference.
05

The plant network

Five integrated steel plants, one special-steel plant, and a national footprint built by the state.

SAIL's ~20 MTPA of crude-steel capacity is spread across five integrated steel plants — each a self-contained city of blast furnaces, coke ovens and rolling mills — plus a special-steel plant and the captive mines that feed them. Each plant has a product personality, shaped by what it was built to make.

Why the plant mix matters

SAIL's product identity is set by these plants: it is heavy in "long" products and plates and rails (from Bhilai, Durgapur, IISCO) — exactly the steel that goes into railways, bridges, buildings and infrastructure — and lighter in high-value flat products (autos, appliances) where JSW and Tata are stronger. That mix ties SAIL's fortunes tightly to government infrastructure and railway spending (§08), and the modernisation plan (§07) is partly about rebalancing toward higher-value flats and away from low-value semi-finished steel.

06

The PSU cost gap — SAIL's defining challenge

Why the same tonne of steel earns SAIL less than it earns JSW or Tata — and why closing that gap is the whole operational story.

Here is the uncomfortable truth that hangs over SAIL: it makes steel less efficiently, and more expensively, than its best private rivals. On the same steel price, a JSW or a Tata Steel India typically earns a much fatter EBITDA per tonne than SAIL. This gap — a product of SAIL's history, ownership and vintage — is both its biggest weakness and its biggest self-help opportunity.

Where the gap comes from

  • A large legacy workforce. As a state enterprise with a social mandate, SAIL carries far more employees per tonne than a private mill. Wage cost is roughly a fifth of total cost — a big, sticky, largely-fixed burden. Historically, SAIL's output-per-employee has been a fraction of JSW's.
  • Older plants, lower productivity. Many of SAIL's furnaces and mills date back decades; they're less energy- and yield-efficient than a modern greenfield plant, and slower to run at full tilt.
  • A weaker product mix. A larger share of low-value semi-finished steel (billets, slabs sold on) instead of high-margin finished products drags the average realisation down.
  • PSU decision-making. Procurement, hiring and pricing move slower and with more constraints than at a nimble private rival.

Why the gap is also the opportunity

The flip side of being inefficient is that there's a lot of room to improve — and improvement drops straight to the bottom line. SAIL's self-help levers are concrete: cut semi-finished steel toward zero (it was ~10% of the mix and is being squeezed down, adding value on every tonne), de-bottleneck and modernise old furnaces to lift yield and cut coke consumption, raise domestic coking-coal use, and hold the line on headcount as older workers retire. Management has flagged that post-modernisation, EBITDA per tonne could climb toward ₹10,000+. Every notch SAIL narrows the gap to its private peers is a direct, self-generated re-rating lever — independent of the steel cycle.

The honest tension: closing a decades-old cost gap is hard, slow work, and the social/employment mandate that comes with state ownership caps how fast SAIL can cut. Bulls see a self-help story with years of runway; bears note that PSUs have promised efficiency gains before, and that the cycle usually matters more to the share price than the margin grind.
07

Modernisation & the ₹1 lakh-crore march to 35 MTPA

The biggest capital-allocation decision in SAIL's history — the opportunity, and the execution/funding risk.

After a decade of digesting its last expansion, SAIL has committed to a fresh, very large growth push: to lift crude-steel capacity from around 20 MTPA today toward ~35 MTPA by 2030, at a total outlay approaching ₹1 lakh crore. It's a brownfield programme — expanding all five existing plants rather than building new ones — which is cheaper and lower-risk than greenfield, but still a huge, multi-year bet.

The funding ramp

  • Annual capex is set to step up sharply: ~₹7,500 Cr in FY26, guided to ~₹15,000 Cr in FY27, and in excess of ~₹20,000 Cr in FY28 as projects hit peak construction.
  • Funding comes from a mix of internal cash flows and fresh borrowing — which is why the balance sheet matters. SAIL cut net debt by ~₹8,148 Cr in FY26 (borrowings down to ~₹31,900 Cr), deliberately building headroom before the capex wave.
  • The prize: post-expansion, a bigger, more modern, more efficient SAIL should earn a higher EBITDA per tonne on more tonnes — the compounding of volume growth and margin repair at once.
The capex tightrope

This is the classic heavy-industry balancing act. Spend big to grow and modernise, and — if you time it right — you emerge from the next downturn bigger, leaner and more profitable. But steel capex is lumpy, long-dated and cyclical-risk-laden: projects take years, cost overruns are common, and if the steel cycle turns down mid-build, you're carrying rising debt and depreciation against falling profits. SAIL's decision to deleverage first, then spend, is prudent — but the market will watch two things like a hawk: are projects on time and on budget, and is net debt staying under control as capex ramps toward ₹20,000 Cr a year. Get it right and this is a decade-long growth story; get the timing wrong and it's a balance-sheet stretch into a down-cycle.

08

The product mix — longs, plates, rails and the infra/railways engine

SAIL doesn't sell to everyone equally — it's wired into India's nation-building, and that's both a strength and a dependency.

Not all steel is the same, and SAIL's product mix is distinctive. It is heavy in "long" products, plates and — uniquely — rails, which makes it the go-to supplier for government infrastructure, railways, defence and construction. That gives it a stable, policy-driven demand anchor that private flat-steel players lack — but it also ties SAIL's fortunes to the government's capex tap.

Flats vs longs — the two families of steel

  • Flat products — hot- and cold-rolled coil, sheet, plate, galvanised steel. Go into autos, appliances, pipes and packaging. Generally higher-value; JSW and Tata dominate here. SAIL (Bokaro, Rourkela) is expanding its flat exposure.
  • Long products — bars, rods, structurals, TMT bars and rails. Go into construction, infrastructure and railways. SAIL is strong here — Bhilai is India's principal source of railway rails, a near-captive franchise with Indian Railways.

Why the infra linkage cuts both ways

India's push on infrastructure — highways, metros, railway modernisation, "dedicated freight corridors", housing — is a powerful structural demand tailwind for exactly the steel SAIL makes, and its rail franchise gives it a recurring, policy-backed order book that private mills can't easily contest. That's the bull case for SAIL's demand side. The catch: heavy dependence on government capex means SAIL's volumes lean on the fiscal cycle and the pace of project execution. When New Delhi is spending hard on infra, SAIL's order books fill; if government capex is squeezed (say, for fiscal reasons), the demand anchor loosens. And the modernisation drive (§07) is partly an attempt to diversify up the value chain into flats so SAIL is less of a pure play on public works.

09

China, imports & the safeguard duty

The external swing factor that can make or break a steel year — mostly beyond SAIL's control.

No discussion of an Indian steelmaker is complete without China. It makes over half the world's steel, and when its vast domestic demand (especially property) weakens, its mills export the surplus — cheaply — into open markets like India. Those imports set a ceiling on domestic steel prices and, in bad years, turn India into a net importer, squeezing every domestic mill's spread. This is the single biggest external variable in SAIL's earnings.

  • The mechanism: cheap Chinese (and other Asian) imports land at prices below Indian mills' costs, forcing domestic producers to either match the low price (losing margin) or lose the volume. Either way, the spread compresses.
  • The referee — safeguard duties: when import pressure gets acute, the government imposes temporary safeguard duties (a tax on imports) to give domestic mills breathing room. In 2025 India levied such a duty on certain flat-steel imports — a direct positive that helped domestic prices firm into FY26 and supported SAIL's recovery.
  • Global trade turbulence: tariffs and trade barriers elsewhere (e.g. US/EU steel tariffs) can redirect displaced tonnes toward India, worsening the import glut — a risk the whole sector watches.
Why this matters more than management does

For a price-taking commodity producer, the China/import/duty backdrop often swings the P&L more than anything SAIL's own managers do in a given year. A favourable setup — firm Chinese demand, cheap coal, an active safeguard duty — can lift SAIL's spread and profits sharply; the reverse can wipe them out even as the company runs flat-out. That's why the same "best-ever volumes" can coincide with soaring profits one year and modest ones the next. SAIL's self-help (cost cuts, mix upgrade, expansion) matters over the long run — but in any single year, keep one eye firmly on Chinese steel exports, the domestic-vs-import price gap, and whether Delhi's protective duty is on or off.

Financials & valuation

Big revenue, thin-and-volatile margins, and a cheap-cyclical multiple with a PSU dividend attached.

SAIL's financials read exactly like what it is: a giant, cyclical, capital-heavy commodity producer. Enormous revenue, but modest and swingy profit; a debt load that ebbs and flows with the cycle and capex; and a low valuation that reflects both the cyclicality and the PSU discount. FY26 was a recovery year — best-ever volumes and a rebounding spread lifted profit ~50%.

How to value a cyclical like SAIL

Valuing SAIL on trailing P/E is a trap. In a cyclical, the P/E looks highest when earnings are low (a trough) and lowest at the peak — the opposite of the intuitive signal. SAIL's ~18–21× P/E on depressed FY26 earnings doesn't mean it's expensive; on peak-cycle earnings the same price would be ~6–7×. That's why analysts lean on price-to-book (~1.1×) and EV/EBITDA instead — and on those, SAIL screens as a cheap, asset-heavy cyclical. The debate: is ~1.1× book fair for a business that earns a sub-cost-of-capital return through the cycle and carries a PSU discount — or is it a call option on the next up-cycle plus the self-help/expansion story? The small dividend (~1% yield) is a modest sweetener; SAIL is a growth-capex-and-cycle bet, not an income stock.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, cyclical, self-help-and-infra story — for investors who can stomach the swings.

SAIL is a genuine leveraged play on three things at once: the steel cycle, India's infrastructure build-out, and a state-owned giant slowly learning to run leaner and bigger. It is cheap on assets, volatile on earnings, and carries both the backing and the baggage of government ownership. The debate is less about the business model — which is well understood — and more about the cycle, execution and the PSU discount.

The bull case

  • Captive iron ore — 100% self-sufficient, a permanent cost edge over ore-buying rivals.
  • Volume + expansion — best-ever FY26 volumes, and a clear runway from ~20 toward ~35 MTPA by 2030.
  • Self-help margin repair — cutting semi-finished output, modernising furnaces, EBITDA/tonne guided toward ₹10,000+.
  • Infra/railway demand anchor — a near-captive rail franchise and structural exposure to India's capex boom.
  • Policy tailwind & cheap on book — safeguard duty support, deleveraging balance sheet, ~1.1× P/B with option value on the up-cycle.

The bear case

  • Deep cyclicality — a price-taker whose thin margins can collapse when steel prices fall or coal spikes.
  • Imported-coal exposure — ~75% of coking coal bought at volatile global prices; inventory write-downs recur.
  • PSU cost gap — high wages, older plants, lower productivity vs JSW/Tata; slow to fix.
  • Capex & execution risk — a ₹1 lakh-crore programme into a cyclical market; overruns and rising debt if the cycle turns.
  • China/import overhang & state control — Chinese dumping caps prices; government ownership brings a social mandate, policy risk and a disinvestment overhang.
The bottom line

SAIL is India's steel furnace, mid-cycle and mid-rebuild — a large, cheap, deeply cyclical PSU with a real cost advantage in ore, a real cost handicap in coal and labour, and a big bet on volume growth and margin repair. It is not a quality compounder to buy and forget; it is a geared play on the steel cycle and India's infrastructure spend, with a self-help kicker if management can narrow the efficiency gap and land the expansion without over-stretching the balance sheet. The three numbers to track each quarter: the steel-price/coking-coal spread (the cycle), EBITDA per tonne (is the self-help working?), and net debt vs capex (is the expansion affordable?). Get a friendly cycle on top of executing self-help, and a cheap cyclical re-rates hard; get a down-cycle mid-capex, and the same leverage bites the other way.
