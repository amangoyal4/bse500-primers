CIE Automotive India, the Spanish-owned forge

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

CIE Automotive India makes the metal "guts" of vehicles — the heavy, safety-critical steel and aluminium parts hidden inside the engine, gearbox, axle and chassis of cars, trucks, tractors and two-wheelers. It is a Tier-1 auto-components supplier spread across six different manufacturing processes — forging, stamping, iron and aluminium casting, gears and machining, magnetics and composites — with the largest slice by far being forgings: crankshafts, front axle beams, steering knuckles, connecting rods and gear blanks, literally shaped by squeezing red-hot steel between dies.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is Mahindra-founded but Spanish-controlled. The business began life as the Mahindra Group's forgings and components arm; in 2013 Spain's CIE Automotive S.A. took majority control, and the company — once called Mahindra CIE Automotive — was renamed CIE Automotive India in 2022–23. Mahindra has since sold its entire stake. Today the Spanish parent owns ~65.7%, and — unusually for an Indian company — the accounts run on a January–December calendar year, not the Indian April–March.
  • Two businesses stapled together. India (~65% of revenue) is a growing, high-teens-margin forging-led business; Europe (~35%) is a mature, lower-margin operation being surgically pruned. In CY2025 consolidated revenue was ~₹9,122 Cr, EBITDA margin ~16%, and net profit ~₹828 Cr — with India doing the heavy lifting and Europe the drag.
  • It is largely indifferent to the EV shift. A forged axle beam, a machined gear or a stamped chassis bracket is needed whether a vehicle runs on a diesel engine or an electric motor — and some EV parts (inverter housings, e-drive gears, battery enclosures) are new business. That "powertrain-agnostic" quality is the reason a metal-basher gets talked about as a structural story rather than a cyclical one. The balance sheet is net cash (~₹1,881 Cr), and the stock trades around 21× earnings.
In plain terms

CIE India is a metal-processing job-shop at industrial scale. Car makers, truck makers and tractor makers design a part, hand CIE the drawing, and CIE turns raw steel bar and aluminium into a finished, machined component delivered to the assembly line — a crankshaft here, a front axle beam there, a gear somewhere else. It doesn't own the vehicle brand or the customer; it owns the process know-how — how to forge a defect-free crankshaft at ~1,200°C, thousands a day, to a tolerance measured in fractions of a millimetre. That process expertise, spread across six technologies and welded to decades-old OEM relationships, is the whole moat. The catch: it lives and dies with how many vehicles India and Europe actually build each year.

01

Auto components & forging 101: how you shape hot steel

No engineering degree required — just what these parts are and why they're hard to make.

Every vehicle is thousands of parts bolted together, and almost none of them are made by the company whose badge is on the bonnet. Carmakers assemble; a pyramid of suppliers make. CIE sits near the top of that pyramid as a "Tier-1" supplier — it ships finished components straight to the vehicle maker (the OEM). Below it, Tier-2 and Tier-3 firms supply raw materials and sub-parts. CIE's specialty is the hard, heavy, load-bearing metal parts — the ones that, if they fail, the vehicle fails.

The four ways to shape metal — and why forging is the toughest

There are only a handful of ways to turn a lump of metal into a precise part, and CIE does most of them under one roof. The differences matter, because they determine cost, strength and how hard the business is to enter.

The metal-shaping toolkit CIE operates
ProcessWhat happensWhat it makes for CIE
ForgingHeat a steel billet to ~1,200°C and squeeze it between dies under enormous force (giant presses / hammers). The grain flows around the shape, giving exceptional strength.Crankshafts, front axle beams, steering knuckles, connecting rods, gear blanks — the safety-critical drivetrain & chassis parts.
CastingMelt metal fully and pour it into a mould. Good for complex shapes; iron for strength, aluminium for lightness.Housings, brackets, structural iron and aluminium parts (incl. EV battery enclosures, inverter housings).
StampingPress flat sheet metal between dies to cut and bend it into shape — fast and cheap at volume.Chassis frames, body-in-white sub-assemblies, brackets — big in the European operations.
Machining & gearsCut, drill and grind a rough part (often a forging) on CNC machines to final precision; cut gear teeth.Finished gears, machined forgings, transmission components. The value-adding finish on top of forging.
Why forging is a high-barrier business

Forging is not glamorous, but it is genuinely hard. A single forging press can cost tens of crores; a forging line needs furnaces, heavy presses, die shops, heat-treatment and metallurgical labs — capital that sits idle if orders dry up. Getting a crankshaft to survive millions of engine cycles without a hairline crack takes years of process control and OEM validation. Once a carmaker has approved your line for a part, switching suppliers means re-validating everything — so incumbency is sticky and pricing holds up better than in lighter, commoditised components. High capital + long qualification + safety-critical failure modes = a moat built out of barriers to entry, not brand.

02

The business model: how a metal supplier actually earns

Volumes × content × margin — with steel passing straight through.

CIE's economics come down to three things: how many vehicles get built (volume), how much CIE content sits in each one (content-per-vehicle), and what margin it keeps after steel and energy. Understand those three and you understand the whole engine.

Steel is a pass-through, not a bet

Raw material — mostly alloy steel, plus aluminium — is by far the largest cost line, often more than half of revenue. Crucially, most CIE contracts have steel-price pass-through clauses: when steel gets more expensive, CIE raises prices to the OEM (usually with a lag of a quarter or so), and when steel falls, prices come down. So CIE is not primarily a bet on the steel price. It matters mainly through timing — the lag between a steel move and the price adjustment can help or hurt a single quarter's margin — and because a higher steel price inflates the revenue number without adding real profit.

The number that actually matters: content-per-vehicle

Because steel washes through, the revenue that CIE truly controls is value-added content per vehicle — how much forging, machining and finishing it puts into each car, truck or tractor. A supplier can grow faster than the auto market in two ways: win more parts per vehicle (a crankshaft and the knuckles and the gears), or move up the value chain (ship a finished machined part instead of a raw forging). When CIE's India revenue grows faster than India's underlying vehicle production, that gap is content-per-vehicle gain — and it's the cleanest sign the business is compounding rather than just riding the cycle.

Capital intensity cuts both ways

  • The upside: heavy presses, furnaces and machining lines are expensive to build and to qualify — which keeps new entrants out and lets incumbents earn decent returns on capital (CIE runs a RONA of ~18% at the India level).
  • The downside: those same assets are fixed costs. In a good year the plant runs full and margins expand; in a downturn the presses sit half-idle and the fixed cost crushes margin. This operating leverage is exactly why an auto-components maker's profits swing more than its revenue.
  • The discipline: CIE guides capex at roughly 5% of sales for organic growth and has spent years paying down debt — it now sits net-cash, which is unusual and valuable in a capital-hungry industry.
The relationship moat: a carmaker validates a supplier's line for a specific part over months of testing before a single unit ships. Once qualified, CIE is designed into that vehicle programme for its whole life (often 5–8 years). That makes revenue sticky — but it also means winning new content happens at the design stage of the next vehicle, years before it shows up in sales. New-order wins today are a preview of revenue two to three years out.
03

The company and who controls it

A Mahindra creation that ended up flying a Spanish flag.

This is the surprise that reframes the whole company: a business the Mahindra Group founded and grew is today majority-owned and controlled by a Spanish multinational, renamed to erase the Mahindra name, and reporting on a European calendar — with Mahindra itself now completely out. The corporate history explains why.

Timeline

  • Dec 2022

Ownership & the calendar quirk

  • Promoter: CIE Automotive S.A. (Spain), holding ~65.7% via its subsidiary Participaciones Internacionales Autometal Dos S.L. The rest is public — a large institutional and retail free float.
  • The January–December calendar. Most Indian companies close their books on 31 March. CIE India closes on 31 December — because its Spanish parent consolidates on a calendar year and wants its Indian subsidiary on the same clock. So when this primer says "CY2025," it means the year ended December 2025, and "Q1 CY2026" is January–March 2026. It's a small thing that trips up first-time readers comparing CIE to Indian peers on April–March years.
  • What the Spanish parent brings: global technology transfer (CIE Automotive is a large, diversified multi-technology group across Europe, the Americas and Asia), management and process discipline, and access to global OEM programmes. What it also brings is related-party dynamics — the parent controls the board, sets strategy, and its own restructuring decisions (e.g. European plant closures) flow through CIE India's numbers.
The tension of control: minority shareholders in CIE India are along for a ride steered from Bilbao. The parent's interests and the minority's usually align — a well-run, cash-generative subsidiary is good for everyone — but on questions like capital return, related-party pricing, and which assets get rationalised, the controlling shareholder has the wheel. That governance reality is part of the investment case, not a footnote.
04

Six technologies under one roof

The diversification that stops any single part, process or customer from sinking the ship.

Most auto-ancillary companies are a one-trick shop — a pure forger, a pure caster, a pure gear-maker. CIE deliberately spans six process technologies: forging, stamping, casting (iron & aluminium), gears/machining, magnetics and composites. That breadth is a genuine risk-reducer — a slump in one process or one customer's model doesn't take the whole company down — and it lets CIE offer a carmaker several parts from one qualified supplier.

What each technology actually supplies

  • Forging — the crown jewel and ~half of India. Hot-forged steel crankshafts, front axle beams, steering knuckles, connecting rods, stabiliser bars, CV joints, differential crowns, gear blanks and spindles. These are the safety-critical, high-barrier parts and where the pricing power lives.
  • Stampings — pressed sheet-metal chassis and body sub-assemblies; volume-driven, thinner-margin, more important in Europe.
  • Castings (iron & aluminium) — structural housings and brackets. Aluminium is the growth end: lighter parts for fuel efficiency and for EVs (battery enclosures, inverter housings). The aluminium-casting subsidiary is being merged into the parent for efficiency.
  • Gears & machining — cutting gear teeth and finishing forgings into ready-to-fit transmission parts; the value-added layer that sits on top of raw forging.
  • Magnetics & composites — small but specialised: soft-magnetic components and composite parts, niche technologies that round out the portfolio.
Why multi-technology is more than a slogan

Diversification here does real work. First, it spreads risk: when tractors boom but two-wheelers slump, a supplier tied to one part on one platform suffers, while CIE — present across drivetrain, chassis and transmission parts on many platforms — averages out. Second, it makes CIE stickier with each customer: a carmaker that buys forgings, machined gears and aluminium castings from one qualified partner isn't going to swap it out lightly. Third, it gives CIE more shots at winning new EV content — because whichever way the EV part mix breaks (more castings? more gears? more machined structural parts?), CIE already runs that process. Breadth is optionality.

05

India vs Europe: two businesses on one calendar

The single most important structural fact about CIE's profit today.

CIE India is really two companies stapled at the balance sheet. One is a growing, high-teens-margin Indian forging business riding a domestic auto cycle. The other is a mature, low-teens-margin European operation being carefully shrunk and repaired. Almost every swing in CIE's reported profit over the last three years traces back to which of the two was winning.

The one chart that tells the story: flat revenue, shrinking profit

Here is the analytical crux, in three numbers. Consolidated revenue was ~₹9,280 Cr in CY2023 and ~₹9,122 Cr in CY2025 — essentially flat over two years. Yet net profit fell from ~₹1,125 Cr (CY2023) to ~₹828 Cr (CY2025) — a ~₹300 Cr drop. That entire decline is Europe cooling: European auto and truck demand slid, plants ran below capacity, and margins compressed, even taking a hit from a Spanish plant (Legazpi) restructuring and a labour-code gratuity charge in CY2025. India, meanwhile, kept quietly growing right through it. So CIE's recent "stagnation" isn't the whole company stalling — it's a strong India engine being masked by a weak European one. Whether the stock re-rates depends on which force wins from here.

The European repair job

  • The footprint: CIE's European operations sit in Italy, Spain, Lithuania and Mexico — gears in Italy, crankshaft forging in Spain (the CIE Galfor legacy), forging in Lithuania. Over the past decade the group has exited weaker European forging (German and UK units sold) to concentrate on the better plants.
  • The problem: European light-vehicle and truck demand has been soft, and energy costs high — a tough backdrop for capital-intensive metal plants. CY2024 Europe revenue fell ~14%; restructuring costs dented CY2025 margins.
  • The turn: by Q1 CY2026, Europe revenue was actually up ~17% year-on-year, helped by prior restructuring and currency — a tentative sign the repair is working. Management hasn't ruled out further portfolio surgery.
The strategic read: the direction of travel is unmistakable — CIE is becoming more Indian and less European, both because India grows faster and because Europe is being pruned. For an investor, that's the point: buy CIE and you're buying a bet that the high-margin India forge keeps compounding while the European drag gets smaller and, eventually, stops being a drag.
06

The customers and the auto cycle

Who buys CIE's parts — and why the business breathes with vehicle production.

CIE supplies the whole spread of Indian vehicle makers across every segment: passenger cars, commercial vehicles (trucks and buses), tractors, two-wheelers and off-highway. That end-market diversity is a strength — but it also means CIE is directly geared to the auto production cycle. When India builds more vehicles, CIE forges more parts; when volumes stall, its presses idle.

CIE's India revenue grew ~8% in CY2025, broadly with — and in places ahead of — underlying vehicle production, aided by content gains. The spread between CIE's growth and vehicle-production growth is the content-per-vehicle signal to watch.

The Mahindra legacy — and why concentration has eased

Historically, the Mahindra Group was CIE's anchor customer — natural, given the company's origins. That once meant meaningful customer concentration: a big single-OEM dependence that made CIE vulnerable to one carmaker's model cycle. Two things have diluted it: CIE has broadened its customer base across two-wheeler makers, tractor makers, and passenger- and commercial-vehicle OEMs; and the Bill Forge acquisition brought a different, precision-forging customer set. Concentration is lower than it was — but the big Indian OEMs still matter a great deal, and a downturn at any of them would be felt.

Why cyclicality is the price of admission

Auto-component makers cannot escape the vehicle cycle — their demand is vehicle production. In a strong year, plants run full and operating leverage lifts margins; in a weak one, fixed costs bite and margins fall. CIE's defences against the cycle are its end-market diversity (cars, trucks, tractors and two-wheelers rarely all slump together), its multi-technology mix, and increasingly its content growth (winning more parts per vehicle so it can grow even in a flat market). What it can't do is decouple entirely — which is why a reader should always hold CIE's results next to the broader India auto-production trend before judging them.

07

The EV question: why a forge barely blinks

The structural force that decides whether CIE is a value trap or a survivor.

Every auto-components investor now asks the same first question: what does the shift from petrol/diesel to electric do to this business? For companies that make engine and fuel parts — pistons, fuel-injection systems, exhaust components — the answer can be existential, because an EV simply deletes those parts. For CIE, the answer is unusually reassuring: most of what it makes doesn't care what turns the wheels.

Powertrain-agnostic — decoded

A vehicle still needs a chassis, an axle, steering, suspension and a way to transmit torque to the wheels — whether the power comes from a combustion engine or an electric motor. Those systems are full of forged and machined metal. Consider CIE's actual product list against the EV transition:

So the honest picture is nuanced, not a free pass: the crankshaft/connecting-rod slice is genuinely at risk in a full-EV world, and that's a real, forged, high-value product for CIE. But it is one slice. The chassis, axle, steering and gear forgings carry straight across — sometimes with more content, because EVs are heavier and need beefier structural parts — and the aluminium and casting side actively gains new EV-only parts.

The genuine surprise for a generalist

The instinct is that an "engine parts" company must be an EV loser. The counter-intuitive reality is the opposite for CIE: because a forge shapes structural metal — chassis, axles, gears, housings — rather than combustion-specific parts, the EV transition is closer to a content-mix reshuffle than a demand cliff. CIE is already booking EV orders (inverter housings, e-drive gears, battery enclosures, parts for electric two- and three-wheelers). The company that looks most exposed on a first glance is, on inspection, one of the better-insulated — its risk is concentrated in a single product line (crankshafts), not the whole catalogue.

08

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, capital-heavy field where CIE competes as the diversified generalist.

Indian auto-components is a large, fragmented industry, but the forging and machined-parts niche has a recognisable set of serious players. CIE's distinctive position is breadth — it is not the biggest pure forger, but it is one of the most diversified multi-technology suppliers, with a global parent behind it.

Where CIE sits in the pack

CIE isn't trying to be the biggest forger — Bharat Forge owns that title — nor the purest EV-driveline play, which the market awards to Sona BLW at a much richer multiple. Its niche is being the diversified, multi-process, cash-generative supplier that a carmaker can lean on for several kinds of parts at once, backed by a global parent's technology. That positioning trades away the "exciting single theme" premium (which is why CIE is cheaper than Sona) for resilience — fewer ways for any one shock to hurt it. The competitive question is whether breadth-and-stability can compound value as fast as the market rewards focused, high-growth peers.

The macro backdrop: Indian auto-component demand rides three tailwinds — rising vehicle production, rising content-per-vehicle (safety, emissions, features, premiumisation), and a "China+1"/localisation push nudging global OEMs to source more from India. CIE is positioned for all three, with the added lever of feeding parts into its parent's global programmes. The offsetting headwinds are the cycle, steel/energy costs and the long, uncertain EV transition.

The one metric that settles the thesis

For CIE, the number that resolves the bull–bear argument is India's revenue growth relative to underlying vehicle production, held against a stable ~17–18% India EBITDA margin — the content-per-vehicle spread. If India keeps growing several points faster than India builds vehicles and holds its margin, then CIE is compounding real value: it's winning more parts and richer processes per vehicle, and the flat consolidated headline is just Europe temporarily masking it. If India's growth collapses back to bare vehicle-production growth and the margin slips, then CIE is simply a cyclical metal supplier and ~21× is already fair. Everything else — Europe's repair, the EV order book, steel timing — feeds into that single spread. Q1 CY2026's ~15% India growth on strong margins is an early tick in the bull's favour; it needs to persist.

Growth runway & capital allocation

India capex, an EV order book, a simpler structure, and cash coming back.

CIE's growth levers are unglamorous but real: put capital into the India business where returns are highest, convert the new-order book (including EV content) into revenue, keep pruning and repairing Europe, and hand surplus cash back to shareholders. Management is guiding capex at ~5% of sales, weighted toward India.

  • Volume — India auto production trending up across two-wheelers, tractors, PVs and CVs, helped by GST cuts and a broad recovery.
  • Content — more parts and richer, more-machined parts per vehicle, plus EV-specific wins that add content the ICE vehicle never had.
  • Mix & margin — a rising India share (higher margin) and a repaired Europe (less drag) can lift the blended margin even without heroic top-line growth.
The next chapter is less about the crankshaft that spins inside a shrinking pool of engines, and more about the axle beams, e-drive gears and battery enclosures that get forged and cast whichever way the powertrain war ends.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A resilient, cash-rich supplier — waiting for India to out-shout Europe.

CIE Automotive India is a well-run, diversified, net-cash metal-components maker with a genuine forging moat, a global parent's technology, and — unusually for an "engine-era" ancillary — a product mix that largely survives the EV transition. It is not a rocket ship. The investment debate is whether a diversified, EV-agnostic, parent-backed supplier deserves to re-rate above its cyclical-metal-basher multiple.

The bull case

  • EV-resilient mix — chassis, axle, steering and gear forgings carry across to EVs; aluminium/castings gain; only crankshafts are truly at risk.
  • India engine compounding — ~17–18% margins, ~18% RONA, growing ahead of vehicle production on content gains.
  • Net cash & disciplined — rare in a capital-heavy sector; ~70% payout, room for M&A or buybacks.
  • Diversification — six technologies, many end-markets, broadened customer base — few single points of failure.
  • Europe turning — restructuring largely done; Europe back to growth in Q1 CY2026, removing the profit drag.

The bear case

  • Cyclicality — demand is vehicle production; a CV/tractor/PV downturn idles capital-heavy presses and compresses margin.
  • European overhang — soft demand, high energy costs and restructuring have masked India's growth for two years; the turn must stick.
  • Controlled by Spain — minority holders don't set strategy; related-party and portfolio decisions sit with the parent.
  • Crankshaft exposure & steel timing — the one product line EVs delete is high-value; steel/energy cost lags can bruise a quarter.
  • Re-rating not guaranteed — flat headline profit since CY2023 gives the market reason to keep it at an ancillary multiple, not a compounder's.
The bottom line

Strip away the headline stagnation and CIE is a high-margin Indian forge shackled to a convalescing European partner, priced at ~21× as if the shackle were permanent. The whole case is arithmetic: does the India business grow its content-per-vehicle fast enough, at a steady ~17–18% margin, to out-shout a Europe that has finally stopped bleeding? Watch three concrete markers through CY2026 — the India revenue-vs-production spread (is content still being won?), the India EBITDA margin (does it hold ~18%?), and the ₹300 Cr EV order book converting into shipped parts. When the forge in Bangalore hammers a glowing billet at 1,200°C, it doesn't know or care whether the axle it becomes ends up under a diesel pickup or an electric scooter — and that indifference, more than anything else, is what a buyer of this stock is really underwriting.
