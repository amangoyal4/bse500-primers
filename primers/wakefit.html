Wakefit, from bed-in-a-box to whole-home

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Wakefit Innovations Limited is a direct-to-consumer (D2C) "sleep and home solutions" brand from Bengaluru. It began in 2016 as an online mattress company — the "bed-in-a-box" model, where a foam mattress is vacuum-compressed, rolled into a manageable carton, and shipped straight to a customer's door after they order it on a website. From that single product it has spread into a broad home and furniture range: beds, sofas, wardrobes, study tables, bookshelves, home décor and sleep accessories such as pillows and bedsheets. It sells this range omni-channel — through its own website and app, through marketplaces like Amazon and Flipkart, and, increasingly, through a fast-growing chain of its own physical experience stores. It makes much of what it sells itself, in its own manufacturing units, and it listed on the NSE and BSE in December 2025.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a brand-and-supply-chain business, not a marketplace. Unlike a Pepperfry or an Amazon that lists other people's furniture, Wakefit designs, manufactures and sells its own products under its own name. That vertical integration is the source of its margin control and its identity — the whole case rests on owning the product, not renting a shelf.
  • It is going from clicks to bricks, deliberately. Wakefit was born online but has spent recent years opening company-owned experience stores — roughly 105 by FY25, from just 23 two years earlier — plus placing product in over 1,500 multi-brand outlets. Furniture and mattresses are things people want to sit on and lie on before buying; the offline push is both a growth engine and a way to acquire customers more cheaply than pure digital advertising.
  • Profitability only just arrived — and it is fragile. Wakefit lost money for years while it invested in brand and stores. In FY26 it reported its first annual net profit as a listed company, ~₹189 Cr on ~₹1,489 Cr of revenue — but ~₹98 Cr of that was a one-off accounting gain (a deferred-tax asset), not operating cash. Strip it out and the underlying profit is a thin sliver on a business that lost ~₹35 Cr the year before. The turn is real but young.
In plain terms

Wakefit is what you get when a scrappy online mattress start-up grows up. It cut out the middleman — no dealer markup, no showroom rent in the early years — by selling foam mattresses directly through a website and shipping them in a box. That worked, so it added more products (furniture, décor) and more ways to buy (its own stores, marketplaces). Because it manufactures its own goods, it keeps the margin a traditional brand would hand to factories and distributors. The catch is that furniture and mattresses are big, considered, infrequent purchases, so winning each customer costs real money in advertising and, now, in store rent and staff. For a decade that spending outran the profit. FY26 is the first year the maths tipped the other way — and even then, half the reported profit is an accounting entry rather than a rupee earned. The business is at the hinge between "growing fast and losing money" and "growing fast and keeping some."

01

The opportunity: a huge, unbranded home market moving online

Why a mattress-and-furniture brand can be a big business in India.

To understand why Wakefit exists, start with the market it sells into. Indians spend a great deal on their homes — mattresses, beds, sofas, wardrobes, and the accessories around them — but the vast majority of that spending has historically gone to unbranded, unorganised sellers: the local carpenter who builds a wardrobe to order, the neighbourhood mattress shop stitching a cotton-and-coir bed, the furniture bazaar with no brand name over the door. Three structural shifts are slowly pulling that money toward branded, organised, often online players like Wakefit. Each is a tailwind that, if it plays out, expands Wakefit's addressable pool for years.

The three shifts that make the market
ShiftWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Unorganised → brandedMost Indian home and mattress spending still goes to unbranded makers. As incomes rise, buyers increasingly want a name they can trust for quality, warranty and consistency. Every rupee that moves from the local carpenter to a brand is new addressable market for Wakefit — the single largest driver behind the whole category.
Offline → online (and omni)Indians have grown comfortable buying big-ticket, considered items online — helped by easy returns, EMI, and trial periods. Furniture and mattresses, once thought "too physical to buy on a screen," are now sold digitally at scale. The end-state is omni-channel: research online, touch it in a store, buy on either.
The "sleep economy"A global and Indian rise in health-consciousness has turned sleep into a product category. Mattresses, pillows, ergonomic bases and accessories are now marketed on wellness and posture, not just price — pushing up ticket sizes and creating room for premium, brand-led selling.
The structural fact that shapes the whole sector: home buying is "considered," not impulsive

A mattress or a sofa is bought rarely — every several years — costs a meaningful chunk of a household's monthly income, and is chosen carefully, often after physically testing it. That single fact drives everything downstream in this business. It means customer acquisition is expensive (you cannot win a buyer with a cheap impulse ad; you must be present at the rare moment they are shopping). It means repeat purchases are slow (nobody buys a mattress monthly), so a brand must widen its range to sell the same household more things over time. And it means trial matters — which is exactly why a company born online eventually has to build physical stores. The economics of the "considered purchase" explain both Wakefit's opportunity and its hardest problem.

How big, and how empty

  • The mattress market — India's organised mattress market runs into the tens of thousands of crore, but branded players still hold only a minority of it; the rest is unorganised. That "empty" space is the room to grow into.
  • The furniture market — even larger and even more fragmented, dominated by local carpentry and small workshops. Branded, organised furniture is a small slice of a very big pie — which is why Wakefit's expansion beyond mattresses into furniture is a far bigger prize than mattresses alone.
  • The online slice — still a minority of both categories, but the fastest-growing part, and the one where a digital-first brand like Wakefit was built to win before extending into physical retail.
02

Bed-in-a-box, then everything: the model explained

The clever logistics idea Wakefit was founded on — and why it kept expanding.

Wakefit's original insight was not a new mattress; it was a new way to sell and ship one. A traditional mattress reaches a buyer through a long chain — factory, distributor, wholesaler, retail showroom — each link adding cost and markup, and the showroom carrying only a handful of bulky beds it must store and display. Wakefit collapsed that chain. It is worth seeing the two models side by side, because the difference is the entire founding logic of the company.

By compressing a foam mattress into a box, Wakefit turned an unshippable object into a parcel — which let it skip the showroom entirely and sell nationally from a website. That logistics trick is the company's origin story.

Two things made this work. First, foam: memory foam and similar materials can be vacuum-compressed and will spring back to full size after unboxing, so the mattress ships small and expands at home. Second, the direct-to-consumer model: by selling on its own website rather than through dealers, Wakefit captured the distribution margin itself, could offer aggressive prices, and — crucially — owned the customer relationship and the data. It also removed a classic buyer objection with a long risk-free trial (return the mattress within a trial window if you don't like it), which is far easier to offer when you control the whole chain.

But a mattress is bought once every several years, so a pure mattress brand hits a ceiling: it wins a customer, then has almost nothing else to sell them for years. The answer was range expansion. Having earned a household's trust with a mattress, Wakefit began selling the rest of the bedroom and then the rest of the home — bed frames, pillows and bedsheets first (natural neighbours of the mattress), then sofas, wardrobes, study tables, storage and décor. Each new category lifts the average order value, deepens the relationship, and turns a one-mattress customer into a repeat home-furnishing customer. The strategic arc is simple: use the mattress to acquire, use the home range to monetise.

Why the expansion into furniture is the real story, not the mattress

It is tempting to file Wakefit under "mattress company." That undersells the plan. The mattress is the door — a trusted, relatively simple product to win a first-time buyer and build brand recognition. The house is the higher-ticket, far larger furniture opportunity behind that door: beds, sofas and wardrobes cost multiples of a mattress and address a market several times bigger and even more unbranded. Wakefit's future revenue and its case for being more than a niche brand ride on how well it converts mattress buyers into whole-home buyers, and how much of India's fragmented furniture spending it can pull onto its own branded, vertically-integrated platform. Watch the furniture mix, not the mattress headline.

03

What Wakefit actually sells

A sleep core, a fast-widening home range, and the channels that carry them.

Wakefit's catalogue splits into a sleep and mattress core — the products it was built on and still leads with — and a broader, faster-growing home and furniture range layered on top. The two have different economics: the sleep core is higher-frequency-of-search, brand-defining and relatively standardised; furniture is higher-ticket, more design-and-logistics-intensive, and the bigger long-run pool. Understanding the split is understanding the business.

The product lines

  • Mattresses — the founding product and the brand's anchor. Foam and hybrid mattresses across price points, sold with trial periods and long warranties. The category that acquires the customer and carries the reputation.
  • Sleep accessories — pillows, bedsheets, comforters, mattress protectors, back-support and other add-ons. Lower ticket but higher frequency and easy to attach to a mattress sale; they raise order value and keep the brand present between big purchases.
  • Furniture — the strategic growth engine: bed frames, sofas, recliners, wardrobes, study and office tables, bookshelves, shoe racks and storage. Higher-ticket, larger addressable market, more design and last-mile complexity — the category that determines whether Wakefit becomes a genuine home brand.
  • Home & décor — furnishings, lighting and décor that round out the "whole-home" proposition and give a store or a website reason to be browsed, not just bought from once.

The three ways to buy

  • Own website & app (D2C) — the original and highest-control channel: full margin, full data, full brand experience. The heart of the "direct-to-consumer" identity.
  • Own experience stores (COCO) — company-owned, company-operated physical stores where buyers can test mattresses and see furniture. The fastest-growing channel and the focus of recent investment (section 06).
  • Marketplaces & multi-brand outlets — Amazon, Flipkart and 1,500-plus third-party retail points that extend reach into towns and buyers the brand's own footprint doesn't touch, at the cost of some margin and control.
Why owning both the product and the channel is the whole point

Most consumer businesses control one end or the other — a brand that outsources manufacturing, or a retailer that sells other people's goods. Wakefit tries to hold both: it designs and manufactures its own products and sells them through channels it largely owns. That double grip is the source of everything distinctive about it — the pricing freedom, the fat gross margin a dealer would otherwise take, the control over quality and customer experience, and the data loop from sale back to design. It is also what makes the business capital-hungry and operationally hard: owning factories and stores means owning inventory, rent, staff and working capital that an asset-light marketplace never touches. The strategy is a bet that control is worth the weight.

04

The founders, the backers, and the road to listing

A bootstrapped-then-funded start-up that reached the public market in under a decade.

Wakefit is a first-generation start-up, not a legacy manufacturer — and its cap table reflects that. It was built by two founders, scaled on venture and growth capital, and reached its IPO in December 2025. Knowing who owns it and how it got here explains both its ambition and the pressure it now carries as a newly-public, still-maturing company.

Ownership & structure

  • Founders: Ankit Garg (CEO) and Chaitanya Ramalingegowda remain the promoters and public face of the company. Their continued involvement is central — this is a founder-led brand, and much of its culture and design sensibility traces to them.
  • Financial investors: the pre-IPO register featured well-known growth and venture funds — Peak XV / Sequoia India, Investcorp and SIG among them. As with most recent consumer IPOs, part of the offer was an offer-for-sale giving early backers partial liquidity, so a share of the IPO proceeds went to selling shareholders rather than into the company.
  • Free float & overhang: investors should check the latest shareholding pattern for exact promoter and investor holdings and for lock-in expiries — a young listing with venture backers can face supply as lock-ins release. Verify current percentages against the company's filings.
The founder-led, venture-funded shape cuts both ways: it gives Wakefit the brand instinct, speed and design focus that built the business, and founders whose interests are tied to the equity. But it also means a relatively short operating history, a balance sheet shaped by years of growth-over-profit investment, and pre-IPO investors who will, in time, look to sell. For a first-time public-market buyer, the quality of governance and capital allocation from here — how disciplined the store roll-out and the marketing spend stay — matters as much as the top-line growth.
05

Making its own goods: where the margin comes from

The vertical-integration decision that separates Wakefit from a furniture marketplace.

The single most important structural choice Wakefit made — the one that decides its margins and its identity — is that it manufactures its own products. It is not a marketplace listing other people's sofas, and it is not a pure brand that designs a mattress and has a contract factory build it. It owns much of the making. That choice is why its gross margins are high for a physical-goods retailer, and why the debate about the company is never about the gross margin but about everything below it.

Consider what a typical branded home purchase pays for on its way to a buyer: raw materials and the factory's conversion cost, the manufacturer's profit, a distributor's margin, a wholesaler's margin, and a retail showroom's margin and rent. By making the product itself and selling it directly, Wakefit collapses most of those layers into one, and keeps the margin each intermediary would have taken. That captured margin is the fuel that pays for the two things this business is expensive at: acquiring customers and running stores.

Each rung captures a margin layer the previous one hands away. Wakefit sits near the top — owning both the making and much of the selling — which is why its gross margin is high; the question is whether operating costs below the gross line leave a profit.

Vertical integration buys more than margin. It buys control over quality (a brand's reputation lives on consistency, which is hard to guarantee through third-party factories), speed (in-house design-to-shelf lets it respond to demand and launch new products faster), and a data loop — because it sees every sale directly, it can feed real demand signals back into what it designs and makes. For a "considered purchase" business where trust and consistency drive repeat and referral, owning the product is not a cost-saving footnote; it is the moat.

The device that fits Wakefit specifically: a high gross margin the operating costs eat

Here is the shape of Wakefit's whole P&L in one sentence: it earns a large gross margin by owning the product, then spends most of it below the gross line on winning and serving customers. The rupees a dealer and a distributor would have taken don't vanish into profit — they are redeployed into advertising, store rent, delivery and staff. So the number that matters is never the gross margin (which vertical integration makes healthy almost by design); it is how much of that gross margin survives the marketing bill and the store-network cost to become an operating profit. A traditional Sheela Foam earns its margin differently and spends it differently; Wakefit's version is high-gross, high-operating-cost, and the entire investment case is whether the second number can be tamed while the first is protected. That trade-off is not transferable to a marketplace peer, which never had the gross margin to begin with.

06

Online, then off it: the omni-channel and CAC crux

Why a company born on the internet is racing to open physical stores.

There is a paradox at the centre of Wakefit's strategy, and it is the most important thing to understand about the company. A brand that made its name by not having stores — by selling online and skipping showroom rent — is now opening its own stores as fast as it prudently can. This is not a retreat from the D2C idea; it is what the economics of a considered purchase eventually force. The store roll-out is simultaneously Wakefit's biggest growth lever and its biggest cost, and it turns on one metric: the cost of acquiring a customer.

Why does a digital-first brand need physical stores at all? Because of the nature of the product. A mattress and a sofa are things people want to touch, sit on, and lie on before spending a month's income on them. A store closes that trust gap in a way a website cannot. But there is a subtler, harder-nosed reason: online customer acquisition has become expensive. Digital advertising on Google, Meta and marketplaces has risen in cost as every D2C brand bids for the same eyeballs; winning a furniture buyer purely through ads can cost a great deal. A well-placed physical store, by contrast, acquires walk-in customers as a by-product of existing — the rent and staff are a fixed cost, but the marginal customer who walks in has a low acquisition cost. Stores also lift the online business around them: a city with a Wakefit store typically sees higher website sales too, as the physical presence builds local trust. This "halo" is why the offline and online channels reinforce rather than cannibalise each other.

But the store strategy is a double-edged sword, and this is where the risk lives. Each new store is a fixed cost from day one — rent, fit-out, inventory, staff — that only pays back once it matures and fills with sales. Open stores faster than they mature and reported profitability sags under the weight of young, not-yet-productive locations, even as revenue grows. Open too slowly and rivals grab the physical shelf and the trust that comes with it. The whole near-term earnings profile of Wakefit is, in effect, a race between new-store drag (young stores losing money) and mature-store contribution (older stores paying back and then some). The metric that captures this — same-store sales and store-level payback — is what a serious analyst watches quarter by quarter.

The crux, stated plainly: stores are how Wakefit lowers CAC — if they mature

Everything about Wakefit's path to durable profit routes through one question: does the store network lower the blended cost of acquiring a customer faster than it raises fixed costs? The bet is that a maturing store acquires walk-in buyers cheaply, lifts the surrounding online sales, and lets the brand lean less on expensive digital advertising — so that as the network seasons, the blended CAC falls and the gross margin captured in section 05 finally drops through to operating profit. The bear worry is the mirror image: that stores are opened faster than they mature, that the fixed-cost base outruns the productivity, and that a brand which never truly solved online acquisition economics simply swaps one expensive channel for another. FY26's turn to profit is the first evidence the good version is happening. One good year is not yet proof.

07

The profit question: how much of it is real?

The one number a first-time reader must look behind — because half of it is an accounting entry.

Wakefit's headline is irresistible: a company that lost money for years posted its first annual net profit — ~₹189 Cr — in FY26, its debut year as a listed company. It is exactly the redemption arc a D2C brand wants to show its new public shareholders. But this is the single place a careful reader must slow down, because a large part of that profit is not a rupee earned selling mattresses. It is an accounting gain that exists because the company used to lose money.

Of the ~₹189 Cr reported net profit, roughly ₹98 Cr came from a deferred-tax asset — a one-off, non-cash accounting item. In plain English: when a company racks up losses, it builds up unused tax deductions it can set against future profits. Once it becomes confident it will actually make profits to use them against, accounting rules let it recognise the value of those past losses as an asset on the balance sheet, and the act of recognising it flows through the profit line as a one-time gain. So the very thing that inflated FY26's profit is a formal acknowledgment that Wakefit spent years losing money — the losses, converted into a future tax shield, showing up now as a paper profit.

None of this means the turnaround is fake. The operating picture did improve: EBITDA had already turned positive in FY25 (~₹60 Cr), revenue grew ~17% in FY26, and even stripping out the tax gain the company appears to have crossed into modest operating profit — a real inflection for a business that had never made money. The point is narrower and important: the ₹189 Cr headline overstates the operating profitability of the year, and a reader who takes it at face value — and, say, divides the share price by it to get a flattering "P/E" — will badly misjudge the business. Read the profit net of the one-off, and read it alongside cash flow.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with

Wakefit's maiden annual profit is, in part, its past losses coming back as an asset. That is the surprise that catches most first-time readers of the accounts: roughly half of the number that "proves" the company turned the corner is a deferred-tax entry that only exists because it spent years failing to make money. It is not a trick — the accounting is entirely standard and the underlying operating turn is real — but it is a trap for the unwary. The fair read of FY26 is this: a genuine, still-thin operating profit of the order of ₹90 Cr, dressed up by a ₹98 Cr non-cash tax gain into a ₹189 Cr headline. The task for the next few years is to make the operating half grow until it dwarfs the accounting half — and to convert that profit into cash rather than into a bigger pile of inventory and store fit-outs.

Why this reframes the valuation: because the reported profit is inflated by a one-off, any multiple built on it is misleading, and the company cannot be valued like a settled, steadily-profitable retailer. It must be judged on underlying, recurring operating profit and its trajectory — is the operating half compounding? — and on cash generation, not on the flattering headline EPS of its first listed year.
08

Unit economics: acquire, monetise, repeat

The handful of operating numbers that decide whether the model compounds.

Below the reported statements sits the machinery that actually determines whether Wakefit is a good business: how much it costs to win a customer, how much that customer is worth, and how often they come back. For a considered-purchase D2C brand, these unit economics are the real scoreboard — the income statement is just their sum. Four levers matter, and they interlock.

The four unit-economics levers
LeverWhat it isWhy it decides the model
Customer acquisition cost (CAC)What Wakefit spends in advertising and channel cost to win one buyer.The largest controllable cost after making the product. Stores and brand strength lower it; digital-ad inflation raises it. The single most important operating number.
Average order value (AOV)How much a customer spends per purchase.Rises as the mix shifts from a lone mattress toward higher-ticket furniture and multi-item home orders. The furniture expansion is, at bottom, an AOV story.
Repeat & cross-sellWhether a mattress buyer returns for a bed, sofa or wardrobe.Because any one product is bought rarely, lifetime value depends on selling the same household more categories over time. Repeat rate turns a one-off sale into an annuity.
Gross margin per orderThe margin left after making and delivering the product.Kept healthy by vertical integration (section 05). It is the pool from which CAC and store costs are paid — the wider it is, the more the model can spend to grow and still profit.

The virtuous version reads like this: vertical integration gives a wide gross margin per order; a maturing store network and a strong brand lower CAC; the furniture range lifts AOV; and cross-sell raises repeat purchases — so each customer is won more cheaply, spends more, and comes back, and the wide gross margin finally converts into operating profit. The vicious version is the same levers running backwards: CAC climbs faster than the brand can lower it, AOV stalls if furniture doesn't scale, repeat stays low because home purchases are simply infrequent, and the gross margin is entirely consumed by acquisition and store costs — which is roughly the world Wakefit lived in during its loss-making years.

Vertical integration works on the first term, the furniture range and cross-sell work on the middle term, and the store network works on the last. For a decade the right-hand side won and the company lost money; FY26 is the first sign the left-hand side is pulling ahead. Whether that lead widens is the entire investment question — and it will be visible in CAC, AOV and repeat long before it is obvious in the annual profit.

09

The rivals: sleep, furniture, and the giants either side

A crowded field where Wakefit is neither a pure mattress maker nor a pure marketplace.

Wakefit competes on two fronts at once, and its awkward, distinctive position is that it fits neatly into neither. On the sleep side it faces entrenched mattress brands and hungry newer ones; on the furniture side it faces online marketplaces and global retail giants. Understanding who sits where — and how Wakefit differs from each — is understanding the competitive stakes.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, Wakefit's differentiation is structural, not just branding: against Sheela Foam it is more digital-native and direct; against Pepperfry or Amazon it actually makes what it sells rather than reselling others' goods; against The Sleep Company it has more scale and a wider home range. It sits in a genuinely distinct spot — an own-manufacturing D2C brand spanning both sleep and furniture — that few rivals occupy fully. Second, that spot is surrounded. It must out-execute legacy mattress brands on digital and stores, out-manufacture the marketplaces on product and margin, and out-last well-funded newer D2C challengers on brand and reach — all while a global giant like IKEA and the horizontal platforms loom over the furniture prize. The position is defensible, but it is defended on many sides at once.

Financials & valuation

A fast-growing, freshly-profitable brand with a short public track record — priced on hope, judged on cash.

Wakefit's financials tell a clean growth story with a messy profitability footnote. Revenue has compounded strongly — roughly ₹812 Cr in FY23 to ~₹1,489 Cr in FY26 — while the bottom line only just crossed into the black, and even then with heavy help from a one-off (section 07). As a December-2025 listing, it has almost no track record as a public company, so its valuation rests less on a settled earnings multiple and more on the market's belief in the growth-plus-margin path ahead.

The one number that settles the thesis: recurring operating profit per rupee of revenue, and its trend

Because the headline EPS is distorted by a one-off, the honest yardstick for Wakefit is not a P/E — it is the underlying operating profit margin and its direction, read with two companions: store-level payback / same-store productivity (is the ₹105-and-rising network maturing into profit, section 06?) and operating cash flow (is the profit real cash, or is it reappearing as inventory and store fit-outs?). If, over the next few years, the recurring operating margin climbs from its current thin level while revenue keeps compounding and stores season, then Wakefit is the rare D2C brand that made the model pay, and the growth deserves a premium. If the margin stalls — CAC creeps back up, young stores stay unproductive, working capital swells — then the FY26 profit will read as a one-off dressed as a milestone. 11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A brand with a real edge and a real question — priced before the answer is in.

Wakefit reaches its first year as a public company with genuine strengths — a strong brand, an integrated supply chain, a maturing store network and a just-crossed profit line — and a genuine unresolved question about whether that profit can be made durable and cash-generative at scale. It is a young listing whose thesis will be proven or disproven by operating data over the next few years, not settled today. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Huge, unbranded market — mattresses and especially furniture are largely unorganised; the shift to branded and online is a multi-year tailwind Wakefit is built to ride.
  • Real vertical-integration edge — owning manufacturing gives a wide gross margin and quality control a marketplace can't match, and funds the growth spend.
  • The store network is maturing — a quadrupling of own stores plus a brand halo can structurally lower blended CAC as locations season.
  • Furniture is the bigger prize — cross-selling the whole home to mattress buyers lifts AOV and repeat, expanding the pool well beyond sleep.
  • Profitability inflection underway — EBITDA turned positive, revenue compounds ~20%+, and even ex the one-off the company crossed into operating profit.

The bear case

  • Profit quality is soft — roughly half of FY26's reported profit is a one-off deferred-tax gain; the underlying operating profit is still thin.
  • Store roll-out is a fixed-cost bet — open faster than stores mature and margins sag; the near-term earnings hinge on execution few new listings get perfectly right.
  • Acquisition costs can bite back — digital-ad inflation and heavy competition can push CAC up faster than the brand can pull it down.
  • Surrounded competitively — legacy Sheela Foam/Duroflex on sleep, marketplaces and IKEA on furniture, and newer D2C challengers on strategy.
  • Short public record & overhang — a December-2025 listing with venture backers, limited history and potential lock-in supply to come.
The bottom line

Wakefit is a well-built brand that did something hard — it turned a foam-mattress logistics trick into India's largest D2C home business, made much of what it sells, and just crossed from losing money into (thinly) making it. The edge is real: integration gives it a margin, the range gives it a bigger wallet to chase, and the stores give it a cheaper way to be found. What is not yet real is proof that the operating profit — the ~₹91 Cr underneath FY26's ₹98 Cr of accounting help — can compound faster than the store rent and marketing bill it must keep paying. The next test is concrete and datable: whether, across the FY27 and FY28 results, the recurring operating margin rises while stores season and cash flow turns firmly positive — the moment the operating half of the profit outgrows the accounting half. Clear that bar and Wakefit graduates from a promising listing into a proven compounder; miss it, and its debut year of profit will look like the exception a growth-hungry model produced once, rather than the new normal.
