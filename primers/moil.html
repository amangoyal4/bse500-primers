MOIL, the manganese under India's steel

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

MOIL Ltd — the initials stand for its old name, Manganese Ore India Limited — is a central-government mining PSU that digs one thing above all others: manganese ore. It runs a cluster of underground and opencast mines, mostly in the Nagpur–Bhandara belt of Maharashtra and the Balaghat–Chhindwara belt of Madhya Pradesh, and it is by far India's largest producer of manganese ore, accounting for roughly 45% of the country's domestic output. Manganese ore is not a glamorous commodity, but it is a non-negotiable one: you cannot make steel without manganese, so MOIL sits quietly at the very bottom of the steel supply chain, selling the ore that ferro-alloy makers turn into the additives every steel mill needs.

Three facts frame everything:

  • MOIL sells the input, not the metal. This is the first thing to get straight. MOIL is not a steelmaker and not a diversified miner — it is a near-pure manganese-ore mining company. It hews ore from the ground, grades it, and sells it to alloy producers who convert it into ferro-manganese and silico-manganese, which then go into steel. MOIL earns on the ore; the steel margin belongs to someone else entirely.
  • Its profit swings on price, not tonnage. MOIL's ore output barely moves year to year — it produced ~17.5 lakh tonnes in FY24 and ~18.03 lakh tonnes in FY25, a ~3% rise — yet FY25 net profit jumped ~30% to ₹382 Cr. Almost the entire difference is the price of manganese ore, a number set by global steel demand and imported-ore competition, not by MOIL. It is a textbook price-taker in a commodity cycle.
  • It is a cash fortress. MOIL is debt-free and net-cash, sitting on a large pile of cash and investments, and it behaves like a classic PSU cash machine — consistent, generous dividends (FY25 total ₹5.63/share) topped up by periodic buybacks, with the Government of India owning roughly 53%. The stock trades around 20× earnings — modest for a monopoly-like resource asset, rich for a no-growth commodity.
In plain terms

MOIL is best pictured as a national ore quarry with a fortress balance sheet. It owns some of the best manganese deposits in the country, it has dug them for over a century, and it prints steady cash doing so. But it controls only one of the two levers on its own profit: it decides how many tonnes to mine, and it does that reliably. What it cannot control is the price each tonne fetches — that is dictated by how much steel the world (and especially China) is making, and by how cheaply high-grade ore can be shipped in from South Africa and Gabon. So MOIL is a stable, cash-rich business bolted onto a volatile, imported-price-linked revenue line. The whole investment question is which of those two natures dominates in any given year.

01

Manganese & steel 101: the ore no mill can skip

No metallurgy degree needed — just why this grey rock is indispensable.

Manganese is the fourth-most-used metal on earth by tonnage, after iron, aluminium and copper — yet almost nobody outside the industry could tell you what it does. The answer is simple and it explains MOIL's entire reason to exist: you cannot make usable steel without manganese. When iron is smelted, it is contaminated with sulphur and oxygen that make the metal brittle. Manganese, added as a ferro-alloy, scavenges those impurities and, as a bonus, hardens and toughens the steel. There is no cheap substitute. Roughly 90% of all manganese mined in the world ends up in steelmaking, which is why manganese-ore demand tracks steel production almost one-for-one.

Manganese is overwhelmingly a steel input. This is why MOIL, despite being a manganese company, effectively rents its fortunes to the global steel cycle — and why the small non-steel slice (batteries, chemicals) is where the company's own value-added ambitions live.

Because manganese is added to steel as an alloy rather than dropped in as raw rock, there is a step in the middle. Miners like MOIL sell manganese ore; a separate set of producers smelt that ore in electric furnaces into ferro-manganese and silico-manganese — iron-manganese and silicon-manganese alloys that steel mills actually buy. MOIL is upstream of that step: its customers are the ferro-alloy makers (and, for its own small value-added output, the alloy market directly). To follow the business you only need a handful of terms.

The economics that decide everything: price per tonne

A steel mill's manganese bill is a tiny fraction of its total cost, so mills rarely haggle over manganese ore the way MOIL might wish — they simply buy the cheapest tonne of the grade they need, from wherever it is cheapest, MOIL or an importer. That leaves MOIL almost entirely a price-taker: it can decide how much to dig, but the price each tonne earns is handed to it by the global market. When Chinese steel output is roaring and imported ore is dear, MOIL's realisation and profit soar on the same tonnes. When steel slows and cheap ore floods in, the same mines earn far less. The rock in the ground never changed; the price tag on it did.

02

What MOIL mines and sells: one ore body, four ways to earn

The mining model — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

MOIL operates a portfolio of around ten mines across two states — a mix of deep underground mines (some among the deepest manganese mines in the country) and shallower opencast pits. The great majority of its revenue is simply selling graded manganese ore. Around that core sit three smaller legs: value-added ferro-manganese, high-purity EMD for batteries, and a modest wind-power business. They all trace back to the same ore body.

The four earning legs, decoded

  • Manganese ore (the core) — the overwhelming majority of revenue. MOIL sells various grades of ore, priced on manganese content, to ferro-alloy plants and, historically, to captive buyers. This is a classic mining business: dig, grade, sell, repeat. Its economics are set by tonnage (stable) × realisation (volatile).
  • Ferro-manganese — MOIL runs a modest ferro-manganese plant (at Balaghat), smelting some of its own ore into alloy. It captures a slice of the downstream conversion margin, but the scale is small next to the ore business.
  • EMD (electrolytic manganese dioxide) — a high-purity manganese chemical for dry-cell batteries, produced at small scale (Dongri Buzurg). Niche and higher-margin, and a toe-hold in the manganese-for-batteries story — though today it is a rounding error in the P&L.
  • Wind power — MOIL owns a small wind-energy portfolio (around 20 MW) built years ago, largely to green its own operations and earn modest power revenue. It is peripheral, but it is why the company is not only a miner.
Why the mining model shapes the risk

A miner's cost base is largely fixed and sticky: the mines, the shafts, the workforce (MOIL is a PSU with a large, unionised, hard-to-flex headcount), and the statutory royalties are broadly there whether ore prices are high or low. Revenue, by contrast, swings with the ore price. Put a mostly fixed cost base under a volatile top line and you get operating leverage in both directions: in a strong-price year, extra revenue drops almost straight to profit; in a weak-price year, the fixed costs bite hard. That is why MOIL's earnings amplify the manganese cycle rather than smoothing it — and why a mildly better ore price can lift profit far more than the price move alone suggests.

03

The PSU, its history, and who controls it

A century-old ore company, government-owned and cash-disciplined.

MOIL is a public-sector undertaking under the Ministry of Steel, with roots that reach back well over a century into the manganese mines of India's Central Provinces. In its modern form it was constituted as Manganese Ore (India) Limited in 1962 — a joint venture of the Government of India with the state governments of Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh — and it was renamed MOIL Limited and listed in 2010. Ownership has always been, and remains, firmly with the state.

Timeline

  • 1890s–1962

  • 1977 onward
    Becomes a Government of India company as the Centre takes a controlling stake; runs for decades as a steady, low-profile mining PSU supplying India's ferro-alloy and steel industry.
  • 2010
    Renamed MOIL Limited and listed on NSE & BSE via a government disinvestment (offer for sale), bringing the ore company to public markets for the first time.
  • 2016–2021
    Successive buybacks and bonus issues return cash to shareholders (and to the Centre); MOIL cements its reputation as a debt-free, high-payout PSU.
  • FY25
    Record ore production of 18.03 lakh tonnes; revenue ₹1,585 Cr (+9%), PAT ₹382 Cr (+30%); total dividend ₹5.63/share.
  • Q1 FY27 (Jun-2026)
    Record first-quarter ore output (~5.08 lakh tonnes); PAT up ~70% YoY to ~₹88 Cr on firmer realisation — the price lever visibly at work.

Ownership & structure

  • The controlling owner is the state. The Government of India (Ministry of Steel) holds roughly 53% of MOIL, having come down from ~71% at listing through periodic offers for sale and buybacks. Because it is a majority government company, disinvestment policy, dividend expectations and the occasional OFS are structural features, not surprises.
  • It is a Miniratna PSU. That status gives MOIL a degree of operational and investment autonomy, but it remains a government company — with the strengths (secure mining leases, a national mandate, cheap capital) and the constraints (a large fixed workforce, pricing under public scrutiny, capital-allocation choices that answer to a ministry) that come with that.
  • Small joint ventures extend the reach. MOIL has formed JVs to move downstream and secure demand — for example ferro-alloy ventures with steel PSUs (SAIL, RINL) — and has been exploring for manganese in new states (Rajasthan, Gujarat, Odisha) to widen its resource base. These are option value, not today's earnings.
Reading a PSU miner without illusions

A government-owned resource company invites two opposite caricatures — "sleepy PSU that destroys value" and "strategic national asset you can't lose money on". MOIL fits neither. It is genuinely well-run at the mining level, has grown output to records, and is scrupulous about cash — but it is also constrained: it cannot shed labour freely, its investment choices pass through a ministry, and its share register carries a majority owner who may sell down at any time. The right lens is to bank the reliability the state ownership provides — secure leases, a fortress balance sheet, dependable payouts — while pricing in the ceiling it imposes on how nimbly the company can be run for the share price alone.

Why the ownership matters for a shareholder: a government majority cuts both ways. It underwrites MOIL's mining leases, its balance-sheet conservatism and its habit of paying out cash — a genuinely shareholder-friendly trait among PSUs. But it also means a minority investor rides alongside an owner whose goals include national supply security and periodic disinvestment, not purely the share price. When the Centre chooses to sell a slice via OFS, it can cap the stock; when it wants dividends, MOIL pays them.
04

A price-taker in a commodity cycle

The single most important idea in the whole case — and it is specific to a miner like this.

Most companies you can understand by studying what they do. MOIL you understand by studying what is done to it. It mines a roughly constant amount of ore every year — that part is well-run and predictable. Its fortunes then rise and fall on a price it has almost no power to set: the price of manganese ore, which is stitched to global steel output (above all China's, the world's dominant steel producer) and to the landed cost of high-grade ore shipped in from South Africa, Gabon and Australia. When that external price is high, MOIL is a money machine; when it is low, the same mines merely tick over.

Ore output rose only ~3% between FY24 and FY25, yet profit jumped ~30%. The mines did roughly the same work; realisation did the rest. This is what a price-taker's P&L looks like — output steady, profit levered to a price MOIL does not control.

Notice what this rules out. MOIL cannot easily grow its way out of a weak-price year by simply digging more — mine capacity, ore grade and the market's appetite all cap how fast tonnage can rise, and flooding a soft market with extra ore would only push the price down further. Nor can it set a floor under its price the way a branded consumer company can; steel mills will always reach for the cheapest acceptable tonne. So the realistic bull-and-bear argument for MOIL is not about market share or a new product — it is a view on the manganese-ore price cycle, and on whether the next few years look like a tight, high-price market or a loose, import-flooded one.

The device that only fits MOIL: same ore, different price

Test it with the numbers. Between FY24 and FY25 MOIL mined about 3% more ore — a rounding difference in operational terms — and yet its net profit rose about 30%, from ~₹293 Cr to ~₹382 Cr. Then look at the June-2026 quarter: a record first-quarter tonnage, but the eye-catching figure was profit up ~70% year-on-year. In both cases the mines did roughly what they always do; the swing came from the price each tonne fetched. For MOIL, the one-line summary is that the company controls the tonnes and the market controls the money. No amount of operational excellence changes that arithmetic — it only ensures MOIL is fully exposed to it, in both directions.

05

The volume lever: mines and the growth ambition

The one thing MOIL can control — and the long-running plan to push it higher.

If price is the lever MOIL cannot pull, tonnage is the one it can — and here the story is genuinely constructive. For years MOIL has carried an explicit ambition to raise ore production substantially, roughly toward doubling output over the long run (management has framed a target of about 3 million tonnes a year against today's ~1.8 million). The lever is mine development: deepening existing underground mines, opening new ones, mechanising, and running exploration in new states. Every extra tonne MOIL commissions is a tonne of volume growth it captures whatever the price does.

Output has climbed steadily to a record ~18 lakh tonnes in FY25. The ~30 lakh-tonne (3 MT) figure is a long-run ambition, not a dated commitment — treat it as the direction of travel, dependent on mine development landing on schedule.

Two things make the volume lever slower and harder than it sounds. First, MOIL's richest ore is underground, and deepening a shaft or sinking a new one is a multi-year, capital-and-permitting-heavy exercise — you cannot conjure tonnes on demand. Second, more tonnes only help profit if the market absorbs them without collapsing the price; pushing a lot of extra domestic ore into a soft market can be self-defeating. So the production ambition is best read as a slow, structural tailwind — real, and value-accretive if delivered, but measured in years and dependent on execution, not a switch management can flip in a weak quarter.

Why mine development is the quiet swing factor

Here is the leverage a patient investor is really buying. MOIL's cost base is largely fixed, so each additional tonne of ore — once a mine is developed — carries a high incremental margin. If MOIL can lift sustainable output meaningfully above today's ~18 lakh tonnes, it grows earnings even in a flat-price world, and it compounds spectacularly in a strong-price one. The catch is the timeline: mine development is measured in years of shaft-sinking, statutory clearances and mechanisation, not quarters. The volume ambition is the part of the MOIL story that rewards patience — and punishes anyone expecting it to arrive fast.

06

The fortress balance sheet

Debt-free, cash-rich, and disciplined about giving cash back.

Whatever the ore price does, one thing about MOIL rarely changes: the balance sheet is a fortress. The company carries no meaningful debt, sits on a large pile of cash and investments, and has a long record of returning that cash to owners through dividends and periodic buybacks. In a commodity business famous for boom-and-bust indebtedness, MOIL's financial conservatism is its most reliable feature — and, for a certain kind of investor, its main attraction.

The cash pile is not idle window-dressing; it is strategic ballast for a cyclical business. When ore prices fall, MOIL does not have to slash investment or scramble for financing the way a levered miner would — it can keep developing mines, keep paying dividends, and simply wait out the down-leg of the cycle. When prices are strong, the cash builds faster and funds the next buyback or capex. In effect the balance sheet decouples MOIL's survival from the commodity cycle, even as its profit stays fully exposed to it. That combination — volatile earnings, unshakeable finances — is unusual and underpins why the market is willing to pay a resource-monopoly-like multiple for a no-growth commodity.

Why net-cash is the shock absorber, not the story

It is tempting to value MOIL as a bond-like dividend payer because of the cash. Resist that. The cash guarantees the company will endure every cycle comfortably and keep paying out — but it does not drive the earnings, which still swing on the ore price. So read the fortress balance sheet as the reason MOIL is low-risk of ruin, not the reason it is high-return. The upside case rests on the ore price and on volume growth; the balance sheet simply ensures the company is still standing, cash intact, to enjoy the next up-cycle whenever it arrives.

07

Grade, realisation, and the value-added legs

Why the mix — not just the tonnage — decides the price MOIL earns.

A tonne of manganese ore is not a tonne of manganese ore. Ore is priced on its manganese content, and MOIL sells a spread of grades — from richer metallurgical ore that commands a premium to lower-grade ore used differently. So MOIL's blended realisation depends not only on the market price but on its own grade mix: sell a richer basket and the average price per tonne rises even if the market is flat. On top of the ore, MOIL runs three small higher-value legs that nudge the mix upward — ferro-manganese, EMD and, peripherally, wind power.

The strategic pull behind the value-added legs is obvious: raw-ore selling leaves MOIL fully exposed to the commodity price, so anything that moves it down the chain toward the alloy and chemical markets earns a bit more per tonne of manganese and diversifies away from the pure ore cycle. That is why MOIL keeps a ferro-manganese plant, an EMD unit, and JV ambitions in ferro-alloys. The reality, though, is that these remain small relative to the ore engine — they improve the mix at the margin rather than changing what MOIL fundamentally is.

A genuine surprise: being India's largest manganese-ore producer does not make MOIL the country's dominant supplier of manganese units. Indian ore, MOIL's included, tends to be medium grade, while the world's richest ore comes from South Africa, Gabon and Australia. Because a ferro-alloy smelter ultimately buys manganese content, not rock, India still imports a large volume of high-grade ore that MOIL's medium-grade output cannot straightforwardly replace. So MOIL leads domestic production yet competes — on its home turf — with imports it cannot always match on grade. The "national champion" and the "price-taking importer's rival" are the same company.
08

The market, the imports, and the competition

A dominant domestic producer that still shares its market with the sea.

Within India, MOIL has no true peer at scale — it produces roughly 45% of the country's domestic manganese ore, dwarfing the scattered private and captive miners. But domestic production is only half the picture. India is a net importer of manganese ore, and MOIL's real competition is not another Indian miner — it is the seaborne market: high-grade ore from South Africa, Gabon and Australia landing at Indian ports, priced against MOIL's medium-grade tonnes.

MOIL leads India's domestic output by a wide margin, but India imports a large share of its manganese needs — chiefly high-grade seaborne ore. The importer, not the domestic miner, is MOIL's price-setting competitor.

This structure explains why MOIL's pricing power is real but bounded. On one hand, it is the entrenched, low-cost, always-available domestic supplier with over a century of mining behind it and secure leases — a genuinely defensible position no newcomer can easily replicate. On the other, the moment MOIL's price drifts above the landed cost of imported ore of comparable value, buyers simply switch to the sea. The import parity price is the invisible ceiling on what MOIL can charge, and it moves with global ore prices, freight and the rupee — none of which MOIL controls.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

MOIL enjoys a domestic near-monopoly and a fortress balance sheet, which sounds like pricing power — but the sea keeps it honest. Its advantage is reliability and proximity: an Indian ferro-alloy plant can source MOIL ore without shipping risk, port delays or currency exposure, and MOIL's leases and scale make it the default domestic partner. Its constraint is grade and global price: for the high-manganese units a smelter sometimes needs, imports win, and MOIL's whole price grid ultimately floats on the seaborne market. The realistic view is neither "unassailable monopoly" nor "commodity pawn" — it is a dominant domestic supplier operating under an imported-price ceiling it cannot lift.

09

Financials & valuation

A steady miner having a good stretch — valued for the cycle to hold.

FY25 was a strong year for MOIL: record ore output, firm realisation, revenue up ~9% and profit up ~30%. The June-2026 quarter extended the momentum with a ~70% jump in profit on record first-quarter tonnage. The market has taken note, and the stock sits on a mid-single-digit-times-cash-flow, roughly 20× earnings valuation — comfortable for a debt-free resource asset, but a price that assumes the ore cycle stays supportive.

The one number that settles the thesis: blended ore realisation (₹/MT)

Because MOIL's tonnage is stable and its cost base is largely fixed, the figure that proves or breaks the case is its blended realisation — the average rupees earned per tonne of ore sold. Read alongside ore production growth (is mine development lifting sustainable volumes?) and the net-cash and payout (is the fortress intact and returning cash?), realisation is the scoreboard: track it quarter to quarter and you know almost everything about where profit is going. If realisation holds firm — because global steel is healthy and imports stay dear — a ~20× multiple on a debt-free, dividend-paying miner is defensible. If manganese-ore prices roll over as they periodically do, the same mines earn far less, profit contracts hard on the operating leverage, and a stock priced for a good cycle re-rates to a poor one. Everything else about MOIL is stable; realisation is where the money is decided.

10

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cash-rich national miner, waiting on the ore price and its own shafts.

MOIL enters the next few years in enviable financial shape — debt-free, cash-heavy, record production behind it and a firm-price tailwind in the June-2026 quarter. The real debate is not about the company's durability, which the balance sheet settles, but about two things it half-controls: whether the manganese-ore price cycle stays supportive, and whether mine development can lift sustainable tonnage enough to grow earnings through a flat-price year.

The bull case

  • Irreplaceable input — no steel without manganese, and India's steel output is set to keep climbing; structural, long-run demand for the ore MOIL digs.
  • Dominant, defensible position — ~45% of domestic output, century-old leases, low cost, always available; a genuine national-champion moat within India.
  • Fortress finances — net-cash, high-margin, consistent dividends and buybacks; endures every down-cycle and compounds cash in the up-cycle.
  • Volume optionality — a real, if slow, ambition to grow output toward ~3 MT via mine development; high-margin incremental tonnes if delivered.
  • Operating leverage — a fixed cost base means firm ore prices drop almost straight to profit, as FY25 and Q1 FY27 showed.

The bear case

  • Price-taker, full stop — profit rides a manganese-ore price MOIL cannot set; a Chinese-steel slowdown or import glut hits earnings hard and fast.
  • Grade disadvantage — medium-grade domestic ore competes with richer seaborne ore; the import-parity price is a ceiling MOIL cannot lift.
  • Slow volume growth — the ~3 MT ambition is years of shaft-sinking and clearances away; production has grown, but the prize is far off.
  • PSU constraints — large fixed workforce, ministry oversight, and periodic government OFS that can cap the stock.
  • Cyclical valuation trap — ~20× earnings in a good-price stretch can look cheap at the top and dear at the bottom of the ore cycle.
The bottom line

MOIL is two businesses wearing one share: a rock-solid, cash-generating national ore franchise, and a fully-exposed bet on the manganese-ore price. The first never really changes — the mines produce, the balance sheet stays a fortress, the dividend arrives. The second is what actually moves the stock, and it answers to global steel and the seaborne ore market, not to anything MOIL does in Nagpur or Balaghat. So the case turns on two checkable prints: the blended realisation per tonne each quarter, which tells you whether the cycle is with MOIL or against it, and the sustainable production run-rate as new mine development comes on, which tells you whether MOIL is quietly growing the base beneath that price. Watch those two numbers; the cash, the dividend and the domestic dominance can be taken as given.
