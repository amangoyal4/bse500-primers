Snapshot

What you're actually buying

IGI is a certifier, not a jeweller and not a miner. Its product is a single sheet of paper — a grading report — and its moat is that the diamond and jewellery trade has agreed to believe that paper.

When a diamond, coloured gemstone or finished jewellery piece changes hands, the buyer wants to know exactly what they are paying for. A diamond's value can swing many-fold on grading distinctions invisible to the naked eye. So an independent third party examines the stone under controlled conditions, measures the 4Cs — carat, cut, colour and clarity — and issues a certificate that both sides can trust and price against. IGI is one of the two names the world's trade accepts for that job; the other is America's GIA. For that examination, IGI charges a fee, and it does so on an enormous throughput of stones, concentrated heavily in India — the world's diamond-cutting hub, where an estimated 9 in 10 of the planet's diamonds are polished.

The business is unusually clean. There is little inventory (IGI never owns the stones it grades), modest capital tied up in labs and instruments, and a fee that recurs every time a stone is graded or re-graded. The result is a company earning close to sixty paise of EBITDA on every rupee of revenue — a margin structure closer to a ratings agency or an exchange than to anything in the jewellery supply chain it serves.

In plain terms

A diamond is worth what two strangers agree it is worth — and they can only agree if a neutral referee has certified exactly what it is. IGI is that referee for a large slice of the world's stones. It gets paid per verdict, keeps almost none of the risk of owning diamonds, and gets paid again every time a stone re-enters the market and needs re-grading.

01

The trust toll-booth

How a certification lab makes money

Strip away the gemology and IGI is a two-sided trust network with a turnstile in the middle. The more the trade accepts its grade, the more everyone in the trade must use it — which makes the grade even more accepted.

Consider the path a polished diamond takes. A rough stone is mined (by others), sold to a cutter, and polished — overwhelmingly in Surat, India. Before that polished stone can move on to a wholesaler, a jewellery brand, or a retail counter, someone has to say authoritatively what it is: its exact weight, its cut grade, where its colour falls on the D-to-Z scale, how clean it is of inclusions, and — increasingly important — whether it was pulled from the earth or grown in a reactor. That verdict is IGI's product. It examines the stone, laser-inscribes a report number on the girdle, and issues a certificate. A fee is collected. The stone, now "carded," can be priced and traded with confidence anywhere in the world.

The elegance of the model is that IGI sits across the flow rather than in it. It doesn't take a position on diamond prices, it doesn't carry unsold inventory through a downturn, and it doesn't finance stones. It simply collects a fee each time value passes the gate. And because a certificate is only useful if buyers recognise the issuer, the business compounds on itself: a grader that everyone already trusts is the grader every new stone must go to, which deepens the trust further. Economists call this a two-sided network effect; a trader would just call it "you card with IGI because your buyer wants IGI paper."

Where the fees come from

  • Diamond certification — the core: grading reports for loose polished diamonds, natural and lab-grown, at scale.
  • Studded jewellery certification — grading and identifying the multiple stones set into a finished piece; a growing line as branded, hallmarked jewellery spreads.
  • Coloured stones and identification — rubies, sapphires, emeralds and origin/treatment reports.
  • Education — IGI runs gemmology schools (~19, approx.) that train the next generation of graders and appraisers; a smaller, steadier revenue line that also seeds the industry with people fluent in IGI standards.
Surprise
A "diamond company" that owns no diamonds. IGI never takes title to a single stone it grades. Its balance sheet carries labs, microscopes, spectrometers and people — not gems. The value it captures is the credibility of its verdict, which is why a scratch on that credibility would matter far more than a scratch on any physical asset it holds.
02

Why an independent grade exists

Industry 101: the 4Cs and the trust gap

A diamond is the rare consumer good where the buyer genuinely cannot verify what they are buying. That information gap is the entire reason a certification industry exists.

Two one-carat diamonds can look identical on a shop counter and differ in wholesale value by several times over, based on distinctions a trained grader needs specialised lighting and magnification to see. The trade codified these into the 4Cs:

The "C"What it measuresWhy it moves price
CaratWeight of the stonePrice rises non-linearly with size; round numbers command premiums
CutHow well the stone is proportioned and finishedDetermines brilliance; a poor cut wastes an otherwise fine stone
ColourHow colourless (D) to tinted (Z) it isSubtle grade steps invisible to laypeople swing value sharply
ClarityFreedom from inclusions and blemishesFlawless is rare; the scale from FL to I compresses huge price gaps

Because these judgments are technical and the stakes are high, neither buyer nor seller can be trusted to grade the stone themselves — the seller is conflicted, and the buyer lacks the instruments. A credible neutral referee resolves the standoff. That is exactly the role a ratings agency plays for a bond, or an exchange's clearing house plays for a trade: it converts a private, contestable claim into a public, tradable fact.

The critical modern addition is origin. A natural diamond and a lab-grown diamond can be chemically and optically identical — both are crystalline carbon — yet historically priced worlds apart. Only laboratory equipment can reliably tell them apart. This makes the certifier's verdict not a nicety but a necessity: without a trusted lab confirming "natural" versus "laboratory-grown," the entire pricing architecture of the diamond trade would collapse into suspicion. That single distinction has become one of IGI's most valuable services.

In plain terms

You can weigh a bag of rice yourself, but you cannot grade a diamond yourself — and neither can the person selling it to you in a way you'd trust. The gap between what the stone is and what either party can prove it is gets filled by a lab. IGI is paid to close that gap, one stone at a time.

03

The lab-grown boom IGI owns

Roughly 65% of the world's lab-grown certifications

Lab-grown diamonds are the fastest-moving story in the jewellery trade — and IGI is estimated to certify around 65% of them globally. This is the single fact that makes IGI different from its larger rival.

Lab-grown diamonds (LGDs) are real diamonds — same carbon lattice, same hardness, same sparkle — produced in weeks inside high-pressure or vapour-deposition reactors rather than over billions of years underground. Their appeal to consumers is simple: a visually equivalent stone at a fraction of the price. Volumes have surged, particularly in the United States and increasingly in India, and much of that production and polishing happens in and around Surat — precisely where IGI's certification muscle is concentrated.

Where GIA — the older, natural-diamond-anchored giant — has historically been more cautious and selective about lab-grown grading, IGI leaned in early and hard. The result is a dominant position in exactly the category that is growing fastest. Every lab-grown stone still needs certifying — arguably more urgently than a natural one, because the buyer's whole reason for confidence is documentary proof that it is what the seller claims, grown to the grade stated.

This positioning cuts two ways, and both matter enormously for the thesis. On the upside, IGI is levered to the single fastest-growing product in its industry, and to India and emerging markets, where jewellery consumption and lab-grown adoption are both rising. On the downside, it ties a large slice of IGI's fortunes to a young category whose economics are still being written — a point the next section confronts head-on.

Why it matters
IGI's edge over GIA is not that it certifies more diamonds overall — GIA is enormous. It is that IGI got to own the growth vector. As the mix of the world's certified diamonds tilts toward lab-grown, it tilts toward IGI's home turf.
04

The lab-grown paradox

The device that makes IGI unlike a diamond producer

Here is the mechanism at the heart of IGI, and it appears in no other primer in this library: IGI is paid the same fee whether the diamond in its microscope is a ₹5,000 lab-grown chip or a ₹5-lakh natural solitaire. It grades stones, not values.

Follow the consequence. The great fear stalking the diamond industry is that lab-grown prices are collapsing — a stone that fetched a premium two years ago may fetch a fraction today, and the deflation shows little sign of stopping. For the companies that own diamonds — the miners, the trading houses, the inventory-heavy wholesalers — that price collapse is a slow catastrophe: their asset is worth less every quarter. But IGI does not own the asset. IGI owns the turnstile. And a turnstile is paid by how many stones pass through it, not how much each stone is worth.

So the very force devastating the diamond producers — cheap, abundant lab-grown stones flooding the market — sends more units through IGI's labs. Falling prices expand the volume of diamonds the world can afford to buy, cut, set and certify. The certifier's fee base grows on unit count even as the per-stone price of the product it certifies falls. This is the closest thing IGI has to a superpower: a structural indifference to the diamond price that has crushed everyone upstream of it.

The price collapse that is a slow catastrophe for anyone holding diamonds is, for the company that merely grades them, a rising tide of billable units.

But the paradox has a sharp second edge, and candour about it is what separates a thesis from a sales pitch. Fee-per-stone is not perfectly insulated. If lab-grown prices keep falling toward true commodity levels — a few hundred rupees for a small melee stone — two things can go wrong at once. First, IGI may have to cut its own certification fee, because no one will pay a grading charge that rivals the stone's price. Second, and more dangerous, some cheap stones may stop being certified at all: below a certain value, a buyer simply won't bother paying to card a stone that costs less than the card. The volume tap that the boom opened could narrow if the product it feeds on becomes too cheap to be worth grading.

In plain terms

IGI wins from cheap lab-grown diamonds the way a highway toll wins from cheap petrol: more cars drive through. But if petrol became so cheap that people stopped driving cars and walked instead, the toll would still lose. The whole IGI question comes down to which of those two forces — more stones, or stones too cheap to certify — wins on the margin.

That tension — exploding volumes versus eroding fee-per-certificate — is not a footnote. It is the core of the entire investment case, and everything in the risk and valuation sections that follow is really a way of asking which side of it prevails.

05

Company, Blackstone & the listing

A global network carved out and floated in India

The listed entity is not a small Indian franchise of a foreign brand. It is the vehicle that acquired the global IGI network — Antwerp, the United States and beyond — and then listed in Mumbai. Behind it stands Blackstone.

IGI's roots run to Antwerp, the historic centre of the diamond trade, where the institute was founded decades ago as an independent grading laboratory. Over time it built out a worldwide footprint of labs and gemmology schools. In the run-up to the listing, private-equity giant Blackstone took control and consolidated the international operations, and the India-incorporated company — International Gemological Institute (India) Limited — became the top holding vehicle, having acquired the global IGI business (including the Antwerp and US operations). It listed on the NSE and BSE in December 2024.

Two features of the ownership structure deserve emphasis, because they shape the stock as much as the business does.

Blackstone control — and the overhang

Blackstone controls the company. That is a double-edged fact. On one edge, a disciplined global sponsor has an incentive to run the asset efficiently and professionalise it. On the other, a private-equity owner does not hold forever; its business model is to buy, improve and sell. A large controlling stake in the hands of a financial owner that will, at some point, sell down is a well-understood pressure on any newly listed stock — an "overhang." Each tranche the sponsor sells adds supply of shares to the market, and the market knows more is coming. For IGI, the pace and pricing of Blackstone's eventual sell-down is one of the concrete, datable events that will shape the share price independently of how many diamonds get graded.

Reporting on a calendar year

Unlike most Indian listed companies, which report on an April–March fiscal year, IGI reports on a calendar year (January–December), a legacy of its international structure. When you read "CY2025," it means the twelve months to December 2025 — worth remembering when comparing IGI's cadence against Indian peers.

The schools are worth a moment. They generate their own modest, steady revenue, but their strategic value is larger than their fees: they train appraisers, retail staff and the trade in IGI's standards and nomenclature. Every graduate who learns to read a diamond the IGI way is a small reinforcement of IGI's grading language as the industry default — a soft moat that compounds quietly alongside the hard one of brand recognition.

The geographic mix also explains IGI's exposure to two structural tailwinds: the shift of diamond certification toward India and emerging markets (where both cutting and, increasingly, consumption are concentrating), and the growth of studded jewellery — finished pieces set with multiple certified stones — as branded, organised jewellery retail spreads across India and beyond.

Why are the margins so extraordinary? Because the marginal cost of grading one more stone is small — a grader's time and some instrument depreciation — while the fee is collected in full. Once the labs, the equipment and the trained staff are in place, incremental volume drops through to profit at a very high rate. There is no raw-material cost of goods in the way a manufacturer has, no inventory to write down, and no working capital tied up financing stones IGI never owns. This is the financial signature of a toll operator, and it is why the market values IGI more like a data or ratings franchise than a jewellery-sector name.

The revenue mix that matters

The composition of that revenue is where the thesis lives. Three splits are worth watching each period:

  • Certification vs. education — certification is the overwhelming majority; education is the smaller, steadier tail.
  • Natural vs. lab-grown — IGI's outsized lab-grown exposure is its growth engine and its chief risk; the mix reveals how dependent the story is on the LGD category.
  • India vs. global — India dominates by volume; the balance shows how the emerging-market shift is playing out.

Growth in CY2025 — revenue up ~17%, profit up ~24% — shows operating leverage doing its work: profit rising faster than revenue as high-margin volume scales over a largely fixed cost base. The durability of that pattern is precisely what the lab-grown paradox will decide.

Valuation

The metric that settles the thesis

At roughly ₹14,400 Cr of market value and about 27 times earnings (approx.), IGI is priced as the high-quality, high-margin, near-monopoly franchise it is. The debate is not whether it is a good business — it plainly is — but whether the fee tap keeps flowing at the volume the price assumes.

A multiple in the high-twenties on a company compounding earnings in the low-twenties percent, with ~60% EBITDA margins, net cash, and a structural network moat, is not obviously stretched by the standards of Indian quality franchises. What makes IGI hard to value is not the margin — that is durable — but the volume-times-fee line at the top, because that line depends on a young lab-grown category whose price trajectory is still being discovered.

So do not reach for a generic "mix-shift re-rating" story. The number that will prove or break IGI is specific and nameable:

The thesis-settling metric

Certification volume growth × realisation (fee) per certificate — read together, with the lab-grown mix and the ~60% margin as the guardrails. If graded-stone volumes keep compounding and the average fee per certificate holds, the lab-grown paradox is resolving in IGI's favour and the multiple is earned. If volumes rise but realisation per certificate falls faster — because lab-grown deflation is dragging fees down or pushing the cheapest stones out of certification entirely — then revenue stalls even as unit counts look healthy, and the premium unwinds. Watch those two lines against each other, not in isolation.

This is why a headline "diamonds certified: up X%" tells you only half the story. A certifier can grade record numbers of stones and still see revenue soften if each grading earns less. Conversely, a slowdown in unit growth is survivable if fee-per-certificate is rising with premiumisation and studded-jewellery work. The signal is the product of the two, quarter after quarter — and IGI, uniquely among the businesses touched by the lab-grown wave, lives or dies by their ratio rather than by the diamond price itself.

The valuation therefore rests on a judgment, not a formula: that IGI's volume growth outruns any fee erosion for long enough that the margin structure keeps translating into compounding profit. The premium multiple is the market's bet that the toll-booth keeps counting more cars than it loses to cheaper fares.

The competitive set

A duopoly at the top, a long tail below

The certification world is effectively a two-name game at the top — IGI and GIA — with a scattering of smaller labs competing at the edges. Trust businesses tend toward oligopoly, because a grade is only worth what the buyer on the other side recognises.

PlayerPositioning
GIA (Gemological Institute of America)The other giant; the historic gold standard for natural diamonds, US-anchored, non-profit institute, more selective on lab-grown
IGIGlobal scale, India-heavy volume, the leader in lab-grown certification (~65% share) and studded jewellery
SGL (Solitaire Gemmological Laboratories)India-focused challenger lab
GCALUS lab known for guaranteed grading
HRD (Antwerp)European lab, historic Antwerp roots

The structural point is that certification does not fragment easily. A buyer wants the report on their stone to be issued by a lab their own future buyer will recognise, which pushes the whole trade toward a handful of accepted names. That concentration is what protects IGI's pricing and margin — a fringe lab cannot simply undercut on fee, because a cheaper certificate that nobody downstream trusts is worthless. The competitive line is therefore drawn less on price than on acceptance: whose paper does the trade honour?

IGI's specific advantage is that it planted its flag in lab-grown early, while GIA's brand equity is deepest in the natural stones whose relative share is shrinking. If the future of the diamond counter skews increasingly toward lab-grown and studded jewellery — as the volumes suggest — IGI is positioned on the growing side of the board. The mirror risk is that GIA, with its formidable brand, chooses to compete harder for lab-grown grading and pressures IGI's share or its fees.

The risks, plainly

What could break a very good business

IGI's quality is not in doubt; its risks are real and concentrated. Set out plainly, they cluster around one asset that never appears on the balance sheet — its credibility — and one product category still finding its price.

  • Lab-grown commoditisation — the core risk. This is the second edge of the paradox from Section 04. If lab-grown prices fall far enough, IGI faces pressure on fee-per-certificate and the possibility that the cheapest stones stop being certified at all. The volume-versus-fee tension is not a side issue; it is the issue, and it is genuinely two-sided.
  • Grading-integrity / reputational risk — existential. A trust business survives on the belief that its verdict is honest and consistent. A credible scandal — inflated grades, compromised standards, a certification fraud traced to IGI — would not dent the business; it could hollow out the very thing customers pay for. For a certifier, reputation is the whole asset, and it is the one that cannot be insured or rebuilt overnight.
  • Diamond-industry cyclicality. Certification volumes ride on jewellery demand. A consumer slump — recession, weak wedding season, a shift in fashion away from diamonds — cuts the number of stones flowing through the labs. The natural-diamond trade is already soft.
  • Concentration. The business is heavily exposed to diamonds (rather than a diversified basket of gemstones and services) and to India. A shock to either the diamond category or the Indian cutting hub hits disproportionately.
  • The Blackstone overhang. A private-equity controlling owner will sell down over time. Each tranche adds share supply, and the anticipation of future selling is itself a weight on the stock, distinct from anything happening in the labs.
  • Competition. GIA is a formidable rival with deep brand equity; SGL, GCAL and HRD contest the edges. A determined push by GIA into lab-grown, or fee competition from challengers accepted by the trade, would pressure share and pricing.
  • Valuation. A high-margin, asset-light near-monopoly at ~27× earnings carries expectations. If volume growth or realisation disappoints, a premium multiple has room to compress.
The one to watch above all
Reputational integrity and the lab-grown fee trajectory are not separate worries — they are the two ends of the same question: will the world keep paying IGI, in volume and in fee, to be believed? Everything else is cyclical noise around that.

Bottom line

What to watch, and when

IGI is a rare thing: a business that sells trust, carries almost no inventory, earns close to sixty paise of EBITDA on the rupee, and dominates the one diamond category that is actually growing. It is also a business whose fortunes hinge on a young product whose price is still falling — and on a reputation that a single scandal could unmake.

The company's genius is its detachment from the asset it lives on. It profits while the diamond producers bleed, because it counts stones rather than owns them, and cheap lab-grown diamonds send more stones past its gate. That is a genuinely unusual place to stand in an industry in the middle of a price war. But the same detachment has a limit: if a stone becomes too cheap to be worth certifying, the gate goes quiet, and no margin, however high, saves a toll no one pays.

So the case does not turn on the diamond price, or on IGI's margins, both of which are largely settled. It turns on the ratio of two lines reported each period — certification volumes and fee-per-certificate — read together with the lab-grown mix. Get a few quarters where volumes compound while realisation holds, and the premium is deserved. Get volumes rising while fees slide, and the story hollows from the inside even as the unit count flatters it.

Three concrete, datable milestones will tell the tale, and each is worth marking on a calendar rather than guessing at: Blackstone's next sell-down (its size and pricing will reset the share-supply overhang), the next certification-volume-and-realisation print (the direct read on whether the paradox is resolving in IGI's favour), and any sign of lab-grown price stabilisation (the moment the fee-erosion fear either recedes or hardens). Until those arrive, IGI remains what it is on paper: the world's referee for diamonds, paid in advance to be believed — and worth exactly as long as the trade keeps believing it, in volume and in fee.
