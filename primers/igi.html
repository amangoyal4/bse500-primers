00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're actually buying, before we go deep.

IGI is a certifier — not a jeweller and not a miner. Its product is a single sheet of paper, a grading report, and its moat is that the world's diamond and jewellery trade has agreed to believe that paper. When a diamond, coloured gemstone or finished jewellery piece changes hands, the buyer needs to know exactly what they are paying for; a stone's value can swing many-fold on grading distinctions invisible to the naked eye. An independent third party examines the stone, measures the 4Cs — carat, cut, colour and clarity — and issues a certificate both sides can price against. IGI is one of the two names the global trade accepts for that job; the other is America's GIA.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It sells trust and owns no diamonds. IGI never takes title to a single stone it grades. Its balance sheet carries labs, microscopes, spectrometers and trained people — not gems. There is almost no inventory, modest capital tied up, and a fee that recurs every time a stone is graded or re-graded. The result is a company earning close to sixty paise of EBITDA on every rupee of revenue (~59.9% margin in CY2025) — a structure closer to a ratings agency or an exchange than to anything in the jewellery supply chain it serves.
  • It owns the fastest-growing category in its industry. IGI is estimated to certify around 65% of the world's lab-grown diamonds. Where GIA leaned cautious on lab-grown, IGI leaned in early — and captured the one diamond category that is actually expanding, concentrated in and around Surat, where an estimated 9 in 10 of the planet's diamonds are polished.
  • Blackstone controls it, and it just listed. Private-equity giant Blackstone consolidated IGI's global network — Antwerp, the United States and beyond — into an India-incorporated company that listed on the NSE and BSE in December 2024. CY2025 was its first full calendar year as a public company: revenue ~₹1,229 Cr, PAT ₹531.6 Cr, at a market cap of ~₹14,400 Cr and roughly 27× earnings.

In plain terms

A diamond is worth whatever two strangers agree it is worth — and they can only agree if a neutral referee has certified exactly what it is. IGI is that referee for a large slice of the world's stones. It gets paid per verdict, keeps almost none of the risk of owning diamonds, and gets paid again every time a stone re-enters the market and needs re-grading. The whole company hangs on one question that runs through this primer: will the world keep paying IGI, in volume and in fee, to be believed?

01

The trust toll-booth: how a certification lab makes money

Strip away the gemology and IGI is a turnstile across the flow of the diamond trade.

IGI is a two-sided trust network with a turnstile in the middle. The more the trade accepts its grade, the more everyone in the trade must use it — which makes the grade even more accepted. Follow the path a polished diamond takes and the model becomes obvious.

A rough stone is mined by others, sold to a cutter, and polished — overwhelmingly in Surat, India. Before that polished stone can move on to a wholesaler, a jewellery brand, or a retail counter, someone has to say authoritatively what it is: its exact weight, its cut grade, where its colour falls on the D-to-Z scale, how clean it is of inclusions, and — increasingly important — whether it was pulled from the earth or grown in a reactor. That verdict is IGI's product. It examines the stone, laser-inscribes a report number on the girdle, and issues a certificate. A fee is collected. The stone, now "carded," can be priced and traded with confidence anywhere in the world.

IGI takes no position on diamond prices, carries no unsold inventory through a downturn, and finances no stones. It simply collects a fee each time value passes the gate — and never leaves the flow.

The elegance of the model is that IGI sits across the flow rather than in it. Because a certificate is only useful if buyers recognise the issuer, the business compounds on itself: a grader everyone already trusts is the grader every new stone must go to, which deepens the trust further. Economists call this a two-sided network effect; a diamond trader would just call it "you card with IGI because your buyer wants IGI paper."

Where the fees come from

  • Diamond certification — the core: grading reports for loose polished diamonds, natural and lab-grown, issued at enormous scale.
  • Studded jewellery certification — grading and identifying the multiple stones set into a finished piece; a growing line as branded, hallmarked jewellery spreads.
  • Coloured stones and identification — rubies, sapphires, emeralds and origin/treatment reports.
  • Education — IGI runs ~19 gemmology schools that train the next generation of graders and appraisers; a smaller, steadier line that also seeds the industry with people fluent in IGI standards.
Why "toll-booth" is the right picture

A toll-booth doesn't own the road, the cars, or the cargo — it owns the gate, and it is paid by how many vehicles pass, not by how valuable each load is. IGI is exactly that. It doesn't carry diamond-price risk, it doesn't finance stones through a downturn, and it doesn't hold inventory that can be written down. It collects a small fee each time a stone crosses from one hand to the next and needs a trusted verdict to move. The catch — which becomes the whole thesis later — is that a toll-booth only earns while traffic keeps flowing past it.

A genuine surprise: a "diamond company" that owns no diamonds. IGI never takes title to a single stone it grades. The value it captures is the credibility of its verdict — which is why a scratch on that credibility would matter far more than a scratch on any physical asset it holds. The most valuable thing on IGI's books doesn't appear on its balance sheet at all.
02

Why an independent grade exists at all

Industry 101 — the 4Cs and the trust gap that only a lab can close.

A diamond is the rare consumer good where the buyer genuinely cannot verify what they are buying. That information gap is the entire reason a certification industry exists. Two one-carat diamonds can look identical on a shop counter and differ in wholesale value by several times over, on distinctions a trained grader needs specialised lighting and magnification to see.

The trade codified these into the 4Cs — the only vocabulary you need to follow the business.

The four grading dimensions — the "4Cs"
The "C"What it measuresWhy it moves price
CaratWeight of the stone.Price rises non-linearly with size; round-number weights command outsized premiums.
CutHow well the stone is proportioned and finished.Determines brilliance; a poor cut wastes an otherwise fine stone's value.
ColourHow colourless (D) to tinted (Z) it is.Subtle grade steps invisible to laypeople swing value sharply.
ClarityFreedom from inclusions and blemishes.Flawless is rare; the scale from FL to I compresses huge price gaps into small letters.

Because these judgments are technical and the stakes are high, neither buyer nor seller can be trusted to grade the stone themselves — the seller is conflicted, and the buyer lacks the instruments. A credible neutral referee resolves the standoff. That is exactly the role a ratings agency plays for a bond, or an exchange's clearing house plays for a trade: it converts a private, contestable claim into a public, tradable fact.

The critical modern addition is origin. A natural diamond and a lab-grown diamond can be chemically and optically identical — both are crystalline carbon — yet historically priced worlds apart. Only laboratory equipment can reliably tell them apart. That makes the certifier's verdict not a nicety but a necessity: without a trusted lab confirming "natural" versus "laboratory-grown," the entire pricing architecture of the diamond trade would collapse into suspicion. That single distinction has become one of IGI's most valuable services.

In plain terms

You can weigh a bag of rice yourself, but you cannot grade a diamond yourself — and neither can the person selling it to you in a way you'd trust. The gap between what the stone is and what either party can prove it is gets filled by a lab. IGI is paid to close that gap, one stone at a time, and the harder the stone is to judge by eye, the more the market needs its paper.

03

The lab-grown boom IGI owns

Roughly 65% of the world's lab-grown certifications — the single fact that sets IGI apart from its bigger rival.

Lab-grown diamonds are the fastest-moving story in the jewellery trade — and IGI is estimated to certify around 65% of them globally. This is the one fact that makes IGI different from GIA, the older and larger natural-diamond giant.

Lab-grown diamonds (LGDs) are real diamonds — same carbon lattice, same hardness, same sparkle — produced in weeks inside high-pressure or vapour-deposition reactors rather than over billions of years underground. Their appeal to consumers is simple: a visually equivalent stone at a fraction of the price. Volumes have surged, particularly in the United States and increasingly in India, and much of that production and polishing happens in and around Surat — precisely where IGI's certification muscle is concentrated.

Where GIA has historically been more cautious and selective about lab-grown grading, IGI leaned in early and hard. The result is a dominant position in exactly the category growing fastest. Every lab-grown stone still needs certifying — arguably more urgently than a natural one, because the buyer's whole reason for confidence is documentary proof that it is what the seller claims, grown to the grade stated.

Natural-diamond certification is far more contested, with GIA the dominant name. IGI's edge is not that it certifies more diamonds overall — GIA is enormous — but that it planted its flag in the segment that is growing.

This positioning cuts two ways, and both matter enormously. On the upside, IGI is levered to the single fastest-growing product in its industry, and to India and emerging markets, where jewellery consumption and lab-grown adoption are both rising. On the downside, it ties a large slice of IGI's fortunes to a young category whose economics are still being written — a tension the next section confronts head-on.

Why owning the growth vector matters

IGI's advantage over GIA is not size; GIA grades more diamonds in total. It is that IGI got to own the growth vector. As the mix of the world's certified diamonds tilts toward lab-grown, it tilts toward IGI's home turf — Surat's cutting floors and IGI's labs. A rival can be bigger today and still be positioned on the shrinking side of the board if the category it dominates is the one going out of fashion.

04

The lab-grown paradox

The single most important idea in the entire investment case — and it fits no other company in this library.

Here is the mechanism at the heart of IGI: it is paid the same fee whether the diamond in its microscope is a ₹5,000 lab-grown chip or a ₹5-lakh natural solitaire. It grades stones, not values. Follow that one fact to its conclusion and the whole company inverts the way the rest of the diamond world works.

The great fear stalking the diamond industry is that lab-grown prices are collapsing — a stone that fetched a premium two years ago may fetch a fraction today, and the deflation shows little sign of stopping. For the companies that own diamonds — the miners, the trading houses, the inventory-heavy wholesalers — that price collapse is a slow catastrophe: their asset is worth less every quarter. But IGI does not own the asset. IGI owns the turnstile. And a turnstile is paid by how many stones pass through it, not how much each stone is worth.

So the very force devastating the diamond producers — cheap, abundant lab-grown stones flooding the market — sends more units through IGI's labs. Falling prices expand the volume of diamonds the world can afford to buy, cut, set and certify. The certifier's fee base grows on unit count even as the per-stone price of the product it certifies falls. This is the closest thing IGI has to a superpower: a structural indifference to the diamond price that has crushed everyone upstream of it.

The price collapse that is a slow catastrophe for anyone holding diamonds is, for the company that merely grades them, a rising tide of billable units.

But the paradox has a sharp second edge, and candour about it is what separates a thesis from a sales pitch. Fee-per-stone is not perfectly insulated. If lab-grown prices keep falling toward true commodity levels — a few hundred rupees for a small melee stone — two things can go wrong at once. First, IGI may have to cut its own certification fee, because no one will pay a grading charge that rivals the stone's price. Second, and more dangerous, some cheap stones may stop being certified at all: below a certain value, a buyer simply won't bother paying to card a stone that costs less than the card. The volume tap that the boom opened could narrow if the product it feeds on becomes too cheap to be worth grading.

The device that only fits IGI: exploding volumes vs eroding fee-per-certificate

Most companies you can track with one operating number. IGI needs two, read against each other: certification volume and fee (realisation) per certificate. It wins from cheap lab-grown diamonds the way a highway toll wins from cheap petrol — more cars drive through. But if petrol became so cheap that people stopped driving and walked instead, the toll would still lose. If graded-stone volumes compound while the average fee holds, the paradox resolves in IGI's favour and the premium is earned. If volumes rise but realisation per certificate falls faster — because deflation drags fees down or pushes the cheapest stones out of certification entirely — revenue stalls even as the unit count flatters it. The entire IGI question is which of those two forces wins on the margin.

That tension — exploding volumes versus eroding fee-per-certificate — is not a footnote. It is the core of the entire investment case, and everything in the risk and valuation sections that follow is really a way of asking which side of it prevails.

05

The company, Blackstone, and the listing

A global network carved out and floated in India — with a private-equity owner behind it.

The listed entity is not a small Indian franchise of a foreign brand. It is the vehicle that acquired the global IGI network — Antwerp, the United States and beyond — and then listed in Mumbai. Behind it stands Blackstone.

IGI's roots run to Antwerp, the historic centre of the diamond trade, where the institute was founded decades ago as an independent grading laboratory. Over time it built out a worldwide footprint of labs and gemmology schools. In the run-up to the listing, private-equity giant Blackstone took control and consolidated the international operations, and the India-incorporated company — International Gemological Institute (India) Limited — became the top holding vehicle, having acquired the global IGI business (including the Antwerp and US operations). It listed on the NSE and BSE in December 2024.

Two features of the ownership structure deserve emphasis, because they shape the stock as much as the business does.

Blackstone control — and the overhang

Blackstone controls the company, and that is a double-edged fact. On one edge, a disciplined global sponsor has every incentive to run the asset efficiently and professionalise it. On the other, a private-equity owner does not hold forever; its business model is to buy, improve and sell. A large controlling stake in the hands of a financial owner that will, at some point, sell down is a well-understood pressure on any newly listed stock — an "overhang." Each tranche the sponsor sells adds share supply to the market, and the market knows more is coming. For IGI, the pace and pricing of Blackstone's eventual sell-down is one of the concrete, datable events that will shape the share price independently of how many diamonds get graded.

Two structural quirks to keep straight

First, the overhang: because a private-equity owner is a temporary owner, the stock carries a known future supply of shares that no operational quarter can erase — you watch for the sell-down as a scheduled event, not a surprise. Second, the calendar: unlike most Indian listed companies, which report on an April–March fiscal year, IGI reports on a calendar year (January–December), a legacy of its international structure. "CY2025" means the twelve months to December 2025 — worth remembering when you line IGI's cadence up against Indian peers reporting on the fiscal-year clock.

06

The global network

Labs, schools and where the volume actually sits.

IGI's physical presence is a web of grading laboratories and gemmology schools spanning the diamond world's key nodes — but the centre of gravity, by sheer throughput, is India.

The group operates roughly 30 laboratories and around 19 gemmology schools worldwide (approx.), positioned in the places where diamonds are cut, traded and sold: Antwerp (the historic trading hub), locations across the United States (the largest consumer market), and above all India, where Surat's cutting and polishing industry generates the enormous stone volumes that flow through IGI's labs. Additional presence spans other Asian and Middle-Eastern trading centres.

The schools are worth a moment. They generate their own modest, steady revenue, but their strategic value is larger than their fees: they train appraisers, retail staff and the trade in IGI's standards and nomenclature. Every graduate who learns to read a diamond the IGI way is a small reinforcement of IGI's grading language as the industry default — a soft moat that compounds quietly alongside the hard one of brand recognition.

Why the map points to two tailwinds

The geographic mix isn't just trivia — it explains where IGI's growth comes from. First, diamond certification is shifting toward India and emerging markets, where both cutting and, increasingly, consumption are concentrating; IGI is physically closest to that shift. Second, the spread of branded, organised jewellery retail is lifting studded jewellery — finished pieces set with multiple certified stones, each of which is billable work. A network built around Surat is positioned squarely on both currents.

The result is a business whose footprint doubles as its strategy: labs planted where the stones are cut, schools planted where the standards are learned, and both concentrated in the market that is taking share of the world's certification volume rather than ceding it.

Why are the margins so extraordinary? Because the marginal cost of grading one more stone is small — a grader's time and some instrument depreciation — while the fee is collected in full. Once the labs, the equipment and the trained staff are in place, incremental volume drops through to profit at a very high rate. There is no raw-material cost of goods the way a manufacturer has, no inventory to write down, and no working capital tied up financing stones IGI never owns. This is the financial signature of a toll operator, and it is why the market values IGI more like a data or ratings franchise than a jewellery-sector name.

The revenue mix that matters

The composition of that revenue is where the thesis lives. Three splits are worth watching each period:

  • Certification vs. education — certification is the overwhelming majority; education is the smaller, steadier tail.
  • Natural vs. lab-grown — IGI's outsized lab-grown exposure is its growth engine and its chief risk; the mix reveals how dependent the story is on the LGD category.
  • India vs. global — India dominates by volume; the balance shows how the emerging-market shift is playing out.
In plain terms

Growth in CY2025 — revenue up ~17%, profit up ~24% — shows operating leverage doing its work: profit rising faster than revenue because high-margin volume scales over a largely fixed cost base. Once the lab is built and staffed, each additional stone graded is almost pure margin. The durability of that pattern is precisely what the lab-grown paradox will decide — leverage cuts both ways, and a fixed cost base amplifies a fee slide just as powerfully as it amplifies volume growth.

08

Valuation and the metric that settles it

A high-quality franchise, priced for it — with one nameable number that proves or breaks the case.

At roughly ₹14,400 Cr of market value and about 27× earnings (approx.), IGI is priced as the high-quality, high-margin, near-monopoly franchise it is. The debate is not whether it is a good business — it plainly is — but whether the fee tap keeps flowing at the volume the price assumes.

A multiple in the high-twenties on a company compounding earnings in the low-twenties percent, with ~60% EBITDA margins, net cash and a structural network moat, is not obviously stretched by the standards of Indian quality franchises. What makes IGI hard to value is not the margin — that is durable — but the volume-times-fee line at the top, because that line depends on a young lab-grown category whose price trajectory is still being discovered.

So do not reach for a generic "mix-shift re-rating" story. The number that will prove or break IGI is specific and nameable.

The one number that settles the thesis: certification volume × fee per certificate

Certification volume growth × realisation (fee) per certificate — read together, with the lab-grown mix and the ~60% margin as the guardrails. If graded-stone volumes keep compounding and the average fee per certificate holds, the lab-grown paradox is resolving in IGI's favour and the multiple is earned. If volumes rise but realisation per certificate falls faster — because lab-grown deflation is dragging fees down or pushing the cheapest stones out of certification entirely — then revenue stalls even as unit counts look healthy, and the premium unwinds. Watch those two lines against each other, not in isolation. Everything else is commentary; their product is the scoreboard.

The competitive set

A duopoly at the top, a long tail below — because a grade is only worth what the other side recognises.

The certification world is effectively a two-name game at the top — IGI and GIA — with a scattering of smaller labs competing at the edges. Trust businesses tend toward oligopoly, because a grade is only worth what the buyer on the other side of the trade recognises.

The structural point is that certification does not fragment easily. A buyer wants the report on their stone issued by a lab their own future buyer will recognise, which pushes the whole trade toward a handful of accepted names. That concentration is what protects IGI's pricing and margin — a fringe lab cannot simply undercut on fee, because a cheaper certificate that nobody downstream trusts is worthless. The competitive line is therefore drawn less on price than on acceptance: whose paper does the trade honour?

The competitive shape to hold in mind

IGI's specific advantage is that it planted its flag in lab-grown early, while GIA's brand equity is deepest in the natural stones whose relative share is shrinking. If the diamond counter skews increasingly toward lab-grown and studded jewellery — as the volumes suggest — IGI sits on the growing side of the board. The mirror risk is symmetrical: GIA, with its formidable brand, could choose to compete harder for lab-grown grading and pressure IGI's share or its fees. Acceptance is the battleground, and it can shift.

10

The risks, plainly

What could break a very good business — clustered around one asset that never appears on the balance sheet.

IGI's quality is not in doubt; its risks are real and concentrated. Set out plainly, they cluster around its credibility — an asset it does not carry on its books — and around one product category still finding its price.

  • Lab-grown commoditisation — the core risk. This is the second edge of the paradox from Section 04. If lab-grown prices fall far enough, IGI faces pressure on fee-per-certificate and the possibility that the cheapest stones stop being certified at all. The volume-versus-fee tension is not a side issue; it is the issue, and it is genuinely two-sided.
  • Grading-integrity / reputational risk — existential. A trust business survives on the belief that its verdict is honest and consistent. A credible scandal — inflated grades, compromised standards, a certification fraud traced to IGI — would not dent the business; it could hollow out the very thing customers pay for. For a certifier, reputation is the whole asset, and it is the one that cannot be insured or rebuilt overnight.
  • Diamond-industry cyclicality. Certification volumes ride on jewellery demand. A consumer slump — recession, a weak wedding season, a fashion shift away from diamonds — cuts the number of stones flowing through the labs. The natural-diamond trade is already soft.
  • Concentration. The business is heavily exposed to diamonds (rather than a diversified basket of gemstones and services) and to India. A shock to either the diamond category or the Indian cutting hub hits disproportionately.
  • The Blackstone overhang. A private-equity controlling owner will sell down over time. Each tranche adds share supply, and the anticipation of future selling is itself a weight on the stock, distinct from anything happening in the labs.
  • Competition. GIA is a formidable rival with deep brand equity; SGL, GCAL and HRD contest the edges. A determined push by GIA into lab-grown, or fee competition from challengers the trade accepts, would pressure share and pricing.
  • Valuation. A high-margin, asset-light near-monopoly at ~27× earnings carries expectations. If volume growth or realisation disappoints, a premium multiple has room to compress.
The one to watch above all: reputational integrity and the lab-grown fee trajectory are not separate worries — they are the two ends of the same question: will the world keep paying IGI, in volume and in fee, to be believed? Everything else is cyclical noise around that single line.
11

The bull and bear case

A rare franchise — asked to prove that cheap diamonds mean more billable verdicts, not fewer.

IGI is a genuinely unusual business: it profits while diamond owners bleed, because it counts stones rather than owns them. The debate is not about the quality of the toll-booth — it is about whether traffic keeps flowing past it as the fares upstream keep falling.

The bull case

  • Asset-light toll model — ~60% EBITDA margins, net cash, recurring per-stone fees and almost no inventory or diamond-price risk.
  • Owns the growth vector — leader in lab-grown certification (~65% share), levered to the fastest-growing category in the industry.
  • Two-sided trust network — acceptance compounds acceptance, protecting pricing and share against fringe undercutting.
  • Indifference to the diamond price — the deflation punishing everyone upstream sends more billable units through IGI's gate.
  • Structural tailwinds — India, emerging-market consumption and studded-jewellery growth, from a duopoly perch beside GIA.

The bear case

  • Fee erosion — lab-grown deflation could drag fee-per-certificate down and push the cheapest stones out of certification entirely.
  • Reputational fragility — a grading-integrity scandal would be existential; the whole asset is credibility.
  • Concentration — heavy exposure to diamonds and to India; the natural-diamond trade is already weak.
  • Blackstone overhang — the sponsor's coming sell-down is a persistent, datable supply weight on the stock.
  • Priced for it — a ~27× multiple leaves the price dependent on volumes and realisation both holding up.

The two columns are really the same coin. Every bull point rests on the fee tap staying open at scale; every bear point is a way the tap could narrow — cheaper stones, a broken reputation, a determined rival, or a market that decides the multiple was too generous. Which column wins is not a matter of taste; it is settled, quarter by quarter, by the two numbers in Section 08.

12

The bottom line

What to watch, and when.

IGI is a rare thing: a business that sells trust, carries almost no inventory, earns close to sixty paise of EBITDA on the rupee, and dominates the one diamond category that is actually growing. It is also a business whose fortunes hinge on a young product whose price is still falling — and on a reputation that a single scandal could unmake.

The company's genius is its detachment from the asset it lives on. It profits while the diamond producers bleed, because it counts stones rather than owns them, and cheap lab-grown diamonds send more stones past its gate. That is a genuinely unusual place to stand in an industry in the middle of a price war. But the same detachment has a limit: if a stone becomes too cheap to be worth certifying, the gate goes quiet, and no margin, however high, saves a toll no one pays.

So the case does not turn on the diamond price, or on IGI's margins, both of which are largely settled. It turns on the ratio of two lines reported each period — certification volume and fee-per-certificate — read together with the lab-grown mix. A few quarters where volumes compound while realisation holds, and the premium is deserved. Volumes rising while fees slide, and the story hollows from the inside even as the unit count flatters it.

The bottom line

Three concrete, datable milestones will tell the tale, and each is worth marking on a calendar rather than guessing at: Blackstone's next sell-down (its size and pricing reset the share-supply overhang), the next certification-volume-and-realisation print (the direct read on whether the paradox is resolving in IGI's favour), and any sign of lab-grown price stabilisation (the moment the fee-erosion fear either recedes or hardens). Until those arrive, IGI remains what it is on paper: the world's referee for diamonds, paid in advance to be believed — and worth exactly as long as the trade keeps believing it, in volume and in fee.
