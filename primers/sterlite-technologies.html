Sterlite Technologies, the glass-strand maker the AI boom went looking for

A complete walk through India's largest optical-fibre manufacturer — how a strand of glass thinner than a hair carries the world's internet, why STL's profit swings so violently on a price it does not set, how a leftover pile of debt nearly buried it during the fibre bust, and why an unexpected buyer — the AI data centre — arrived just in time to change the maths.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Sterlite Technologies — everyone calls it STL — makes the optical fibre and optical-fibre cable that carry nearly all the world's internet, phone and data traffic as pulses of light. It is India's largest optical-fibre manufacturer and one of a small club of vertically integrated producers globally, running roughly 50 million fibre-kilometres of drawing capacity. Beyond the glass itself, STL builds and integrates fibre networks — connectivity products, fibre-to-the-home rollouts, and system integration for telcos, governments and, increasingly, data-centre operators. It sits inside Anil Agarwal's Vedanta/Sterlite group, with Twin Star Overseas as the anchor promoter.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a commodity cyclical wearing a technology label. Optical fibre is capital-heavy and its price booms and busts with global telecom and data-centre capex. When Chinese oversupply met weak telco spending in 2022–24, fibre prices collapsed and STL's margins and profits went with them — the company reported a net loss of ~₹123 Cr in FY25. Fibre is a technology product priced like a commodity, and that tension is the whole story.
  • Debt amplified the down-cycle — and is now being unwound. STL over-built during the last up-cycle and carried real net debt (~₹1,128 Cr at FY26 close) into the trough, which is exactly when a cyclical can least afford it. FY26 turned the corner: revenue rose ~19% to ₹4,745 Cr, EBITDA reached ₹628 Cr (13.2% margin), profit returned at ~₹56 Cr, and by Q1 FY27 the company reported it had turned net-debt-free.
  • An unexpected demand wave arrived: AI. The order book swelled to ₹7,309 Cr on a ~110% jump in order intake, and STL landed a multi-year hyperscaler award valued at more than $1.11 bn (~₹10,000 Cr) to supply optical connectivity for AI data-centre build-outs across FY27–FY29. The stock has re-rated hard on it. The debate is whether this is a durable structural turn or a cyclical spike a leveraged company is finally lucky enough to catch.
In plain terms

STL is a glass factory whose fortunes are set by two dials it doesn't fully control: the global price of fibre and the pace of network building. Turn both up and a plant that lost money starts minting it; turn both down and the same plant — same furnaces, same people — bleeds. For years the dials pointed down while a pile of debt sat on top, magnifying every wobble. Now the dials have swung up violently, powered by a buyer almost nobody had modelled two years ago: the AI data centre, which needs staggering amounts of optical cable to wire its chips together. The question is whether STL can pay down the debt and lock in the demand before the fibre cycle, which always turns, turns again.

01

Optical fibre 101: how a hair of glass runs the internet

No physics degree needed — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

An optical fibre is a strand of ultra-pure glass, roughly the thickness of a human hair, that carries information as flashes of light. A laser blinks on and off billions of times a second at one end; the light bounces down the glass core with almost no loss; a detector reads it at the other end. That is how a video call crosses an ocean and how one chip talks to another inside a data centre. STL sits on the supply side: it draws the fibre, sheathes it into cable, and helps build the networks that light it up. Five terms unlock the business.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Optical fibreThe bare glass strand itself, drawn from a glass preform. This is the core commodity — measured in fibre-kilometres (fkm). STL runs ~50 million fkm of drawing capacity, among the largest integrated bases in the world.
PreformThe fat glass rod that fibre is drawn from — like a boiled sweet stretched into a thread. Making preforms in-house is the hard, high-value step; it is what separates a true fibre maker from a mere cable assembler.
Optical-fibre cable (OFC)Many fibres bundled, buffered and armoured into a rugged cable you can bury, hang on poles, or run under the sea. STL sells both the bare fibre and finished cable — the more it converts fibre into cable and connectivity products, the more value it captures.
FTTH"Fibre-to-the-home" — running fibre all the way to houses and offices so they get true broadband. The mass-market demand driver for cable, tied to telco and government rollouts.
DCI"Data-centre interconnect" — the dense web of optical links inside and between data centres. Historically a niche; with AI it has become one of the fastest-growing pulls on high-count fibre and connectivity products anywhere.
The economics that decide everything: a technology good priced like a raw material

Here is the trap at the heart of fibre. Drawing fibre needs deep expertise, huge furnaces and years of capital — it feels like a differentiated technology business. But once a plant exists, the glass it makes is largely fungible: a metre of standard single-mode fibre from India, China or Europe does much the same job. So buyers shop on price, and price is set by the balance of global capacity against global demand, not by any one maker's cleverness. That means STL earns fat margins when the world is short of fibre and thin-to-negative margins when the world is swimming in it — regardless of how well it runs its own factory. The skill is in capital timing: building capacity into a shortage and paying down debt before the glut. Get the timing wrong and even a well-run plant loses money.

Who buys optical fibre and cable

  • Telcos — the traditional bulk buyer: Jio, Airtel, Vodafone Idea in India and carriers worldwide, laying fibre for 5G backhaul and broadband. Their capex cycles set the base demand.
  • Governments & PSUs — national broadband and defence networks (India's BharatNet is the archetype). Large, lumpy, tender-driven, and prone to slow payment.
  • Data-centre operators & hyperscalers — the fast-rising new class. AI clusters need vast optical interconnect, and the biggest cloud players now buy fibre and connectivity directly. This is where STL's largest recent award came from.
02

What STL actually sells: from the furnace to the finished network

The integrated model — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

STL's advantage is that it spans the whole chain, from melting glass to lighting a live network. After a 2025 reshaping, the listed company is centred on its Optical Networking Business — fibre, cable and connectivity — which did the overwhelming majority of FY26 revenue, while the pure software/IT-services arm was hived off (see §3). Follow the chain and the economics fall into place: value rises the further right you go, from raw glass toward a finished, connected network.

The revenue lines, decoded

  • Optical fibre & cable — the heart of the business. STL makes its own preforms and draws fibre, then converts much of it into cable. FY26 Optical Networking revenue was ~₹4,486 Cr, up ~20%, roughly 95% of the company. This is where the fibre-price cycle hits hardest — and where a shortage flows straight to margin.
  • Connectivity products — higher-spec kit including data-centre interconnect and optical connectivity assemblies. These are designed-in, harder to substitute, and command better economics than bare fibre. Critically, this is the category the hyperscaler AI award pulls on.
  • Network deployment / system integration — actually building and integrating fibre networks for telcos and governments (FTTH, BharatNet-type projects, enterprise). Project-based and working-capital-heavy, but it pulls STL's own fibre and cable through with it.
Why "integrated" cuts both ways

Because STL draws its own fibre and cables it and builds networks, it captures margin at every step and can guarantee supply to a big customer end-to-end — a real edge when fibre is scarce and hyperscalers want a partner who can commit volume years out. That is the upside of owning the whole chain. The downside is that it also owns the whole chain's risk: when fibre prices crash, STL feels it in the raw glass and in the cable margin and in the working capital tied up across long deployment projects. Integration concentrates the cycle rather than diluting it — which is precisely why the debt (next sections) mattered so much.

03

The company, the group, and who controls it

A Vedanta/Agarwal-group company, freshly simplified by a demerger.

STL traces back to 1988 and to Anil Agarwal, the industrialist behind the Vedanta/Sterlite empire in metals, mining and power. Today the promoter group holds roughly 44%, anchored by Twin Star Overseas Ltd (~42.9%). In 2025 the group made its most consequential structural move in years: it demerged the digital software-services arm, sharpening STL back into an optical-networking pure-play.

  • FY26

Ownership & the demerger that matters

  • Promoters: the Agarwal / Vedanta-Sterlite group via Twin Star Overseas (~42.9%) and associates, ~44% in total. As with most Agarwal-group companies, watch promoter pledge levels and related-party dealings directly in the filings.
  • The demerger: STL's software and IT-services business (STL Digital) was carved out into a separately listed company in 2025 — reported as STL Networks Ltd / Invenia, focused on digital infrastructure, system integration and data-centre projects. The point was to leave the listed STL as a cleaner optical-networking play and let the services business raise and deploy its own capital.
  • Don't confuse the Sterlites: "Sterlite" is a shared group brand. STL (this company, optical fibre) is distinct from Vedanta's metals/power arms and from other Sterlite-branded entities. When you read "Sterlite," check whether it means the fibre company or a group cousin.
Why the structure matters: a cyclical fibre business does not want a capital-hungry, lower-margin services arm competing for cash during a downturn — that is a recipe for starving the core when it can least afford it. Splitting the digital business off in 2025, at the bottom of the cycle, was a deliberate move to concentrate STL's balance sheet on the one thing that scales with a fibre up-cycle: making and selling glass.
04

The one idea: fibre-price cycle × leverage

The single most important lens on this company — and it is specific to STL.

Most companies you can read off one line: revenue up, revenue down. STL you have to read as a product of three moving parts: the global fibre price (which STL does not set), times STL's volume (roughly its ~50 mn fkm capacity and how full it runs), all sitting on top of a debt load that magnifies whatever the first two do. Move any one and the profit line lurches. That is not a metaphor — it is the arithmetic of the last three years.

Same ~50 mn fkm of capacity, broadly the same people and plants — yet the company moved from a full-year loss to a single quarter earning several times FY26's whole-year profit. The difference is almost entirely which way the fibre-price cycle points and how full the lines run. That is operating leverage on a commodity, and it is the whole reason the stock moves in multiples, not percentages.
The device that only fits STL: price × volume × debt

Picture STL's earnings as a lever with three hands on it. The first hand is the global fibre price — set in Wuhan and Wroclaw as much as in Pune. The second is STL's volume — how much of that 50 mn fkm actually sells and at what mix (bare fibre versus higher-value connectivity). The third is the debt, which doesn't push the lever but decides how hard STL gets thrown when it swings: in the trough, interest and working-capital strain turned a thin operating loss into a real net loss; in the up-cycle, a lighter balance sheet lets the same volume drop far more to the bottom line. This is why a single quarter's revenue tells you almost nothing on its own. You have to know where in the cycle that revenue was earned, at what margin, and against how much debt. Read those three together and STL is legible; read revenue alone and you'll be whipsawed.

This framing also explains why STL's stock behaves the way it does. A commodity cyclical with debt is a coiled thing: small moves in the underlying price produce large moves in profit, and large moves in profit produce even larger moves in a leveraged equity. The rally of 2026 is that mechanism running in reverse of the 2022–24 collapse — not a new company, but the same lever finally being pushed the right way.

05

The debt, and the deleveraging that had to come first

The defining risk of a cyclical — and the change that lets STL take the up-cycle's test.

If the fibre price is the engine, debt is the thing that decides whether the car survives the potholes. STL built capacity aggressively in the last up-cycle, funded partly with borrowing, and then the cycle turned. Carrying net debt into a demand-and-price trough is the classic way a decent cyclical business gets into real trouble: revenue falls, margins compress, but the interest bill and the working capital tied up in slow-paying network projects don't. That combination is what pushed STL to a net loss in FY25 — the single most important vulnerability in the whole story.

STL cut net debt to ~₹1,128 Cr by FY26 close (net debt/equity ~0.50) and reported turning net-debt-free in Q1 FY27, as the demand surge threw off cash. A fibre cyclical that funds its own working capital is a fundamentally safer animal than one servicing debt into a downturn.

The mechanism of the recovery is worth being precise about. Deleveraging did not come from financial engineering alone — it came because the cycle turned at the same time. Rising fibre demand and prices lifted margins; higher-value connectivity orders improved the mix; the demerger of the cash-absorbing digital arm stopped a drain; and the resulting cash flow paid down borrowings. Each helped the next. But note the dependency chain: the deleveraging is enabled by the up-cycle, not independent of it. That is the fragility to keep in view.

Why net-debt-free is the permission slip

A fibre maker chasing a multi-year hyperscaler contract has to spend ahead of revenue — expand capacity, buy raw glass, carry inventory and receivables for months. Do that while heavily indebted and any air-pocket in demand or any slow-paying government project can force a retreat exactly when it should be pressing forward. Clearing the net debt changes STL's posture from defensive to opportunistic: it can now fund a capacity expansion (management has flagged capex of roughly ₹500 Cr a year, ~₹1,500 Cr over three years) to serve the AI award, and it can absorb a slow quarter without a lender dictating terms. It does not make STL less cyclical — nothing does — but it removes the specific failure mode that turned the last downturn into a loss.

The honest caveat: "net-debt-free" was reported into the teeth of a demand boom. The real test of the balance sheet is not how it looks at the top of a cycle but how much debt STL takes back on to build capacity — and whether it stays disciplined enough to be lightly levered when the fibre price next rolls over. Deleveraging in an up-cycle is easy; staying deleveraged into the next glut is the discipline that actually matters.
06

The surprise buyer: how AI turned into a fibre order

The demand wave almost nobody modelled two years ago — and the reason STL re-rated.

Ask a generalist what AI needs and they'll say chips, power and cooling. The quieter answer is glass. An AI training cluster is tens of thousands of processors that must talk to each other at colossal speed, and inside and between data centres that traffic runs on optical interconnect — dense bundles of fibre and connectivity hardware. As AI clusters scale, the amount of fibre per data centre rises sharply, and the hyperscalers building them have started buying optical connectivity directly, years in advance. That is the demand STL walked into.

The hyperscaler award is the headline. A single US-based cloud giant handed STL a Product Award Letter valued at more than $1.11 bn to supply optical connectivity for AI data-centre build-outs, with volumes allocated across FY27 to FY29. For a company whose entire FY26 revenue was ~₹4,745 Cr, a single ~₹10,000 Cr multi-year commitment is transformative — it converts a chunk of the future from "hope the telco cycle cooperates" to "contracted, high-spec, dollar-denominated demand."

The genuine surprise most miss

The instinctive read on optical fibre is that it is a mature, low-growth telecom input — the plumbing of a broadband build-out that already happened. AI quietly rewrote that. Because AI compute scales by wiring ever more chips together, and because those links are optical, the AI capex wave lands on fibre makers as a demand shock they did not create and could not have forecast from telco trends. A business the market had written down as a beaten commodity supplier suddenly had a hyperscaler competing to lock in its output. The re-rating of STL's stock through 2026 — up several-fold from its low — is the market repricing "commodity has-been" to "AI-infrastructure supplier" in real time. Whether that new label is durable or a cyclical overshoot is the crux of §10.

The nuance: a big product-award letter is a commitment to allocate volume, not a guaranteed rupee figure delivered on a fixed date — actual off-take depends on the hyperscaler's build pace and on STL's ability to expand capacity and hold quality. It de-risks demand far more than it de-risks execution. The order book is the promise; shipped, margin-bearing volume is the proof.
07

The China swing: from an eight-year glut to a scramble

Why the single biggest external force on STL's margin sits in another country.

No account of STL is complete without China, because Chinese capacity is the swing factor in global fibre pricing. China is by far the largest producer and consumer of optical fibre; when its makers over-build or its domestic demand softens, the surplus floods export markets and crushes prices worldwide. That is exactly what happened in 2022–24 — a wave of oversupply that dragged fibre prices down and put STL and its peers into the trough. The remarkable turn of 2025–26 is that the same force has flipped the other way.

Reported Chinese domestic fibre prices swung from ~32 yuan/core-km in the glut to ~240 — a near eight-fold move — as data-centre and AI demand tightened supply within weeks. Global fibre prices rose 70%+ from late 2025. STL doesn't set these prices; it lives inside them. The same lever that broke its margin in 2024 is now inflating it.

Two things make this swing matter for STL specifically. First, when global fibre is scarce and Chinese suppliers are prioritising their own booming domestic AI build-out, Western and Indian buyers actively want non-Chinese supply they can rely on — and a large integrated Indian maker like STL becomes a strategically attractive partner, not just a price quote. Second, the same tightness lets STL push mix and price up together, which is what shows up as margin expansion. The company doesn't need to out-innovate China to win here; it needs to be a trusted, sizeable source of supply when the world is short.

The uncomfortable symmetry

The very feature that makes STL's current numbers so good is the feature that makes them dangerous to extrapolate. Fibre pricing is set by a global supply-demand balance that has just proven it can move eight-fold in either direction — and China can add capacity faster than almost anyone. Today the shortage is real and AI-driven; but every previous fibre shortage has eventually invited a supply response that reversed it. STL's job through this window is to convert the good years into a lighter balance sheet, higher-value connectivity mix, and contracted demand — armour for the next time Chinese capacity comes back on and the price whips down again. The cycle is not abolished; it is being ridden.

08

The order book — and the concentration behind it

A record backlog is a strength; where it comes from is the risk.

STL exited FY26 with an open order book of ₹7,309 Cr, built on order intake that jumped roughly 110% year-on-year — the visible sign that the demand turn is real, not just a price move. The book is heavily weighted toward large-scale data-centre and telecom projects across North America, Europe and India. That geographic and end-market breadth is genuinely reassuring. The catch is what sits underneath a record book for a project-and-tender business: concentration.

The pattern to internalise: STL's demand comes from a small number of very large buyers, each capable of moving a full quarter. A record order book concentrated in a handful of hyperscaler, telco and government relationships is powerful when they all pull together — as now — but it also means the fortunes of the company can hinge on one contract's pace or one government's payment cycle. This is the flip side of landing a ~₹10,000 Cr award: the bigger the anchor customer, the more the company's near-term shape is set by that customer's decisions.

Watch the receivables, not just the wins: for a network-integration and government-project supplier, the gap between booking revenue and collecting cash is where cyclicals get hurt. A swelling order book is only good news if it converts into paid revenue on a reasonable cycle. Track working-capital days alongside the headline backlog — a book that grows while receivables balloon is a warning, not a triumph.
09

The industry and the rivals

A global scale game with a few giants — and a busy Indian pack riding the same wave.

Optical fibre is a scale business: making preforms and drawing fibre at quality needs deep capital and know-how, so the field is a handful of large integrated players globally, plus a cluster of Indian cable makers. STL competes on two fronts at once — against global fibre majors for the big international and hyperscaler contracts, and against domestic peers for Indian telco and government work.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two forces define STL's position. First, it is a mid-sized global player: much larger than the Indian cable pack, but a fraction of Prysmian or the Chinese majors — big enough to win a hyperscaler award, not big enough to set global prices. Second, in an up-cycle this is less a share-stealing brawl than a rising tide — when fibre is scarce, STL, HFCL and the rest can all be busy at once, which is why Indian fibre stocks have moved together in 2026. The competitive test comes later: when supply catches up and the tide goes out, the winners will be whoever locked in the stickiest connectivity contracts and the lightest balance sheet during the good years. That is the race STL is actually running.

Financials & valuation

A real turn in the numbers — priced as if the up-cycle is now the new normal.

The question the valuation now poses is not whether the recovery is real — it plainly is — but whether investors are extrapolating a cyclical peak into perpetuity.

The metric that settles the thesis — and it is not one quarter's revenue

For a leveraged commodity cyclical, the single most misleading number is a headline revenue print, because it hides where in the cycle and at what margin it was earned. The trio that actually settles STL's case is: (1) the net-debt trajectory — does STL stay lightly levered even as it spends the ~₹1,500 Cr capex to serve the AI award, or does debt creep back; (2) the optical/fibre-segment EBITDA margin — is the shortage and the connectivity mix genuinely lifting profitability toward and beyond the mid-teens, or is it a fleeting price spike; and (3) the order book's conversion — does the ₹7,309 Cr backlog turn into shipped, collected revenue on a sensible cycle, with the hyperscaler off-take arriving as scheduled through FY27–FY29. The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A leveraged cyclical caught a genuine demand wave — now it has to build the armour before the tide turns.

STL enters FY27 transformed from its trough: profitable, reportedly net-debt-free, holding a record order book and an anchor hyperscaler contract, and riding a fibre-price up-cycle powered by AI. The debate is no longer whether the business can survive — the FY25 low answered that — but whether this is a durable structural re-rating or a well-timed catch of a cycle that will, as fibre cycles always do, eventually turn.

The bull case

  • AI is a new, structural demand leg — data-centre interconnect adds a buyer class that didn't exist at scale before, on top of telco and government fibre.
  • Anchor contract — a >$1.11 bn multi-year hyperscaler award (FY27–FY29) converts a chunk of demand from cyclical hope to contracted volume.
  • Deleveraged — net-debt-free by Q1 FY27 lets STL fund capacity and mix-up without a lender's leash.
  • Non-China supply premium — global buyers want a large, reliable, non-Chinese fibre source; STL fits, and the shortage lets it price for it.
  • Operating leverage — with the plant near full and mix shifting to connectivity, incremental revenue drops hard to profit, as Q1 FY27 showed.

The bear case

  • It's still a commodity cyclical — fibre prices just proved they can move eight-fold; Chinese capacity can come back and crush margins again.
  • Leverage risk rebuilds — funding a capacity expansion into a demand peak is exactly how a cyclical re-levers itself before the next glut.
  • Customer concentration — a single hyperscaler and a few telcos/governments can each swing a year; award letters are allocations, not guarantees.
  • Working-capital drag — government projects pay slowly; a swelling order book can mask stretching receivables.
  • Valuation extrapolates the peak — a several-fold re-rating prices durable growth into a business whose profits are inherently mean-reverting.
The bottom line

STL spent 2022–24 as the cautionary tale of a fibre maker that over-built into a glut and let debt turn a cyclical dip into a loss. Then the two forces it couldn't control both swung its way at once: fibre went scarce, and AI arrived as a buyer no telco model had predicted. The company used the window to do the one thing that mattered — clear its debt and land a multi-year anchor. What it must now prove is discipline through the next half-cycle: expanding capacity for the AI award without re-levering, and locking in enough high-margin connectivity business that the eventual return of Chinese supply is a dent rather than a crater. The scoreboard to watch through FY27–FY29 is concrete: the fibre-segment EBITDA margin holding in the mid-teens or better, the net-debt line staying near zero even as the ₹1,500 Cr capex lands, and the hyperscaler volumes shipping on schedule. Those three prints, quarter after quarter, will tell you whether STL has become an AI-infrastructure supplier or simply enjoyed a very good turn of an old, unforgiving wheel.
