Latent View Analytics, the pure-play data-science shop with a Silicon-Valley address book

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Latent View Analytics is a data-and-AI analytics consultancy. It does not build broad enterprise software, run IT helpdesks, or maintain someone's legacy systems — the things a generalist Indian IT firm sells. Instead it takes an enterprise's raw, scattered data and turns it into decisions: what price to set, how much demand to expect next quarter, which customers are about to leave, where a marketing dollar works hardest. It builds the data pipelines, the statistical and machine-learning models, and the dashboards that sit on top — and, increasingly, the generative-AI systems layered over all of it. Its customers are overwhelmingly large US enterprises in technology, banking, and consumer goods.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a rare pure-play — focus is the whole point. Latent View is one of the largest listed pure-play data-analytics companies in India. That focus is its pitch: while a generalist IT major does analytics as one line item among fifty, this firm does nothing else, so it should ride the enterprise data-and-AI budget harder than a diversified peer — if that budget keeps compounding. In FY26 revenue rose 24.3% to ₹1,060.2 Cr ($119.8M, +19.4% in dollars), with an adjusted EBITDA margin of 23.0% and PAT of ₹202.1 Cr.
  • The focus comes with a matching risk: concentration. Around 87% of revenue is from the US, and the book leans on a small number of very large accounts. That vulnerability was not theoretical in FY26 — the company's largest technology account shrank by roughly $6.5–7M during the year. Remarkably, the firm still grew ~24% overall, because the rest of the book more than filled the hole. That single dynamic — a big client subtracting while the whole grows — is the entire investment argument, playing out live.
  • It is debt-free and cash-rich — almost awkwardly so. The November-2021 IPO was roughly 326× oversubscribed and left the company sitting on a very large net-cash pile relative to its size. Latent View earns strong margins and carries hundreds of crores of idle cash, which flatters the balance sheet but drags return on equity. Deploying that cash — as it began to with the ~$39.1M purchase of ~70% of Decision Point Analytics — is as much of the story as the analytics itself. Market cap sits near ₹9,600 Cr, a premium ~47–48× earnings (approx.) multiple.
In plain terms

Every large company today collects far more data than it can use — sales by store, clicks by user, prices by region, shipments by week. Latent View is the outside team you hire to turn that flood into answers a manager can act on this quarter. It employs about 1,744 people, most of them data scientists and engineers, and rents their brains (plus a growing kit of reusable software accelerators) to a few dozen big American firms. The good news and the bad news are the same sentence: it is small, sharp, and pointed straight at one of the fastest-growing lines in corporate budgets — but it points at that budget through a narrow window of a handful of US clients, and it is sitting on a pile of cash it has yet to prove it can make earn its keep.

01

Analytics 101: what a data-analytics firm actually does

No statistics degree needed — just the ladder that turns raw data into a decision.

Enterprises are drowning in data and starving for decisions. A big consumer-goods company might know every transaction in every store, but still not know what price to set in Ohio next month, or how many units to ship to avoid both stockouts and waste. Bridging that gap — from raw rows to a confident business call — is a ladder with rungs, and an analytics firm sells help on every rung. To follow Latent View, you need to know the five rungs and the words that go with them.

The ladder from data to decision
RungWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Data engineeringBuilding the plumbing — pipelines that collect, clean, join and store data so it's usable. Unglamorous but essential: no model works on messy, scattered data. A large, steady share of analytics work is just getting the data ready.
Business / decision analyticsAnswering specific business questions with that data — pricing, demand forecasting, customer churn, marketing-mix (which ad channels actually drive sales). This is Latent View's historical heartland: analytics tied directly to a P&L decision.
Data science / MLStatistical and machine-learning models that predict and optimise — forecasting demand, scoring risk, recommending the next best action. The higher-value, harder-to-copy rung, where skilled data scientists earn their premium.
Consulting & visualisationFraming the problem and delivering the answer a human can act on — dashboards, reports, and the advice around them. Analytics is worthless if the client can't use it, so packaging matters as much as the maths.
AI / GenAIThe newest rung: large-language-model systems, chat interfaces over enterprise data, AI agents that automate analysis. Already ~28% of Latent View's FY26 revenue — the fastest-growing and most contested part of the story.
The economics that decide everything: value per analyst

A pure-play analytics firm is, at its core, a business that rents out skilled brains by the hour and hopes to charge more for them each year. Its raw material is data scientists and engineers; its output is decisions. Two levers set profitability. The first is billing rate — how much a client pays per person, which rises as the work moves from plumbing (data engineering) toward high-end modelling and AI. The second is utilisation — what share of a paid analyst's time is billed to a client rather than sitting on the bench. Grow revenue faster than headcount, keep utilisation high, and margins expand. Hire ahead of demand or drift down-market into commodity plumbing, and margins compress. Everything Latent View does — moving up the value ladder, building reusable software, adding GenAI — is ultimately an attempt to earn more per person.

Who buys enterprise analytics

  • Technology firms — the Silicon-Valley platforms, semiconductor and hardware giants that run vast data operations and outsource specialist analytics. Latent View's largest single vertical, and the source of its biggest accounts.
  • BFSI (banking, financial services, insurance) — banks and payment firms using analytics for risk, fraud, pricing and customer targeting.
  • CPG & retail (consumer packaged goods) — the food, beverage and household-products giants that live and die on pricing, promotions, demand forecasting and shelf strategy. A vertical Latent View just doubled down on by buying Decision Point.
02

What Latent View actually sells: one skill, layered up the value chain

The service mix — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Latent View's edge is that it does the whole analytics journey for a client rather than one slice of it. A large enterprise can hand it a data problem — "we don't know how to price this product line" or "our forecasts are always wrong" — and get back a working pipeline, a model, a dashboard, and the people to run all three. That single engagement runs along a value chain, and the money gets better the further up you go.

The service lines, decoded

  • Data engineering — building and running the pipelines that feed everything else. Steady, essential, but the most commoditisable rung. A firm that stays here alone competes mostly on price.
  • Data science & advanced analytics — the predictive and optimisation modelling that is genuinely hard to do well. This is where a specialist earns a premium over a generalist IT vendor, and where Latent View wants the mix to sit.
  • Business/decision analytics & consulting — the domain expertise (pricing, forecasting, marketing-mix, customer analytics) that frames a business problem and turns a model's output into a decision. This is the historical core of the franchise.
  • GenAI & platforms — the newest and fastest-growing layer: LLM-based systems, AI agents, and reusable accelerators/IP that reduce the firm's dependence on billing pure headcount. At ~28% of FY26 revenue, it is already material, not a side experiment.
Why "moving up the ladder" is the whole game

A pure-play analytics firm has one strategic imperative: keep climbing away from commodity work. Data engineering can be done by a thousand vendors and priced to the floor. Advanced modelling, deep domain consulting, and proprietary GenAI accelerators cannot — they need scarce talent and reusable software, and they command higher rates. So Latent View's success is not really measured by headline revenue growth; it's measured by whether the revenue is drifting up the ladder or down it. A firm growing 24% while sliding toward cheaper plumbing is quietly getting worse; a firm growing 24% while mix shifts toward data science and GenAI is getting structurally stronger. The service mix, not the top line, is the tell.

03

The company, the founder, and who controls it

A Chennai-born, founder-controlled analytics house.

Latent View Analytics was built in Chennai by founder A.V. Venkatraman, who still holds roughly 57.11% of the company himself — an unusually high personal stake for a listed IT-services name. Total promoter holding is around 65.1%. It grew up as a specialist analytics outsourcing partner to US enterprises long before "data science" was a fashionable phrase, and listed in late 2021 into one of the most oversubscribed IPOs India had seen.

  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter / founder: A.V. Venkatraman personally owns ~57.11%; total promoter group ~65.1%. This is a genuinely founder-led company, which cuts both ways — deep alignment and long-term thinking, but also concentrated control over big decisions like how to spend the cash pile.
  • People, not plants: the "assets" are ~1,744 employees, most of them data scientists, engineers and consultants. There is no factory, no inventory — the balance sheet is essentially people, receivables, and a large cash-and-investments balance.
  • Balance sheet: debt-free, with a substantial net-cash and treasury-investment pile (approx. several hundred crore) that is a meaningful share of net worth. That cash is both the safety net and the strategic question mark (see §08).
Why the founder stake matters here: with ~57% held by one person, Latent View's capital-allocation choices — pay a big dividend, buy back stock, or spend the cash on acquisitions like Decision Point — are effectively the founder's to make. That concentration is a strength when the founder is a disciplined operator and a risk if the cash is deployed into value-destructive M&A. For a cash-heavy company, "who decides how the cash is used" is not a footnote; it is a core part of the thesis.
04

The people-plus-IP model: from renting brains to selling platforms

How a headcount business tries to escape the headcount trap.

Every analytics consultancy starts life as a people business: hire smart analysts, bill them to clients, take a margin. The trouble with a pure people business is that growth requires proportional hiring — to double revenue you roughly double the team — and margins are permanently capped by wage inflation and the bench. Latent View's strategic project is to bolt an IP-and-platform layer onto that people engine, so it can grow revenue faster than headcount.

The lower the ratio of new hires to new revenue, the more valuable the growth. Reusable accelerators and GenAI products are the escape hatch from the 1:1 headcount treadmill — the difference between a services firm and a scalable one.

The metrics that reveal whether this is working are unglamorous but decisive. Revenue per employee — roughly ₹6M per head in FY26 (approx., ~$73k) — should rise over time if the mix is improving. Utilisation — the share of paid analysts actually billed to clients — should stay high; a bulging bench is margin quietly leaking. And the AI/GenAI revenue share (~28%) is the clearest signal that reusable, higher-value work is displacing pure body-shopping. When these move the right way together, the 23% adjusted EBITDA margin can hold or expand even as wages rise.

The tension every analytics consultancy lives with

There is a permanent pull between two identities. A consultancy sells bespoke, expert work — high touch, high rate, but hard to scale, because every new project needs new people. A product/platform company sells the same software many times — enormously scalable, but requires building genuine, defensible IP that clients will pay for repeatedly. Latent View is trying to stand with one foot in each: keep the premium consulting relationships while layering reusable accelerators and GenAI on top so it doesn't have to hire an analyst for every rupee of growth. Whether it becomes a genuinely scalable analytics-and-AI platform or stays a very good boutique that must hire to grow is the difference between a services multiple and a software multiple on the stock.

05

Pure-play leverage versus concentration: the $7M hole the book filled

The single most important idea in the entire investment case — and it is unique to this company.

Latent View's focus is a two-edged sword, and both edges are sharp. Being a pure-play means the whole company is levered to one thing: the enterprise data-and-AI budget. If that budget compounds, a focused specialist should out-grow a diversified IT major that only dabbles in analytics. But being pure-play and small means the revenue rides on a handful of large US accounts — so the same focus that gives the upside gives the fragility. In FY26 you could watch both edges cut at once, in a single number.

The largest technology account shrank ~$6.5–7M in FY26. To still add ~$19.5M net, the rest of the book had to grow by roughly $26M — enough to backfill the loss and drive the ~19.4% dollar growth. That backfill is the diversification thesis, proved in real time.

This is the entire company in one picture. The bear's nightmare — a giant client cutting back — actually happened, and it was not small: a top account fell by the better part of $7M. The bull's answer — that the business is broadening beyond any single client — also happened, and it won: the rest of the book grew enough to absorb the loss and still post ~24% INR growth. The device that only fits Latent View: growth minus its own biggest client

Most firms you judge on the top line. Latent View you have to judge on the top line net of its largest account's direction. Ask two questions every year, not one. First: did revenue grow? (In FY26, yes — 24%.) Second, and more revealing: could it grow even while its biggest client shrank? (In FY26, also yes.) A company that can lose ~$7M at its single largest account and still compound ~19% in dollars is telling you the book is broadening faster than the top is narrowing. That is the good version. The bad version is the same subtraction happening in a year when the rest of the book can't backfill it — then the headline growth collapses and the concentration risk stops being a talking point and becomes the story. FY26 answered the question in the affirmative. The thesis lives on whether FY27 and FY28 keep answering it that way.

06

The GenAI question: tailwind or undertow?

Already ~28% of revenue — and genuinely double-edged.

Generative AI is the loudest topic hanging over every analytics firm, and Latent View sits right in its path. On one telling, GenAI is the biggest tailwind imaginable: it expands what enterprises want to do with data, and a specialist is perfectly placed to build the systems. On another, it is the undertow that could commoditise the very work analytics firms bill for. Both readings are plausible, which is exactly why the market can't settle the stock.

GenAI as tailwind

  • Expands the addressable work — enterprises want chat over their data, AI agents, and LLM apps built on their own systems; someone has to build and integrate them.
  • Plays to a specialist's strength — a pure-play data-and-AI firm can move faster and deeper than a generalist IT vendor bolting AI onto everything.
  • Already real, not a pitch — ~28% of FY26 revenue is AI/GenAI-related, so this is a live growth engine, not a slide in a deck.
  • Higher-value layer — done right, GenAI accelerators are reusable IP that lift value per analyst rather than commodity plumbing.

GenAI as undertow

  • Commoditisation — if AI tools let clients do more analytics themselves, the volume of billed human work per problem could fall.
  • Pricing pressure — automation can compress the rates charged for tasks that used to need scarce analysts.
  • Bigger, better-funded rivals — hyperscalers and IT majors are pouring capital into GenAI; a small firm must run to stay differentiated.
  • Talent & wage inflation — the scarce AI skills that win work also cost the most to hire and keep.
The honest read on GenAI here

The truthful answer is that nobody yet knows which edge wins, and Latent View's ~28% GenAI revenue share proves it is participating rather than proves it has won. What tilts the odds is what kind of GenAI revenue it is. If GenAI shows up as reusable accelerators and platforms that lift value per analyst, it is a genuine tailwind and a mix-improver. If it shows up as more bodies doing bespoke integration at rates that keep slipping, it is growth that quietly erodes margin — the undertow wearing a tailwind's clothes. So the ~28% figure alone settles nothing; the thing to watch is whether GenAI arrives with rising value per analyst and a stable-to-higher EBITDA margin, or whether the top line grows while the margin drifts down.

07

The Decision Point deal: buying a vertical, and deploying the cash

Two problems addressed with one cheque.

In FY26 Latent View acquired roughly 70% of Decision Point Analytics for about $39.1M. On the surface it's a bolt-on. Underneath, it does two things at once — it deepens a specific vertical, and it starts spending the idle-cash pile that has dogged the equity story since the IPO.

What Decision Point brings

  • CPG / RGM depth. Decision Point specialises in analytics for consumer-packaged-goods companies — and specifically revenue growth management (RGM), the discipline of optimising pricing, promotions, trade spend and product mix. For a CPG giant, RGM is one of the highest-value analytics problems there is, and domain-specific expertise commands a premium.
  • Vertical diversification. Latent View's biggest accounts skew toward technology. Adding CPG depth broadens the client base away from tech — directly addressing the concentration risk that §05 laid bare. A more CPG-heavy book is, structurally, a less tech-dependent one.
  • Domain IP, not just headcount. RGM analytics leans on reusable frameworks and domain models, not purely bespoke labour — the kind of capability that fits the "people-plus-IP" ambition rather than adding another commodity delivery team.
Why this acquisition is really two decisions

Read it as a capital-allocation move and a strategy move stapled together. As strategy, it buys a stronger position in CPG/RGM — a high-value vertical that lessens the lean on technology clients. As capital allocation, it is the clearest sign yet that management intends to put the IPO cash to work rather than let it sit earning treasury yields and dragging return on equity. Both readings are favourable in principle. The risk sits in the execution: buying 70% of a firm means integrating a partly-owned business, retaining its key people, and actually cross-selling RGM into Latent View's accounts. An acquisition that deploys idle cash into a genuinely accretive capability is exactly what a cash-heavy company should do; an acquisition that deploys cash into an integration headache is exactly the trap a cash-heavy company invites. Which one this is will show up in the vertical mix and the margin over the next two years.

The link back to the cash pile: Decision Point cost ~$39.1M — roughly ₹340 Cr — and the company funded it without strain, which itself tells you how large the treasury balance had grown. The deal is the first real answer to the "what will you do with all that cash?" question that has hung over the stock since 2021.
08

The cash-pile problem: when a fortress balance sheet becomes a drag

The under-discussed half of the story — capital allocation.

Most companies would kill for Latent View's balance sheet: debt-free, with a large net-cash pile relative to its size, thrown off by a 326×-oversubscribed IPO and years of cash-generative operations. But past a point, cash stops being a fortress and starts being a drag — and for Latent View, that point arrived a while ago. Half of this equity story is not about analytics at all; it is about what the company does with the money.

The mechanism is simple arithmetic. Cash parked in treasury instruments earns a modest yield — far below the ~20%+ returns the operating analytics business earns on the capital it actually uses. So every rupee sitting idle dilutes the blended return on equity. A firm that earns terrific returns on its operating capital can still post a mediocre overall ROE simply because a large slug of its equity is doing nothing. That is the paradox: the same cash that makes the company un-killable also makes it look less profitable than its underlying business really is.

The bigger the idle-cash slug relative to operating capital, the more it pulls the reported ROE down toward the treasury yield — flattering the balance sheet while depressing the headline profitability ratio.

This is why capital allocation is a first-order issue here, not a footnote. The company has, in effect, three real choices for the cash: return it (dividends or buybacks, which lift per-share value and ROE), invest it (acquisitions like Decision Point, or building IP, which can raise returns if done well), or let it sit (the status quo that quietly drags ROE and, worse, invites a low-quality deal when the pressure to "do something" builds). The Decision Point purchase is the first meaningful step off the status quo.

The genuine surprise buried in the balance sheet

A first-time reader assumes a fast-growing analytics stock is priced on its analytics. But a striking share of Latent View's per-share value is simply cash — and the return the company earns on that cash is itself a live valuation lever. Strip the cash out and the operating business is smaller and higher-returning than the consolidated numbers suggest; leave it in and the reported ROE looks pedestrian for a firm growing 24%. The counter-intuitive consequence: a boring finance decision — pay a special dividend, run a buyback, or make one good acquisition — could move the stock as much as a good sales quarter. For a data-science company, the most important model to get right might be the capital-allocation one.

09

The industry and the rivals: a crowded, well-funded field

Pure-plays, consultancies, IT majors, and the client's own in-house team.

Analytics is not a niche Latent View has to itself — it is a contested field attacked from four directions at once. The competition ranges from focused analytics pure-plays to the biggest consulting and IT firms on earth, and — increasingly — the client's own internal team. Understanding who Latent View is up against is understanding how durable its pricing can be.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two forces define the field. First, this is a market where bigger, better-capitalised players are pouring money into AI — Accenture, the IT majors, the funded pure-plays like Fractal — so a small firm must keep out-specialising to stay relevant; it cannot out-spend anyone. Second, the most insidious competitor isn't a rival vendor at all — it's the client building its own global capability centre and pulling analytics in-house. The largest accounts are exactly the ones with the scale to insource, which is part of why a big client can shrink (as one did in FY26) even when overall demand is booming. Latent View's defence against both is the same: keep climbing the value ladder into work — deep domain modelling, proprietary GenAI accelerators — that is too specialised for a generalist and too costly for a client to replicate internally.

If Latent View keeps growing ~20%+ while its largest accounts become a smaller slice of the whole, margin holds, and the cash starts working, the premium is earned. If growth ever leans harder on the same few US accounts, or the margin slips as GenAI commoditises, the multiple has a great deal of room to compress. Headline growth is the applause; concentration-adjusted growth is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A focused, cash-rich specialist asked to prove its focus is a strength, not a trap.

Latent View enters FY27 as a rare thing: a profitable, debt-free, founder-led pure-play pointed straight at enterprise data-and-AI spend, having just shown it can grow even as its biggest client pulled back. The open questions are the ones focus creates — whether the client base keeps broadening, whether GenAI lifts the mix rather than eroding it, and whether the cash finally goes to work.

The bull case

  • Pure-play leverage — a focused specialist riding one of the fastest-growing lines in corporate budgets; should out-grow generalist IT if data/AI spend compounds.
  • Diversification proven — grew ~24% in FY26 despite its top tech account shrinking ~$7M; the book is broadening faster than the top is narrowing.
  • GenAI already real — ~28% of revenue, a live engine and a route up the value ladder into higher-rate, reusable work.
  • Fortress balance sheet — debt-free, cash-rich, able to fund M&A (Decision Point), buybacks or dividends without strain.
  • Founder alignment & margins — ~57% founder-owned, 23% adjusted EBITDA margin; a high-quality, well-run boutique.

The bear case

  • Client concentration — the defining risk, just demonstrated: a few large US accounts drive the book, and one can shrink hard in any year.
  • US geographic concentration — ~87% US ties the firm to American tech-spending cycles and the dollar.
  • Idle-cash drag — a large low-returning cash pile depresses ROE and invites value-destructive M&A if pressure to deploy builds.
  • GenAI double-edge — the same wave that expands demand could commoditise analytics work and compress pricing.
  • Rich valuation & competition — ~47–48× earnings against Fractal, Mu Sigma, Tiger, Accenture/Big-Four, IT-majors' units and clients' own GCCs.
The bottom line

Latent View is really two companies wearing one ticker: a sharp, high-margin data-and-AI consultancy that just proved it can grow through the loss of a major client, and a cash-heavy balance sheet whose returns depend on decisions that have nothing to do with data science. The FY26 stress test — a top account subtracting ~$7M while the whole still grew ~19% in dollars — was the best possible answer to the concentration bears, but it was one answer, in one year, and the top accounts are still large enough to ask the question again. The next few years turn on a single, checkable pattern: does revenue keep growing ~20%+ while its biggest clients become a smaller share of the book, its margin holds near 23%, and the cash pile starts earning its keep? Watch the client-mix disclosure and the margin line each quarter alongside the headline — for this company, the applause is the growth, but the verdict is whether that growth is getting less concentrated, not just larger.
