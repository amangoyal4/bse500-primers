Tech Mahindra, the telecom-heavy IT major on a margin-recovery mission

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Tech Mahindra is an IT-services company and the technology arm of the Mahindra group: like TCS, Infosys, Wipro and HCLTech, it supplies large global enterprises — telecom operators, manufacturers, banks, retailers and healthcare firms across the US and Europe — with software engineers and consultants who build, run and modernise the technology those businesses depend on. It is the country's fifth-largest IT-services firm, with FY26 revenue of US$6.39 billion (₹56,815 crore) and about 148,000 employees. Its defining feature is a heavy tilt to telecom: roughly a third of revenue comes from the Communications vertical, far more than any large peer.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The margin-recovery turnaround is the whole story. Tech Mahindra's operating margin collapsed to about 9% in FY24 — far below TCS (~25%), Infosys (~21%) and HCLTech. Under a plan called "Project Fortius", new management is rebuilding it, targeting a 15% EBIT margin by FY27. FY26 was the proof-of-progress year: EBIT margin rose 290 basis points to 12.6%, with the March quarter exiting at 13.8% — the tenth consecutive quarter of margin expansion.
  • Uniquely telecom-heavy — a double-edged sword. ~33% of revenue comes from Communications (telecom operators and their suppliers). That deep telecom-domain expertise — networks, 5G, billing software (Comviva) — is a genuine differentiator no peer matches. But it is also a concentration risk: global telecom operators have been slashing capital spending, and that downturn has been a direct drag on Tech Mahindra's growth for years.
  • A turnaround CEO from a rival. In December 2023 the company hired Mohit Joshi, a former president of Infosys, as MD & CEO — the outsider brought in to fix margins, prune a bloated portfolio and get growth back to at least the sector average. FY26's margin gains and record deal bookings ($3.8 bn TCV, +42%) are his early evidence; the growth line (+0.6% constant currency) is the part still to turn.
In plain terms

Think of Tech Mahindra as a global outsourcing partner that rents out brainpower — the same basic business as TCS and Infosys — but with an unusual amount of that brainpower pointed at telecom companies. For years it did two things badly relative to peers: it earned a much thinner profit margin, and it leaned on a telecom industry that stopped spending. The entire investment question today is a turnaround one: can new management rebuild the margin to 15% while the telecom slump eases and growth returns? FY26 shows the margin genuinely climbing (12.6%, up from ~9%) — but revenue is still barely growing, so the recovery is real on profitability and still unproven on growth.

01

IT-services economics 101: how Tech Mahindra earns

No engineering degree required — just the levers that turn people into profit, and why Tech Mahindra's were slipping.

An IT-services firm is, financially, a labour-arbitrage business: it employs a large pool of engineers at (largely Indian) cost, places them on client projects, and bills the client at a higher rate. Profit is the gap between what it pays its people and what it charges for them — multiplied across ~148,000 employees. Tech Mahindra plays exactly this game as TCS and Infosys. The difference, until recently, was in execution: the same levers that protect a rival's 25% margin had been allowed to slip, dragging Tech Mahindra's margin to the bottom of the group. Understanding those levers is the key to understanding the turnaround.

The only levers you need — and where Tech Mahindra had slipped
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for the margin recovery)
HeadcountThe number of billable employees — the raw capacity. Tech Mahindra ended FY26 with ~147,600 people (down ~1,100 on the year). More heads only helps if they're billed; in a soft market the firm is holding or trimming headcount while lifting output.
UtilisationThe share of employees actually working on (billable) client projects vs sitting "on the bench". Higher utilisation = more revenue on the same payroll. Raising utilisation has been one of Fortius's most direct margin levers.
The pyramidThe mix of cheap juniors to expensive seniors. A "fat" base of freshers under fewer senior architects keeps average cost low. Tech Mahindra's pyramid had grown top-heavy and expensive — rebuilding it (hiring freshers, thinning senior layers) is a core Fortius action.
SubcontractorsOutside contractors hired to fill skill gaps — expensive, margin-dilutive. Tech Mahindra had leaned on them heavily; cutting subcontractor costs is one of the fastest, most visible margin fixes in the turnaround.
Pricing / billing rateWhat the client pays per hour or per outcome. Rises with skill (cloud, AI, data, network engineering) and falls when clients squeeze. Weeding out low-margin, sub-scale accounts ("portfolio pruning") lifts the average.
AttritionHow fast people quit. High attrition means costly re-hiring and training. Tech Mahindra's FY26 IT attrition was a moderate, stable ~12.1% (trailing 12 months) — a healthy backdrop for the recovery.
Constant currency — the phrase you'll see everywhere

Tech Mahindra earns in dollars, pounds and euros but reports in rupees, so currency swings can flatter or hurt headline growth. To strip that out, the industry quotes growth in "constant currency" (cc) — what growth would have been if exchange rates hadn't moved. That's why Tech Mahindra's FY26 rupee revenue rose 7.2% (helped by a weaker rupee) while dollar revenue rose only 1.9% and constant-currency revenue rose just 0.6%. The cc figure is the one analysts care about, because it measures real volume and pricing, not the currency tailwind — and for Tech Mahindra the cc number is unflattering: growth is barely positive. Always read the cc figure as the "true" growth.

Time-and-material vs fixed-price — two ways to bill

  • Time-and-material (T&M) — the client pays for hours worked at agreed rates. Lower risk, but revenue is directly tied to bodies on the project.
  • Fixed-price / outcome-based — the firm commits to deliver a defined outcome for a set fee. Higher margin if delivered efficiently (and the natural home for AI-driven productivity), but the firm carries the execution risk. As automation rises, the mix tilts toward fixed-price.

Two structural things matter about the customer base. First, Tech Mahindra is unusually concentrated in telecom clients (see §03) — winning and keeping a handful of mega-carriers moves the whole company. Second, it runs a large Business Process Services (BPS) arm — a call-centre / back-office operation employing roughly 64,000 people (see §07) — which is a genuinely different, lower-margin, more people-intensive business bolted onto the core IT-services engine.

02

Project Fortius — the margin-recovery turnaround

The core investment case: rebuild a broken margin from ~9% toward 15%. This is what an analyst leads with for Tech Mahindra.

Here is the single most important thing to understand about Tech Mahindra as an investment: it is a margin-recovery story. For years its operating margin sat well below every large Indian IT peer — and in FY24 it collapsed to roughly 9%, against TCS's ~25% and Infosys's ~21%. A company selling the same services to the same clients as its rivals was keeping only a fraction of the profit they did. In 2024 management launched a three-year fix branded "Project Fortius", with a headline target of a 15% EBIT margin by FY27 (year to March 2027), alongside a 30% return on capital and returning ~85% of free cash flow to shareholders.

Why the margin fell — and how you rebuild one

An IT-services margin doesn't collapse for one reason; it erodes across several levers at once. Tech Mahindra's had grown an expensive, top-heavy pyramid (too many high-cost seniors), heavy reliance on subcontractors (outside hands are dilutive), low utilisation (too many people on the bench), and a tail of low-margin, sub-scale accounts won on price. Rebuilding the margin means reversing each of these — and, crucially, these are levers management controls, which is why margin has recovered even while revenue barely grew:

  • Rebuild the pyramid. Hire cheaper freshers at the base, thin the expensive senior layers — lowering average cost per employee without losing capacity.
  • Cut subcontractors. Replace expensive external contractors with in-house staff; one of the fastest, most visible margin gains.
  • Lift utilisation. Get more of the payroll onto billable work and off the bench — more revenue on the same cost base.
  • Prune the portfolio. Exit or reprice loss-making and sub-scale accounts, even at the cost of some revenue, to lift the average margin of what remains.
  • AI-led productivity. Management explicitly targets ~7 percentage points of the journey to 15% coming from AI-driven delivery efficiency (see §04) — doing the same work with fewer hours.

Why this is the whole story

A turnaround has two clocks. The fast clock is margin — largely within management's control (utilisation, pyramid, subcontractors, cost), and it has clearly moved: ten straight quarters of expansion, from ~9% to 13.8% at the FY26 exit. The slow clock is growth — dependent on the market, and it has not yet turned (FY26 revenue up just 0.6% cc). The bet is that as margin approaches 15% and telecom spending recovers, growth follows and the two clocks align. The reason margin is the headline is simple arithmetic: at ~$6.4 bn of revenue, every point of margin is worth roughly ₹550–570 crore of operating profit, so closing the gap from 12.6% toward 15% is a large, high-visibility profit lever — and it is the single biggest driver of whether the stock re-rates.

What to watch each quarter: (1) does EBIT margin keep climbing toward 15%, or stall in the low-13s? (2) is the gain "quality" (utilisation, pyramid) or one-off (currency, a good quarter)? (3) does growth finally accelerate, so margin isn't rebuilt on a shrinking base? The turnaround is only complete when a 15% margin sits on growing revenue.
03

The telecom concentration — differentiator and drag

A third of revenue rides on the global telecom industry. Deep expertise, and a spending slump, all in one number.

Tech Mahindra's second defining feature is its unusual dependence on telecom. Its Communications vertical is ~33% of revenue — by far the highest telecom exposure of any large Indian IT firm (peers typically run 10–20%). This is a legacy of its founding: the company was born in 1986 as a joint venture with British Telecom to serve telecom clients, and telecom DNA still runs through it. That concentration is both its biggest edge and its biggest vulnerability — a genuine double-edged sword.

The differentiator: deep telecom domain expertise

  • Networks & 5G. Few IT-services firms understand a telecom network as intimately. Tech Mahindra does network design, deployment, operations and modernisation — including 5G / network services — and increasingly AI-driven network operations (e.g. a 5G "network digital twin" built with Microsoft). When a carrier wants to run or upgrade its network, Tech Mahindra is a natural first call.
  • Telecom software products (Comviva). It owns Comviva, a telecom-software business (mobile financial services, messaging, digital billing) that sells products, not just services, to carriers worldwide — higher-margin, differentiated intellectual property (see §07).
  • End-to-end for carriers. From billing systems (BSS/OSS) to customer-experience platforms to running the back office, Tech Mahindra can serve a telecom operator across its whole technology stack — a breadth that wins large, sticky, multi-year deals.

The drag: a global telecom-capex slump

The flip side is stark. Telecom operators worldwide spent heavily to build out 4G and then 5G — and, with those big builds now behind them, they have slashed capital and technology spending. Industry trackers put global telecom capex down roughly 8% in 2024 (down ~10% in the first half), with carriers shifting from "build" to "efficiency" and expecting flat-to-declining spend for several years. Because a third of Tech Mahindra's revenue rides on that industry, this downturn has been a direct, sustained drag on its growth — precisely the years when peers with lighter telecom exposure grew faster.

The mechanism, in plain terms

Imagine a builder whose biggest customer is a single mega-developer. When that developer is putting up towers, the builder is flat-out and growing. But once the towers are up, the developer stops building and asks only for cheaper maintenance — and the builder's order book shrinks even though it did nothing wrong. That's Tech Mahindra and telecom. The global 5G "build" is largely finished; carriers have pivoted from expansion to cost-cutting, freezing discretionary projects and squeezing suppliers. Because telecom is a third of the book, this hits Tech Mahindra harder than a peer with 15% telecom exposure — the same industry headwind bites roughly twice as deep. The concentration that makes Tech Mahindra the telecom specialist is exactly what makes its growth swing with the telecom cycle.

04

The demand cycle and the generative-AI question

The sector-wide backdrop: soft discretionary spend, and an AI wave that is both threat and opportunity to the people-based model.

Two forces sit above every Indian IT firm right now, and both bear on Tech Mahindra. First, a soft demand cycle: Western clients — squeezed by rates, tariffs and uncertainty — have frozen or delayed discretionary technology projects, keeping industry growth muted (global IT-services growth has been running in the low-to-mid single digits). Second, and bigger: generative AI. Does it help these firms or hollow them out? They earn by selling human effort, and AI can write code, test software and run processes — exactly the work they bill for. So AI is simultaneously a tailwind (clients hire them to build AI; productivity lifts margins) and a threat (if the same output needs fewer people, revenue-per-project could deflate).

The pricing/productivity dynamic, explained

The heart of the AI question is what happens to the price×hours equation. In the old model, a firm bills, say, 100 engineer-hours to build something. If AI lets it deliver the same thing in 60 hours, one of two things happens:

  • Deflation (the threat): if the firm still charges per hour, revenue on that job falls ~40% — AI eats the top line, and a linear people-based business shrinks.
  • Value pricing (the opportunity): if the firm charges for the outcome (a working capability) rather than the hours, it keeps the price, delivers with fewer people, and the saved hours drop straight to margin. This is why the industry is racing to sell "outcomes" and platforms, not bodies.

For Tech Mahindra specifically, AI is doubly important because it is a named lever inside the margin plan — management targets roughly 7 percentage points of AI-driven delivery productivity as part of the journey to a 15% margin. So AI is not just a sector theme here; it is baked into the turnaround thesis.

Tech Mahindra's AI answer

  • Platforms — TechM "Orion" & "Helix". An agentic AI platform (Orion) and an AI/data framework (Helix) to embed AI across delivery and client solutions, plus a library of ~350 industry-specific AI agents aimed especially at telecom and manufacturing use-cases.
  • AI in the network. Playing to its telecom strength — AI-driven network operations, autonomous networks and the 5G "digital twin" work — turning its domain depth into an AI edge carriers can't easily get elsewhere.
  • AI-led delivery. Using AI internally to raise productivity and code output, feeding the Fortius margin target directly.
The bull/bear of AI, in one frame

Bull: AI is the biggest enterprise-tech shift in a generation, and someone has to implement it — re-platform data, build the agents, integrate and run them. That's services work that could reset client budgets upward, and Tech Mahindra's deep telecom/manufacturing domain lets it sell AI where generalists can't. AI also makes its own delivery cheaper, feeding the margin plan. Bear: if AI lets clients achieve the same outcome with a fraction of the engineers, the "bill people by the hour" model deflates — and Tech Mahindra, still rebuilding from a low margin with heavy exposure to cost-focused telecom clients, has less cushion than a higher-margin peer to absorb that. The honest answer is that both are true; the question is the net and the timing.

05

The company and the Mahindras

From a British Telecom joint venture, through the Satyam rescue, to a Mahindra-group IT major.

Tech Mahindra's history is unusually eventful for an IT firm. It began in 1986 as Mahindra–British Telecom, a joint venture between the Mahindra group and BT to serve telecom clients — which is why telecom is in its DNA. Its defining moment came in 2009, when it won a government-run auction to rescue Satyam Computer Services, the scandal-hit IT giant, for about $600 million. Merging Satyam in (as "Mahindra Satyam", completed 2013) instantly transformed Tech Mahindra from a telecom-focused niche player into a broad-based, top-five Indian IT major.

Ownership & control

Tech Mahindra is a Mahindra-group company: the promoter group (Mahindra & Mahindra and associated entities, chaired by Anand Mahindra) holds roughly 35% (~34.97%). This is a moderate promoter stake — enough for clear control and strategic direction, but leaving a large ~65% free float, so institutions (foreign and domestic) and retail investors own most of the traded shares.

  • Mahindra group ~35%. A large, respected Indian conglomerate (SUVs, tractors, finance, more) provides a stable, long-term anchor owner and the group's governance and brand — useful ballast for a multi-year turnaround.
  • Large free float ~65%. Unlike promoter-heavy Wipro (~73% family-held), Tech Mahindra's high free float means the market has a real say, and the stock is liquid and index-heavy (a Nifty/Sensex constituent).
  • High dividend payout. Tech Mahindra is a generous dividend payer — FY26's total was a record ₹51 per share (a dividend yield of roughly 3.5%+), consistent with the Fortius commitment to return ~85% of free cash flow to shareholders.
  • Balance sheet: net-cash / effectively debt-free at the operating level, funding both the turnaround and the high payout.
Why the ownership matters to an investor

Two implications. First, stability and patience: a ~35% Mahindra-group anchor is a long-term owner that can back a multi-year turnaround and hire an outsider CEO (Mohit Joshi) without fear of losing control — a genuine asset. Second, a real free float: with ~65% in public hands and a big dividend, Tech Mahindra behaves like a widely-held, income-friendly large-cap, and minority shareholders have more influence over sentiment than at a promoter-dominated peer. The high, ~85%-of-FCF payout policy is a signal in itself: management is telling you the near-term story is margin and cash returns, not reinvesting every rupee into breakneck growth.

Verticals and geography — where the revenue comes from

A telecom-anchored, Americas-heavy book — but more geographically balanced than most Indian IT.

We covered the vertical mix in §03 (Communications ~33%, then Manufacturing, BFSI, Tech/Media, Retail and Healthcare). The other axis is geography. Like all Indian IT, Tech Mahindra leans on the Americas — but notably less than peers: it has an unusually strong Europe and Rest-of-World footprint, a legacy of its telecom roots (European and emerging-market carriers) and the global Comviva business. That geographic balance is a modest diversifier against a purely US-driven slowdown.

07

BPS, Comviva and the portfolio

Two distinctive businesses bolted onto the core — a big BPO arm and a telecom-software products house — plus the pruning of everything else.

Tech Mahindra is not only a classic IT-services firm. Two sizeable, distinctive businesses sit alongside the core, and understanding them explains both the margin drag and the differentiation.

Business Process Services (BPS) — the big BPO arm

Tech Mahindra runs a large Business Process Services operation — the "BPO" business of call centres, customer support and back-office processing, employing roughly 64,000 people (nearly half the group's total headcount). BPS is a genuinely different economic model from IT services: it's more people-intensive and lower-margin, competing partly on scale and cost. It deepens client relationships (running a telecom operator's customer service as well as its software) but structurally weighs on the group's blended margin — which is one reason Tech Mahindra's headline margin sits below IT-pure peers, and why AI-driven automation of BPO work is both a threat and a margin opportunity here.

Comviva — telecom software products

Acquired in 2012, Comviva is Tech Mahindra's telecom-software products business — mobile financial services, digital payments/messaging and billing platforms sold to carriers across emerging markets and beyond. Unlike people-based services, Comviva sells product / IP, which is higher-margin and differentiated. Management has singled it out as a standout performer, delivering double-digit growth and outpacing the rest of the company — a small but strategically valuable piece that reinforces the telecom-specialist identity and lifts the margin mix.

Portfolio pruning — the deliberate shrinking

A core Fortius action has been pruning the portfolio: deliberately exiting or repricing low-margin, sub-scale accounts and units, even at the cost of near-term revenue. This is why revenue growth has been so muted (0.6% cc in FY26) even as margin climbed — some of the "lost" revenue was unprofitable revenue the company chose to shed. It's a classic turnaround trade-off: shrink the bad revenue to lift the average, then grow the good revenue from a healthier base.

In plain terms

Picture Tech Mahindra as three businesses under one roof: a core IT-services engine (build and run software, telecom-heavy), a large BPO/back-office arm (people-intensive, lower-margin), and a small software-products jewel (Comviva, high-margin). The turnaround is partly about rebalancing this mix — automating and improving the BPO, growing the products, and cutting the unprofitable bits of services. When you see revenue barely growing but margin rising, that's the pruning at work: management is choosing quality of revenue over quantity, betting that a leaner, higher-margin base is the platform for the growth phase to come.

08

The leadership reset under Mohit Joshi

An outsider from a rival, hired to fix the margin and reset the culture — the human engine of the turnaround.

Every turnaround needs an owner, and Tech Mahindra's is Mohit Joshi, who became MD & CEO in December 2023, succeeding long-time chief C. P. Gurnani. The choice was deliberate and unusual: Joshi is an outsider from a direct rival — a former president of Infosys, where he ran the large financial-services business and the software/AI portfolio (including the Finacle banking platform). The Mahindra board went outside the company, and to a higher-margin competitor, precisely because the fix required importing operating discipline the incumbent culture hadn't delivered.

What the Joshi strategy actually is

  • Margin first — Project Fortius. The blunt priority: rebuild the margin to 15% by FY27 via the levers in §02 (pyramid, subcontractors, utilisation, pruning, AI). Ten straight quarters of margin expansion are the visible result.
  • Prune, then grow. Exit low-margin accounts and simplify a sprawling structure — accepting weak near-term growth to build a cleaner, more profitable base.
  • AI-led reorientation. Reposition the company as "AI-first" (Orion/Helix, ~350 AI agents), using AI both to win work and to hit the productivity part of the margin target.
  • Win bigger deals. Concentrate on large, integrated transformation deals — evidenced by FY26 total contract value (TCV) of $3.8 bn, up ~42% YoY, with several consecutive quarters of $1 bn+ deal wins. Bookings are the leading indicator management points to.

The turnaround, honestly framed

Joshi's Tech Mahindra has moved two of the three clocks. Margin is up sharply (the fast clock — largely in management's control). Bookings are up strongly ($3.8 bn TCV, +42%), the best forward visibility in years. The one that hasn't turned is revenue growth (0.6% cc), held back by the telecom slump and deliberate pruning. Investors have seen IT turnarounds "almost turn the corner" before, so the market is watching for one thing above all: the point where a ~15% margin sits on genuinely re-accelerating revenue. Until then, this is a well-executed margin recovery still waiting for its growth chapter.

09

Financials & valuation

Profits recovering fast off a low base — but priced richly for a business still barely growing.

Tech Mahindra's financials tell the turnaround story cleanly: revenue barely growing, profit recovering sharply. FY26 revenue was $6.39 bn / ₹56,815 crore (+0.6% cc, +7.2% in rupees), but because margin jumped 290 bps, EBIT rose ~39% to ₹7,152 crore and net profit (PAT) rose ~13% to ₹4,811 crore. The profit line is climbing far faster than the revenue line — the signature of a margin recovery.

The valuation debate — a re-rating bet with a catch

Here's the twist that makes Tech Mahindra interesting. You'd expect the weakest grower with the lowest margin to be the cheapest stock — as Wipro is. But Tech Mahindra trades at a trailing P/E of ~27–29×, a level comparable to or richer than higher-margin, faster-growing TCS and Infosys. Why? Because the market is pricing the recovery, not the present: on current depressed earnings the multiple looks high, but on FY27 earnings — with margin at 15% instead of 12.6% and profit sharply higher — the forward multiple compresses to something far more reasonable. The bull case is exactly this re-rating: as EBIT margin climbs toward 15% and profit catches up to (and past) the old FY22 peak, the "expensive" P/E is revealed as cheap in hindsight. The bear case is execution risk: if margin stalls in the low-13s and growth stays stuck under the telecom cloud, investors are paying a premium multiple for a business that hasn't earned it — and the stock de-rates. The competitive frame — Tech Mahindra vs the majors

Same services, same clients — but the smallest and lowest-margin of the big names, with a telecom tilt none of them shares.

Indian IT at the top is TCS, Infosys, HCLTech and Wipro — and Tech Mahindra a clear #5, smaller and (until the recovery) lower-margin than all of them. It sells broadly the same services to broadly the same Western clients; what sets it apart is its telecom concentration and its margin-recovery position — it has the most room to improve profitability, from the lowest base.

Why the frame matters

Because all five sell essentially the same thing, the contest is fought on execution, growth and margin discipline. On margin, Tech Mahindra has been the laggard — which, paradoxically, is the opportunity: it has the most room to improve and, unlike peers already at 21–25%, a credible path to add several margin points. The bet is not that Tech Mahindra out-earns TCS — it's that it closes a chunk of the gap, from 12.6% toward 15%, while growth normalises with the telecom cycle. Convergence, not leadership, is the realistic prize — and because Mohit Joshi is importing Infosys-honed discipline, the market is giving him the benefit of the doubt (hence the premium multiple). The differentiator that no peer can copy remains its telecom depth: when carrier spending turns, Tech Mahindra is the most levered to the recovery.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A margin recovery well underway, a telecom cycle yet to turn, and a valuation already betting on success.

Tech Mahindra is a Mahindra-group IT major mid-way through a genuine margin turnaround: profitability up sharply from a ~9% low toward a 15% target, record deal bookings, a steady new CEO — but revenue still barely growing under a telecom-spending cloud, and a stock priced for the recovery to work. The investment debate is one question in two parts: does the margin reach 15%, and does growth re-accelerate — before the premium multiple runs out of patience?

The bull case

  • Margin recovery is real and controllable — ten straight quarters of expansion to a 13.8% exit rate; the path to 15% (FY27) rests on levers management holds (pyramid, subcontractors, utilisation, AI).
  • Profit leverage — every margin point is ~₹550 Cr of operating profit; hitting 15% on even modestly higher revenue lifts EPS sharply and compresses the forward P/E.
  • Record deal book — FY26 TCV $3.8 bn (+42%) with $1 bn+ quarterly wins gives the best growth visibility in years.
  • Telecom optionality — most levered of all peers to a telecom-capex recovery; deep 5G/network/AI domain and the Comviva products jewel.
  • Credible leadership & anchor owner — an ex-Infosys CEO importing operating discipline, backed by a patient Mahindra-group owner and a ~3.5%+ dividend.

The bear case

  • Growth hasn't turned — FY26 revenue up just 0.6% cc; margin rebuilt partly by shrinking (pruning) the base, not growing it.
  • Telecom concentration cuts both ways — a third of revenue tied to a global telecom-capex slump that may stay muted for years.
  • Priced for success — ~27–29× trailing P/E is a premium, not a discount; if margin stalls or growth disappoints, the multiple de-rates.
  • Still the lowest margin — even at 12.6% it trails every peer; closing the gap gets harder as the easy cost cuts are used up.
  • AI & demand risk — soft discretionary spend and AI deflation could hit a lower-margin, telecom-heavy firm harder than better-cushioned peers.
The bottom line

Tech Mahindra is the margin-recovery corner of Indian IT: the #5 major, telecom-heavy and once the least profitable of the group, now executing a credible plan under an ex-Infosys CEO to rebuild its margin from ~9% to 15% by FY27 — and doing visibly well on that front (12.6% and climbing, ten straight quarters). What the story still needs is its second half: revenue growth. That depends on two things largely outside management's control — the global telecom-capex cycle turning, and the AI/discretionary-spend backdrop cooperating. The stock already prices in a good chunk of success (a premium multiple on depressed earnings), so the reward-for-effort is finer than at a cheaper turnaround like Wipro. Watch three numbers each quarter: EBIT margin (does it march to 15%?), constant-currency revenue growth (does it finally re-accelerate?), and telecom/Communications trend (has the carrier slump bottomed?). Get the growth to join the margin, and a re-rating follows; if margin stalls while growth stays stuck, the premium unwinds.

Tech Mahindra's story fits in one line: an ex-Infosys CEO is rebuilding a broken margin toward 15% — the question is whether growth, still pinned down by a telecom slump, turns up before the market's patience runs out.
