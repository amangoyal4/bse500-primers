00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

ONGC is India's national oil company — the state-controlled giant that finds and pumps most of the crude oil and natural gas produced within India's own borders and seas. It is an "upstream" exploration-and-production (E&P) business: its core job is to drill for hydrocarbons and sell them. Around that core it has bolted a full energy group — a controlling stake in refiner-marketer HPCL, refiner MRPL, petrochemicals through OPaL, and overseas oil & gas assets via ONGC Videsh (OVL).

Three facts frame everything:

  • Two very different companies in one ticker. The standalone ONGC (just the E&P parent) earned ₹32,894 Cr net profit in FY26 on the crude and gas it pumps. The consolidated group — which folds in HPCL's huge refining revenue — booked ₹6.62 lakh crore of revenue and ₹49,793 Cr profit (up ~30%). When you read an "ONGC number," always check which one it is; they tell different stories.
  • It is a price-taker fighting a slow decline. ONGC sells crude into a world market it cannot control, so its profit swings with global oil prices. Meanwhile its biggest fields — chiefly the giant offshore Mumbai High — are old and naturally declining. FY26 standalone output was 18.36 million tonnes of crude and 19.53 BCM of gas, both slightly down year-on-year. Holding output flat is itself the win.
  • A high-yield dividend PSU on a green tightrope. ONGC paid a record ₹13.25/share dividend (₹16,669 Cr) in FY26 — among the most generous payouts on the market — while pledging ~₹2 lakh crore toward net-zero (Scope 1 & 2) by 2038. It must keep pumping fossil fuels to fund a pivot away from them.
In plain terms

Think of ONGC as India's biggest oil-and-gas well, wrapped around a government cash machine. It digs hydrocarbons out of the ground and sea and sells them at world prices it doesn't set — so when oil is dear it mints money, and when oil is cheap it doesn't. Its oldest wells are tiring, so a lot of its effort and money just goes to stop output from falling, with deep-water new fields and ageing-field revival the two hopes for growth. It owns a refining giant (HPCL) that smooths the ride, and it funnels a large slice of its profit straight to shareholders — overwhelmingly the Indian government, which owns ~59% and leans on that dividend cheque.

01

Upstream economics 101: how an oil explorer earns

No petroleum-engineering degree required — just the handful of levers that drive an E&P.

An upstream oil & gas company is, at heart, a volume-times-price-minus-cost business. It spends money finding and developing fields (exploration + development capex), then earns by pumping a certain volume of crude and gas each year and selling it at a realisation (price received per unit), against a largely fixed cost of lifting each barrel out of the ground. Four ideas tell you almost the whole story.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and ONGC's number)
Production / volumeHow much it physically pumps. Crude is measured in million tonnes (MMT); gas in billion cubic metres (BCM). FY26 standalone: ~18.36 MMT crude, ~19.53 BCM gas.
RealisationThe price actually received per unit. Crude is quoted in US$ per barrel (1 tonne ≈ 7.3 barrels); gas in US$ per mmBtu (an energy unit). FY26 crude realisation ran ~$61–67/bbl; APM gas was capped near $6.4–6.5/mmBtu.
Lifting / finding costWhat it costs to get a barrel out (operating cost) and to discover/develop it (capex per barrel). India's mature fields are relatively cheap to operate but increasingly costly to sustain.
Reserves & "reserve life"How much oil & gas it has in the ground and roughly how many years of production that supports. A reserve replacement ratio above 1× means it's finding at least as much as it pumps.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Upstream profit ≈ Volume × (Realisation − Cost per unit) − levies & royalty. So an E&P grows profit in three ways — pump more (volume), sell higher (realisation, mostly out of its control), or lift cheaper (cost). For ONGC the cruel arithmetic is that volume is slowly falling and price is set by a market it can't influence — so the company's value hinges on arresting the decline and on where global oil sits. This is the opposite of a software firm: its main input (the oil price) is set by the world, not by the company.

The value chain — where ONGC sits

The oil & gas industry runs in three stages. ONGC's heart is the first, "upstream" stage — but through subsidiaries it reaches into the second and third too.

02

The price-taker problem: why ONGC can't set its own price

The single force that explains most of ONGC's quarter-to-quarter swings.

Crude oil is a globally traded commodity with one broadly common price (set by benchmarks like Brent and by OPEC+ supply decisions). A barrel of ONGC's Mumbai High crude is, give or take quality adjustments, worth the same as a barrel from Saudi Arabia or Texas. That makes ONGC a textbook "price-taker": it sells at whatever the world price is and cannot raise its price to protect profit. Its only controllable levers are how much it pumps and how cheaply.

Same wells, same effort — yet realisation fell ~25% as global crude eased. That is the price-taker problem in one chart: revenue per barrel is decided in London and Vienna, not in New Delhi.

Notice what this means for the financials: ONGC's standalone FY26 net profit actually fell ~7.6% to ₹32,894 Cr even though it pumped roughly the same volume — purely because crude realisation was lower than the prior year. The volume barely moved; the price did all the damage. This is why an oil major's earnings can look volatile and "low quality" to outsiders: they are riding a commodity cycle.

The "sweet spot" and the squeeze

For ONGC there's a Goldilocks band for oil. Too low (say sub-$60 Brent) and realisation barely covers the cost of sustaining ageing fields, hammering profit. Too high (say $90+) historically triggered a different problem — the government would lean on ONGC to share fuel subsidies or imposed a windfall tax, clawing back the upside (see §07). Analysts often cite ~$75–80 Brent as the comfortable middle: high enough to earn well, not so high that the state reaches in. ONGC is unusual in that it can be hurt at both ends of the oil cycle.

03

The company and who controls it

A 1956 nationalised explorer that grew into a Maharatna energy group.

ONGC was created by the Indian state in 1956 to find domestic oil and reduce dependence on imports. It made the era-defining Bombay High (Mumbai High) offshore discovery in 1974 — still the single field that built modern India's energy security — and over the decades became a "Maharatna" public-sector enterprise (the top tier of operational autonomy granted to India's largest, most successful PSUs). It remains majority-owned by the Government of India (~58.9%).

Timeline

  • 1956
    The Oil & Natural Gas Commission is set up by the state to explore for and produce India's own hydrocarbons.
  • 1974
    Discovery of Bombay High (Mumbai High) in the Arabian Sea — the giant offshore field that becomes the backbone of Indian oil for half a century.
  • 1993–94
    Reorganised as a limited company and partially listed; the government begins selling small stakes while retaining control.
  • 2003
    ONGC Videsh (OVL) ramps up overseas — acquiring stakes in fields from Russia (Sakhalin) to Sudan, Vietnam and beyond, to add reserves abroad.
  • 2018
    Acquires the government's 51.11% stake in HPCL for ~₹37,000 Cr — bolting a giant refining-and-marketing business onto the upstream group (now ~54.9% held).
  • 2022–24
    Windfall tax (SAED) imposed (2022) then later scrapped; KG-DWN-98/2 deep-water field begins first oil (Jan 2024) — the long-awaited new growth source.
  • FY26
    Signs a technical-services pact with bp to revive Mumbai High; arrests the offshore decline; posts record ₹16,669 Cr dividend payout; advances the ~₹2 lakh-crore net-zero-by-2038 plan.

Ownership & the group structure

  • Government of India — ~58.9%. The dominant shareholder. This makes ONGC's board, capital allocation and dividend partly an instrument of state policy: New Delhi relies on ONGC's dividend and (historically) on its willingness to absorb fuel-subsidy and windfall-tax burdens.
  • HPCL — ~54.9% subsidiary. Hindustan Petroleum, a top-3 Indian refiner-marketer with ~22,000+ fuel stations. Consolidated into ONGC's accounts since 2018; the reason consolidated revenue dwarfs standalone.
  • MRPL — ~88.6% subsidiary. Mangalore Refinery & Petrochemicals, a ~15 MMTPA refinery on the west coast.
  • ONGC Videsh (OVL) — 100%. The overseas E&P arm holding oil & gas assets across ~15 countries.
  • OPaL & others. ONGC Petro additions Ltd (petrochemicals at Dahej), plus stakes in pipelines and LNG ventures — the midstream/petchem tentacles.
The "PSU discount": because the government can direct ONGC toward national objectives (subsidy-sharing, strategic acquisitions, sometimes buying other PSUs) that a purely profit-maximising firm might avoid, the market typically values ONGC at a low single-digit P/E — a persistent governance discount. The flip side: that same state ownership underwrites a very large, reliable dividend.
04

The production-decline fight: ageing fields & Mumbai High

The defining operational story — and why holding output flat counts as success.

ONGC's central challenge is geological, not financial: oil fields naturally decline. A reservoir under pressure flows freely when young, then production tails off as pressure drops and water encroaches. ONGC's crown jewel, Mumbai High, has been producing for half a century and is deep in that decline phase — yet it still anchors the company. Much of ONGC's enormous annual effort and capex goes simply to slow the fall of its mature fields, not to grow.

Output has hovered near these levels for years and ONGC even cut its FY26 production targets (to ~20 MMT crude / ~20 BCM gas) as new fields underdelivered. The headline ambition is modest: stabilise, then grow.

Why mature fields are so hard to grow

  • Natural decline. Without intervention, a producing field's output can fall ~5–10%+ a year. To keep total output flat, ONGC must add new production each year just to offset that — like running up a downward escalator.
  • Enhanced recovery is expensive. Squeezing more out of Mumbai High needs advanced techniques — water/gas injection, infill drilling, better reservoir modelling — which cost a lot per incremental barrel and have diminishing returns.
  • "Old wells dying, new wells nearby." Management's own framing: a chunk of capex simply replaces output from depleting wells with output from new ones close by — effort that shows up as stable production, not growth.
The bp partnership — bringing in outside know-how

In a notable move, ONGC signed a Technical Services Contract with bp (the global oil major) in early 2025 to revive Mumbai High and the wider Western Offshore Basin. bp brings world-class subsurface modelling, reservoir management and production-optimisation expertise; it is paid a fixed fee for two years, then a service fee linked to incremental production — so bp only wins big if output actually rises. The early read in FY26 was encouraging: the partnership moderated the decline and even delivered some growth from existing wells, with Mumbai High oil and gas running slightly above baseline targets. It's an admission that ONGC needed help — and a pragmatic bet that paying for expertise beats watching the asset fade.

Why this matters for the investment case: ONGC's value is far more sensitive to whether it can stop the decline than to almost anything else it does. A company that merely holds production flat while oil prices are decent is a cash cow; one whose output keeps slipping is slowly liquidating itself. The bp tie-up and the new fields (next section) are the two levers that decide which of those ONGC is.

05

KG-DWN-98/2 and the new-fields hope

The deep-water mega-project meant to bend the curve back up.

If Mumbai High is the declining past, ONGC's clearest growth bet is KG-DWN-98/2 — a deep-water block in the Krishna-Godavari basin off the east coast (Bay of Bengal). Deep-water means drilling under thousands of metres of seawater: technically hard, hugely expensive, but home to large untapped reserves. First oil flowed in January 2024, and the project is the single biggest swing factor for ONGC's volume trajectory over the next few years.

Here's the honest picture. KG-98/2 has been a partial disappointment on crude: oil output peaked around 35,000 bpd in 2024 and then slid to ~28,000 bpd — well short of the ~45,000 bpd once hoped — which is exactly why ONGC trimmed its FY26 output guidance. But the gas story is more promising: gas from the block was ramping from ~3 mmscmd toward an expected peak of ~10 mmscmd by mid-2026. Management has increasingly framed gas, not oil, as the growth engine, with roughly ₹33,000 crore of offshore projects in development to lift volumes.

Why deep-water projects so often slip

Deep-water fields are the upstream industry's hardest engineering. The reservoirs sit kilometres below the seabed under enormous pressure; flow rates, water cut and reservoir behaviour are uncertain until you actually produce; and a single subsea problem can cost months. ONGC's KG-98/2 underdelivery on oil is, sadly, typical of the genre — and a reminder that an E&P's growth pipeline carries real execution risk, not just price risk. The reason it still matters: even with the shortfall, KG-98/2 plus the bp-led offshore revival are the difference between ONGC's output stabilising and continuing to drift lower. The market is watching the gas ramp closely.

The gas tilt is strategic, not just opportunistic. India wants to lift natural gas from ~6% to ~15% of its energy mix as a cleaner "bridge" fuel, and ONGC is positioning gas as its growth lever — both because new discoveries are gas-prone and because gas faces a friendlier long-term demand outlook than oil in a decarbonising world. ONGC has said gas will drive its revenue growth, backed by tens of thousands of crores of projects.
06

The administered gas-price regime (APM)

Why ONGC's gas earnings are capped by a government formula — and how the cap is loosening.

Here is a quirk that makes ONGC unlike a free-market oil firm: much of the gas it sells is priced by the government, not the market. Gas from ONGC's old "nomination" fields falls under the Administered Price Mechanism (APM) — a formula the state sets, complete with a ceiling that caps how much ONGC can earn even when global gas prices soar. This protects Indian consumers (fertiliser, power, city-gas users) but transfers value away from ONGC.

How the formula works (decoded)

  • Legacy / APM gas is priced at 10% of the monthly Indian crude basket price, but is bounded by a floor of $4 and a ceiling of $6.5/mmBtu — recently nudged up by $0.25 a year (to ~$6.75 from Apr 2025; ONGC realised roughly $6.4/mmBtu in Q4 FY26). The ceiling is the bind: in a high-price world, ONGC's gas is sold for far less than it's worth.
  • New-well gas — gas from newly drilled wells and well interventions — gets a 20% premium over the APM price (priced at 12% of the crude basket), fetching around $7.7/mmBtu. This deliberately rewards ONGC for investing in new production rather than just milking old fields.
  • Deep-water / difficult fields (like KG-98/2) sit under a separate, market-linked pricing formula with a higher ceiling — which is part of why ONGC is so keen to tilt growth toward those new gas volumes.

Why this is a two-edged sword

The APM cap limits ONGC's upside when energy prices spike — its gas can't ride the boom. But it also cushions the downside: the floor (~$4) and the formula keep gas realisation relatively stable even when oil crashes, making gas a steadier earner than crude. As ONGC tilts its growth toward gas (especially premium new-well and deep-water gas), a rising share of its output earns prices that are less ceiling-constrained and more market-linked. The direction of travel — annual ceiling hikes, a new-well premium, market pricing for hard fields — is gradually pro-ONGC, but the legacy cap is still a meaningful drag on what is otherwise a scarce, valuable resource.

07

The windfall tax & the old subsidy burden

How the state has repeatedly reached into ONGC's pocket when oil ran hot.

A recurring theme in ONGC's life is that when oil prices spike, the government finds ways to capture the windfall — historically via subsidy-sharing, more recently via a special tax. For investors this is the most insidious risk: it means ONGC doesn't fully enjoy the upside of high oil, breaking the normal link between a soaring oil price and a soaring oil-stock.

The two regimes, in plain English

  • The old subsidy-sharing burden (pre-2014-ish). When India still controlled retail fuel prices and the state-owned marketers (HPCL/BPCL/IOC) sold petrol, diesel, LPG and kerosene below cost, the government made upstream producers like ONGC share that loss — by forcing ONGC to give the marketers a discount on its crude. In bad years this "subsidy burden" wiped out a huge slice of ONGC's profit. Fuel-price deregulation (diesel freed in 2014) largely ended this mechanism, a structural positive for ONGC.
  • The windfall tax / SAED (2022–24). When oil prices surged after 2022, the government imposed a Special Additional Excise Duty (SAED) — a "windfall tax" on domestically produced crude (initially ₹23,250/tonne) to claw back the extra profit. It raised roughly ₹25,000 Cr in year one, then less as oil eased. The levy was reviewed every fortnight and eventually scrapped entirely once oil prices normalised — and its removal directly boosted ONGC (statutory levies fell sharply in FY26).
The overhang that never fully leaves: the windfall tax is gone for now, but the precedent is set — if Brent spikes well above ~$80–90 again, a future government could reimpose it. So the market prices in a soft "ceiling" on how much ONGC can earn in an oil boom, which is part of why the stock trades at a low multiple. ONGC enjoys high-oil upside only within a band the state implicitly tolerates.
Why this caps the multiple

Put the gas cap (§06) and the windfall overhang (here) together and you see ONGC's core dilemma: it is squeezed at both ends of the oil cycle by policy. Low oil → weak realisation and thin margins on ageing fields. High oil → windfall-tax risk and louder subsidy pressure. The "best" outcome for ONGC is a stable, moderate oil price (~$75–80) in a period when no levy is in force — precisely the FY26 setup that let it post a record consolidated profit and dividend. Investors who don't price this asymmetry tend to be surprised that ONGC doesn't behave like a pure oil-price play.

Why the integration matters — the natural hedge

Refining and upstream often move in opposite directions on the oil cycle. When crude prices fall, ONGC's upstream realisation drops — but a refiner like HPCL buys cheaper crude and can earn better refining margins (and avoids inventory losses). When crude spikes, the upstream booms while refiners get squeezed. So owning HPCL smooths the consolidated earnings versus the wild swings of a pure E&P.

The catch with the HPCL stake

The 2018 HPCL acquisition was controversial. The government effectively sold one PSU (HPCL) to another (ONGC) to meet its own divestment target — and ONGC borrowed ~₹25,000+ Cr to fund it, denting a once-pristine balance sheet. Critics called it a "left-pocket-to-right-pocket" deal that served the exchequer more than ONGC shareholders, and ONGC doesn't even get full operational control proportionate to its stake in some respects. The defence: it genuinely diversified ONGC downstream and added a large, steady profit stream (HPCL's ~₹17,000 Cr is now bigger than ONGC's own standalone profit). Whether it created value depends on whether you think ONGC overpaid for a hedge it could have built more cheaply.

The dividend — ONGC's defining shareholder feature

ONGC is one of India's premier high-yield dividend stocks. FY26's ₹13.25/share payout (a record ₹16,669 Cr) translates into a dividend yield often in the ~5–7% range — exceptional for a large-cap. The biggest beneficiary is the government (~58.9%), which is precisely why ONGC pays so much: the state, as majority owner, wants the cash, and ONGC's heavy free cash flow in good years funds it comfortably. For an income-oriented investor, the dividend is a large part of the thesis.

How to read the cheap multiple

ONGC typically trades at a single-digit P/E and around book value — far below the market. Three reasons: (1) cyclicality — commodity earnings are seen as low-quality and unpredictable; (2) the policy overhang — windfall-tax risk and the gas cap limit upside, while government control raises the spectre of value-diluting directives; and (3) the terminal-decline fear — in a decarbonising world, the market is reluctant to pay up for an oil producer whose output is flat-to-falling. The bull rebuttal: a sub-7× P/E with a ~6% dividend yield already prices in a lot of pessimism, and any sustained oil strength, production growth, or value-unlock (e.g. listing a green arm) is upside the market isn't paying for.

10

The energy-transition pivot

An oil company pledging ₹2 lakh crore to a lower-carbon future it must fund with oil.

Every fossil-fuel major faces the same long-term question: what happens as the world decarbonises? ONGC's answer is a large, explicit pivot. It has committed roughly ₹2 lakh crore (~$24bn) toward net-zero Scope 1 & 2 emissions by 2038 — investing in renewables, green hydrogen, biofuels, and carbon capture, alongside a dedicated arm, ONGC Green Ltd.

The honest paradox

ONGC must keep producing oil & gas to fund its escape from oil & gas. The cash that pays for offshore wind and green hydrogen comes from the very hydrocarbons it's trying to transition away from. That isn't hypocrisy so much as the basic arithmetic of any incumbent energy transition — but it does mean ONGC's green spend competes with two other claims on cash: the heavy capex needed to sustain upstream production, and the large dividend the government expects. Three demands, one cash flow. How ONGC balances "keep the dividend / sustain the wells / build the green arm" is the strategic tension of the next decade — and a potential value-unlock if the green business is ever listed separately at a richer multiple than the parent.

For now the transition is a directional story, not a near-term earnings driver: renewables are a tiny slice of a group still ~99% defined by hydrocarbons. The market gives ONGC little credit for it today — which is either an opportunity (if the pivot creates real value) or a fair reflection of how small and uncertain it remains.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, high-yield national champion — fighting decline, policy and decarbonisation at once.

ONGC is a paradox: a strategically vital, cash-generative, dividend-rich national champion that the market values cheaply because it is cyclical, policy-bound, and producing a commodity the world is trying to use less of. The investment debate is whether the deep value and yield outweigh the structural headwinds.

The bull case

  • Deep value & fat yield — single-digit P/E, near book value, ~5–7% dividend yield; a lot of pessimism already priced in.
  • Decline arrested — the bp partnership moderated the Mumbai High slide, and gas (KG-98/2 ramp, new fields) offers a genuine growth lever.
  • Policy tailwinds — windfall tax scrapped, APM gas ceiling rising annually, new-well/deep-water gas priced higher — all pro-ONGC.
  • Integrated hedge — HPCL/MRPL smooth the commodity cycle; consolidated profit hit a record in FY26 despite soft crude.
  • Optionality — a separately listed green arm, or sustained $80+ oil, would be upside the market isn't paying for.

The bear case

  • Price-taker on a falling asset — earnings ride a commodity ONGC can't price, on fields that naturally decline.
  • Policy squeeze both ways — windfall-tax overhang if oil spikes; the APM cap limits gas upside; subsidy history could recur.
  • Execution risk — KG-98/2 oil underdelivered; deep-water projects routinely slip; reserve replacement is a constant battle.
  • Government control — capital allocation (e.g. the debt-funded HPCL buy) can serve the exchequer over minority holders; a persistent PSU discount.
  • Energy transition — long-run demand erosion for oil; the green pivot is costly, small today, and competes with dividend & sustaining capex for cash.
The bottom line

ONGC is best understood as a high-yield, deep-value cyclical rather than a growth stock. You are buying India's irreplaceable domestic oil-and-gas franchise — and a near-certain large dividend — at a price that assumes little goes right. The story over the coming years turns on three numbers to watch each quarter: production (is the decline truly arrested, and is gas ramping?), realisation (where is oil, and is any windfall levy back?), and the dividend (is the cash cow still paying?). Get those right and a sub-7× stock with a 6% yield is a sturdy income compounder; get them wrong — a fading field, a fresh tax, a stalled new project — and the "cheap" multiple is cheap for a reason.
