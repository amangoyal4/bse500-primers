00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

HDFC Bank is India's largest private-sector bank — it takes deposits from savers and lends to households, businesses and corporates. After absorbing its own parent, mortgage giant HDFC Ltd, in July 2023, it became a ~₹43.6 lakh crore balance sheet with ~9,700 branches, the country's most valuable bank by market capitalisation (~₹12.3 lakh crore) and one of the ten largest banks in the world.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A legendary compounder, now very large. For roughly three decades HDFC Bank grew earnings ~20% a year with pristine asset quality. FY26 standalone profit was ₹74,670 Cr (+10.9%) on net interest income of ~₹1.29 lakh Cr. But at this size, the law of large numbers is real — growth has cooled to the low-to-mid teens, and the debate is whether it re-accelerates.
  • The merger is still being digested. Swallowing HDFC Ltd bolted on a huge, low-yielding mortgage book funded largely by bonds, not deposits. That pushed the loan-to-deposit ratio (LDR) well above 100% at merger. The whole FY24–FY27 story is raising deposits faster than loans to pull that ratio back to a comfortable ~85–90%. In FY26 it worked: deposits grew 14.4% while advances grew 12%, and LDR fell to ~96%.
  • Asset quality remains best-in-class. Gross bad loans (GNPA) were just 1.15% and net NPAs 0.38% at March 2026, with capital adequacy a fortress-like 19.7%. The bank is deliberately growing loans slower than deposits — sacrificing near-term margin for balance-sheet safety. That discipline is the franchise.
In plain terms

Think of HDFC Bank as a ₹31-lakh-crore deposit machine wired to a ₹30-lakh-crore loan book. It pays savers a little, lends that money out for more, and the gap is its profit. The twist of the last three years: it merged with its parent — a home-loan company that had lots of loans but few deposits — so the combined bank suddenly had too many loans for its deposits. The entire job since has been to go out and gather cheap deposits fast enough to "feed" that loan book, without chasing risky borrowers. It is doing exactly that — slowly, deliberately, and at the cost of looking less exciting than it used to.

01

Bank economics 101: how a bank actually earns

No finance degree required — just the five numbers that drive everything.

A bank is, at its core, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors) and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap between what it earns on loans and pays on deposits — scaled to its assets — is the engine. Five terms tell you almost the whole story.

The only banking units you need
TermWhat it means (and HDFC Bank's number)
NIINet Interest Income — interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings. The core revenue line. HDFC Bank: ~₹1.29 lakh Cr in FY26.
NIMNet Interest Margin — NII as a % of the bank's interest-earning assets. The "spread", standardised. HDFC Bank: ~3.38% (on total assets) in Q4 FY26 — depressed by the low-yielding merged mortgages.
CASACurrent + Savings Account deposits — money in everyday accounts that pays little or no interest. The cheaper your funding, the fatter your margin. HDFC Bank: ~34.1% of deposits are CASA.
LDR / CD ratioLoan-to-Deposit ratio — loans divided by deposits. Above ~90% means you're lending nearly everything you take in (stretched); the merger pushed HDFC's above 100%. Now ~96% and falling.
GNPA / NNPAGross / Net Non-Performing Assets — the % of loans that have gone bad (borrowers not paying). The single best gauge of lending discipline. HDFC Bank: 1.15% / 0.38% — among the cleanest in India.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. So a bank gets richer four ways — lend more (grow advances), fund cheaper (more CASA, lower deposit cost), lose less (low NPAs), and earn fees (cards, insurance, payments, wealth). The art is doing all four without reaching for risky borrowers to juice growth. HDFC Bank's three-decade reputation is built on refusing that temptation — its bad-loan ratio has stayed near 1% through every credit cycle India has thrown at it.

Why "cheap funding" is the whole game

  • CASA is gold. A current account pays 0% and a savings account ~3%; a fixed deposit pays 6–7%. The more of your funding that sits in CASA, the lower your cost of money — and the wider your margin on the same loan.
  • Deposits are sticky and scarce. Indian household savings are increasingly chasing higher returns (mutual funds, equities), so every bank is fighting for the same deposits. Winning deposits cheaply is the hardest, most valuable skill in banking — and the crux of HDFC Bank's post-merger task.
  • Fee income smooths the ride. Beyond interest, the bank earns fees on credit cards (it's India's largest card issuer), payments, third-party insurance and mutual-fund distribution, and wealth management — income that doesn't consume capital and cushions margin swings.
02

How the money flows: deposits → loans → margin

Read this once and the entire bank — and the merger problem — clicks into place.

A bank is a conveyor belt for money. Savers' deposits come in one end; loans go out the other; the spread between the two is harvested as net interest income. Two things govern how much profit drips out: how cheap the deposits are (CASA mix) and how much of the deposit base is lent out (the LDR). The merger broke the balance at the front of this belt — which is why the whole story is about refilling it.

The merger dumped a huge pile of Stage-3 loans (HDFC Ltd's mortgages) onto the belt without matching Stage-1/2 deposits — so the belt ran "loan-heavy". Fixing it means feeding far more cheap deposits in at the left.

The loan-to-deposit ratio, in one picture

If a bank has ₹100 of deposits and ₹100 of loans, its LDR is 100% — it has lent out every rupee it took in, with no buffer. Regulators and rating agencies get nervous above ~90%. At the HDFC Ltd merger, the combined LDR jumped to ~110% because HDFC Ltd funded its mortgages with bonds, not deposits. Pulling that back to a safe ~85–90% is a multi-year deleveraging — done not by shrinking loans, but by out-growing them with deposits.

Why deposits growing faster than loans is good news here

In a normal year you'd cheer when a bank's loans grow faster than deposits — it's lending more. But HDFC Bank is the unusual case where the opposite is the win. It inherited too many loans relative to deposits, so growing deposits faster repairs the balance sheet, lowers the LDR, reduces reliance on expensive bond funding, and sets up cheaper funding (and therefore wider margins) later. The cost is that lending — and thus near-term margin and earnings growth — is deliberately throttled while the repair happens. FY26 shows the repair is working.

03

The company and who controls it

From a 1994 start-up to India's most valuable bank — with no promoter at all.

HDFC Bank was founded in 1994, promoted by the housing-finance pioneer HDFC Ltd as part of India's liberalisation-era licensing of private banks. Under Aditya Puri (CEO 1994–2020) it became the gold standard of Indian retail banking; Sashidhar Jagdishan took over in 2020 and steered the historic merger.

Timeline

  • 1994
    HDFC Bank incorporated, promoted by HDFC Ltd among the first batch of new private banks after India's banking reforms.
  • 1995–2020
    Three decades of ~20%+ annual earnings growth under Aditya Puri, with bad loans held near ~1% through every cycle — the benchmark for Indian private banking.
  • 2008
    Acquires Centurion Bank of Punjab, an early scale-up that widened the branch network.
  • Oct 2020
    Sashidhar Jagdishan becomes MD & CEO; the leadership transition is managed without a stumble.
  • 1 Jul 2023
    Merger with parent HDFC Ltd completes — a ~$40bn all-stock deal, the largest in Indian corporate history. The bank absorbs the country's biggest mortgage book and becomes a top-10 global bank.
  • FY24–FY25
    The deposit-mobilisation phase — deposits out-grow loans to pull the elevated LDR back down; HDB Financial Services (the NBFC arm) is listed.
  • FY26
    Standalone PAT ₹74,670 Cr (+10.9%); deposits +14.4% vs advances +12%; LDR back to ~96%; GNPA 1.15%; total dividend ₹15.5/share. The digestion is visibly progressing.

Ownership & management

  • No promoter. A defining quirk: after the merger, HDFC Ltd ceased to exist, so HDFC Bank now has 0% promoter holding — it is a fully professionally-managed, widely-held institution, a rarity among large Indian companies.
  • Foreign and domestic institutions dominate the register. Foreign institutional investors (FIIs) hold roughly ~44%, with large and rising domestic institutional / mutual-fund ownership. It is one of the most widely-held stocks in every India-focused index and fund.
  • Management: Sashidhar Jagdishan (MD & CEO) leads a deep professional bench. Continuity of culture — conservative underwriting, retail focus, technology investment — is the franchise asset, not any single owner.
  • A holding-company structure: alongside the bank sit listed/large subsidiaries — HDFC Life, HDFC ERGO, HDFC AMC, HDB Financial Services, HDFC Securities — making the consolidated group a financial-services conglomerate.
04

The HDFC Ltd mega-merger — and its long digestion

The single most important event in the bank's history, and the force shaping every number today.

On 1 July 2023, HDFC Bank did something almost no bank ever does: it absorbed its own parent. Housing Development Finance Corporation (HDFC Ltd) — India's largest and most respected mortgage lender — merged into the bank it had created in 1994. The ~$40bn all-stock deal was the largest in Indian corporate history and created a top-10 global bank. It was strategically brilliant and operationally indigestible at the same time — and understanding why is the key to the whole investment case.

Why do it at all?

  • Regulation made the old structure costly. Tightening rules were eroding the advantages of running a separate non-bank mortgage lender; folding it into the bank gave HDFC Ltd's mortgages access to the bank's cheap deposit funding.
  • A cross-sell goldmine. Roughly 70% of HDFC Ltd's mortgage customers did not bank with HDFC Bank. The merger handed the bank a vast base to sell deposits, cards, insurance and investments into — and made it India's clear mortgage leader overnight.
  • One balance sheet, more firepower. Combined, the entity could underwrite larger loans, deepen the retail franchise, and rank among the largest banks on earth.

The catch: a loan-heavy balance sheet

HDFC Ltd, as a non-bank, funded its mortgages mostly with bonds and borrowings — not deposits. So the merger bolted on a giant pile of loans without a matching pile of deposits. Three knock-on effects defined FY24–FY26:

The mental model: a snake swallowing a large meal

The merger is best pictured as a snake that swallowed something almost its own size. The meal (HDFC Ltd's ₹6-lakh-crore-plus mortgage book) is nutritious — quality home loans, a leadership position, a huge cross-sell base — but digesting it takes years. During digestion the bank looks sluggish: loan growth is throttled, margins are compressed, earnings grow in the low teens rather than the old ~20%. The bull case is simply that digestion ends — once deposits catch up and the LDR normalises around ~85–90%, the bank can lend freely again, margins recover, and a far larger HDFC Bank resumes compounding. FY26's deposits-beat-loans result is the clearest sign yet that the meal is going down.

Why this is the section that matters: almost every "weak" HDFC Bank number of the last three years — soft NIM, sub-15% earnings growth, deliberately slow loan growth — traces back to merger digestion, not a broken franchise. Conversely, the entire re-rating case rests on that digestion finishing on schedule. Watch the LDR and deposit growth every quarter: they are the merger's vital signs.
05

The deposit-mobilisation race

The hardest, most valuable thing in Indian banking right now — and HDFC Bank's central task.

Because the merger left the bank loan-heavy, its growth is now gated by deposits: it can only lend as fast as it can gather funding. And it must do so in the toughest deposit market in years — Indian households are shifting savings into mutual funds, equities and higher-yield products, so every bank is scrapping for the same shrinking pool of deposits. Winning that race cheaply is what separates a great bank from a good one.

CASA share has drifted down across the industry as savers chase yield — so banks lean on costlier term deposits to fund growth. Rebuilding CASA is the key to lower funding cost and wider margins.

HDFC Bank's weapons in the deposit war

  • The largest private branch network — ~9,700 branches and ~21,000 ATMs, deliberately expanded into semi-urban and rural India where deposits are stickier and CASA-rich. Branches are deposit-gathering machines; HDFC has been adding them aggressively.
  • The merger's own cross-sell base — the ~70% of former HDFC Ltd mortgage customers who didn't bank with HDFC Bank are a captive pool to convert into salary accounts and savers.
  • Trust and brand — in a deposit market built on safety, the HDFC name and a 30-year clean record are a genuine funding advantage; nervous savers gravitate to the strongest bank.
  • Payments and salary relationships — being India's largest credit-card issuer and a payroll-banking leader feeds low-cost current and salary accounts.
Why this race decides the next five years

If HDFC Bank keeps mobilising deposits faster than the system — as it did in FY26 (+14.4% vs system growth in the ~10–11% range) — three good things happen at once: the LDR normalises, dependence on expensive bond funding falls, and the funding cost edges down. That combination quietly widens the margin and lets loan growth re-accelerate without taking risk. If it can't — if deposits stay scarce and expensive — the LDR stays stuck, loan growth stays throttled, and the long-promised margin recovery keeps slipping. Every quarter, the deposit-growth number is the leading indicator for everything else.

06

Asset quality and the 30-year track record

The franchise asset that no merger or cycle has dented — and the reason for the premium.

A bank can grow fast for a while by lending recklessly — then blow up when the loans go bad. HDFC Bank's defining trait is that it has done the opposite for three decades: grow steadily while keeping bad loans extraordinarily low. At March 2026, gross NPAs were just 1.15% and net NPAs 0.38% — and crucially, much of even that is well-secured retail and agri lending.

A ~19.7% capital ratio against ~1% bad loans is "fortress" territory — it means the bank could absorb a severe downturn and keep lending. The contrast with weaker peers is the entire premium.
What "provisioning" means and why it matters

When a loan looks shaky, the bank sets aside money to cover the expected loss — a provision, which is a charge against profit. A conservative bank provisions early and heavily, so a bad loan rarely springs a nasty surprise. HDFC Bank's credit cost (provisions as a share of loans) ran around ~0.35% in Q4 FY26 — very low — and it carries extra "floating"/contingent provisions as a rainy-day buffer. The net NPA of just 0.38% means almost all recognised bad loans are already provided for. This is the quiet machinery behind the bank's reputation: bad news is taken on the chin in advance, not deferred.

Why the record is durable, not lucky

  • Retail-skewed, granular book — millions of small, secured loans (mortgages, salaried personal loans, cars) diversify risk far better than a few large corporate exposures that can sink a bank.
  • Underwriting culture — three decades of saying "no" to bad credit, even when rivals chased it, is embedded in how the bank operates. Culture, not a formula, is the moat.
  • Through-the-cycle proof — the demonetisation shock, the NBFC crisis, COVID, and now the merger: through each, the GNPA stayed near ~1%. That consistency is what earns the valuation premium.
The honest caveat: exceptional asset quality is partly a choice to under-grow risky segments — which costs some yield and growth. And no bank is immune to a systemic shock or an unsecured-lending cycle turning. But on the evidence of 30 years and four crises, the burden of proof sits firmly with the doubters.
07

The industry and the rivals

A vast, under-penetrated market — private banks taking share from public-sector incumbents.

Indian banking is large, growing and structurally favourable to well-run private banks. Credit penetration is still low versus developed economies, the economy is compounding nominal GDP at ~10%+, and the long arc is private banks (HDFC, ICICI, Axis, Kotak) steadily winning share from state-owned banks on service, technology and underwriting. HDFC Bank is the scale leader of that cohort.

The competitive tension to watch

The live debate is HDFC Bank vs ICICI Bank. For the last few years ICICI has out-grown HDFC on loans, NIM and return on equity — partly because it wasn't weighed down by a merger digestion. Bears argue ICICI has structurally overtaken the old king; bulls argue HDFC is simply mid-repair and, once the LDR normalises, its unmatched deposit franchise and scale let it close the gap. Meanwhile the secular tailwind — private banks taking share from public-sector banks, and formal credit deepening across India — lifts the whole cohort. The question isn't whether HDFC Bank grows; it's whether it grows fast enough, soon enough to justify its premium versus a surging ICICI.

The regulatory backdrop: banks operate under the Reserve Bank of India's tight prudential regime — capital rules (Basel III), cash-reserve (CRR) and statutory-liquidity (SLR) requirements, priority-sector lending quotas, and rules on provisioning and unsecured credit. The RBI's stance on deposit competition, LDR norms, and project/unsecured lending directly shapes HDFC Bank's room to manoeuvre. Regulatory comfort with the bank's discipline is itself part of the franchise.
08

The law of large numbers

The structural force unique to a company this big — the bear's most durable argument.

For ~25 years, "buy HDFC Bank and forget it" worked because the bank compounded earnings at ~20% a year, doubling every ~3–4 years. But arithmetic eventually bites: a ₹43.6-lakh-crore balance sheet earning ₹75,000 Cr cannot grow at the rate a ₹50,000-crore bank could. To grow 20%, today's HDFC Bank must add more new business in a single year than most Indian banks have in total. That's the law of large numbers — and it, not the merger, is the deeper question over a decade.

Put bluntly: a large share of HDFC Bank's slowdown is not a fixable merger problem — it is the unavoidable maths of being one of the world's largest banks in a single (if fast-growing) country. The merger drag will pass; the size will not.

Why this is serious — but not the end of the story

Three offsets keep the case alive despite the size. (1) The market itself is enormous and under-penetrated. India's credit-to-GDP is far below developed peers; nominal GDP compounds ~10%+; formalisation and rising incomes mean the pie is still growing fast — a big bank can grow at a healthy mid-teens rate for a long time just by riding the country. (2) Share gains from public-sector banks add growth on top of the market's. (3) Fee and subsidiary income (cards, insurance via HDFC Life/ERGO, asset management via HDFC AMC, the listed HDB Financial Services) grow capital-light and faster than the loan book. Net: the realistic ask isn't a return to ~20% — it's durable, high-quality ~13–17% compounding on a vast base, with returns on equity climbing back toward the high-teens as the merger drag clears. For a bank this safe, that can still be a fine outcome — just not the rocket of the 2000s.

The honest tension: bulls underwrite mid-teens compounding for a decade from share gains, fee income and a re-rating as the merger clears; bears argue a single-country bank at ~10% of India's banking system has a structural growth ceiling, and that a nimbler ICICI will keep out-earning it. Watch loan growth and ROE together: if advances re-accelerate toward the mid-teens and ROE climbs back toward ~17%+, the size worry is being out-run; if both stall, the law of large numbers is winning.

The debate is less "is it a good bank" (it plainly is) and more "how fast does growth and ROE recover".

How to think about the valuation

HDFC Bank trades around ~₹12.3 lakh crore market cap on FY26 earnings — roughly the high-teens times earnings and a little over ~2× book value, with a ~1% dividend yield. For most of its history the stock commanded a far richer premium (3–4× book); the merger digestion and the ICICI comparison have de-rated it to a more ordinary multiple. Bulls see that as the opportunity: a best-in-class, fortress-capital, ~14–15% ROE bank at a near-market multiple, with margin and ROE recovery still to come as the LDR normalises. Bears counter that the premium should compress permanently given the slower structural growth and a sharper rival. For a bank, the multiple that matters is price-to-book against ROE: as ROE climbs back toward ~17%+, a re-rating is the bull's prize; if ROE stalls near ~14%, the current multiple is about right.

Deposit-led re-acceleration, a cross-sell goldmine, and a financial-services group.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely great bank, mid-repair — the debate is recovery speed, not quality.

HDFC Bank is one of the highest-quality banks in the world: fortress capital, pristine asset quality, an unmatched deposit and distribution franchise, and a professional, widely-held ownership structure. The investment debate is almost entirely about how quickly growth and returns recover from the merger — and whether a surging ICICI has permanently changed the pecking order.

The bull case

  • Best-in-class quality — ~1% bad loans through every cycle, ~19.7% capital, conservative underwriting culture as the moat.
  • Merger digestion progressing — FY26 deposits (+14.4%) out-grew loans (+12%); LDR down to ~96% and falling toward ~85–90%.
  • Unmatched deposit & distribution franchise — ~9,700 branches, India's #1 card issuer, a huge cross-sell base from the merger.
  • Margin & ROE recovery ahead — cheaper deposit funding and a normalised LDR set up wider NIM and higher returns.
  • Reasonable valuation — de-rated to ~2× book / high-teens P/E for a ~14–15% ROE fortress bank — a rare entry multiple.

The bear case

  • Law of large numbers — a top-10 global bank in one country has a structurally lower growth ceiling; ~20% is gone for good.
  • ICICI is out-running it — higher NIM, ROE and loan growth at the closest rival; the premium may compress further.
  • Deposit war — savers shifting to mutual funds/equities makes cheap deposits scarce; CASA share has drifted down.
  • Margin stuck low — NIM near ~3.4% with a slow, uncertain path back up; recovery keeps getting pushed out.
  • Macro & regulation — rate cuts squeeze loan yields faster than deposit costs; RBI rules on LDR/unsecured credit, and any unsecured-lending cycle, are perennial risks.
The bottom line

HDFC Bank is a structurally advantaged, fortress-quality franchise in the middle of a once-in-a-lifetime merger digestion. The business risk is low; the questions are about pace — how fast deposits refill the balance sheet, how soon the LDR and margin normalise, and whether the bank can re-accelerate to durable mid-teens growth before the market's patience (and the ICICI comparison) wears thin. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter — deposit growth, the LDR, and ROE. Get those right and a de-rated, world-class bank compounds handsomely; stall on them, and the law of large numbers and a sharper rival keep the lid on.
