00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Yes Bank is a mid-sized Indian private-sector bank with a ₹4.69 lakh crore balance sheet and one of the most extraordinary corporate histories in the country. Founded in 2004 by Rana Kapoor, it grew explosively for a decade by lending aggressively to risky corporate borrowers — then imploded in 2020, ran out of capital, suffered a depositor run, and was placed under an RBI moratorium. It did not go bust: the RBI engineered a reconstruction in which State Bank of India and a consortium of banks injected fresh capital and took control. Six years on, the bank is profitable, cleaned up, and — as of 2025 — owned at the top by Japan's Sumitomo Mitsui Banking Corporation (SMBC), the largest single shareholder.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A new, powerful anchor owner. In September 2025 SMBC — one of Japan's three "megabanks" — completed the purchase of a 20% stake from SBI and the rescue consortium, becoming Yes Bank's largest shareholder, and is moving to 24.2% via a further buy from a Carlyle affiliate. SBI, the 2020 rescuer, has cut its stake to ~10%. This is the re-rating thesis in one line: a shaky, ownerless turnaround bank now has a deep-pocketed, credible global parent.
  • The recovery is real, but the returns are still low. FY26 net profit was ₹3,476 Cr (up 44.5%), asset quality is clean (net NPAs 0.2%), and deposits are rebuilt. But the bank's margin (NIM ~2.6%) and return on assets (~0.8%) remain well below quality peers — this is a bank that has stopped bleeding but has not yet learned to earn like HDFC or ICICI. The whole investment question is whether it can.
  • It has no promoter. Even with SMBC at 24.2%, Yes Bank is — by RBI's own ruling — a professionally-managed bank with no promoter. SMBC deliberately stays just under 25% (to avoid triggering a mandatory open offer and promoter classification), so it is a powerful strategic anchor and largest shareholder, but not a controlling promoter.
In plain terms

Think of Yes Bank as a bank that crashed, was towed to the workshop, rebuilt part by part, and has just been bought by a careful new owner. In 2020 it nearly failed — too many bad corporate loans, not enough capital, and panicked depositors pulling money out. India's central bank stepped in, SBI and other banks put in fresh money, and the bank was saved rather than allowed to collapse. Since then, management has spent years doing the unglamorous work: selling off the bad loans, winning depositors back, and shifting to safer, smaller retail loans. Now Japan's SMBC — a giant, conservative global bank — has bought the biggest stake. The bet is that a strong, cheap-funded, well-connected parent can finally turn a survivor into a quality bank. The honest catch: it isn't there yet. It earns a thin margin and a low return, and that gap is the entire story from here.

01

Bank economics 101: how a bank actually earns (and how it dies)

No finance degree required — just the six numbers that drive everything, plus the one that kills.

A bank is, at its core, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors) and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap between what it earns on loans and what it pays on deposits — scaled to its assets — is the engine. But a bank is also a confidence machine: it lends out most of the money it takes in, so if too many depositors ask for their money back at once, or too many loans go bad at once, it can fail almost overnight. Yes Bank is the rare stock where you need both halves of this — how a bank earns, and how it dies — because it did both.

The banking units you need — with Yes Bank's FY26 number
TermWhat it means (and Yes Bank's FY26 number)
NIINet Interest Income — interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings. The core revenue line. Yes Bank: ~₹9,776 Cr in FY26.
NIMNet Interest Margin — NII as a % of interest-earning assets; the "spread", standardised. Yes Bank: ~2.6% for FY26 (2.7% in Q4). Structurally low — big private banks run ~3.4–4.4%. This is Yes Bank's central weakness and its biggest improvement lever.
CASACurrent + Savings Account deposits — everyday money paying little or no interest. Cheaper funding = fatter margin. Yes Bank: CASA ratio 35.1% (CASA crossed ₹1 lakh Cr in FY26) — rebuilt from the 2020 collapse.
GNPA / NNPAGross / Net Non-Performing Assets — the % of loans gone bad. The gauge of lending discipline (and the thing that killed the old Yes Bank). Now: 1.3% / 0.2% — cleaned up and reported clean.

RoA / RoEReturn on Assets / Equity — profit per rupee of assets / of shareholder capital. Yes Bank: ~0.8% RoA, ~7% RoE in FY26 — low; a healthy bank targets ~1.5%+ RoA and mid-teens RoE. Closing this gap is the entire thesis.
The simple profit equation — and the two ways a bank dies

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. A bank gets richer four ways — lend more, fund cheaper (more CASA), lose less (low NPAs), and earn fees. But a bank dies two ways, and they are linked. (1) Too many loans go bad: loan losses eat into the thin cushion of capital a bank holds; if losses exceed the cushion, the bank is insolvent. (2) A depositor run: because a bank has lent out most of the money it holds, if depositors lose confidence and rush to withdraw, it simply doesn't have the cash — a "run". The two feed each other: bad-loan news scares depositors, and a run forces fire-sales that create more losses. In 2020, Yes Bank suffered both at once. Understanding that doom-loop is the key to understanding everything that followed.

Why capital is the thing that keeps a bank alive

  • Capital is the shock absorber. A bank funds its loans mostly with other people's money (deposits) and a thin sliver of its own (shareholders' capital). That sliver — measured as the CET1 ratio and total capital adequacy (CRAR) — is what absorbs loan losses before depositors are touched. Regulators mandate a minimum. When Yes Bank's capital fell below the minimum in 2019–20, it legally could not continue without a rescue.
  • Losing capital is fatal; rebuilding it is slow. Yes Bank's core CET1 ratio collapsed to ~6.3% in 2020. The rescue re-capitalised it; by FY26 CET1 was back to a comfortable ~13.8%. That journey — from below-minimum to comfortable — is what "rebuilding a bank" literally means.
  • This is why a strong owner matters. A well-capitalised parent (like SMBC) is a standing promise that the bank will never again be short of capital — which itself makes deposits stickier and funding cheaper, feeding right back into the profit equation.
02

The 2020 collapse: how a fast-growing bank nearly died

The single most important event in Yes Bank's history — and the reason everything after it is a "turnaround".

Yes Bank was founded in 2004 by Rana Kapoor and Ashok Kapur and grew at a blistering pace for over a decade by doing the thing that eventually killed it: lending aggressively to large, risky corporate borrowers — often stressed groups and promoters that safer banks avoided — at attractive yields. For years this looked like brilliance: fast loan growth, a soaring share price, a "new-age" private bank. It was, in truth, a concentrated bet on hope. When India's corporate-credit cycle turned sour (the IL&FS, DHFL and infrastructure blow-ups of 2018–19), a dangerous share of Yes Bank's loan book went bad at once.

The doom-loop, step by step

  • Bad loans surged. Exposure to stressed groups (DHFL, Anil Ambani's Reliance, Essel, IL&FS and others) turned into non-performing assets. To cover them, the bank had to book huge provisions — charges that wiped out profits and ate into capital.
  • Capital ran out. By late 2019 the bank's capital had fallen toward — and then below — regulatory minimums. It repeatedly tried and failed to raise fresh equity from investors. A bank that cannot raise capital while losing it is on a countdown.
  • Depositors panicked. As the news worsened, depositors and institutions began pulling money out. Deposits fell sharply through late 2019 and early 2020 — a slow-motion run that drains the very cash a bank needs to survive.
  • The RBI stepped in. On 5 March 2020, the RBI superseded the board and imposed a moratorium, capping withdrawals at ₹50,000 per depositor. For a few days, ordinary customers could not freely access their own money — the most visible sign a bank has failed.
Why this happened — the lesson in one paragraph

Yes Bank's failure was not a freak accident; it was the predictable end of a business model. It chased growth and yield over safety — lending big to risky borrowers because they paid more and let the loan book grow fast. That works right up until the credit cycle turns, and then the concentrated bets all sour together. A well-run bank spreads risk across millions of small, secured loans so no single default can sink it; the old Yes Bank did the opposite. The whole modern strategy — retail, granular, secured, cautious — is a deliberate, permanent repudiation of what caused 2020. When you read later that Yes Bank is "de-risking the book", this is what it is running away from.

A bank can survive low profits for years. It cannot survive a week without the confidence of its depositors. In March 2020, Yes Bank lost both — and had to be rebuilt from the capital up.
Founder's fate: Rana Kapoor was arrested in March 2020 by the Enforcement Directorate on money-laundering allegations tied to the bank's lending, and the old regime was entirely swept away. The point for an investor today is that the people, culture and lending practices that caused the collapse are gone — the bank has been under new management (and now new ownership) since 2020. It is, in a real sense, a different institution wearing the same name.
03

How the money flows now: retail deposits in, granular loans out

Read this once and the rebuilt bank — and where it still falls short — clicks into place.

A bank is a conveyor belt for money: deposits come in one end, loans go out the other, and the spread between the two is harvested as net interest income. The new Yes Bank has deliberately rebuilt both ends to be safer than the version that failed — more granular retail deposits at the front, more secured retail and SME loans at the back. The problem it is still solving is in the middle: the spread it harvests (its NIM) is thin, dragged down by low-cost-funding gaps and a chunk of money forced into low-yield mandated investments.

A drag sits alongside Stage 3: because Yes Bank has historically missed priority-sector lending targets, it is forced to park ~₹28,000 Cr in low-yield RIDF/mandated deposits — money earning almost nothing that would otherwise fund real loans. Shrinking that pool is a key margin lever (§06).

The two levers that set the spread

A bank's margin is just (what it earns on loans) minus (what it pays for funding). Yes Bank can widen that thin gap two ways:

  • Pay less for funding. Grow cheap money — CASA and granular retail deposits — faster than costly bulk deposits, and shed high-cost borrowings. Yes Bank's CASA crossed ₹1 lakh crore and its CASA ratio hit 35.1% in FY26; that repricing lifted NIM by ~20 bps.
  • Earn more on loans. Shift the mix toward higher-yielding retail and SME segments, and — crucially — free up the money trapped in low-yield RIDF/mandated deposits to redeploy into real loans. Both blades of the scissors are moving, slowly, in the right direction.
Why Yes Bank's margin is so much lower than HDFC's

Three reasons, all fixable in principle. One: its funding is still relatively expensive — years of rebuilding trust meant paying up for deposits, so its cost of funds sits above the best banks. Two: a big slug of its assets is stuck in low-yield mandated investments (RIDF) because it fell short on priority-sector lending — dead-weight that earns a pittance. Three: it is still rebuilding the high-yield, granular retail engine (credit cards, personal loans, small-business loans) that lets a bank earn a fat spread safely. Fix all three — cheaper funding, less RIDF drag, more high-yield retail — and management believes NIM can climb from ~2.6% toward 3.25–3.5% over two to three years. That single move, if it lands, roughly rebuilds the bank's entire profitability.

04

The SMBC transformation — from rescue-consortium to a Japanese megabank

The defining event of the modern era, and the entire re-rating thesis. Read this one closely.

Between 2020 and 2025, Yes Bank was an orphan of its own rescue: owned by State Bank of India and a consortium of banks that had stepped in to save it, none of whom wanted to run it long-term. That changed in 2025. Sumitomo Mitsui Banking Corporation (SMBC) — the core banking arm of Japan's SMBC Group, one of the country's three "megabanks" and one of the largest banks in the world — agreed to buy in. In September 2025 it completed the purchase of a 20% stake, becoming Yes Bank's single largest shareholder, and it is moving to 24.2% by buying a further 4.2% from a Carlyle Group affiliate. This is the event the whole investment case now turns on.

Why a strong global parent is the catalyst

  • Capital & permanence. A megabank shareholder is a standing guarantee that Yes Bank will never again run short of capital. That alone re-rates a bank that once failed for lack of it — it removes the tail risk that hung over the stock.
  • Credibility & cheaper funding. Trust is a bank's cheapest raw material. Being visibly backed by SMBC makes deposits stickier and lets Yes Bank fund itself more cheaply over time — attacking the exact weakness (high funding cost) that keeps its margin thin. Depositors and rating agencies reward a strong parent.
  • Low-cost-funding & risk know-how. SMBC runs one of the world's great low-cost, low-risk banking machines. Its playbook — disciplined underwriting, cheap liabilities, treasury and technology expertise — is exactly what a recovering bank needs to import.
  • Corporate & cross-border relationships. SMBC banks a vast roster of global and Japanese corporates operating in India. Plugging Yes Bank into that network opens up trade finance, corporate lending and cross-border business a mid-sized Indian bank could never win alone.
  • Board representation. SMBC gets two non-executive nominee directors — real influence over strategy and governance, without the legal status (or open-offer trigger) of a controlling promoter.
The nuance most headlines miss — SMBC is an anchor, not a promoter

It is tempting to read "Japanese giant takes over Yes Bank" — but the reality is more precise, and it matters. SMBC has deliberately structured its stake to stay below 25% (24.2%, with RBI approval to go up to 24.99%). Crossing 25% would trigger a mandatory open offer to buy shares from all investors and would classify SMBC as a "promoter" with all the attendant obligations. The RBI has explicitly stated SMBC will not be classified as a promoter. So Yes Bank remains, on paper, a no-promoter, professionally-run bank with SMBC as a powerful strategic anchor investor and largest shareholder — not a parent that consolidates it. In practice SMBC's capital, credibility and two board seats give it enormous influence; but "controlling owner" overstates it. An honest thesis leans on the influence and credibility, not on a full takeover that hasn't happened.

Why this is the section that matters most: for six years Yes Bank was a turnaround with no natural owner — the reason it traded at a discount and struggled to raise its game. The SMBC deal supplies the one thing money can't quickly buy: a credible, deep-pocketed, globally-networked anchor whose mere presence lowers funding costs, removes capital fear, and opens doors. If the turnaround (§05) and the margin recovery (§06) are the work, SMBC is the enabler that could make the work pay off faster. The bull case is essentially: "a decent recovering bank + a great new backer = re-rating." Watch for SMBC-driven changes — funding cost, corporate wins, capital-raising — as the tangible proof the anchor is working.
05

The turnaround: how you actually rebuild a broken bank

The unglamorous six-year grind that took Yes Bank from moratorium to profitable — explained honestly.

Saving Yes Bank in March 2020 was the easy part; rebuilding it took the six years since. A bank in Yes Bank's state has to do three hard things at once, each of which takes years: refill its capital, get the bad loans off its books, and win back the depositors who fled. Doing any one of these is difficult; doing all three, in the middle of COVID, is the real achievement — and understanding how it was done tells you how any broken bank recovers.

The three jobs of a bank recovery

  • 1 · Refill the capital. The 2020 reconstruction injected fresh equity: SBI led with an initial ~48% stake, and a consortium (ICICI, HDFC, Axis, Kotak, Federal, Bandhan, IDFC First and others) put in the rest, with a lock-in so they couldn't dump the shares. A ₹15,000 Cr follow-on capital raise in mid-2020 rebuilt the buffer further. CET1 climbed from ~6.3% (2020) to ~13.8% (FY26). Capital first — nothing else is possible without it.
  • 2 · Amputate the bad loans. You cannot heal a bank while a mountain of dud loans festers on its books. In 2022, Yes Bank sold a ~₹48,000 Cr pool of stressed loans to JC Flowers ARC (an asset reconstruction company) through a "Swiss challenge" auction. This is the crucial move: it took the toxic legacy off the balance sheet in one transaction, letting management run a clean bank going forward while recoveries on the old pool trickle back over time (~₹1,559 Cr of security-receipt recoveries in FY26, expected to taper). Reported GNPA/NNPA are now a clean 1.3% / 0.2%.
  • 3 · Win back deposits. A bank that suffered a run has to painstakingly re-earn trust — often by paying up for deposits at first, then gradually shifting to cheaper, stickier retail money as confidence returns. Yes Bank rebuilt deposits to ₹3.19 lakh crore (up 12% in FY26) and pushed its CASA ratio to 35.1% — a genuine franchise rebuild (§07).

06

The honest weakness: low NIM, low RoA — and the road up

Where Yes Bank still falls short of a quality bank, and exactly how it plans to close the gap.

Here is the uncomfortable truth a fair primer must state plainly: Yes Bank, even fully recovered, is not yet a good bank by the numbers that matter most. Its NIM of ~2.6% and RoA of ~0.8% are well below the ~3.4–4.4% margins and ~1.5–2.2% RoAs of HDFC and ICICI. Being safe is not the same as being profitable — and Yes Bank has achieved the former without yet achieving the latter. The entire forward-looking case rests on a single question: can it lift these numbers toward peer levels? Management has a clear, credible plan; execution is unproven.

The four levers — and why each one moves the needle

  • Cut the cost of deposits. Reprice down as trust returns, grow CASA and granular retail deposits faster than costly bulk money. FY26 already saw deposit repricing lift NIM ~20 bps — the single biggest lever.
  • Drain the RIDF drag. Because Yes Bank has historically missed priority-sector lending (PSL) targets — the RBI's rule that banks lend a set share to agriculture, small business and weaker sections — it is penalised by having to park money in low-yield RIDF/mandated deposits (~₹27,900 Cr, ~6% of assets in FY26). As it lends more to genuine priority sectors and PSL compliance improves, that trapped money (earning almost nothing) can be redeployed into real loans. Management expects RIDF to fall ₹6,500–9,000 Cr in FY27 — pure margin uplift.
  • Tilt to higher-yield retail & SME. Grow credit cards, personal loans, small-business and SME lending — segments that earn a fatter spread than large corporate loans — carefully, so as not to re-import risk.
  • Operating leverage & fees. Yes Bank's cost-to-income ratio is high (~66.7% FY26, improving from ~71%). As income grows faster than costs (in FY26 income rose 11.7% while opex rose just 4.6% — positive "jaws"), and fee income builds, more of each rupee drops to profit.
Why a NIM move from 2.6% to ~3.25% is transformational

Net interest margin is the master dial for a bank. On Yes Bank's roughly ₹3-lakh-crore-plus of interest-earning assets, lifting NIM by even 0.5–0.9 percentage points adds thousands of crores of near-pure profit — because the cost base barely moves. Management's stated ambition is to reach a 3.25–3.5% NIM over two to three years, chiefly by draining the RIDF drag, cutting funding costs, and mixing in higher-yield retail. If it gets there, RoA naturally climbs toward the ~1.5% that marks a genuinely good bank — and a bank re-rates on durable, low-risk profitability, not on a recovery story. That arithmetic — a thin-margin survivor becoming a normal-margin bank — is the whole reason the stock is interesting. The risk is that it's slow and never quite arrives.

07

The deposit-franchise rebuild and the retail mix-shift

The quiet, foundational work — replacing hot corporate money with sticky household money.

The pre-2020 Yes Bank funded itself heavily with bulk and wholesale deposits — big, rate-sensitive money that fled the moment trust cracked, turning a crisis into a run. The single most important structural repair since then has been to replace that hot money with granular retail deposits: millions of small household savings and current accounts that are stickier, cheaper, and far less likely to stampede. This is slow, expensive, branch-by-branch work — and it is the bedrock everything else stands on.

Retail deposits are ~58% of the base and CASA crossed ₹1 lakh crore (35.1% ratio, the first time above 35% since the reconstruction). A stickier, cheaper base than the wholesale-heavy funding that failed in 2020.

Why the deposit rebuild is the whole foundation

  • Stickiness = survival. Granular retail deposits don't run the way bulk corporate money does. Rebuilding a retail base is, quite literally, rebuilding the bank's resilience — the thing whose absence caused the 2020 run.
  • Cheaper funding = wider margin. CASA and retail money is cheaper than bulk term deposits. Every point of CASA-ratio gain feeds directly into the NIM-improvement story (§06). CASA crossing ₹1 lakh crore and the ratio topping 35% is real, measurable progress.
  • It de-risks the credit-to-deposit ratio. Yes Bank's loan-to-deposit ratio has come down to ~85.7% (from 88%) — meaning it is funding more of its lending with stable deposits rather than stretching. A lower, deposit-funded ratio is a safer, more sustainable bank.
  • The retail-loan mirror. Alongside retail deposits, the loan book has been retail-ised too — retail & SME are ~58% of advances (up from ~49% in FY22), a deliberate move away from the concentrated corporate lending that blew up (§08).
Why "boring granular deposits" is the hardest thing in banking right now

Across India, savers are moving money out of bank deposits and into mutual funds and equities — so every bank is fighting over a shrinking pool of deposits. That makes gathering cheap, sticky retail money the single hardest and most valuable job in the industry. For a bank that suffered a run only six years ago, doing this from a standing start is genuinely impressive — and it's where an SMBC-backed brand (§04) helps most: a globally-credible parent makes depositors more comfortable, which over time lets Yes Bank gather deposits a little more cheaply. The deposit franchise isn't glamorous, but it is the quiet floor under the entire recovery.

08

The loan book today: granular by design

What Yes Bank actually lends against now — deliberately unlike the book that failed.

The new Yes Bank's loan book is a conscious inversion of the old one. Where the pre-2020 bank was dominated by chunky, risky corporate exposures, today's ~₹2.72 lakh crore book is far more granular and secured, tilted toward retail and SME borrowers. The stated aim is to hold retail & SME at roughly 60% of the book — a structural safety choice, even though it means growing more slowly than the old "lend big, grow fast" model.

A ~58 : 16 : 26 retail-SME / mid-corp / corporate split — up from a wholesale-heavy book pre-2020. Retail & SME rose from ~49% (FY22) to ~58–59%. Granular and secured by design.

What this mix tells you

  • Retail & SME (~58%). Home loans, gold loans, personal loans, credit cards, vehicle finance, small-business and SME lending — many small, secured exposures. No single default can dent the bank; this is the granularity that makes a bank safe.
  • Corporate (~26%) — but chosen carefully. Large-corporate lending is back, but selective and better-underwritten — and this is exactly where SMBC's global corporate relationships (§04) can help Yes Bank win good-quality, well-priced business rather than the desperate high-yield deals of the old era.
  • Mid-corporate (~16%). A middle layer of mid-sized-company lending, rounding out a diversified book.
  • The trade-off is growth vs safety. A granular retail book grows more slowly and, on plain vanilla products, earns thinner spreads than aggressive corporate lending — but it is durable. Management guides to 13–15% advances growth, in line with the industry: steady, not spectacular. That's the deliberate post-crisis discipline.
The tension to watch: the higher-yield retail products that lift NIM (personal loans, cards, microfinance-adjacent lending) are also the ones most prone to losses in a downturn — Q4 FY26 retail slippages ran ~2.8%. Yes Bank's whole credibility now rests on growing these without repeating the concentration mistakes of the past. Watch slippages and credit cost each quarter: rising alongside stable-to-falling NPAs = healthy growth; rising with deteriorating NPAs = the old risk creeping back.
09

The industry and where Yes Bank sits

A mid-sized private bank in a fiercely competitive, deposit-scarce market.

Indian banking splits into public-sector banks (SBI and the government-owned lenders, ~half the system), large private banks (HDFC, ICICI, Axis, Kotak — the quality leaders with the best margins and returns), and a tail of mid-sized and small private banks — where Yes Bank now sits, alongside IndusInd, IDBI, IDFC First, RBL and others. It is no longer the swaggering "new-age" challenger of the 2010s; it is a recovering mid-cap bank trying to earn its way back into the quality tier.

The forces shaping the whole sector

  • The deposit war. With savers shifting to mutual funds and equities, deposits are scarce and every bank is competing for them. This is the industry's binding constraint — and it bears hardest on banks (like Yes) still rebuilding a cheap deposit base.
  • Rate cycle. When the RBI cuts rates, loan yields typically reprice down faster than deposit costs, squeezing margins in the short run — a headwind Yes Bank is navigating even as it tries to lift NIM structurally.
  • Regulation on unsecured & retail credit. The RBI has tightened rules on unsecured lending and risk weights — which touches exactly the higher-yield retail segments Yes Bank is trying to grow.
  • Consolidation & foreign capital. The SMBC deal is part of a broader theme of global banks and capital taking large stakes in Indian lenders — a vote of confidence in India's banking growth that specifically benefits Yes Bank.

Financials & valuation

The numbers, and the price-to-book lens that governs a turnaround bank.

FY26 was a genuine step forward: net profit of ₹3,476 Cr, up 44.5%, on rebuilt deposits, clean asset quality and improving efficiency. But the level of profitability remains modest — a ~0.8% RoA and ~7% RoE are recovering, not good. For a bank like this, the market's key valuation lens is price-to-book (P/B): what you pay for each rupee of the bank's net worth, judged against the return it earns on that net worth.

Why P/B is the right lens — and why ~1.5× is a debate, not a verdict

For a bank, price-to-book is the master valuation gauge: a bank's "book value" is its net worth, and the fair multiple on it is driven by the return it earns on that book (RoE). A high-RoE bank like HDFC (RoE mid-teens+) commands ~2.5–3× book; a bank earning a ~7% RoE, by the same logic, "deserves" a lower multiple. So Yes Bank at ~1.5× book with a P/E of ~22× is not obviously cheap on today's low returns — the high P/E simply reflects thin earnings. The bull case is forward-looking: if the SMBC anchor plus the margin levers lift RoE from ~7% toward low-double-digits, the fair multiple rises and the earnings grow at the same time — a double re-rating. The bear case: on current returns, the stock already prices in a lot of that hope. This is the crux of the valuation debate — you are paying a full multiple for a recovery that must still be delivered.

One-offs to flag: part of recent profitability is helped by recoveries on the old JC-Flowers stressed pool (~₹1,559 Cr of security-receipt recoveries in FY26, guided to taper to ~₹800–1,000 Cr in FY27) — non-recurring income that flatters the run-rate. Strip it out and the core operating profitability is even more modest, which is why the durable-NIM-improvement story (§06) matters so much: it has to replace fading recovery income with real, repeatable earnings. Yes Bank also pays no dividend — capital is being retained to fund the rebuild.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

Where this goes — and the honest case on both sides.

Yes Bank in mid-2026 is a genuine paradox: a bank that is safe but not yet profitable, rescued but not yet re-rated, and backed by a global giant but still earning like a struggler. The next few years decide which word wins. The bull owns the direction — a credible new anchor, clean book, rebuilt deposits, clear margin levers. The bear owns the level — the returns are still low, the improvement is slow, and the price already assumes success.

The bull case

  • A powerful new anchor. SMBC — a Japanese megabank, now largest shareholder at 24.2% with two board seats — brings capital, credibility, cheap-funding know-how and global corporate relationships. The one thing a turnaround can't manufacture.
  • Clear margin levers. Draining the ~₹28k Cr RIDF drag, cheaper funding, and a richer retail mix can lift NIM from ~2.6% toward 3.25–3.5% — which mechanically rebuilds RoA and RoE.
  • Clean, de-risked balance sheet. The toxic legacy is gone (JC Flowers), NPAs are low (0.2% net), capital is comfortable (~13.8% CET1). The downside protection is real.
  • Rebuilt deposit franchise. CASA past ₹1 lakh crore (35.1%), retail ~58% of deposits — a stickier, cheaper base than the one that failed.
  • Turnaround optionality. A recovering bank with a great new owner, at ~1.5× book, offers a genuine re-rating path if returns normalise — a rare combination in Indian banking.

The bear case

  • Returns are still poor. ~0.8% RoA and ~7% RoE are well below any quality bank; even the improvement plan only targets peer margins over 2–3 years — and execution is unproven.
  • Not cheap for the returns. ~1.5× book and ~22× earnings already price in a lot of hoped-for improvement; on today's numbers it is arguably expensive, not a bargain.
  • SMBC is an anchor, not a saviour. Capped below 25% and explicitly not a promoter — the influence is real but a full parent-backed transformation isn't guaranteed; benefits accrue slowly.
  • Reach-for-yield risk. Lifting NIM means growing higher-yield retail (cards, personal loans) — the exact segments that turn ugly in a downturn (retail slippages ~2.8%). The whole thesis depends on not re-importing risk.
  • Fading one-offs + deposit war + rate cuts. Recovery income is tapering, deposits are scarce and costly to gather, and rate cuts squeeze yields — three headwinds against a slow, thin-margin grind.
The bottom line

Yes Bank is the purest turnaround-plus-new-owner stock in Indian banking. The near-death and the rescue are history; the balance sheet is clean and the deposits are rebuilt. What's left is the hardest part — turning a safe bank into a profitable one — and the arrival of Japan's SMBC as anchor shareholder is the catalyst that could make it happen faster than a lone turnaround ever could. But be clear-eyed: today this is a low-NIM, low-RoA bank whose stock already carries a full multiple for a recovery it must still deliver. Watch three things every quarter: NIM and RoA (are they climbing toward peer levels?), credit cost and retail slippages (is the clean book holding as it reaches for yield?), and the SMBC effect (is funding cost falling and are corporate/cross-border wins appearing?). Get those right and a rescued survivor re-rates into a quality bank; stall on them and it stays a safe, sub-par name that promised more than it earned.
