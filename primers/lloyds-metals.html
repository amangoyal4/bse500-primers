Lloyds Metals and Energy, the iron-ore mine that is becoming a steel company

A complete walk through one of India's most extraordinary multibaggers — how owning a giant merchant iron-ore mine in Gadchiroli became a licence to print money, why a merchant mining lease is one of the rarest assets in Indian industry, and how the company is now pouring that cash into becoming a fully-integrated, mine-to-steel producer.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Lloyds Metals and Energy owns and operates the Surjagarh iron-ore mine in Gadchiroli district, Maharashtra — one of the largest merchant (open-market) iron-ore mines in India. For most of its life the company was a modest, loss-making sponge-iron maker. Then, from around 2021, it began ramping up its own captive mine, and the economics changed completely: it mines cheap, high-grade iron ore and sells it, and that single asset turned it into one of the great Indian multibaggers of the decade.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A mine is the whole story. A large, low-cost, own-account iron-ore mine is a regulatory scarcity — mining leases like this are almost impossible to obtain in India, and selling ore you dug for a few hundred rupees a tonne at market prices of several thousand is extraordinarily profitable. Consolidated revenue exploded from ~₹692 Cr (FY22) to ~₹17,113 Cr (FY26), EBITDA margin sits near 34%, and FY26 profit (PAT) was ~₹3,829 Cr — with iron-ore output up 120% to 21.96 million tonnes.
  • It is transforming from a miner into an integrated steel-maker. Rather than just selling raw ore forever, Lloyds is spending heavily to move up the value chain — a beneficiation plant, a long-distance slurry pipeline, giant pellet plants, sponge-iron (DRI), and eventually an integrated steel plant at Konsari, Gadchiroli. This "mine-to-metal" integration is the defining strategy of the next five years.
  • It is a commodity stock, priced like a growth stock. Earnings ride the iron-ore and steel cycle; the shares have been volatile after a huge multi-year run. At a ~₹1.02 lakh-crore market cap and ~27× earnings, the market is paying for the mine expansion and the integration to both deliver — while a copper-cobalt acquisition in the Democratic Republic of Congo has added a controversial new wrinkle.
In plain terms

Think of Lloyds Metals as a company that got hold of a golden goose — a huge iron-ore mine — that almost nobody else in India can legally own. Digging iron ore out of the ground costs it very little; selling that ore earns a fortune, because iron ore is the basic feedstock every steel mill in the world needs. For years the goose mostly laid eggs the company simply sold. Now it is using those enormous egg-profits to build its own kitchen — pipelines, pellet plants, and eventually a steel plant — so that instead of selling raw ore, it can sell steel, which is worth far more per tonne. The prize if it works is a low-cost, fully-integrated steel company; the risk is that it is spending tens of thousands of crores on a bet whose payoff depends on volatile commodity prices and flawless execution in one of India's most remote and difficult districts.

01

Iron-ore economics 101: why a mine is a money machine

No commodities background required — just the handful of ideas that drive everything.

To understand Lloyds you first have to understand why owning an iron-ore mine is so unusually profitable. Iron ore is the raw rock from which almost all steel is made. A mine is, financially, the extreme opposite of a factory: nearly all the value is in the right to extract a resource that sits in the ground for free, and the cost of getting it out is small relative to what it sells for.

The only concepts you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for Lloyds)
Iron oreThe rock (mostly iron oxide) that steel is made from. Higher "grade" (iron content, e.g. 62% Fe) sells for more. Lloyds' Surjagarh ore is high-grade hematite — premium feedstock.
Merchant vs captive mineA captive mine feeds the owner's own steel plant; a merchant mine sells ore on the open market. Lloyds' mine is unusual — it can do both, and the merchant model means it earns the full market price on ore.
Mining cost vs realisationThe cash cost to extract a tonne is a few hundred rupees; the market realisation is several thousand. That gap — the "resource rent" — is why a mine's margins dwarf a manufacturer's. Lloyds' EBITDA margin is ~34%; a typical steel mill's is far lower.
MTPA"Million tonnes per annum" — the mine's permitted/production capacity. More permitted tonnes = more ore you're legally allowed to sell = more profit. Lloyds has just won clearance to expand to 55 MTPA.
Value additionEach step up the chain — ore → pellet → sponge iron → steel — adds price per tonne. A tonne of steel sells for many times a tonne of raw ore. This is the logic behind Lloyds' whole integration plan.
Why the mining lease is the moat

In India, you cannot simply decide to open a large iron-ore mine. Mining leases are granted by the state, tied to specific parcels of land, valid for decades, and — since a 2015 law change — mostly auctioned at fierce competition or held by legacy owners. New large merchant leases almost never come to market. So the lease itself is the scarce, protected asset: it is a multi-decade, government-granted right to extract and sell a commodity the whole steel industry needs, from a specific patch of ground that no competitor can replicate. Lloyds holds a lease over Surjagarh valid to 2057. That is the entire foundation of the equity story — not the factories, not the brand, but the piece of paper (and the ore beneath it) that lets it sell iron ore no one else can.

The two ways a mine grows

  • Dig more (volume) — lift permitted capacity and actually mine more tonnes. Lloyds went from ~3 MTPA (FY22) to 10 MTPA (FY24) to ~26 MTPA of direct-sale ore, on the road to 55 MTPA. Each extra tonne, at a fixed low cost, is almost pure profit.
  • Sell it for more (value) — instead of shipping raw ore, beneficiate and pelletise it, then convert to sponge iron and steel — capturing the higher price at each stage rather than handing that margin to someone else downstream.
02

Surjagarh: the crown-jewel mine

The single asset that explains the entire company — and the whole share price.

Everything about Lloyds Metals radiates from one location: the Surjagarh iron-ore mine in Gadchiroli, Maharashtra. It is among the largest merchant iron-ore mining operations in India, sits on very large reserves, and holds a lease running to 2057. Understanding Surjagarh is understanding the stock.

What makes it the crown jewel

  • Scale. The permitted capacity has been expanded step by step — 3 → 10 → 26 MTPA — and in 2025 the company secured environmental clearance to take Surjagarh all the way to 55 MTPA, which would make it the single largest iron-ore mine in the country. FY26 actual output was 21.96 million tonnes, up 120% year-on-year — the mine is ramping hard into that permit.
  • Grade & cost. Surjagarh yields high-grade hematite ("direct-shipping" quality) that needs little processing to sell, at a low extraction cost. High grade + low cost = the fat resource-rent margin that powers the whole P&L.
  • The reserve runway. Beyond the 156 MnT of high-grade hematite lies ~701 MnT of BHQ (Banded Hematite Quartzite) — lower-grade ore that becomes economic once you build a beneficiation plant to concentrate it. That is why the expansion plan pairs 26 MTPA of direct-sale hematite with up to 45 MnT of beneficiated BHQ: it unlocks a vastly larger, longer-life resource.
  • Regulatory scarcity. As Section 01 explained, a lease like this simply cannot be bought off the shelf. Lloyds was awarded Surjagarh back in 2007; the value of that award has compounded as ore prices rose and the permit expanded. No competitor can conjure an equivalent asset.
Why "merchant" is the magic word

Most captive iron-ore mines in India belong to steel giants (Tata, JSW, SAIL) that consume the ore themselves — the mine is a cost centre feeding the mill. Lloyds' mine is different: it has historically sold much of its ore on the open market to other steel and pellet makers. That means Lloyds captures the full market price of iron ore directly, at mine-gate cost — the purest, highest-margin way to monetise the resource. As the company builds its own downstream plants it will increasingly consume the ore internally, but the merchant optionality — sell the ore, or convert it, whichever is more profitable — is a rare and valuable flexibility.

The location nuance: Surjagarh sits in Gadchiroli, historically one of India's most Naxal-affected (Maoist-insurgency) districts. That is both a risk (Section 07) and, oddly, part of the moat — the security, logistics and community challenges of operating there are exactly why few others could develop this deposit, and why the asset stayed available long enough for Lloyds and its mining partner to build it out.
03

The value chain and the business model

From rock in the ground to (eventually) rolled steel — and where Lloyds earns at each step.

Steel-making is a chain of value-adding steps, and each step commands a higher price per tonne than the one before. Lloyds started at the very top of the chain (raw ore) and is now deliberately building down it, so it captures more of that value in-house instead of selling a low-value product and letting others reap the rest.

The further right you go, the more processed the product and the higher the price per tonne — but the tougher the competition and the thinner the margin. Lloyds' edge is that it starts with the cheapest possible raw material: its own ore.

What the company actually does today

  • Iron-ore mining & sales — the dominant profit driver: mining Surjagarh hematite and selling most of it as direct-shipping ore, plus feeding its own plants.
  • Pellets — Lloyds has commissioned large pellet plants at Konsari (a 4 MTPA plant hit full utilisation within months; a second 4 MTPA line was commissioned in May 2026 — together ~8 MTPA). Pellets are uniform, high-grade balls of iron ore that command a premium over raw fines and are the feed for DRI and blast furnaces.
  • Sponge iron / DRI — the company's original business, at Ghugus (Chandrapur) and a newer DRI unit at Konsari. DRI ("directly reduced iron") is solid metallic iron made without a blast furnace — a stepping stone toward steel.
  • Steel (in build) — the planned integrated steel plant at Konsari is the destination: converting its own ore, pellets and sponge iron into finished steel, the highest-value product in the chain.
  • Captive power — waste-heat and captive generation to run the plants cheaply, an important cost lever in an energy-hungry industry.
Why integrate at all — the value-addition logic

Selling raw iron ore is fabulously profitable, so why bother building steel plants? Two reasons. First, price: a tonne of finished steel sells for several times a tonne of raw ore, so converting your own ore captures that entire uplift instead of handing it to a downstream buyer. Second, stability: raw-ore prices are volatile and ore sales can be capped by policy or logistics; owning the full chain lets Lloyds turn a swing commodity into higher-value, somewhat steadier products, and guarantees a home for its expanding ore volumes. The catch is that each downstream step is more competitive and lower-margin than the mine, and costs a fortune to build — which is the entire capex story in Section 05.

04

The forward-integration transformation

The defining strategy: turning a merchant miner into an integrated steel producer.

If the mine is the crown jewel, the forward-integration programme is the plot of the story. Lloyds is deploying the mine's enormous cash flows to build, from scratch, a full mine-to-steel complex in and around Gadchiroli. This is the single biggest thing the company is doing — and the single biggest thing an analyst leads with after the mine itself.

The three physical pillars of integration

  • Beneficiation — unlocking the low-grade ore. A beneficiation plant upgrades the vast BHQ resource (~701 MnT) into usable high-grade concentrate. Without it, most of Surjagarh's reserve is uneconomic; with it, the mine's life and volume potential multiply — which is why beneficiation is the key that turns the 55-MTPA permit into a decades-long resource.
  • The slurry pipeline — moving ore cheaply. Trucking millions of tonnes of ore along remote Gadchiroli roads is slow, costly and environmentally heavy. Lloyds is building a ~195 km slurry pipeline that pumps beneficiated ore, mixed with water, straight from the mine to the plants — targeted to cut logistics cost by over ₹500 a tonne. At 20-plus million tonnes, that is a structural cost advantage worth hundreds of crores a year, and it removes a physical bottleneck on volume growth.
  • Pellets → DRI → steel — climbing the chain. The ~8 MTPA of pellet capacity, the DRI units, and the planned integrated steel plant at Konsari (management guiding to first production around FY28, ramping toward ~1 million tonnes) complete the march from raw ore to finished steel.

    • Why this is the whole bet

    The transformation is elegant in theory: use the world's cheapest raw material (your own captive, low-cost ore) to feed downstream plants, and you become a structurally low-cost integrated steel producer — the kind that stays profitable even when steel prices fall, because your input cost is a fraction of a rival who has to buy ore. That is genuinely how the best integrated steel companies win. But the theory only pays off if the plants are built on time and on budget, the slurry pipeline works, the ore keeps flowing, and steel prices hold up long enough to earn a return on tens of thousands of crores of capex. Integration turns a simple, spectacularly profitable mining business into a complex, capital-intensive industrial project — trading certainty today for a bigger, but riskier, prize tomorrow.

    The funding tension in plain terms

    Here is the balancing act. The Surjagarh mine throws off enormous operating cash — several thousand crore a year — which pays for a lot of the build. But the total programme is larger than even that torrent of cash, so Lloyds has taken on meaningful debt (borrowings had risen to roughly ₹20,000 Cr by FY26). Management's stated discipline is to keep net debt around 1.0–1.5× EBITDA and to fund the rest from internal accruals, with no near-term plan to issue new shares. Because EBITDA is itself rising fast as ore volumes grow, that ratio can stay in the comfort zone if the mine keeps ramping and ore prices hold. The risk is the mirror image: if ore prices fall or a project slips, EBITDA disappoints, the debt looks larger against it, and the capex has to be funded on worse terms. For a company this heavily mid-build, the balance sheet — not just the P&L — is the thing to watch each quarter.

    06

    The commodity cycle: what really moves earnings

    A mining and steel company lives and dies by two prices it does not control.

    However clever the strategy, Lloyds is at heart a commodity company. Its earnings are geared to two prices set by global and Indian markets — the iron-ore price and the steel price — and neither is under its control. Understanding this cyclicality is essential to reading the volatile share price.

    The two levers of the cycle

    • Iron-ore price — set largely by global seaborne markets (dominated by Chinese demand) and by Indian domestic supply/policy. When ore prices are high, Lloyds' merchant sales are spectacularly profitable; when they fall, the fat resource-rent margin compresses. Today, most of Lloyds' profit is still ore-driven.
    • Steel price & spreads — as integration proceeds, the spread between steel prices and input costs (the "steel spread") matters more. Cheap captive ore widens that spread versus rivals, but steel prices themselves swing with the construction/auto cycle, imports (Chinese steel dumping is a perennial threat), and government tariffs/safeguard duties.
    The low-cost-position cushion

    Here is why the integration strategy is partly a defence against the cycle. In any commodity, the winner is the lowest-cost producer — the one who still makes money when prices are on the floor and everyone else is losing. Because Lloyds mines its own ore at a fraction of the market price, its cost of making steel could sit near the very bottom of the industry cost curve. So even in a downturn, when high-cost steel mills bleed, a fully-integrated Lloyds should stay profitable — and can even gain share as weaker rivals cut back. That low-cost position is the real prize of owning the mine: it doesn't remove the cycle, but it lets the company survive and thrive through it. The flip side: today, before integration is complete, the company is still highly exposed to raw iron-ore prices, so a sharp fall in ore would hit earnings hard and fast.

    How to read a bad quarter: because a mining company's costs are largely fixed and its selling price floats, even a modest fall in the iron-ore price can swing profits sharply. Don't confuse a price-driven earnings dip with a broken business — check whether volumes (tonnes mined, pellets made) kept rising. Volume growth with a soft price is a cyclical wobble; volume stalling is a structural worry.
    07

    Owners: Lloyds, Thriveni & the Gadchiroli context

    Who controls the company, the crucial mining partnership, and the security backdrop.

    Lloyds Metals is controlled by a promoter group centred on the Lloyds/Agarwal family, together with Thriveni Earthmovers — the mining contractor that became a co-promoter and is central to how the mine actually gets dug. Understanding this partnership, and the unusual place where the mine sits, is key to the risk picture.

    The ownership structure

    • Promoter group ~62%. Combined promoter holding was around 61.65% (Mar 2026) — a firmly promoter-controlled company. This includes the founding Lloyds/Agarwal interests and Thriveni.
    • Thriveni Earthmovers — the mine developer/operator (MDO). Thriveni is one of India's largest mining contractors. It joined Lloyds as a co-promoter in 2021 and holds roughly ~18%. Crucially, Thriveni is also the operator that physically develops and runs the Surjagarh mine — so the partner who does the digging is also an owner, aligning incentives around ramping production. Thriveni's operational muscle is a big reason Lloyds could scale Surjagarh so fast.
    • The related-party dimension. Because Thriveni is both a large shareholder and the paid mining contractor (and involved in the DRC venture), Lloyds carries significant related-party transactions — mining-contract payments, the DRC deal — which investors watch closely for fairness and governance. This is a recurring point of scrutiny.
    The Gadchiroli / security nuance: Surjagarh lies in Gadchiroli, long one of India's most Naxal (Maoist-insurgency) affected districts. The mine has seen violence in the past — including a 2016 arson attack that destroyed dozens of trucks and earth-movers, and earlier attacks on personnel. This is a genuine operating risk. But two things have shifted: Maoist activity in the district has declined sharply under sustained security operations, and Lloyds has become the anchor of a deliberate development-through-industry story — creating thousands of jobs, running community programmes, and even employing surrendered former Naxalites. The company frames Gadchiroli's transformation "from Naxal stronghold to steel hub" as part of its ESG narrative; critics and some local villagers, meanwhile, have raised land, environmental and displacement concerns. It is a nuanced picture: real security and social risk, partly offset by a real (and strategically important) development role.
    Why the location cuts both ways

    The remoteness and security burden of Gadchiroli are exactly why this world-class deposit stayed underdeveloped for so long — few companies had the appetite or the operating capability to work there. That difficulty is part of the moat: it kept competitors out and let Lloyds and Thriveni build a dominant position. But the same factors are ongoing risks: any resurgence of insurgency, a serious security incident, community/land disputes, or environmental litigation could disrupt mining or delay the expansion — and because the entire company hangs on this one mine, disruption there is disruption everywhere.

    08

    The DRC copper bet

    A recent, controversial diversification a long way from Gadchiroli.

    In early 2026 Lloyds made a striking move well outside its comfort zone: acquiring the Chemaf copper-cobalt operations in the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC). It is a small headline price with potentially large consequences — and a genuine debate about whether it fits the story.

    • What was bought. Through a joint venture (Lloyds ~49% alongside a partner), Lloyds acquired the Chemaf Group's copper and cobalt assets in the DRC for a modest headline sum (~US$30 million of equity). The strategic pitch: enter the battery-metals supply chain (copper and cobalt are critical for electrification), and use Thriveni's large-scale mining capability to restart and expand distressed but valuable assets — targeting ~100,000 TPA copper and ~20,000 TPA cobalt over time.
    • Why it's controversial. Three things worry sceptics. (1) Capability gap — copper-cobalt hydrometallurgy is a completely different technical discipline from Indian iron-ore mining. (2) Hidden debt — the deal reportedly comes with roughly ~US$1 billion of legacy Chemaf debt and obligations (including wage arrears to thousands of workers), turning a small purchase price into a large commitment. (3) Country & governance risk — the DRC is a difficult, politically sensitive jurisdiction, and the transaction is entangled with related-party (Thriveni) structures that drew shareholder scrutiny.
    Why it matters to the thesis: the core Lloyds story is beautifully simple — a great mine, integrating into steel. The DRC copper venture complicates that story: it adds a new commodity, a new continent, a new technical challenge and a potential billion-dollar liability, at exactly the time the company is mid-way through its own heavy domestic capex. Bulls see cheap optionality on battery metals via a partner with mining muscle; bears see distraction, balance-sheet risk and governance questions. Either way, it is now a variable to track that has nothing to do with iron ore.

    How to think about the valuation

    At ~₹1.02 lakh crore market cap on ~₹3,829 Cr of FY26 profit, Lloyds trades around 27× trailing earnings — and several times book value. For a commodity business, that is a premium multiple: cyclical miners and steelmakers typically trade at low double-digit or even single-digit P/Es, because their earnings can halve in a bad year. The market is clearly not pricing Lloyds as an ordinary steel stock. It is paying up for two things: the sheer scarcity and growth of the mine (55-MTPA permit, decades-long reserve, ramping volumes) and the optionality of the integration (a low-cost steel company in the making). Bulls argue a unique, hard-to-replicate mine with a multi-decade runway deserves to trade like a growth asset, not a commodity. Bears argue that no commodity stock escapes the cycle forever, that the multiple leaves no room for an ore-price fall or a capex overrun, that the debt is climbing, and that the DRC venture and related-party structure add governance risk — a combination that can de-rate a re-rated commodity stock brutally when sentiment turns. Both are right at different points in the cycle, which is exactly why the shares have been so volatile.

    10

    The growth runway

    Three stacked engines: more ore, more value-add, and (maybe) copper.

    Lloyds' growth levers are unusually clear and unusually large. The question is execution, not opportunity.

    Growth engines, stacked

    • Volume — ramp the mine to 55 MTPA. FY26 output was 21.96 MnT against a 55-MTPA permit; simply mining more of what it is already allowed to extract, at low incremental cost, is the biggest and most certain lever. Each extra tonne is highly profitable.
    • Value-add — climb the chain. The ~8 MTPA of pellets, expanded DRI, and the Konsari steel plant (targeted ~FY28) progressively convert low-price ore into higher-price products, lifting revenue per tonne and cushioning the cycle.
    • Cost — the slurry pipeline & captive power. The ~195 km pipeline (>₹500/tonne saving) and captive generation drive down unit costs — management has flagged targeted savings running into thousands of crores a year by FY28.
    • Optionality — DRC copper/cobalt. A high-risk, high-variance call option on battery metals, separate from the core.
    The next chapter is less about proving the mine — that's done — and more about whether Lloyds can turn cheap ore into cheap steel without tripping over the capex, the cycle, or the debt.
    11

    The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

    A genuinely rare asset — attached to a big, capital-intensive, cyclical transformation.

    Lloyds Metals is one of the most interesting equity stories in Indian materials: a scarce, low-cost, long-life iron-ore mine funding an ambitious march up the value chain into steel. The upside is a low-cost integrated producer with a decades-long resource; the risk is that a simple, spectacular mining business is being turned into a complex industrial bet at the top of a cycle.

    The bull case

    • A rare, scarce asset — a large low-cost merchant iron-ore mine, lease to 2057, 55-MTPA permit, ~857 MnT reserves. Near-impossible to replicate.
    • Explosive, real earnings — revenue ~₹17,113 Cr and PAT ~₹3,829 Cr in FY26, ~34% margins, high ROCE/ROE — this is cash, not promise.
    • Integration = low-cost steel — own ore + pellets + DRI + steel could put Lloyds near the bottom of the industry cost curve, profitable through the cycle.
    • Long volume runway — FY26 output (22 MnT) is well below the 55-MTPA permit; ramping alone drives years of growth.
    • Aligned mining partner — Thriveni's operating muscle and skin in the game accelerated the ramp.

    The bear case

    • Commodity cyclicality — a fall in iron-ore/steel prices can slash earnings fast; the ~27× multiple prices in little downside.
    • Capex & execution risk — a huge, multi-year build (steel plant, pipeline, beneficiation) with real overrun/delay risk.
    • Rising debt — borrowings climbing toward ₹20,000 Cr; the net-debt/EBITDA cushion depends on volumes and prices holding.
    • Single-asset, difficult location — everything hinges on one mine in Naxal-affected Gadchiroli; any disruption there hits the whole company.
    • Governance & the DRC bet — large related-party (Thriveni) transactions and a ~$1bn-debt copper venture in the DRC add risk far from the core story.
    The bottom line

    Lloyds Metals owns something genuinely rare in Indian industry — a big, cheap, long-life iron-ore mine that the market simply cannot manufacture more of. That asset alone has already created one of the decade's great multibaggers. The next act is harder and less certain: spending the mine's cash to build a fully-integrated steel complex in a remote district, while managing a rising debt load, a volatile commodity cycle, and a controversial foreign copper venture. Get the integration right and Lloyds becomes a structurally low-cost steel producer with a moat few can match; get the capex, cycle or governance wrong and a re-rated commodity stock has a long way to fall. The two numbers to watch each quarter are tonnes (is the mine ramping toward 55 MTPA?) and net debt vs EBITDA (is the transformation funding itself?). The mine is the certainty; the transformation is the wager.
