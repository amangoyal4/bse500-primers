00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Jyoti CNC Automation makes the machines that make things. Specifically, it builds CNC machine tools — big, computer-controlled metal-cutting machines that carve, drill, mill and turn a solid block of steel, aluminium or titanium into a finished, precise component. Car engine parts, aircraft brackets, missile bodies, moulds, medical implants: none of them exists until a CNC machine cuts it. Jyoti is India's leading domestic maker of these machines, run out of Rajkot, Gujarat, and it is the country's biggest name in the hardest category of all — simultaneous 5-axis machines, the ones aerospace and defence work demands.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It sells capital equipment against an order book. A CNC machine is a lumpy, engineered purchase, not an off-the-shelf good — customers place orders months ahead, and Jyoti builds to that backlog. The single most-watched number is therefore the order book: ₹4,732 Cr at March 2026, more than two years of revenue, and roughly 41% of it aerospace & defence. FY26 consolidated revenue was ~₹2,093 Cr at a ~25% EBITDA margin.
  • It is an import-substitution play. India is the world's 4th-largest consumer of machine tools but only about 9th in production — roughly two-thirds of what the country uses is imported, mostly from Japan, Germany, Taiwan and China. Jyoti is one of the few domestic firms that can build the sophisticated machines Indian factories would otherwise fly in. It is, quite literally, trying to make the machines that make "Make in India."
  • The bottleneck is its own factory. Here is the twist: in FY26 Jyoti had more orders than it could physically build. Both its plants ran above 100% utilisation in peak months, revenue grew ~15–20% against a guided 35–40%, and the fix is a new line that lifts annual capacity from 6,000 to 16,000 machines by around September 2026. The whole investment case now turns on whether that new capacity fills — and converts the backlog to revenue — at the margins bulls expect.
In plain terms

A CNC machine is a robot sculptor for metal: you feed it a design file and a raw block, and it cuts the block down to an exact part, to tolerances finer than a human hair. Every factory that makes engine components, aircraft parts, tools, moulds or implants needs a room full of them — they are the "mother machines" underneath all manufacturing. Jyoti's business is designing and assembling those mother machines in India and selling them to manufacturers, plus servicing them for decades afterward. What makes it interesting right now is that India is finally building serious factories (defence, aerospace, electronics, EVs), those factories need CNC machines, and Jyoti is one of the very few Indian firms that can supply the high-end ones instead of importing them — if it can build them fast enough.

01

Machine tools 101: the machines that make the parts

No engineering degree required — just what a CNC machine is and why it sits at the base of everything.

Almost every metal object around you started as a plain lump of metal and was cut down to shape. The device that does the cutting is a machine tool. A "CNC" machine tool is one where a computer — the Computer Numerical Control — drives the cutting, so instead of a skilled worker turning handwheels, a program tells the machine exactly where to move its cutting tool, how fast, and how deep, thousands of times a minute, to micron precision.

The three verbs of metal-cutting

  • Turning — the metal spins and a fixed tool shaves it, like a potter's wheel for steel. Makes round things: shafts, bolts, rollers. Jyoti's machines for this are CNC turning centres (lathes).
  • Milling — the metal stays still (or moves on a bed) and a spinning tool carves into it from above and the sides. Makes flat faces, slots, complex pockets. Jyoti's machines are machining centres — vertical (VMC) or horizontal (HMC).
  • Multi-axis machining — the top tier. The tool and the part can both tilt and swivel so the machine reaches every face of a complex shape in one setup. A 5-axis machine moves in five directions at once; it's what you need to carve a curved turbine blade or an impeller in a single go.
Why "axes" is the whole ball game

The number of axes is the single best shorthand for how advanced — and how valuable — a machine is. A basic 3-axis machine moves the tool left-right, forward-back and up-down: fine for simple parts. A 5-axis machine adds two rotations, so it can approach a part from any angle without stopping to re-clamp it. That matters enormously for aerospace and defence, where parts are curved, thin-walled and unforgiving: fewer setups means fewer errors and truer geometry. A 3-axis machine might sell for ₹15–30 lakh; a genuine simultaneous 5-axis machine can run well past ₹2 crore. The move "up the axes" is exactly the move up the value chain — and it is Jyoti's stated strategy.

Why these are called the "mother machines"

Machine tools sit at a peculiar spot in the economy: they are the tools that build the tools. You cannot make a car engine without machine tools; you cannot make the machine that stamps car bodies without machine tools; and you cannot even make a machine tool without other machine tools. A country's ability to manufacture anything advanced is gated by whether it has access to good machine tools — which is why they are treated as strategic, and why importing all of them leaves a nation's factory floor dependent on foreign suppliers, foreign spares and, in the case of defence work, foreign export-control regimes.

The strategic angle in one line: a machine tool is a slow, deliberate purchase with a 10–20 year working life. Whoever supplies the machine also supplies decades of spares, service, retrofits and the next upgrade — so winning the original sale is winning a very long relationship. That is why domestic capability matters, and why a global maker fights hard to keep an Indian buyer on imports.
02

How Jyoti actually earns money

An order-book business with a long aftermarket tail — and a heavy working-capital appetite.

Jyoti's economics are those of a project-and-product capital-goods maker, not a fast-moving consumer business. It designs a range of machines, wins orders from manufacturers, assembles the machines (largely at Rajkot), ships and installs them, and then earns a long tail of spares and service. Three features define the model.

1 · The order book is the visibility

Because a CNC machine takes months from order to delivery, Jyoti always carries a backlog of orders it has won but not yet built. That backlog — ₹4,732 Cr at March 2026, more than two years of current revenue — is the market's window into future sales. A rising order book says demand is strong and revenue is coming; a stalling one is an early warning. Watch two things together: order intake (new orders won each quarter) and book-to-bill (orders won ÷ revenue billed) — above 1.0 means the backlog is still growing.

2 · The machine sale, then the aftermarket annuity

The upfront machine sale is the bulk of revenue, but every installed machine becomes a decades-long customer for spare parts, consumables, service contracts, retrofits and training. Jyoti says it has installed roughly 130,000 machines across 60 countries over its life — an enormous installed base that throws off higher-margin, recurring aftermarket income and, crucially, keeps customers coming back for their next machine.

3 · Working capital is the tax on growth

Building machines to order ties up cash: Jyoti buys steel, castings, imported spindles and controllers, and pays for them long before the customer pays for the finished machine. The faster it grows, the more cash gets locked in inventory and receivables. This is the model's Achilles heel — growth consumes cash — and it shows up directly in the balance sheet (net debt rose from ~₹246 Cr in March 2025 to ~₹523 Cr by September 2025 as the order book swelled).

The mental model that makes it click

Jyoti is a picks-and-shovels supplier to Indian manufacturing. It does not make cars, planes or phones — it sells the equipment to everyone who does. That gives it a broad, diversified pull (whoever is building factories this year is a customer) but also ties its fortunes to the industrial capex cycle: when manufacturers are confident and expanding, orders pour in; when they pause, machine-tool orders are among the first things deferred. The order book is the shock absorber — a fat backlog can carry revenue through a soft patch — but it is not permanent insulation.

03

The company and who controls it

A Rajkot family firm that bought a 180-year-old French aerospace-machining house.

Jyoti is a founder-controlled, engineering-led business. Parakramsinh Jadeja — Chairman & Managing Director — built it from a small Rajkot enterprise in the late 1980s into India's leading CNC machine-tool maker, and the promoter group still owns roughly 62.5% of the company. The defining strategic move of its history was an audacious one for a mid-size Gujarati firm: in 2007 it acquired a storied French machine-tool house many times its age.

  • 2010s

    IPO — a ~₹1,000 Cr all-fresh issue at ₹315–331/share; lists on NSE & BSE on 16 Jan 2024. The proceeds are used mainly to repay debt, sharply deleveraging a previously borrowing-heavy balance sheet.

  • FY26

Ownership & control

  • Promoter: the Jadeja family holds ~62.5%, so this is very much a founder-run company — engineering decisions and capital decisions sit with the same people who built the machines. That alignment is a genuine strength in a technically deep business; the flip side is the usual concentration risk of a promoter-controlled firm.
  • The Huron dimension: owning a French aerospace-machining subsidiary is unusual for an Indian mid-cap, and it cuts both ways — it gives Jyoti credibility and technology in the highest-value niche, but it also exposes it to European operations and export-control regimes (in FY26, an export-control investigation at Huron deferred ~₹67 Cr of revenue — a reminder that aerospace/defence machining is a regulated business).
  • Balance sheet: the IPO cleared most legacy debt, but working capital for the growing order book has pushed net debt back up (~₹523 Cr by Sep 2025). This is not a debt-free compounder — it is a growth capital-goods firm that must fund its own working capital.
04

The product ladder: from commodity lathe to aerospace 5-axis

Over 200 machine variants — but the money and the moat sit at the top of the range.

Jyoti sells more than 200 machine variants, but the way to understand the portfolio is as a ladder. At the bottom are high-volume, competitive, lower-priced machines; at the top are scarce, engineering-intensive machines that few in India can build. Jyoti's strategy is to keep climbing that ladder, because each rung up means higher price, fatter margin and less competition.

What sits on each rung

  • CNC turning centres — lathes for round parts. The bread-and-butter of general engineering and auto-component shops; competitive, price-sensitive, high-volume.
  • Vertical machining centres (VMC) — the single largest-selling category across Indian industry; Jyoti's volume engine, sold to auto, general engineering and tool rooms.
  • Horizontal machining centres (HMC) — more productive, more automated, used in higher-throughput manufacturing; a step up in price and complexity.
  • 5-axis & multi-tasking machines — the strategic tier. Jyoti is India's leading maker of simultaneous 5-axis machines (roughly a tenth of the domestic market), the category aerospace, defence, space, medical and die-mould customers need. This is the rung the growth story is built on.
  • Huron high-precision machines — the French line, aimed at the most demanding aerospace/defence milling — the scarce, top-of-range products that anchor Jyoti's premium credibility.
Why the mix matters more than the machine count

Jyoti shipped ~5,550 machines in FY26 at an average realisation of roughly ₹36 lakh each — but that average hides everything. A single high-end 5-axis machine can be worth ten or more entry-level VMCs. So the company can grow revenue and margin without shipping many more units, simply by selling a richer mix: more machines above ₹2 crore, fewer commodity boxes. That mix-up is already visible — roughly 40% of the FY26 order book is machines priced above ₹2 crore, largely aerospace-specification. The metric to watch is not "how many machines" but "what fraction of value is high-end."

05

The import-substitution case: making the machines that make "Make in India"

India buys machine tools like a rich country and builds them like a poor one — that gap is the opportunity.

Here is the structural fact underneath Jyoti's whole story. India is one of the world's largest consumers of machine tools — the 4th-largest — but a distant 9th in production. The mismatch is filled by imports: by most estimates, roughly two-thirds of the machine tools India uses are imported, and about 60% of the critical components (spindles, controllers, high-precision parts) come from abroad — chiefly Japan, Germany, Taiwan and China. Every one of those imports is a rupee that could, in principle, be spent on a domestically built machine.

Why does this matter now, when it has been true for decades? Because two forces are pushing hard on it at once:

  • The capex & "Atmanirbhar" push. India is in an unusually strong factory-building phase — production-linked incentives, defence indigenisation, an electronics/EMS surge, and a "China+1" shift of global manufacturing toward India. Every new factory needs CNC machines, and government policy explicitly wants more of them built domestically rather than imported. A domestic machine-tool leader is a direct beneficiary.
  • Strategic autonomy in defence. For aerospace and defence, importing the machines that cut your missile and jet parts means depending on foreign export licences — precisely the kind of dependency the government is trying to remove. A trusted Indian supplier of 5-axis machines is not just cheaper; it is strategically preferred.
The real limit of the import-substitution story

Import substitution is a real, durable tailwind — but it is not a free win, and it deserves a caveat. Jyoti still imports some of its own critical inputs (high-end spindles, CNC control systems), so it is substituting the machine while remaining exposed on components and on forex. And the imports it competes against are made by the best machine-tool firms on earth. The opportunity is enormous precisely because so little of it has been captured; the reason so little has been captured is that building world-class machine tools is genuinely hard. Jyoti's edge is that, via Huron and decades of its own R&D, it can already build at the high end — which is where import substitution is most valuable and least contested.

06

Aerospace, defence & the order book

The high-value niche that a Rajkot machine-tool firm turns out to dominate — and the surprise at the centre of the story.

If there is one thing to understand about why Jyoti is valued the way it is, it is this: a company from Rajkot — a city better known for casting and bearings than for aerospace — builds the sophisticated 5-axis machines that carve the parts inside jet engines, missiles and satellites. That capability, inherited partly from the Huron acquisition and built out over two decades, is the highest-value, least-contested corner of the machine-tool market, and it is where Jyoti's order book is concentrated.

Why aerospace & defence is the sweet spot

  • It demands the hardest machines. Turbine blades, engine casings, structural brackets and missile bodies are curved, thin-walled, exotic-alloy parts that only high-end multi-axis machines can cut accurately. That rules out most competitors and lets Jyoti charge premium prices.
  • It is being indigenised on purpose. India's defence and aerospace build-out — HAL, DRDO, private defence firms, the space sector, and global aerospace supply chains setting up in India — needs domestic machining capacity. A trusted Indian 5-axis supplier is strategically favoured over imports.
  • The orders are big and sticky. Aerospace-grade machines cost crores each, come with long service relationships, and pull through spares and future orders. Roughly 40% of the FY26 order book is machines above ₹2 crore — a mix that lifts both revenue per unit and margin.
The surprise a generalist should take away

The instinctive picture of a machine-tool company is a commodity metal-basher competing on price with Chinese and Taiwanese imports. For the bottom of Jyoti's range, that's roughly right. But the part that drives the valuation is the opposite: a genuinely high-tech, hard-to-replicate franchise in simultaneous 5-axis aerospace machining, seeded by a 180-year-old French acquisition and now feeding India's defence indigenisation drive. The same company sells a ₹20-lakh lathe to a Rajkot job-shop and a multi-crore 5-axis machine that helps make a jet engine. The bull case is entirely about the second business growing faster than the first.

The one number that settles the thesis: not the order book's size, but its conversion — how much of that ₹4,732 Cr backlog turns into billed revenue each year at high-value mix and target margin, once capacity allows. A record backlog that cannot be built and shipped is a promise, not a profit. Watch the pairing of high-value order intake (is the aerospace/5-axis share of new orders rising?) and revenue conversion (is billed revenue finally catching the backlog?).
07

The capacity crunch: when your own roof is the bottleneck

The rare industrial company held back not by demand but by how many machines it can physically build.

Most companies wish they had Jyoti's problem, and most investors misread it. In FY26, Jyoti did not grow revenue slowly because orders dried up — orders were overflowing. It grew slowly because it ran out of factory. During peak months its plants — the main Rajkot facility plus the French Huron unit — ran at utilisation above 100%, and the company still could not build machines fast enough to match its backlog. Revenue grew roughly 15–20% while management had guided to 35–40%. The gap was not lost demand; it was un-built machines.

Same order book, same demand, same 5-axis leadership — the difference between a 15% year and a 40% year sat entirely in how many machines the factory could physically finish.

This is the non-transferable device at the heart of Jyoti: it is a maker of the machines that expand everyone else's capacity, that had itself hit a capacity wall. The fix is literal — a new production line at Rajkot that lifts annual capacity from 6,000 machines to 16,000 machines, with commercial operations targeted for around September 2026 (Q2 FY27). If it comes on schedule and fills, the backlog that has been backing up can finally flow through to revenue.

Why this reframes the whole case

A demand-constrained company is a bet on the market. A capacity-constrained company with a two-year backlog is a bet on execution — a very different, and in some ways more knowable, risk. The upside is unusually visible: the orders already exist; Jyoti just has to build them. The catch is that the new capacity has to (1) come up on time, (2) hit quality on complex 5-axis machines from day one, and (3) fill before the industrial cycle turns. The company is deliberately adding a big fixed cost — a new plant — to chase a backlog; if demand were to soften during the ramp, that same fixed cost becomes operating deleverage instead of leverage. September 2026 is the date on which this stops being a plan and becomes a number.

08

The industry and the rivals

A global oligopoly at the top, a crowded field at the bottom, and Jyoti straddling both.

The machine-tool market splits sharply by tier. At the high end, a handful of global giants dominate 5-axis and precision machining worldwide. At the volume end, Jyoti competes with imports and a fragmented set of domestic makers. Its strategic position depends on defending the middle-to-high ground where its Huron pedigree gives it an edge that most Indian rivals lack.

The competitive tension to watch

Two squeezes bracket Jyoti. From above, the global majors set the technology bar and win the most exacting aerospace work; Jyoti's job is to keep closing that gap fast enough to take domestic aerospace/defence orders that would otherwise be imported. From below, cheap Taiwanese and Chinese imports and domestic peers keep the entry range price-competitive and low-margin. Jyoti's answer to both is the same — climb the axes. The more its mix tilts to 5-axis and Huron-grade machines, the further it sits from the commodity knife-fight and the closer it gets to the pricing power of the global majors. If that climb stalls, it risks being stuck in the low-margin middle.

Financials & valuation

Fast growth, improving margins, a rich multiple — and a working-capital caveat.

The market prices it as a structural-growth capital-goods name, not a cheap cyclical.

How to read the ~45× multiple

At a ~₹18,000 Cr market cap on FY26 profits, Jyoti trades around 45× trailing earnings — expensive for a capital-goods maker, and only defensible on a specific belief: that the ₹4,732 Cr order book, the aerospace/5-axis mix, and the 16,000-machine capacity together deliver several years of profit growth well above the market's, with margins expanding as the high-value share rises. The number that will prove or disprove that is the order book's revenue conversion at high-value mix — can Jyoti actually build and bill the backlog it has won, at rising realisations, once the new plant is live? If FY27–FY28 revenue re-accelerates toward the guided range and margins hold, the multiple is earned. If the ramp slips, working capital balloons, or a capex cycle softens demand, a 45× multiple on a working-capital-heavy cyclical is exposed to a sharp de-rating.

10

The risks, honestly

A high-quality niche does not make the cyclicality, the working capital or the price go away.

Jyoti's strengths are real, but the risk list is specific and worth holding clearly alongside the bull case.

  • Cyclicality. Machine-tool demand tracks the industrial capex cycle. When manufacturers pause expansion — a slowdown, a rate shock, a demand scare — new machine orders are among the first things deferred. The order book cushions a soft patch but does not eliminate the cycle; Jyoti is a leveraged bet on India's capex staying strong.
  • Working capital & lumpy execution. Growth locks cash into inventory and receivables (net debt rose to ~₹523 Cr by Sep 2025). Revenue is lumpy — big machines ship in bunches — so quarters can swing, and a fast ramp can strain the balance sheet even when orders are healthy.
  • Execution on the capacity ramp. The entire near-term thesis rests on the 16,000-machine expansion coming up on time and producing quality 5-axis machines from the start. Delays, teething issues or a slower-than-hoped fill would push out the revenue conversion the multiple is paying for.
  • Competition & technology intensity. Global majors (DMG Mori, Mazak, Makino) set the frontier and out-resource Jyoti on R&D; Taiwanese/Chinese imports pressure the low end. Staying ahead requires continuous, expensive engineering — a permanent cost of doing business.
  • Forex & imported components. Jyoti still imports high-precision inputs (spindles, controllers), so a weaker rupee or supply disruption hits costs — an irony for an import-substitution play, and a real margin variable.
  • Regulatory / Huron export-control exposure. Aerospace/defence machining is regulated; the FY26 Huron export-control investigation that deferred ~₹67 Cr of revenue is a live reminder that the high-value niche carries compliance risk the commodity business does not.
  • Valuation. At ~45× earnings, the stock already embeds years of successful execution. Any disappointment lands against a rich starting point.
The single biggest swing factor: whether the new Rajkot capacity converts the backlog to billed revenue on schedule and at a richer mix. Get that right and FY27–FY28 growth re-accelerates hard; slip on the ramp, or hit a capex-cycle air-pocket while carrying a new plant's fixed costs, and both growth and the multiple are at risk at the same time.
11

The future, and the bull/bear

A genuinely strong franchise in a strategically important niche — priced for the ramp to work.

Jyoti is one of the clearer ways to own India's manufacturing and defence-indigenisation build-out at the equipment layer: a domestic leader in the high-value 5-axis machines the country would otherwise import, with a two-year backlog and a step-change in capacity arriving. The debate is about execution and price, not about whether the franchise is real.

The bull case

  • High-end moat — India's leading simultaneous-5-axis maker, with Huron aerospace pedigree few domestic rivals can match.
  • Structural tailwinds — capex boom, defence/aerospace indigenisation, EMS/EV/China+1, and a two-thirds-import market to substitute into.
  • Visible demand — a ₹4,732 Cr order book (>2× revenue), ~41% aerospace/defence, mix tilting above ₹2 Cr/machine.
  • Capacity unlock — 6,000→16,000 machines by ~Sep 2026 turns a supply constraint into a revenue catalyst.
  • Aligned, expert ownership — a founder group that has built machine-tool capability for three decades.

The bear case

  • Cyclical demand — orders track industrial capex; a slowdown defers machine purchases fast.
  • Working-capital drag — growth consumes cash; net debt has risen with the backlog.
  • Ramp risk — the thesis leans on a new plant coming up on time, at quality, and filling.
  • Tough competition — global majors above, cheap imports below, ongoing R&D cost to stay in the game.
  • Forex & compliance — imported components and aerospace export-control exposure (the FY26 Huron deferment).
  • Rich valuation — ~45× earnings on a working-capital-heavy cyclical assumes the ramp works.
The bottom line

Jyoti CNC has done the hard part that most Indian capital-goods stories never reach: it can already build the machines at the top of the range, in the strategically prized aerospace/defence niche, with orders stacked up two years deep. What it has to prove is prosaic and dated — that a new production line at Rajkot comes alive around September 2026 and starts turning that ₹4,732 Cr backlog into billed, high-margin revenue. Everything the ~45× multiple is paying for runs through that plant's ramp. If the machines that make everyone else's parts can finally be built fast enough to match the order book, Jyoti grows into the leader of an import-dependent market it is uniquely placed to take. Watch one figure above all others each quarter: how much of the backlog actually converts to revenue as the new capacity comes on.
