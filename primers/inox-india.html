00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

INOX India — known in its industry by the brand INOXCVA — makes cryogenic equipment: the specially engineered, double-walled, vacuum-insulated tanks, vessels, transport containers and complete systems that hold liquids at extraordinarily low temperatures without boiling them away. Liquid oxygen at about −183 °C, liquid nitrogen at −196 °C, LNG (liquefied natural gas) at about −162 °C, liquid hydrogen colder still. Wherever a gas has to be chilled into a liquid to be stored or shipped economically, something has to hold that liquid safely — and in India, more often than not, that something is built in Vadodara, Gujarat by INOX India. It is the country's market leader in cryogenic tanks and systems, and a genuinely global exporter of them.

Three facts frame everything:

  • One engineering skill, several growing end-markets. The same discipline — safely containing a super-cold liquid — is sold into industrial & medical gases (oxygen, nitrogen, argon), LNG (transport and distribution fuel), clean energy (increasingly liquid hydrogen), and science, space & defence (turnkey cryogenic systems). In FY26 the Industrial Gas division was roughly half of revenue, LNG had grown to ~28%, and the technology-heavy Cryo Scientific division was ~14% — a mix that is deliberately shifting toward the faster-growing, higher-engineering legs.
  • The story is the backlog and the export book, not one quarter. INOX India exited FY26 with a ~₹1,514 Cr order book, and ~63% of it is exports. This is not a domestic capital-goods name that happens to ship abroad; it is a qualified global supplier whose backlog gives multi-quarter visibility. FY26 total income rose ~21% to ~₹1,632 Cr with adjusted EBITDA margin near 23.8% — the business compounds on order intake and execution, not a single sale.
  • Debt-free, cash-generative, and richly valued. A December-2023 IPO, the company runs net-cash with strong margins and returns — and the market prices it accordingly, at roughly 70× earnings. That multiple embeds not just today's industrial-gas cash engine but the scaling LNG leg and the optionality of hydrogen. The debate lives in whether those newer legs scale fast enough to justify it.
In plain terms

Cold is a container problem. Turning a gas into a liquid shrinks it hundreds of times over, which is the only affordable way to store or move a lot of it — but the moment you have a super-cold liquid, you need a vessel that keeps heat out well enough that it doesn't just boil off. INOX India builds those vessels, from a small hospital oxygen tank to a giant LNG storage sphere to the cryogenic plumbing of a fusion experiment. Its value is not one product; it is being the qualified, trusted, hard-to-replace maker of safety-critical cold-storage kit across a set of end-markets that are all, for different reasons, growing at once. The catch is that this leadership is priced as a certainty, and the newest, most exciting legs — LNG at scale and hydrogen — are still early.

01

Cryogenics 101: why cold is a business

No physics degree needed — just why anyone freezes a gas in the first place, and what that demands of the tank.

"Cryogenic" simply means very cold — conventionally below about −150 °C. The reason industry cares is not the cold itself but what the cold does: it turns gases into liquids. A gas is mostly empty space; a litre of it holds very little. Chill that same gas until it condenses into a liquid and it shrinks dramatically — liquid oxygen takes up hundreds of times less volume than the gas, LNG about 600 times less than natural gas. Suddenly you can store an enormous quantity in a sensible tank, and truck, rail or ship it economically. Cryogenics is, at heart, a logistics technology: it is how you move a lot of gas without a pipeline.

But a super-cold liquid is a restless thing. The whole world around it is warmer, and heat leaking in makes it boil — turning your valuable liquid back into gas you have to vent or lose. So the entire craft of a cryogenic vessel is keeping heat out. That is why these are not ordinary tanks. To follow the business, you need a handful of ideas.

The only concepts you need

The reason this is safety-critical, not commodity

A cryogenic vessel holds a large volume of extremely cold, often flammable or asphyxiating, sometimes high-pressure liquid, frequently moving down a highway or riding on a ship. If the insulation fails, the contents warm, expand and pressurise; a badly built tank is a serious hazard. That is precisely why customers — industrial-gas majors, shipyards, hospitals, space agencies — will not buy from just anyone. They buy from a maker whose designs are proven, certified and approved to the relevant international codes, with a long field record of tanks that didn't fail. The barrier to entry isn't a machine you can buy; it's decades of qualified, incident-free engineering. Hold that thought — it is the entire moat.

Who needs cryogenic equipment

  • Industrial-gas companies — the Linde, Air Liquide, Air Products and INOX Air Products of the world, who produce oxygen, nitrogen and argon and need tanks, transport containers and on-site vessels to distribute them to steel mills, chemical plants and hospitals.
  • LNG distributors and users — as natural gas is moved and used as a fuel away from pipelines, it travels and is stored as a cryogenic liquid, needing tanks, transport trailers and re-gasification kit.
  • Healthcare — hospitals storing liquid medical oxygen; a demand pillar that the pandemic made vivid and permanent.
  • Science, space & defence — rocket propellant handling, superconducting magnets, fusion experiments and defence programmes all need bespoke cryogenic systems.
  • Clean energy (emerging) — the hydrogen economy, where liquid hydrogen at −253 °C is one way to store and move the fuel, needs the most demanding cryogenic vessels of all.
02

What INOX India actually sells: one skill, three-plus divisions

The segment map — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

INOX India runs the same core competence — designing and building vacuum-insulated cryogenic equipment — across a set of divisions with very different maturities and margins. Understanding the split is the fastest way to understand the investment case, because the three legs are on three different clocks: one is a proven cash engine, one is a fast-scaling growth leg, and one is a high-engineering specialty with the longest runway.

The divisions, decoded

  • Industrial Gas (~50%) — the bread and butter. Standard and semi-standard cryogenic products for the gas industry: transport tanks that ride on trucks and rail, liquid cylinders, bulk on-site storage vessels, and its Cryoseal valves and fittings line. High volumes, a repeat-order relationship with the world's gas majors, and the segment where INOX's scale and cost position are strongest. This is the leg that funds everything else.
  • LNG (~28%) — the fastest-growing division. As India and the world move natural gas around and use it as a transport and industrial fuel away from pipelines, LNG has to be stored and hauled as a cryogenic liquid. INOX builds the tanks, transport trailers, small-scale storage and distribution equipment — and in FY26 booked its first marine LNG tank order (from Cochin Shipyard), a step into LNG-fuelled ships. This segment posted its best-ever year at ~₹457 Cr.
  • Cryo Scientific (~14%) — the deep-engineering, turnkey end. Bespoke cryogenic systems and complete plants for research, space and defence — the kind of work where a customer hands over a specification, not an order form, and INOX designs and delivers the whole installation. Lower volume, but the highest engineering content and the strongest evidence of the moat.
  • Clean Energy (emerging) — not yet a big revenue line, but the option that excites the market: liquid-hydrogen storage and handling, plus other clean-energy cryogenic fluids. Same skill, hardest application, longest payoff.
Why one skill across four markets is the real design of the business

The important insight is that these are not four unrelated businesses bolted together — they are four applications of a single, transferable capability. The engineer who knows how to keep liquid nitrogen from boiling in a truck tank is most of the way to keeping LNG cold on a ship and liquid hydrogen cold in a fuel station. So each new end-market is not a leap into the unknown; it is the same vacuum-insulation know-how pointed at a new, growing demand pool. That is what lets a Vadodara tank-maker credibly say it can serve the hydrogen economy: it is not learning a new trade, it is aiming an old one at a new customer. The risk in that story is timing, not capability — the skill is real; whether the newest markets arrive on the schedule the valuation assumes is the open question.

03

The company, the group, and who controls it

A half-century-old Vadodara engineering house, part of the INOX (Jain family) group, listed only in December 2023.

INOX India is older than it looks on a stock screen. The listing is recent — a December-2023 IPO — but the company traces back to 1976, when it was incorporated in Gujarat as Baroda Oxygen Limited. Over nearly five decades it grew into INOXCVA, India's leading cryogenic-equipment maker, long before it ever sold a share to the public. It belongs to the broader INOX group controlled by the Jain family — the same industrial house associated with the INOX and Gujarat Fluorochemicals lineage.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Jain family and associated group entities hold roughly ~75% (about 74.9%). Because the IPO was an offer-for-sale, the company itself raised no fresh capital in the listing — it was already self-funding. Promoter-group members have declared no encumbrance (no pledging) on their shares for FY26, a cleaner signal than many promoter-heavy Indian names.
  • Don't confuse the Inox names: this is a recurring trap. INOX India (INOXCVA) — this company — makes cryogenic tanks. Inox Wind (INOXWIND) makes wind turbines. PVR INOX runs cinemas. They share a brand heritage and family lineage but are separate listed companies with separate businesses. A primer reader should keep INOXCVA firmly in the cold-storage box.
  • Balance sheet: effectively debt-free / net-cash, with strong return ratios and cash generation. FY26 adjusted PAT of ~₹261 Cr on ~₹1,632 Cr of income, funding its own capex — including the new Kandla plant — without leaning on borrowings.
  • Footprint: a cluster of manufacturing sites in Gujarat (around Vadodara/Kalol) plus international sales and service reach; the Kandla facility, on land already acquired, will be the 5th plant and is guided to commission within roughly 9–10 months of the FY26 update.
Why "old company, new listing" matters: unlike a freshly-minted IPO with an unproven model, INOX India brought a ~half-century operating record and an installed base of tanks in the field to the market in 2023. The public investor is not underwriting whether the company can build cryogenic equipment — that is long settled — but whether a proven, market-leading engineering business can grow its newer LNG and clean-energy legs fast enough to earn a premium multiple. That is a very different risk than most recent listings carry.
04

The qualification moat: why so few can do this

The single most important reason INOX India earns its margins — and it can't be bought off a shelf.

Ask why a company that makes what sounds like "metal tanks" earns EBITDA margins in the mid-20s and trades at a premium, and the answer is one word: qualification. Cryogenic equipment is safety-critical, and the customers who buy it — global gas majors, shipyards, space agencies, defence programmes — cannot afford a failure. So they do not buy on price from whoever bids lowest. They buy from a short list of makers whose designs are certified to international codes, approved by the customer's own engineers, and backed by a long field record of vessels that performed. Getting onto that list takes years and an unblemished track record. That is the moat, and it is not a machine you can purchase.

Money alone doesn't buy your way in — the binding constraints are certification, customer approval and a spotless field record, all of which take years to accumulate. That is why the global cryo-equipment field is a handful of names, not hundreds.

This shows up in the numbers as durable pricing and repeat business. A gas major that has qualified INOX as a vendor, run its tanks safely for years and integrated them into its logistics does not casually switch to an untested supplier to save a few percent — the switching risk is a safety and reliability risk, not just a cost one. That stickiness is why INOX can hold mid-20s margins in what a casual observer might mistake for a commodity, and why its export business — selling into the home markets of the world's gas majors — is such a strong endorsement.

The moat, stated as a test

Here is the way to sanity-check the moat: ask what would happen if a well-funded newcomer decided tomorrow to take INOX India's business. It could buy land, steel and welding machines within a year. It could not, within a year, earn ASME and international certifications, get itself onto Linde's or Air Liquide's approved-vendor lists, or — most of all — accumulate the decades of in-service tanks that prove its designs don't fail. By the time it had, INOX would have moved further ahead on the LNG and hydrogen frontier. The barrier isn't a secret formula; it is elapsed time under scrutiny, and that is the one thing capital can't compress.

05

The order book and the export engine

The single most important idea in the investment case — and the reason to watch backlog, not quarters.

A cryogenic-equipment company is a project and order-intake business. Large tanks and systems are built to order and delivered over months; a big LNG or scientific job is lumpy by nature. So the number that actually tells you where the company is heading is not last quarter's revenue — it is the order book, and how fast new orders are replacing delivered ones. INOX India exited FY26 with a ~₹1,514 Cr backlog, and the single most striking fact about it is where the demand comes from: ~63% is exports.

Sit with the export figure, because it upends the natural assumption. One might expect a Vadodara cryogenics firm to be a play on India's gas and LNG build-out, with exports as a nice extra. The device that fits only INOX India: one company, three clocks

Most industrial companies run on one clock — a single business cycle you track quarter to quarter. INOX India runs on three at once, and holding them apart is the whole analytical trick. The Industrial Gas clock ticks steadily — a mature, cash-generative base compounding with the world's gas demand, ~50% of revenue, the part you can bank. The LNG clock is accelerating — from ~17% to ~28% of revenue in a single year, powered by gas-on-wheels and now marine LNG, the leg that is scaling. And the clean-energy clock has barely started — liquid hydrogen, real but years from meaningful revenue, the leg that is pure option. A bull is buying all three clocks and betting the fast two keep their pace; a bear is paying today for a steady first clock while the exciting two are still small or unproven. The thesis is settled not by any single quarter but by order backlog growth, book-to-bill, the LNG/clean-energy share of revenue, and the EBITDA margin — read together, over quarters, they tell you which clocks are actually keeping time.

06

The LNG leg: gas-on-wheels and the pipeline India doesn't have

The fastest-scaling division, and the clearest read on where growth comes from.

If the industrial-gas division is the cash engine, LNG is the accelerator — the segment that jumped from roughly 17% to 28% of revenue in a year and posted a best-ever ~₹457 Cr. To understand why, you have to understand a simple gap in India's energy system: the country wants to burn much more natural gas — it is cleaner than coal or diesel — but it does not have pipelines reaching everywhere the gas is needed. Cryogenics is the workaround.

Here is the mechanism. Natural gas straight from a field or an import terminal can only travel by pipeline. But chill it to about −162 °C and it becomes LNG, a liquid that takes up ~600 times less space — and now it can ride a truck, a rail wagon or a small ship to places no pipeline reaches: a factory in the interior, a city-gas hub, a bus depot, a remote power plant. This is the industry's "LNG-on-wheels": using cryogenic transport tanks as a virtual pipeline. Every one of those trucks, storage stations and re-gasification units is cryogenic equipment — INOX's product.

Why the marine-LNG order is a bigger deal than its size

The FY26 first marine LNG tank order from Cochin Shipyard is small in rupees but large in what it signals. Ships are among the hardest, most regulated cryogenic applications going — a fuel tank that has to survive a rolling deck and a marine safety regime. Winning a shipyard's trust for LNG fuel tanks is a qualification credential that opens a whole new global end-market as shipping decarbonises and switches to LNG. It is a live example of the three-clock idea from Section 05: the same tank-making skill, pointed at a brand-new demand pool, converting the moat into a fresh growth leg. Watch whether that first order becomes a repeatable line rather than a one-off — that is the tell that marine LNG is a real business, not a press release.

The domestic driver behind all this is policy and economics pulling in the same direction: India wants to lift gas from a low-teens share of its energy mix toward ~15%, LNG is how gas reaches beyond the pipeline map, and diesel-to-LNG switching in trucking and industry is increasingly economic. Each of those trends converts, at the equipment level, into demand for exactly what INOX builds. The LNG clock is the one most directly geared to India's own energy transition.

07

The hydrogen option: the coldest, longest bet

The clean-energy leg that excites the multiple — and why it is still an option, not a business.

The most alluring part of the INOX India story, and the part most responsible for its premium valuation, is the smallest today: clean energy, above all liquid hydrogen. The logic is elegant. Hydrogen is a leading candidate to decarbonise heavy transport, industry and long-duration energy storage — and one of the ways to store and move hydrogen densely is to liquefy it at about −253 °C, colder than almost anything else industry handles. That is the hardest cryogenic problem there is, and the maker best placed to solve it is the one that already builds the world's LNG and industrial-gas tanks. On paper, INOX India is that maker.

This is the same "one skill, new market" pattern (Section 02) taken to its extreme: liquid hydrogen is cryogenics on hard mode, and a company that has spent decades keeping liquid oxygen and LNG cold has the right starting point to build hydrogen storage, transport and fuelling equipment. If a hydrogen economy arrives at scale, the equipment layer beneath it — the tanks and systems — is a market INOX is structurally positioned to serve.

The honest caveat on hydrogen: a large part of the bull case here is optionality, not current earnings. Liquid-hydrogen demand at industrial scale depends on green-hydrogen economics that are still improving, infrastructure that is still being planned, and timelines measured in years, not quarters. INOX is right to be qualified and ready; investors should be clear-eyed that this leg may contribute little revenue for some time. It is a genuine call on the future — but a valuation that already prices it in is leaning on a market that hasn't fully arrived. Size the hydrogen leg as a warrant on the energy transition, not as a line you can bank in FY27.

How to weigh an option you can't yet measure

The disciplined way to handle the hydrogen leg is to separate what you are paying for. The industrial-gas and LNG divisions are real, cash-generative and growing — you can value them on order books and margins today. The hydrogen leg is different: its value is the right to supply a market that may become very large, backed by a capability that is genuinely rare. Treat it as an option whose payoff is real but whose timing is uncertain, and the question becomes concrete: is the premium the market pays over the value of the proven divisions a reasonable price for that option? When liquid-hydrogen orders start appearing in the backlog in size, the option starts becoming a business — and that transition, visible in the order book, is the single cleanest signal to watch for.

08

Cryo Scientific: the surprise inside a tank company

The smallest division, the deepest engineering, and the best proof of what INOX can really do.

Here is the part that surprises a smart generalist: a Vadodara company best known for industrial-gas tanks also builds bespoke cryogenic systems for some of the most advanced science and defence programmes in the world. That is the Cryo Scientific division — around 14% of revenue, but far more than 14% of the story, because it is the clearest evidence that INOX operates at the frontier of its craft, not just its commodity end.

Cryo Scientific delivers turnkey engineered systems — a customer arrives with a specification for a research installation and INOX designs, builds and commissions the whole cryogenic plant. The applications read like a tour of hard engineering: cryogenic distribution and cooling systems for fusion and high-energy-physics research, propellant and test systems adjacent to space programmes, superconducting-magnet cooling, and specialised defence and aerospace work. In FY26 this division's frontier credentials were underlined by a ~₹200 Cr aerospace order from a leading US space company — an American space customer buying critical cryogenic hardware from Gujarat.

A tank-maker you can locate on a map of Gujarat also supplies cryogenic systems to fusion research and a US space programme — that is the range of a real engineering moat, not a commodity.
Why the smallest division carries outsized weight

Cryo Scientific matters out of proportion to its revenue for two reasons. First, it is proof of capability: winning the trust of fusion labs and space customers is the ultimate certification, and it makes the claim "we can build hydrogen equipment" credible in a way a marketing slide never could. Second, it is a pipeline of hard problems — the engineering demanded at the scientific frontier tends to migrate, years later, into commercial products. A company solving today's hardest cryogenic problems for a research reactor is building exactly the muscle it will need for tomorrow's liquid-hydrogen infrastructure. The division is small on the P&L and large on the moat — and that asymmetry is easy to miss if you only read the revenue split.

09

The industry and the rivals

A consolidated global field — one dominant Indian leader, a big US major, and a handful of specialists.

Cryogenic-equipment making is a concentrated business — for exactly the qualification reasons in Section 04, it is a game of a few scaled, certified players rather than a fragmented crowd. In India, INOX India is the clear leader with no listed domestic peer of similar scale in pure cryogenics. Globally, the reference competitor is America's Chart Industries, with a scattering of regional specialists around it.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two features define the field. First, INOX's position is strong at home and credible abroad: it is the default choice in India and a qualified exporter into the majors' own markets, which is rare for an Indian capital-goods company and hard to dislodge. Second, the competition that matters most is not a domestic rival — there isn't a listed one of similar scale — but Chart Industries and the global majors' own capabilities, especially on the LNG and hydrogen frontier where the biggest future orders will be fought over. INOX's edge in that fight is a lower cost base and a full-range qualification; its challenge is that the global players are larger and better capitalised. The near-term contest is less about losing existing industrial-gas share and more about who captures the new LNG-marine and hydrogen equipment markets as they open.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A proven market leader in structurally growing markets — asked to grow its newest legs at a premium price.

INOX India enters FY27 as a debt-free market leader with a ~₹1,514 Cr order book, a widening export base, a scaling LNG franchise and a genuine — if early — hydrogen option. The debate is not whether the business is good; it plainly is. It is whether the two younger clocks keep their pace fast enough to earn a multiple that already assumes they will.

The bull case

  • Dominant, hard-to-replace leader — a qualification moat (certifications, approved-vendor status, decades of field record) that money alone can't buy.
  • Multiple structural tailwinds at once — industrial & medical gas, India's gas/LNG build-out, and the hydrogen frontier all pulling the same product.
  • Export-majority order book — ~63% of backlog from the world's most demanding buyers; the moat expressed as revenue visibility.
  • Scaling LNG leg — up to ~28% of revenue and into marine LNG; the growth engine is already visibly turning.
  • Debt-free and high-return — mid-20s margins, self-funded capex (Kandla), clean promoter holding with no pledging.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~70× earnings prices in continued growth-leg scaling; a mix stall or margin slip invites a sharp de-rating.
  • Order lumpiness & end-market concentration — large cryo orders are chunky; a slowdown in industrial-gas or LNG capex hits intake and quarters swing.
  • Hydrogen is early — a big slice of the bull case is optionality on a market that may take years to arrive at scale.
  • Execution, input & FX risk — new capacity (Kandla), stainless/aluminium prices, and a heavy export book expose it to currency and global-cycle swings.
  • Formidable global competition — Chart Industries and the gas majors' own capabilities contest exactly the LNG/hydrogen future INOX is priced for.
The bottom line

INOX India is a rare thing in Indian capital goods: a debt-free, high-margin, globally qualified market leader in a product — safety-critical cryogenic equipment — whose end-markets are all growing for durable, structural reasons. The moat is real, the export book proves it, and the LNG leg is already scaling. What the ~70× multiple asks is that the newer clocks keep ticking faster: that LNG keeps taking share of the mix, that marine LNG becomes a repeatable line, and that hydrogen graduates from a capability into orders. The place all of that shows up first is the same place the whole story lives — the order book, and specifically the LNG-and-clean-energy share of it. Watch that share climb, watch book-to-bill hold above one, and watch the margin stay in the mid-20s: when the Kandla plant commissions and the first liquid-hydrogen orders appear in the backlog, you'll know the option is turning into a business. Until then, the cash engine pays the bills while the market waits to see how fast the other two clocks run.
