00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

TBO Tek — the name is short for "Travel Boutique Online" — runs a business-to-business travel marketplace. It does not sell holidays to you and me. Instead it sits in the middle of the travel trade: on one side it plugs in travel suppliers (hotels, airlines, car rentals, airport transfers, sightseeing, insurance); on the other side it plugs in travel buyers (retail travel agents, tour operators and smaller online agencies). A booking flows across the platform, and TBO keeps a thin slice of its value. It is asset-light — it owns no hotels and no aircraft — and it operates in 100-plus countries.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a toll-keeper on a very large flow. In FY26 roughly ₹36,809 Cr of bookings (Gross Transaction Value, or GTV) passed across the platform — up ~19% on FY25 — from which TBO recorded ~₹2,677 Cr of revenue and ~₹244 Cr of net profit. It keeps only a small percentage of the money that moves through it; the whole game is growing that flow and gently lifting the slice.
  • The profit lives in hotels, not flights. Air ticketing is a high-volume, wafer-thin-margin business that mostly serves to keep agents logging in daily. The money is made in hotels and ancillaries — which in FY26 were roughly 62% of GTV but the large majority of gross profit. Shifting the mix toward hotels is the single most important operating lever.
  • The moat is a two-sided network. Each extra supplier makes the platform more useful to every agent, and each extra agent makes it more attractive to every supplier. Once both sides are wired in, they are sticky — which is why TBO can compound if it keeps adding suppliers, buyers and new countries faster than rivals can prise them away.
In plain terms

Picture the travel world as an enormous, chaotic wholesale market. On one side stand millions of small hotels scattered across every country; on the other, hundreds of thousands of small travel agents trying to book them for their customers. No single hotel can build a connection to every agent, and no single agent can integrate with every hotel — the plumbing would be impossible. TBO builds that plumbing once, in the middle: a hotel lists on TBO and instantly reaches the whole agent network; an agent connects to TBO once and can book a vast global inventory of rooms and flights. TBO's reward is a modest margin on each booking it helps happen. It never touches the hotel keys or the boarding pass — it runs the marketplace where the two sides meet.

01

Travel distribution 101: why a middleman exists at all

No industry knowledge assumed — just why the travel trade needs plumbing.

To understand TBO you first have to understand a peculiar fact about travel: it is one of the most fragmented industries on earth. There is no single "hotel company" the way there is a handful of giant car makers. There are well over a million hotels and guesthouses worldwide, most of them independent, family-run and tiny. And the people who sell travel are just as scattered — countless small agencies, sub-agents and tour operators in bazaars, high streets and back offices across dozens of countries.

That fragmentation creates a matching problem. A traveller in one country wants a room in another; the agent booking it has never heard of that specific hotel and has no way to check its price, availability or reliability. Multiply that by every hotel and every agent on the planet and you have chaos — which is exactly the gap a distributor fills.

The three ways travel gets distributed

  • Direct — you book on a hotel's own website or an airline's app. Efficient for big brands with strong websites, useless for the small independent hotel nobody can find.
  • Consumer online travel agencies (OTAs) — MakeMyTrip, Booking.com, Expedia. These sell to travellers directly. TBO is not one of these; it sits a layer behind them.
  • B2B distribution / bedbanks & aggregators — the wholesale layer that supplies inventory to the trade: to travel agents and tour operators, not to end-travellers. This is TBO's world. It aggregates supply from many hotels and airlines and redistributes it to many buyers through a single technology pipe.
Why the wholesale layer survives the internet

It is tempting to assume the internet should have deleted the middleman — why not let every small hotel connect to every small agent directly? Because the cost of building and maintaining hundreds of thousands of individual integrations, in different languages, currencies, tax regimes and payment systems, is prohibitive for a small player on either side. A distributor does that dirty work once and shares it. The small hotel in a second-tier town gets global demand it could never reach alone; the small agent gets a world of inventory through one login, one contract, one payment relationship. The distributor earns its keep by making a hard problem disappear — and the harder and more fragmented the market, the more valuable the plumbing.

TBO's chosen hunting ground is precisely the long tail — the independent hotels and the small, non-metro agents that the giant OTAs find too costly to service one by one. That is a large, under-digitised, slow-to-consolidate market, and it is where a patient aggregator can build durable relationships.

02

The business model: GTV × take-rate

Two numbers explain how a marketplace like this makes money. Learn them and the rest follows.

TBO's economics rest on two ideas. The first is GTV — Gross Transaction Value, the total rupee value of all the bookings that flow across the platform. The second is the take-rate — the share of that flow TBO keeps as its own revenue. Almost everything about the business is a story about growing the first and nudging up the second.

The simple revenue equation

Roughly: Revenue ≈ GTV × take-rate, and GTV ≈ number of active buyers × bookings per buyer × value per booking. So TBO can grow in several stacking ways at once — sign up more agents, get each agent to book more often, push higher-value products (a hotel stay is worth more per booking than a domestic flight), and add more countries. Because the platform's costs are largely fixed technology and people, once a booking is flowing the incremental cost of the next one is small — so revenue growth pulls profit up behind it. The whole model is a thin percentage applied to an ever-larger base.

03

The company and who controls it

From a small Delhi travel-tech firm to a listed global platform.

TBO was built by two travel entrepreneurs — Gaurav Bhatnagar and Ankush Nijhawan — who founded the business in 2006 and spent the better part of two decades assembling supply, buyers and technology before listing it on the Indian exchanges in May 2024. It is one of the more recent large IPOs on this list, so its public track record is still short — a point worth remembering when reading its valuation.

04

Air vs. non-air: where the money actually is

The most counter-intuitive fact about TBO — and the key to reading its results.

A newcomer would guess that a travel platform makes its money on flights, because flights are the biggest, most visible part of any trip. For TBO the opposite is true. Air ticketing is a huge, near-zero-margin business; the profit is concentrated in hotels and ancillaries. Understanding this split is the difference between reading TBO's numbers correctly and misreading them entirely.

Why air is thin and hotels are fat

  • Airlines set the price and keep the value. A flight is a commodity with a published fare; the airline controls it and pays distributors very little. Booking a ticket is high-volume but yields a tiny margin — sometimes barely more than the cost of processing it.
  • Hotels are fragmented and opaque. There is no single published price for a room in a small independent hotel; rates, availability and terms vary endlessly. That messiness is precisely where an aggregator adds value — and gets paid a real margin for sourcing, packaging and guaranteeing supply.
  • Ancillaries stack on top. Transfers, sightseeing, car rentals and insurance attach to a trip and carry their own margins, sweetening the non-air mix further.
Why TBO still bothers with loss-leader flights

If air barely makes money, why run it at all? Because flights are the habit that brings the agent back every day. An agent selling a package needs the flight, the hotel and the transfers together; the platform that can do the whole trip in one place wins the agent's routine. Air is the daily footfall — the reason an agent logs in — and once they are in the door, TBO cross-sells the profitable hotel and ancillary bookings alongside. So air is best understood not as a profit centre but as customer-acquisition and engagement that funds itself. The strategic aim is to keep the flights flowing while steadily lifting the share of high-margin hotels attached to them.

This is why "mix" is such a loaded word in every TBO result. When management says the hotel share of GTV rose, they are really saying the profit quality of the whole business improved — even if total GTV grew only modestly. A percentage point of mix shift toward hotels does more for the bottom line than several points of air-ticket growth.

05

The network-effect moat, in arithmetic

The defining force for TBO — why a marketplace, once built, is hard to unbuild.

TBO's real asset is not its software or even its supply contracts — it is the two-sided network those things create. This is the force an analyst leads with, because it is what separates a durable compounder from a replaceable intermediary. It is also easy to hand-wave about, so let's make it concrete with numbers.

The two-sided-add math

Suppose the platform has S suppliers and B buyers. The value of the network is roughly the number of useful connections it makes possible — on the order of S × B. Now add one more supplier. That single hotel doesn't just gain one customer; it becomes reachable by all B buyers at once — so the network's connection count rises by B, not by one. Symmetrically, adding one more agent makes them a potential customer for all S suppliers. So every single addition to either side increases the platform's usefulness by the size of the other side. With tens of thousands of buyers and over a million supply points, each new member joins a network that is already enormously valuable to them — and makes it a little more valuable to everyone else. That compounding is why a mature marketplace is so much more than the sum of its parts, and so painful for a newcomer to replicate from a standing start.

The same math explains the stickiness. A supplier that leaves TBO loses reach to tens of thousands of agents at once; an agent that leaves loses one-login access to a world of inventory. Neither side switches lightly, because the cost of leaving is the whole other side of the market. A rival can undercut on price for a while, but it cannot cheaply hand a new agent the supply depth — or hand a new hotel the demand reach — that TBO already has. The plumbing, once laid and full of water, is expensive to re-lay.

The toll-keeper, sized

Here is the non-transferable way to hold TBO in your head — a picture that fits no other company on this list:

TBO stands beside a river of roughly ₹36,800 crore of travel bookings a year. It owns none of the water — not one hotel room, not one aircraft seat. It simply operates the bridge everyone crosses, and dips a cup in as they pass. That cup came up with about ₹2,677 crore of revenue and ₹244 crore of profit in FY26. Widen the river (more GTV) or deepen the cup (a richer hotel mix, a slightly higher take), and the same bridge earns more — without TBO ever having to build a hotel.

07

The growth engines, stacked

Four levers that can compound at the same time — plus the acquisition question.

Because TBO's revenue is GTV times a take-rate, its growth levers are additive: several can fire together, and each one multiplies the others. This is what gives an asset-light marketplace its compounding potential — no factory to build, just more flow to route through the same pipe.

  • More buyers. Sign up more travel agents, especially internationally where TBO is still under-penetrated. Each new agent is a fresh, recurring source of bookings — and, per the network math, makes the platform worth more to every supplier.
  • More per buyer. Get existing agents to route a larger share of their bookings through TBO — deeper wallet-share as they trust the platform with more of their business.
  • Better mix. Shift GTV toward high-margin hotels and ancillaries and away from thin air tickets. This lifts gross profit even if total GTV grows only modestly — the single most powerful margin lever.
  • New geographies. Replicate the model in fresh countries, organically and through acquisitions, widening the river TBO tolls.

The acquisition strategy — buying reach

TBO has used tuck-in acquisitions to enter or deepen markets — buying a local distributor to instantly inherit its suppliers and agent relationships rather than building them from zero. In a network business this can be smart: an acquisition doesn't just add revenue, it plugs a whole sub-network into TBO's platform, and the two sides become more valuable together. It is a faster way to seed the network effect in a market where TBO would otherwise start cold.

The double edge of buying growth

Acquisitions are also where asset-light platforms can stumble. Each deal must be integrated — technology merged, teams retained, cultures blended, the acquired supply and agents actually migrated onto TBO's rails rather than left running in parallel. Pay too much, or fumble the integration, and an acquisition dilutes the very returns that make the model attractive. It also complicates the accounts: part of FY26's optically large revenue jump reflected acquired businesses being consolidated, which is why underlying GTV and gross-profit growth are the truer gauges. Acquisitions can accelerate the network flywheel — or quietly clog it. The record on integration is a thing to watch deal by deal.

The next chapters won't be about opening warehouses or plants — TBO has neither. They'll be about the hotel share of GTV climbing, the international agent count rising, and each new acquisition actually landing on the platform rather than beside it.

The industry and the rivals

A crowded middle — global bedbanks, airline programmes, GDS pipes, and the ever-present threat of going direct.

TBO is not alone in the middle of the travel trade. B2B travel distribution is a competitive layer with several well-funded incumbents, and the constant background threat that suppliers and buyers simply bypass the middleman altogether. Mapping the rivals shows both how contested the space is and where TBO's particular edge — the long-tail, emerging-market agent — actually sits.

Where TBO's edge actually lives

Against giants like Hotelbeds and Expedia, TBO does not win by being the biggest bedbank in Europe — it wins by being the most useful platform for the small, non-metro, emerging-market agent who is too much trouble for a global giant to court one by one. Serving thousands of tiny agents in dozens of countries is operationally messy: many languages, small ticket sizes, local payment quirks, hand-holding support. That messiness is a barrier the big players find unattractive — and a moat for the player that specialises in it. TBO's bet is that the long tail of world travel is enormous, durable and under-served, and that owning the agent relationship there is worth more than chasing the metropolitan volume the giants already fight over.

The dis-intermediation question: the permanent worry for any middleman is that both sides eventually connect directly and cut it out. For large branded hotels and airlines with strong direct channels, that pressure is real and ongoing. But for the independent hotel in a second-tier town and the small agent across a border, direct connection is exactly the expensive, impractical plumbing they can't build themselves — which is why the long-tail middle is the most defensible ground in travel distribution, and the ground TBO has chosen.

How to read the multiple

At roughly ₹15,500 Cr of market value on ~₹244 Cr of FY26 profit, TBO trades in the region of 60× or more trailing earnings (some screens show an even higher figure on a lower trailing base — treat the exact number as approximate). That is a rich, growth-platform valuation, and it prices in years of continued GTV compounding, mix improvement toward hotels, and successful international expansion. The question a buyer is really answering is not "is this a good business" — an asset-light, cash-generative network with a genuine moat usually is — but "how much of the next decade of that compounding is already in the price." A short public track record (listed only May 2024) and travel's proven cyclicality both argue for humility about paying up.

The one metric that settles the thesis: not revenue, and not any single quarter's take-rate — both are muddied by accounting and mix. Watch hotel-and-ancillary (non-air) GTV growth and the gross profit it carries, together with the international agent count. If non-air GTV keeps compounding faster than air, gross profit keeps outgrowing GTV, and international MTBs keep rising, the durable-network-compounder case is being proven in the numbers. If non-air mix stalls, gross-margin flattens, and international growth fades back toward the Indian base, the premium multiple has nothing left to justify it. That triplet — non-air GTV, gross profit, international buyers — is the scoreboard.
10

The risks, laid out plainly

A high-quality model is not a risk-free one — here is what can go wrong.

TBO's risks are specific to a global, thin-margin, credit-extending travel intermediary. None is hypothetical; several have already bitten the industry within living memory.

  • Travel cyclicality and shocks. This is the biggest one, and it is not theoretical. A pandemic, a recession, an oil spike or a regional conflict hits travel volumes hard and fast — COVID-19 froze the entire industry and nearly took TBO's flow to zero. A marketplace with fixed costs and a thin toll is highly geared to volume, so a demand shock hurts disproportionately. Travel always comes back, but the timing is out of anyone's control.
  • Take-rate and competitive pressure. TBO keeps a small slice; well-funded rivals (Hotelbeds/HBX, WebBeds, Expedia's B2B arms) and supplier direct-connects can compete that slice thinner. A structural squeeze on the take-rate would undercut the whole GTV-times-a-percentage model.
  • Concentration and foreign exchange. A global business earns and pays in many currencies; swings in the rupee against the euro, dollar and dirham move reported results independent of the underlying bookings. Regional or supplier concentration in any one market adds to the volatility.
  • Acquisition and integration risk. Growth-by-acquisition can dilute returns if deals are overpriced or poorly integrated — and it clouds the accounts, making the underlying trend harder to read.
  • Technology and payment / credit risk. The platform lives or dies on uptime and trust; an outage or a security breach is an existential kind of embarrassment for a marketplace. And crucially, TBO extends credit to its agents — it lets them book now and settle later. That working-capital service is part of why agents are loyal, but it exposes TBO to defaults, especially if a downturn strains the small operators it serves.
  • Rich valuation. Even if everything operational goes right, a 60×-plus multiple sets a high bar: the stock can de-rate on any wobble in growth, mix or margin, quite apart from how the business itself performs.
The honest framing of the debate: the argument over TBO comes down to two readings of the same facts. One sees a durable, network-effect compounder quietly organising the vast, fragmented, under-digitised long tail of global travel — a rare asset-light business with a real moat and a decade of runway. The other sees a cyclical, thin-take-rate intermediary, exposed to travel shocks and dis-intermediation, that happens to be priced richly on a short public record. Which reading wins will be settled not by rhetoric but by the non-air GTV, gross-profit and international-agent numbers printing quarter after quarter.
11

The future, the bull, and the bear

A genuinely good business model, wrapped in travel's cyclicality and a premium price.

TBO is one of the cleaner expressions of the asset-light-marketplace idea on the Indian market: no hotels, no aircraft, a thin toll on a huge and fragmented flow, and a two-sided network that grows more valuable with every member added. The debate is about durability and price, not whether the model works — it plainly does.

The bull case

  • Network-effect moat — a two-sided marketplace that gets stickier and more valuable with every supplier and agent, hard to displace once full.
  • Asset-light compounding — no fixed plant; profit should grow faster than GTV as tech costs spread over more flow.
  • Mix tailwind — hotels and ancillaries taking a larger share lifts profit quality even at modest GTV growth.
  • Global, long runway — 100-plus countries, international buyers outgrowing the Indian base, a vast under-digitised long tail still to onboard.
  • Founder-operators, net cash — aligned promoters who know the trade, funding growth and deals without leverage.

The bear case

  • Travel is cyclical and shock-prone — COVID proved how fast the flow can vanish; a thin-margin, fixed-cost tollbooth is highly geared to volume.
  • Take-rate under pressure — big bedbanks, Expedia's B2B arms and supplier direct-connects can grind the slice thinner.
  • Integration & accounting noise — acquisition-led growth can dilute returns and muddy the underlying trend.
  • Credit & FX exposure — extending credit to small agents invites defaults in a downturn; multi-currency earnings add volatility.
  • Premium multiple, short record — 60×-plus on a business listed only in 2024 leaves the stock exposed to any stumble in growth or mix.
The bottom line

TBO is a real network business doing a genuinely hard thing — organising the messy, borderless long tail of world travel and taking a modest, recurring cut of the traffic it routes. The model's quality is not in serious doubt; its durability through a travel cycle and the price being paid for it are. The story over the coming years will be told in a specific triplet, printed every quarter: the growth of non-air (hotel & ancillary) GTV, the gross profit it carries, and the count of international agents booking each month. Keep those three climbing together and the compounding is real and the moat is widening; let them drift — hotel mix stalling, gross-margin flattening, international growth fading back toward India — and a richly valued tollbooth on a cyclical river has little to fall back on.
