Vedant Fashions, the wedding-wear franchise machine

A complete walk through the company behind Manyavar — India's leading branded men's wedding and celebration wear — and its sister brands Mohey, Twamev, Manthan and Mebaz. Why an asset-light franchise model lets a clothing retailer earn margins a soap or software company would envy, how it monetises the vast Indian wedding without paying for the stores, and why the whole debate now is one word: growth.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Vedant Fashions Limited is the company behind Manyavar — the country's best-known branded name in men's Indian wedding and celebration wear — plus a stable of sister brands: Mohey (women's ethnic), Twamev (premium), Manthan (value) and Mebaz (a southern regional label it acquired). It dresses the Indian groom, the wedding guest and the festival-goer in kurtas, sherwanis, indo-westerns, lehengas and saris, sold through a national web of nearly 670 exclusive-brand stores. It listed in February 2022 and remains firmly controlled by its founder, Ravi Modi.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It barely owns its own shops — and that is the whole point. The overwhelming majority of Manyavar stores are franchisee-owned and franchisee-operated (FOFO): an outside entrepreneur puts up the money for the store, the fit-out, the rent and the staff; Vedant supplies the product, the brand, the store design and the marketing. Vedant therefore runs a huge retail footprint while carrying almost none of a retailer's fixed costs — which is why it earns margins that look nothing like clothing retail.
  • The economics are extraordinary for apparel. In FY26 Vedant turned ~₹1,436 Cr of revenue into a ~65.7% gross margin, a ~44% EBITDA margin and ~₹376 Cr of net profit — a ~26% net margin, at a return on capital in the mid-20s-to-30s percent. Those are consumer-staples-and-software numbers, produced by a business that sells festive clothes.
  • The debate is no longer margins — it is growth. Here is the catch. Revenue has been remarkably flat since FY23 (₹1,355 Cr then, ₹1,436 Cr now), profit is slightly below its FY23 peak, and the store count has plateaued. A brilliant, cash-rich, high-margin franchise has run into a growth air-pocket — subdued consumer sentiment, uneven wedding-date calendars, and the sheer maturity of its core. Yet it still trades at ~27× earnings. The stock is a bet that the machine re-accelerates.
In plain terms

Vedant Fashions has done something unusual: it turned a clothing chain into something closer to a brand-and-design licensing operation. It designs the sherwani, prints the marketing, and dictates how the store looks — then hands the actual shop to a franchisee who funds it and runs it. The customer pays a full retail price at a Manyavar store; the franchisee keeps a slice for carrying the store; Vedant books the rest, and because it never paid for the rent, the fit-out or the shop staff, almost all of that flows to profit. The result is an apparel company with the margins and cash generation of a far "lighter" business. The one thing this beautiful model cannot manufacture on its own is faster sales — and that, right now, is exactly what it is short of.

01

The category: dressing the Indian wedding

Why "celebration wear" is a large, resilient, and quietly premiumising market.

To understand Vedant you first have to understand the occasion it sells into. India hosts an estimated ~10 million weddings a year, each one a multi-day, multi-outfit, multi-guest event on which families spend a strikingly large share of their savings — often more than on any purchase bar a house. Add the festival calendar (Diwali, Eid, Durga Puja, Navratri, Karva Chauth) and a stream of engagements, receptions and family functions, and you have a deep, recurring demand for ethnic celebration wear that is largely non-discretionary at the moment of need: you cannot turn up to your own wedding underdressed.

Historically this market was served almost entirely by the unorganised trade — the local tailor who stitched a sherwani to measure, and the neighbourhood ethnic-wear shop with no brand, no fixed pricing and no guarantee of fit or finish. Manyavar's entire rise is the story of a branded, ready-to-wear, fixed-price alternative displacing that fragmented base: a customer walks in, finds a designed, quality-checked, consistently-sized outfit on the rack, and walks out the same day. That shift — from unbranded tailoring to a trusted national brand — is the structural tailwind under the whole business.

Why weddings make a good business — and a lumpy one

The wedding occasion has two faces for an investor. On the bright side it is resilient and premiumising: families defer many things in a downturn, but rarely their daughter's wedding, and as incomes rise they trade the local tailor for a brand and buy a more expensive outfit. On the awkward side it is lumpy and calendar-driven: the number of astrologically-auspicious wedding dates (muhurats) varies materially from year to year, so a quarter or even a full year can be soft simply because the Hindu calendar handed out fewer wedding days — through no fault of the company. Vedant's sales therefore wobble with a calendar it does not control, which is one reason its quarter-to-quarter numbers can look erratic even when the franchise is perfectly healthy.

02

The asset-light machine: how Vedant runs 670 shops it doesn't fund

The single most important structural fact about this company — grasp it and the margins stop looking impossible.

A normal apparel chain that runs 670 stores signs 670 leases, funds 670 fit-outs, pays 670 stores' worth of rent and staff, and carries all of that as fixed cost on its own balance sheet. When sales dip, those costs stay — which is why fashion retail is a famously thin-margin, operationally-geared business. Vedant does something structurally different: it pushes almost all of that cost off its own books and onto its franchisees. This is the engine of the entire investment case.

The two store models

  • FOFO — Franchisee-Owned, Franchisee-Operated — the dominant model. An independent business owner funds the store: the property/lease, the interior fit-out (which can run into crores for a large flagship), the working capital and the store staff. Vedant supplies the merchandise, the store design, the brand and the national advertising. The franchisee retains a commission on retail sales (reported around the ~29–30% range) for carrying the store; Vedant keeps the rest. Crucially, Vedant's capital is barely in the store at all.
  • COFO — Company-Owned, Franchisee-Operated — a smaller slice. Here Vedant takes on the store's lease/space itself but the franchisee still operates it, earning a lower commission (around ~18–19%). This model is used selectively — for flagship or high-street locations Vedant wants to secure directly.
  • Manufacturing is outsourced too — the asset-light philosophy runs all the way back. Vedant designs in-house (its real intellectual property) but manufactures through a network of 400+ third-party vendors and artisans. It owns the brand and the design; it does not own factories full of tailors.

Vedant sits at the two ends that scale cheaply — brand/design and the high-margin cut of the sale — and hands the two capital-hungry middles (factories, stores) to others. That is the whole trick.

Why does an entrepreneur happily fund a Manyavar store? Because Manyavar's brand pull and disciplined merchandising make the store a genuinely attractive, relatively low-risk investment: a proven, occasion-driven brand with national advertising behind it, tight inventory control that limits dead stock, and a fit that customers trust. In effect Vedant sells not just clothes but a franchise proposition — and a strong brand is what makes that proposition sell. The franchisee gets a bankable format; Vedant gets store expansion funded by other people's balance sheets.

The one construct that captures this company: a brand company wearing a retailer's clothes

Read Vedant's economics through this single lens: it is really a brand-and-design house that has outsourced both ends of the physical chain — factories to vendors, stores to franchisees — and kept for itself only the two things that carry almost no capital and almost all the value: owning the brand and owning the design. A conventional retailer's profit is what survives after rent, fit-out depreciation and store payroll; Vedant has arranged for someone else to bear those, so its profit is what survives after almost nothing. That is why a business selling sherwanis posts a ~44% EBITDA margin — a figure that would be arithmetically impossible if Vedant paid for the 670 stores its brand appears above.

03

The brand ladder: from Manyavar to Mohey and beyond

One dominant brand, and a portfolio built to widen the addressable occasion.

Vedant is, for now, overwhelmingly a one-brand company — Manyavar is the engine — but management has deliberately built a ladder of brands to reach more of the wedding party, more of the price spectrum, and more of the country. Understanding the portfolio is understanding where growth is supposed to come from.

The brand portfolio and what each is for
BrandWho it dressesRole in the story
ManyavarMen — the mid-premium core; sherwanis, kurtas, indo-westerns for grooms and guestsThe flagship and the vast majority of revenue. The name the whole company is known by; the proof that a national ethnic-wear brand can be built.
MoheyWomen — lehengas, saris, gowns; often within Manyavar stores and standalone EBOsThe single biggest growth option. Women's celebration wear is a larger spend than men's; if Mohey works, it roughly doubles the addressable wedding.

MebazRegional — a South-India (Telangana/Andhra) ethnic label Vedant acquiredBuys instant regional depth and a family-wear range; a bolt-on into a market with its own tastes.

The logic of the ladder is to grow without cannibalising. Manyavar owns the mid-premium male core. Twamev reaches up to a customer who wants something more exclusive; Manthan reaches down to one who wants a sharper price. Mebaz reaches sideways into a region with distinct preferences. And Mohey reaches across the aisle — to the bride, the mother, the sister and the guest — into a women's celebration-wear spend that is, in aggregate, larger than the men's market Manyavar already leads.

Why Mohey is the number to watch on the portfolio

Manyavar's dominance of men's wedding wear is also its ceiling: it already leads a market it has largely organised, so its own growth increasingly depends on the calendar and on premiumisation rather than on grabbing more men. The genuinely large untapped prize is women's celebration wear, where the branded penetration is far lower and the spend per wedding is higher — but where competition is fiercer, tastes are more fragmented, and "repeat-wear" resistance (a bride does not want to be seen twice in the same lehenga) makes the design and inventory problem harder. Mohey is Vedant's attempt to run the same asset-light, branded playbook on that bigger, harder market. How fast Mohey scales is, more than any other single lever, what decides whether Vedant is a mature single-brand story or a multi-decade compounder.

04

The company, the founder, and who controls it

A founder-built brand, still founder-controlled, that IPO'd as a rare profitable listing.

Vedant Fashions is unusual among recent Indian listings in that it is not a venture-funded, loss-making growth story dressed up for an IPO — it is a founder-owned, highly profitable, cash-generative brand that had been built patiently over two decades before it ever sold a share to the public. Understanding the founder and the ownership explains both its discipline and its texture.

Timeline

  • 1999
    Ravi Modi founds the business in Kolkata, building on a family cloth-trading background, and launches the Manyavar brand for men's ethnic wear.
  • 2000s
    Manyavar pioneers branded, ready-to-wear Indian celebration wear and rolls out an exclusive-brand-outlet (EBO) franchise network — displacing the local tailor with a fixed-price, consistent-fit national brand.
  • 2010s
    Portfolio widens: Mohey (women's), Twamev (premium) and Manthan (value) are added; the brand leans hard into celebrity and IPL-scale marketing to cement its wedding-wear ownership.
  • Feb 2022
    IPO on NSE & BSE (BSE scrip 543463; NSE symbol MANYAVAR) — a pure offer-for-sale: existing holders sold down; the company raised no fresh money because it did not need any.
  • FY23
    Post-pandemic revenue peak — ~₹1,355 Cr and ~₹429 Cr profit as weddings and festivals came roaring back.
  • FY24–FY26
    A growth plateau: revenue drifts sideways (~₹1,368 → ~₹1,436 Cr), profit eases below the FY23 peak, and the store network stops expanding much — subdued sentiment and thin wedding-date calendars bite. Margins stay elite; growth does not.

Ownership & structure

  • Founder control: Ravi Modi (with the Ravi Modi Family Trust and family) remains the dominant promoter, holding a large controlling stake — this is a founder-run business, not a professionally-diluted one. The founder's family wealth is overwhelmingly this single company, which aligns them tightly with the share price.
  • An offer-for-sale listing: the 2022 IPO raised no primary capital — a deliberate signal that the business self-funds its (modest) capital needs from its own cash flows. Private-equity backers and the promoter sold part of their holdings; the company's balance sheet was untouched.
  • A cash-rich balance sheet: because the model consumes so little capital, Vedant accumulates cash and pays generous dividends (a ₹7.75/share final dividend recommended for FY26 on a ₹1 face value). It has no need for debt to grow.
Why the ownership shape matters: a founder who has built the brand over 25 years and whose fortune rides on it tends to run a tight, brand-protective, discipline-first operation — which shows up in the margins and the low capital intensity. The flip side of a dominant founder and a near-single-brand company is key-person and single-brand concentration: the strategy, the capital allocation (buy back? diversify? sit on cash?) and the brand's positioning all rest heavily on one person's judgement. Minority investors are, in effect, backing Ravi Modi's continued stewardship of the Manyavar name.
05

Two revenue numbers, and the gap between them

The most illuminating single idea in the accounts — and it exists because the model is asset-light.

If you only ever learn one thing about reading Vedant's numbers, make it this: the company reports two different "sales" figures, and the distance between them is the franchise model made visible. One is what customers pay across the whole network — the system-wide retail sales, which crossed ~₹2,000 Cr in FY26. The other is what Vedant itself books as revenue — ~₹1,436 Cr. The difference is not lost money; it is the franchisees' share, the reward for owning and running the stores Vedant does not.

Now sit with what that gap buys Vedant. That ~₹614 Cr the franchisees keep is, in effect, the fee for taking every store's fixed cost and capital off Vedant's balance sheet. A conventional retailer would "keep" that slice as revenue — and then hand most of it straight back out as rent, store depreciation and payroll, ending up with a thin margin. Vedant hands the slice away at the top and, in exchange, its remaining ~₹1,436 Cr is almost pure of store costs — so an enormous share of it survives to EBITDA. The two-number gap and the fat margin are the same fact seen from two ends.

Why the gap is a feature, not a leakage

It is tempting to look at the ₹2,050 Cr customers pay and the ₹1,436 Cr Vedant books and see "lost" revenue. That reads it backwards. The franchisee's ~30% is precisely what a normal retailer doesn't get to keep either — it is the true economic cost of running physical stores, simply paid to a partner instead of appearing as Vedant's own rent-and-payroll line. By routing it to franchisees, Vedant converts a variable stream of someone else's fixed costs into a clean, high-margin wholesale-style top line for itself. The company grows its retail footprint using franchisees' capital while keeping its own reported P&L light. Watch both numbers each year: retail sales tell you how the brand is really selling to consumers; booked revenue tells you what Vedant keeps.

06

Apparel with software-like economics

The genuinely surprising fact: a sherwani retailer that prints cash like an asset-light platform.

Here is the fact that surprises almost everyone the first time they see this P&L. Clothing retail is supposed to be a grind — low margins, high fixed costs, brutal markdowns, fashion risk. Vedant is the opposite: it converts a strikingly high share of every rupee it books into cash, throws off far more profit than it needs to reinvest, and sits on a growing pile of surplus cash. The margins belong to a different industry entirely.

A ~44% EBITDA margin and ~26% net margin are staples-and-software territory — remarkable for a business that sells festive clothing. The reason is section 02: Vedant does not carry the store costs that flatten a normal retailer's margins.

The rarest part is not even the margin — it is the return on capital alongside a fortress balance sheet. Because franchisees fund the stores and vendors fund the factories, Vedant needs very little capital of its own to run and grow, so its return on that small capital base is high (reported returns on capital have sat in the mid-20s to low-30s percent, depending on how one treats the cash). And because it generates far more cash than it can reinvest in such a light model, it hoards surplus cash and pays it out — the ₹7.75/share FY26 dividend is a symptom of a company that simply cannot spend all it earns.

The surprise, and the sting in its tail

The surprise is real: this is one of the most profitable, least capital-hungry consumer businesses on the Indian market, and it sells wedding clothes. But the same lightness that produces those returns creates a subtle problem. A business that needs almost no capital to run also has almost nowhere obvious to reinvest its cash at those same returns — you can only open so many ethnic-wear stores in a maturing market. So the cash builds up, dragging on the return ratios (a big idle cash pile lowers reported return on total equity even as the operating business stays superb), and management faces the harder question of what to do with it: buy growth (Mohey, Mebaz), return it (dividends, buybacks), or sit on it. Elite economics with limited reinvestment runway is a nicer problem than most — but it is a real one, and it is why "what's the growth?" has replaced "what's the margin?" as the central question.

07

The growth engine — and why it stalled

The uncomfortable core of the current story: a superb model that has stopped compounding.

For a retailer, growth comes from two places: opening more stores (adding retail area) and selling more from each existing store (same-store-sales growth). Vedant's issue over FY24–FY26 is that both dials have gone quiet at once — the store count has plateaued and like-to-like growth has been low-single-digit — which is why a business with world-class margins has posted flat revenue for three years.

What choked the growth? Three things at once. Weak discretionary sentiment across mid-market consumption crimped store footfall and conversion. Thin wedding-date calendars in some quarters simply meant fewer big-ticket occasions. And the maturity of the core — Manyavar already leads organised men's wedding wear — means the easy store-expansion runway of the early years has narrowed. The green shoots are that Q1 FY27 showed domestic same-store-sales growth back to ~3.8%, profit up ~15%, and margins holding near 44–45% — a firmer start, but still a long way from the double-digit compounding the valuation implicitly wants.

The lever that has to reawaken: same-store-sales growth

When a chain has largely finished its easy store-opening phase, its growth has to come increasingly from selling more out of the stores it already has — richer product, higher average tickets, more categories per store (women's Mohey inside a Manyavar shop), better conversion. That is why same-store-sales growth is now the single most important operating number Vedant reports. Store additions can still help at the margin, and premiumisation lifts the ticket, but the flat FY24–FY26 stretch was fundamentally a same-store problem — the existing base stopped growing. The FY27 recovery in that number is the first thing a bull has to see continue.

08

The industry and the rivals

A fragmented market with one clear branded leader — and rising organised competition.

Vedant's competitive position is genuinely strong in its core: in branded men's Indian wedding wear, Manyavar has no rival of comparable national scale, brand recall or store network. But "branded" is the operative word — its real competition is less another listed apparel company and more the vast unorganised trade it has spent two decades converting, plus a growing set of organised players circling the edges of ethnic and celebration wear.

Vedant competes from a position of unusual financial strength, but the easy battles are behind it and the contested ones are ahead.

09

The risk stack

What can go wrong when you sell a discretionary occasion at a premium multiple.

Vedant's risks are not, for the most part, about the quality of the business — the model and the margins are sound. They are about demand, dependence and price: whether the occasion-driven top line grows, whether the single brand and single founder keep delivering, and whether a rich valuation leaves room for the growth wobble the last three years have shown is real.

The risk that ties the others together

Notice how the risks interlock around one pivot: growth against price. The business quality is not really in question — few consumer companies match its margins or capital efficiency. What is in question is whether an occasion-driven, largely single-brand, largely-mature franchise can grow fast enough to justify a premium multiple, when the last three years delivered flat revenue and the reacceleration depends on levers (women's wear, premiumisation, sentiment) that are either contested or outside its control. A cheaper stock could absorb a few more flat years comfortably; at ~27× earnings, the market is implicitly paying for the growth to come back. That is the tension every other risk feeds into.

10

Financials & valuation

Elite quality, flat growth, premium price — a stock whose whole case rests on one number reawakening.

Vedant's financials are a study in contrast: the quality metrics (gross margin, EBITDA margin, return on capital, cash generation) are exceptional and steady; the growth metrics (revenue, profit, store count) have been flat-to-soft for three years. The valuation — around 27× trailing earnings, well above a typical retailer — is not pricing the quality alone; it is pricing an expectation that the growth returns. So the analytical job here is narrow and specific: decide whether that reacceleration is coming.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A magnificent machine asking the one question it can't answer with margins: can it grow?

Vedant Fashions enters FY27 with everything a business could want except momentum: a dominant brand, elite margins, a fortress balance sheet, a founder aligned to the last share, and a franchise model that grows retail footprint on other people's capital. What it has lacked for three years is growth — and the entire investment debate now turns on whether the Q1 FY27 firming is the start of a reacceleration or a pause in a plateau.

The bull case

  • Best-in-class economics — ~44% EBITDA and ~26% net margins with mid-20s%+ returns on capital; an asset-light model few apparel names anywhere can match.
  • Structural conversion runway — the unorganised trade is still the biggest share of ethnic wear; every tailor-to-Manyavar switch is durable share gained.
  • The Mohey option — women's celebration wear is a larger spend than men's; if Mohey scales on the same model, it materially widens the runway.
  • Cash machine, aligned owner — surplus cash, generous dividends, no debt, and a founder whose fortune is the share price.
  • Early reacceleration signs — Q1 FY27 domestic SSSG back to ~3.8% and profit up ~15%, with margins intact — the first evidence the stall may be ending.

The bear case

  • Three flat years — revenue barely grew FY23→FY26 and profit slipped below its FY23 peak; the compounding thesis is, so far, unproven post-reopening.
  • Single-brand, single-founder concentration — Manyavar is almost the whole company; any brand or key-person stumble is systemic.
  • Contested frontier — the growth prizes (women's, premium) are exactly where organised competition (ABFRL, designers) is thickest.
  • Occasion cyclicality — discretionary spend on an uneven wedding-date calendar makes the top line lumpy and partly out of the company's hands.
  • Premium price on flat growth — ~27× earnings implicitly pays for a reacceleration that hasn't yet arrived; disappoint, and the multiple can de-rate even if margins hold.
The bottom line

Vedant Fashions is one of the finest business models on the Indian consumer market — a brand-and-design house that dressed the Indian wedding and let franchisees pay for the shops, earning margins its industry is not supposed to produce. Nothing about that quality is in doubt. The doubt is arithmetic: a company this good has grown its top line barely 2% a year for three years, and the market is still paying a premium multiple for the years when it grew faster. The next test is concrete and datable — whether same-store-sales growth builds on the Q1 FY27 turn and holds mid-single-digit-or-better through the FY27 wedding seasons, while Mohey's women's rollout finally starts moving the needle. Sustain that, and a superb franchise is compounding again off an elite base; stay flat, and investors own a magnificent, cash-rich business that has, for now, simply stopped getting bigger — priced as though it hasn't.
