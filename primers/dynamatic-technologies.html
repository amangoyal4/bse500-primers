Dynamatic Technologies, tractor pumps to Airbus wings

A plain-English walk through one of India's most unusual engineering companies — a firm that pumps the hydraulics inside most of the country's tractors and, in the same breath, is the sole source of a flight-critical part on every Airbus A320 in the sky. How it makes money across aerospace, hydraulics and metallurgy, why the whole investment case turns on a single ratio buried in the balance sheet, and what happens as a global jet-building boom collides with a heavy pile of debt.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Dynamatic Technologies is a Bengaluru-based high-precision engineering company that machines, forges, casts and assembles complex metal parts to tolerances measured in microns. It runs three very different businesses under one name: aerospace (building wing, fuselage and pylon structures for Airbus, Boeing, Bell and Indian defence), hydraulics (gear pumps and systems for tractors and off-highway machines) and metallurgy (turbocharger housings and precision castings for the auto industry). What ties them together is not the end-market but the skill: making hard metal do exact things, reliably, at scale, for customers who cannot tolerate a defect.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Aerospace is the story; the other two pay the bills. Aerospace is now the largest and fastest-growing segment — ~48% of FY26 revenue and growing ~28% a year — while hydraulics (~30%) and metallurgy (~22%) are steadier, more cyclical, cash-generating bases. The bet is a steady mix-shift toward higher-value aerospace work, where a single part can take years to qualify and, once qualified, sells for the life of an aircraft programme.
  • It is climbing the value chain, not just growing. Dynamatic began as a build-to-print shop — you send the drawing, it makes the part. It is deliberately moving toward build-to-spec and full design-and-assembly work, where it owns the engineering. The clearest proof: it re-engineered the 40-year-old Airbus A320 flap-track beam from ~200 parts down to ~20, and it is the single global source of that assembly. Owning the design, not just the drawing, is where the margin lives.
  • The balance sheet is the hinge. Revenue grew 15.5% in FY26 — yet net profit fell ~25% to ₹32 Cr, because roughly ₹638 Cr of borrowings and a heavy working-capital cycle sit on top of a thin-margin business. Net leverage has been stuck near 3.4× net-debt-to-EBITDA for years. The stock trades near 130× earnings — pricing in an aerospace ramp that lifts margins and a deleveraging that finally lets the profit through.
In plain terms

Dynamatic is a contract engineer that turns raw metal into flight-critical hardware. Picture three workshops sharing one set of hands: one builds tractor pumps by the thousand, one casts turbo housings for car engines, and one — the prized one — assembles the intricate wing and fuselage structures that Airbus and Boeing bolt onto jets rolling off the line at record rates. The prize workshop is winning more and better work every year. The catch is that the whole company carries a debt load big enough that fast top-line growth still leaves only a sliver of profit. The investment question is simple to state and hard to answer: can the aerospace mix and a lighter balance sheet arrive fast enough to turn all that revenue into real earnings?

01

Aerostructures 101: what a company like this actually makes

No engineering degree needed — just the handful of ideas that explain the money.

A modern airliner is not built by one company. Airbus and Boeing are really integrators: they design the aircraft, then buy tens of thousands of parts and sub-assemblies from a global pyramid of suppliers, and put them together. Dynamatic sits inside that pyramid, making the metal structures — called aerostructures — that carry loads and moving parts on the airframe. To follow the business, you need five ideas.

The five ideas that run this business
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
AerostructureA load-bearing metal structure on an aircraft — a flap-track beam, a pylon, a fuselage panel, a door. Not the engine or electronics; the precision-machined "bones" that hold the aircraft together and let control surfaces move. Dynamatic's marquee product line.
Build-to-printThe customer hands you a finished drawing; you manufacture exactly to it. Lower risk, lower margin, easily re-sourced. Where Dynamatic started, and still a big share of the work.
Build-to-spec / design partnerThe customer hands you a requirement; you engineer the part and own the design. Far stickier, higher-margin, and much harder to move to a rival. Where Dynamatic is trying to go — and partly already is.
QualificationBefore a part flies, it must pass years of testing and audits to be certified for a specific aircraft programme. Slow and expensive to earn — but once you hold it, you are effectively locked in for the programme's multi-decade life. A moat made of paperwork and trust.
ShipsetThe full set of a given part needed for one aircraft. Revenue in aerospace is often counted in shipsets per month × the price per shipset. Ramp the rate, and revenue compounds without winning a single new programme.
The economics that decide everything: the qualification lock

An aerospace part is nothing like an auto part. A car component can be re-sourced to a cheaper vendor next quarter; a flight-critical aerostructure cannot. To put a part on an Airbus wing, a supplier spends years proving the material, the process and the quality system, and the airframer certifies that exact supplier on that exact programme. Switching later means re-qualifying — so airframers almost never do it. That is why a single-source position on a long-lived programme is so valuable: the price is negotiated hard, but the volume is near-guaranteed for the twenty-plus years the aircraft is built and supported. Dynamatic's whole aerospace strategy is to accumulate these locks, then push the rate on each one higher.

Who buys from a firm like Dynamatic

  • Global airframers & Tier-1s — Airbus, Boeing, and the big first-tier suppliers (Spirit AeroSystems, Bell) that sit between the airframer and the smaller shops. Dynamatic supplies both directly and through these Tier-1s.
  • Defence & PSUs — Hindustan Aeronautics (HAL) and Indian defence programmes (Sukhoi work, the Deesa facility), plus emerging platforms where the government wants a domestic supply chain rather than imports.
  • Off-highway & auto OEMs — for the hydraulics and metallurgy arms: tractor makers, off-highway equipment firms, and passenger-vehicle engine programmes that need turbocharger housings and exhaust castings.
02

The segment map — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Dynamatic reports in three segments, and they could almost be three different companies. They share a metallurgy-and-machining DNA and a management, but their customers, cycles and margins diverge sharply. Understanding the mix — and how it is shifting — is most of the analysis.

The growth is as lopsided as the mix. In FY26 aerospace grew ~27.6%, while hydraulics (~6.4%) and metallurgy (~6.9%) grew at barely a quarter of that pace. The three engines, decoded

  • Aerospace — the marquee. Complex aerostructures and assemblies for global OEMs: Airbus (A320-family flap-track beams, pylons), Boeing (aft-pylon and cabin structures), Bell, and Tier-1 Spirit AeroSystems, plus Indian defence work with HAL and Sukhoi-related programmes and the Deesa facility. Long qualification cycles, single-source positions, and the highest strategic value in the group.
  • Hydraulics — the cash base. Gear pumps and hydraulic systems for tractors and off-highway equipment, where Dynamatic is a leading supplier — with a commanding share of India's tractor-pump market and a meaningful global position, plus exports. Cyclical with the farm economy, but a dependable generator of cash and machining know-how.
  • Metallurgy — the foundry arm. Precision castings and machined components — turbocharger housings, exhaust and thermal parts for the auto industry — made partly in advanced European foundries. The most cyclical, auto-tied leg, now being deliberately re-pointed toward higher-value aerospace and defence castings.
Why three unlike businesses actually belong together

The instinct is to see a scattered conglomerate. The tighter reading is that all three are the same capability — turning metal into exact, certified, load-bearing parts — pointed at different customers. Hydraulics taught the company high-volume precision machining; metallurgy gave it foundry and casting depth in advanced materials; aerospace demands both, at the highest quality tier that exists. So the older businesses are not just cash cows; they are the training ground and the metallurgical toolkit that the aerospace arm draws on. When Dynamatic talks about redirecting foundry capacity from car turbos toward aircraft and defence castings, that is the flywheel turning: skills earned in the cyclical businesses being reinvested up the value ladder.

03

The company, the founder, and the global footprint

A founder-run engineering house with factories on two continents.

Dynamatic is closely identified with its founder and Chairman-Managing-Director, Dr Udayant Malhoutra, who has run the company for decades and is the architect of its push from humble hydraulics into global aerospace. It is not a sprawling promoter group; it is one operator's long bet that an Indian engineering firm could earn a seat at the top table of aircraft manufacturing.

  • 2010s

  • FY24–FY25

  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter: the founding family, led by Dr Udayant Malhoutra, holds roughly 41–42% (about 41.5% as of mid-2026). A founder-CMD with a long tenure — a strength for strategic continuity, but it also means the company's direction rides heavily on one person.
  • Global operations: manufacturing in India (Bengaluru and other sites), the UK (Oldland Aerospace at Bristol/Swindon), and Germany (foundry). Roughly a thousand-plus engineers and scientists across dedicated R&D centres — the design muscle behind the build-to-spec ambition.
  • Balance sheet: the persistent tension in the story. Consolidated borrowings of roughly ₹638 Cr (net debt around ₹530–540 Cr), a debt-to-equity near 0.8×, and net leverage that has hovered near 3.4× net-debt-to-EBITDA for several years — high for a business with single-digit net margins.
Why the global footprint cuts both ways: owning UK and German aerospace and foundry operations gives Dynamatic certified capability, proximity to European airframers, and a foothold in advanced-material castings that would take years to build from scratch in India. It also imports European cost structures and currency exposure, and means the group's fortunes are tied to how efficiently those overseas plants run — not only to India's low-cost engineering advantage.
04

The flap-track beam and the climb up the value chain

The single most important idea about what makes Dynamatic special — and it is unique to this company.

If you remember one thing about Dynamatic's aerospace arm, make it this: the money is not in making more parts, it is in owning more of the engineering. The clearest illustration is the part the company is most known for — the flap-track beam on the Airbus A320 family.

The flap-track beam is the structure that guides an aircraft's wing flaps as they extend and retract on take-off and landing — flight-critical hardware that carries big loads and cannot fail. Dynamatic, through its UK Oldland Aerospace operation, is the single-source manufacturer of flap-track-beam assemblies for the entire Airbus single-aisle family (A318, A319, A320, A321), supplied via Tier-1 Spirit AeroSystems, and a Tier-1 supplier of the equivalent beam on the wide-body A330. It has delivered well over 13,000 shipsets with a near-perfect on-time record. That single-source lock on the world's best-selling jet family is the anchor of the whole aerospace business.

Dynamatic re-engineered a four-decade-old assembly from roughly 200 parts to about 20, using a more monolithic structure — fewer joints, fewer fasteners, less weight, less assembly labour. Crucially, doing that redesign moved the company from making Airbus's drawing to owning the design — the leap from build-to-print to design partner.
The device that only fits Dynamatic: the same beam, worth more each rung it climbs

Watch one part travel up the value ladder and you have the entire thesis. As a build-to-print flap-track beam, Dynamatic simply executed Airbus's decades-old drawing — replaceable in principle, priced like a machine shop. By re-engineering that same beam from ~200 parts to ~20, it became the design authority on a flight-critical structure for the best-selling aircraft family on earth: single-source, near-impossible to switch, and paid for the engineering, not just the metal. Nothing about the aircraft changed. What changed is who owns the intelligence in the part — and that is the difference between a contract machinist's margin and an aerospace design house's margin. Every new programme win (A220 doors, Falcon 6X fuselage, Chinook pylon) is an attempt to repeat that climb on another part.

The pipeline of climbs

  • A220 doors — Dynamatic delivered its first complete shipset of Airbus A220 doors ahead of schedule, at roughly USD 0.5 million per shipset, with the rate targeted to scale from a couple a month toward ~10 a month over a few years — a full assembly, not a part.
  • Dassault Falcon 6X rear fuselage — a complete rear-fuselage assembly for the business jet, at a run-rate for tens of aircraft a year: exactly the "own the whole structure" work the company is chasing.
  • Boeing & Bell — aft-pylon and cabin/cabinet structures for Boeing, and rotorcraft work for Bell, plus the Chinook CH-47 aft-pylon and cargo-ramp assemblies — broadening beyond Airbus so no single OEM defines the book.
  • Dynauton & defence IP — the earliest, most speculative rung: proprietary unmanned-systems work (gimbals, autopilots, radars, a tethered drone), and consortium ambitions on Indian defence platforms. Small today, but the top of the ladder where the company would own the product, not just the structure.
A genuine surprise: the humble tractor-pump maker from Bengaluru holds a single-source position on a flight-critical part fitted to every Airbus A318/A319/A320/A321 ever built — over 13,000 shipsets delivered. The same company that supplies the hydraulics inside a large share of India's tractors is a chokepoint in the supply chain of the best-selling airliner family in aviation history. Those two facts living inside one mid-cap is the least intuitive thing about Dynamatic.
05

The aerospace super-cycle it is riding

The structural tailwind — a global jet-building boom with a decade-long order backlog.

Dynamatic's aerospace ramp is not happening in a vacuum. The global commercial-aircraft industry is in one of the strongest up-cycles in its history: after the pandemic gutted air travel, passenger demand has come roaring back, airlines are re-fleeting with fuel-efficient narrow-bodies, and Airbus and Boeing are sitting on order backlogs stretching well past a decade. Both are straining to lift production rates on their best-sellers — the A320neo family and the 737 MAX. Every rate increase pulls more shipsets through the entire supply chain beneath them.

The insight that separates aerospace from a normal order book

A construction or capital-goods order book can vanish if a customer cancels or a cycle turns. An aerospace single-source position works differently: once you are qualified on a programme, your revenue rises and falls with the aircraft's production rate, set years ahead by an airframer with a decade of orders in hand. That converts a supplier's revenue into something closer to a rate-linked annuity than a project pipeline — highly visible, slow to move, and very hard to lose. Dynamatic's aerospace growth is therefore less about winning a fresh deal each quarter and more about the industry's build rates climbing and its own shipset rates ramping on programmes it already holds.

06

The debt that eats the profit

Why revenue grew 15% while profit fell 25% — the number that settles the whole case.

Here is the fact that stops a first-time reader cold: in FY26, Dynamatic's revenue rose 15.5% — and its net profit fell about 25%, to ₹32 Cr. A growing company earning less money is not a paradox once you see where the profit goes. Between the operating line and the bottom line sit two heavy costs: interest on a large debt load, and the front-loaded expense of ramping new aerospace programmes. The debt is the wedge that keeps a decent EBITDA from reaching the shareholder.

Sales and operating profit grew in lockstep at ~15%. But net profit went the other way — a ~25% fall — as interest cost and ramp expenses swallowed the gain. The gap between the blue bars and the red one is the balance-sheet problem, in a single frame.

Why is the debt there at all? Because building an aerospace franchise is brutally capital-hungry. You buy expensive machine tools, fund years of unpaid qualification work, carry big inventories and long receivables, and run overseas plants — all before a new programme pays back. Dynamatic financed that climb with borrowings, and the result is a business whose operating engine is respectable but whose net leverage has been stuck near 3.4× net-debt-to-EBITDA for several years running — high for a company with single-digit net margins.

Why the balance sheet, not the order book, is the scoreboard

Most growth stories ask you to watch the top line. Dynamatic asks the opposite: the top line is already growing nicely and the market knows it. What is unresolved is whether that growth ever reaches the owner. At ~3.4× net-debt-to-EBITDA, a large slice of every year's operating profit is pre-committed to lenders, and the aerospace ramp keeps demanding fresh working capital, so the debt refuses to shrink. Two things can break the logjam: aerospace margins rising as the mix improves (lifting EBITDA faster than debt), and the working-capital cycle finally easing as new programmes mature. Q1 FY27 — EBITDA margin up to ~13% and PAT nearly doubling — is the first hint both might be starting. But one quarter is a hint, not a trend. The ratio is the truth-teller here.

The uncomfortable arithmetic: a ₹32 Cr net profit against roughly ₹638 Cr of borrowings means interest is a first-order claim on earnings, not a footnote. If aerospace margins expand and leverage falls toward ~2×, profit can inflect sharply off a low base. If they don't, the company keeps running hard just to stand still on the bottom line — growing revenue, delivering aircraft parts, and handing much of the reward to its lenders.
07

Hydraulics: the tractor-pump base

The steady, cash-generating leg that funds the aerospace ambition.

Long before it touched an aircraft, Dynamatic made hydraulic gear pumps — the compact, high-precision pumps that power a tractor's hydraulics: raising and lowering implements, steering, lifting loads. It is the business the company was born in, and it remains a quiet powerhouse: Dynamatic is a leading supplier to India's farm-equipment industry, with a commanding share of the domestic tractor-pump market and a meaningful global footprint, exports included.

Why does a near-monopoly niche business matter to the story? Because it does two jobs. First, it generates cash — steady volumes, entrenched OEM relationships, and pricing power in a product customers cannot easily re-source — which helps fund the capital-hungry aerospace climb. Second, it is the machining school: high-volume, high-precision pump manufacturing is where the company honed the tolerances and process discipline that aerospace later demanded. The tractor pump is humble; the discipline it instilled is not.

The catch with a farm-linked cash cow

Hydraulics is dependable, but it is not immune to the cycle it serves. Tractor demand rises and falls with the rural economy — the monsoon, crop prices, farm incomes, credit availability — so a weak agricultural year drags this segment down regardless of how well aerospace is doing. That is fine as long as you read hydraulics for what it is: a mature, cash-throwing base with a cyclical top line, not a growth engine. Its job in the group is to be steady and to bankroll the climb up the value ladder — and, increasingly, to lend its precision-machining muscle to aerospace sub-assemblies where the two skills overlap.

08

Metallurgy and the defence pivot

The foundry arm — most cyclical today, most repurposable tomorrow.

The third leg is metallurgy: precision castings and machined components made partly in advanced European foundries. Historically its bread and butter has been the auto industry — turbocharger housings, exhaust manifolds and thermal components that must survive extreme heat and pressure inside a car's engine bay. It is skilled work in tough alloys, but it is also the group's most cyclical and lowest-margin segment, tied to global automotive volumes.

What makes metallurgy interesting is not its present but its option value. The same foundries that pour turbo housings can, with the right qualifications, pour high-value aerospace and defence castings — structural parts in specialised alloys where far fewer suppliers can compete and margins are richer. Management has signalled a deliberate re-pointing of this capability toward aircraft and defence work, including exploring defence-related castings such as artillery components. In effect, the plan is to take the group's most commodity-like leg and drag it up the same value ladder aerospace has climbed.

Why owning European foundries is the quiet strategic asset

Advanced-material casting is not something you spin up quickly; it takes qualified foundries, metallurgical know-how, and a track record customers trust. Dynamatic already owns that in Europe — capacity that would cost years and heavy capital to replicate. The metallurgy segment's auto exposure makes it look like a cyclical drag on a spreadsheet, but the underlying asset is a certified foundry base that can be aimed at aerospace and defence. If that redirection works, a low-margin auto-castings line quietly becomes a second aerospace-adjacent business. If it doesn't, metallurgy stays what it is today — a cyclical, capital-consuming leg whose main virtue is the skills it feeds into the rest of the group.

Read this segment as a call on execution, not a number: the reported metallurgy revenue tells you about last year's car market. The thing worth tracking is qualification progress — how much of that foundry capacity gets certified for aircraft and defence castings, and how fast. That conversion, not the current auto-tied top line, is where any re-rating of this segment would come from.
09

Forex, customer concentration and the execution test

The risks that sit underneath a good story — worth naming plainly.

Dynamatic is a genuinely global engineering business, and that brings exposures a purely domestic company would not carry. Three deserve special attention: currency, customer concentration, and the plain difficulty of executing a fast aerospace ramp while carrying heavy debt.

Forex: a two-edged export exposure

A large share of aerospace revenue is earned in foreign currency — euros and dollars from Airbus, Boeing, Spirit and European customers — while much of the cost base (Indian labour, some inputs) is in rupees. A weaker rupee generally helps reported revenue and can lift margins; a stronger rupee does the reverse. But because Dynamatic also runs UK and German plants with euro/sterling costs, the net currency picture is not a simple one-way bet — it is a web of exposures across three currencies that management has to hedge and manage actively. Currency swings can move a given quarter's numbers as much as operations do.

Customer concentration: the price of single-source strength

The very thing that makes the aerospace business valuable — deep single-source positions with a handful of giant customers — is also a concentration risk. A large slice of aerospace revenue leans on Airbus programmes (directly and via Spirit AeroSystems). That is a durable, sticky relationship, but it means a production-rate cut, a programme delay, or a dispute at one big customer would land hard. Diversifying across Boeing, Bell, Dassault and Indian defence is partly an answer to exactly this — spreading the book so no single airframer's decisions define the year.

Execution: the ramp is the risk

Ramping a new aerospace programme — taking A220 doors from a couple a month toward ten, standing up Falcon 6X fuselage production, qualifying foundry capacity for defence — is operationally hard and cash-intensive. Slip a delivery schedule, mis-price a fixed contract, or let working capital balloon, and a leveraged balance sheet turns a normal operational hiccup into a financial one. This is the everyday risk that does not make headlines: not a lost contract, but simply the grind of delivering a fast ramp cleanly, on time, quarter after quarter, with limited financial slack to absorb mistakes.

10

Financials & valuation

A real growth business with a thin bottom line — priced as if the inflection is a certainty.

FY26 was a year of strong revenue growth, a rising aerospace mix, and a squeezed bottom line as interest and ramp costs bit. Q1 FY27 hinted at the profit inflection the bulls are waiting for. The market has already paid up for that inflection in full — which makes the gap between operating progress and the share price the central tension.

Revenue has compounded steadily in the low-to-mid teens for years — roughly ₹1,250 Cr in FY24, ~₹1,404 Cr in FY25 and ~₹1,621 Cr in FY26 — led all the way by aerospace. The debate has never been about the top line. It is about what happens below it.

The one number that settles the thesis: net-debt-to-EBITDA, not revenue

At ~130× trailing earnings, Dynamatic is not priced as a cyclical engineering firm; it is priced as a company whose profit is about to inflect several-fold. For that to be justified, one number has to move: net-debt-to-EBITDA has to fall — from ~3.4× toward ~2× and below — because that is what proves the aerospace mix is lifting EBITDA faster than the ramp is consuming cash. Read it alongside three supporting gauges: the aerospace EBIT margin (is the build-to-spec climb actually earning more?), the working-capital cycle in days (is the balance sheet releasing cash as programmes mature?), and the aerospace shipset run-rates on A220 doors and Falcon 6X (is the ramp on schedule?). If leverage falls while aerospace margins rise, the low profit base inflects and the multiple has something to grow into. If leverage stays glued near 3.4×, the P/E is discounting an event that keeps not arriving. Revenue is the easy part; the deleveraging is the exam.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A rare Indian aerospace franchise — asked to prove its profits can escape its balance sheet.

Dynamatic is one of very few Indian companies with a genuine, qualified seat in the global aircraft supply chain, riding a super-cycle at exactly the right moment, while deliberately climbing from making parts to owning designs. The honest debate is not whether the business is real — it plainly is — but whether the earnings can outrun the interest bill fast enough to justify the price the market has already paid.

The bull case

  • Structural aerospace super-cycle — a decade-plus Airbus/Boeing backlog and rising narrow-body build rates pull Dynamatic's shipset volumes higher for years, largely regardless of the economy.
  • Single-source moats — the A320 flap-track-beam lock (13,000+ shipsets) plus A220 doors, Falcon 6X fuselage and Chinook work are sticky, multi-decade, hard-to-replace positions.
  • Mix-shift compounding — aerospace growing ~4× faster than the other legs steadily lifts group margin quality; Q1 FY27's jump to a ~13% EBITDA margin is early proof.
  • Value-chain climb — moving from build-to-print to design partner (the 200-to-20-part redesign) captures engineering margin, not just machining margin.
  • Deleveraging optionality — if EBITDA rises and working capital eases, a thin profit base can inflect sharply as the interest wedge narrows.

The bear case

  • The debt wedge — ~3.4× net leverage stuck for years; interest and ramp costs already turned 15% revenue growth into a 25% profit fall in FY26.
  • Priced for the inflection — a ~130× P/E on a ₹32 Cr profit assumes the deleveraging and margin lift are near-certain; any delay leaves the multiple badly exposed.
  • Customer & programme concentration — heavy reliance on Airbus (directly and via Spirit); one rate cut or programme slip lands hard on a leveraged book.
  • Ramp-execution risk — scaling A220 doors and Falcon 6X cleanly, on fixed-price terms, with limited financial slack, is operationally unforgiving.
  • Cyclical drag & forex — hydraulics rides the monsoon-and-rural cycle, metallurgy the auto cycle, and three-currency exposure can swing quarters on its own.
The bottom line

Dynamatic has done the hard, decade-long work of turning a tractor-pump maker into a qualified global aerostructures supplier with single-source locks on the world's best-selling jets — and it is climbing from making parts to owning designs just as a historic aircraft-building boom lifts every rate beneath it. What it has not yet done is get that success past its own lenders: a heavy debt load has kept the bottom line thin even as revenue and operating profit climb. The next several quarters answer the only question that matters here in one checkable figure — whether net-debt-to-EBITDA finally breaks below ~3× while the aerospace margin keeps rising. Watch the A220-door and Falcon-6X ramp rates for the operational signal, and watch that leverage ratio for the financial verdict; the flap-track beam already proved the company can build anything Airbus needs — the balance sheet is what has yet to prove it can keep the reward.
