Raymond Lifestyle, one iconic brand, four unequal engines

A full walk through the branded-apparel and suiting-fabric business demerged out of the old Raymond group in 2024 — the world's largest maker of worsted suiting cloth, owner of Park Avenue, ColorPlus and Ethnix, and of "The Raymond Shop" chain that reaches deep into small-town India. Why a ₹6,888 Cr icon earns only ₹46 Cr of profit, where the real margin actually hides, and what has to happen for the number to move.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Raymond Lifestyle Limited (RLL) is the branded-apparel and suiting-fabric business carved out of the century-old Raymond group and listed on its own in September 2024. It is the maker of the cloth in a Raymond suit — the world's largest integrated producer of worsted suiting fabric — and the owner of a stable of menswear brands (Raymond Ready-to-Wear, Park Avenue, ColorPlus, Parx, and the ethnic-wear label Ethnix by Raymond). It also runs a large B2B garmenting arm that stitches shirts and trousers for global labels, and a specialist high-value cotton shirting fabric business. Tying it together is "The Raymond Shop" (TRS) — a franchise retail chain of 1,100-plus stores that reaches into towns most apparel brands never bother with.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a demerger story, and the name matters. The old Raymond Ltd split into three separately listed companies: Raymond Ltd (engineering, tools, aerospace components), Raymond Realty (the Thane land-bank real-estate business), and Raymond Lifestyle — this company, the pure apparel-and-fabric play. If you are looking at a chart of "Raymond," make sure you know which of the three you are holding. This primer is about Lifestyle only.
  • Huge revenue, thin profit — and that gap is the whole debate. FY26 revenue was a record ~₹6,888 Cr, but net profit was only ~₹46 Cr — a net margin of well under 1%. The business generates ~₹804 Cr of EBITDA that then gets almost entirely eaten by depreciation, finance costs and the drag of businesses still running near breakeven. The bull case is not "buy a cheap earnings stream"; it is "buy an under-earning icon and wait for the margin to widen."
  • The profit is lopsided across the four segments. The famous suiting fabric and a tiny, obscure cotton-shirting arm do most of the earning; the large branded-apparel and garmenting revenues barely make money today. Understanding which rupee of Raymond's revenue is profitable is the single most useful thing a first-time reader can carry away.
In plain terms

Raymond Lifestyle is a company most Indians already know the front of and almost none know the back of. The front is the wedding-season suit, the "Complete Man" advertising, the shop in the district town. The back is a lumpy portfolio of four businesses stitched into one share: a genuinely world-class fabric mill, a fast-scaling but still-thin branded-apparel arm, an export garment factory that swings with global orders, and a small specialist cotton-shirting unit that quietly out-earns all of them per rupee. The share price is not really tracking whether Raymond sells more suits — it is tracking whether the three low-margin engines can be dragged up toward the profitability their rivals already enjoy. FY26 was the first clean full year to judge that on, and the answer so far is "slowly."

01

Fibre-to-fashion 101: how an apparel company earns

No fashion-industry background needed — just the value chain and where money sticks.

A branded-clothing business can sit at very different points of a chain that runs from raw fibre to the garment on a shelf, and where it sits decides how it earns. Raymond Lifestyle is unusual because it sits at almost every point at once — it spins and weaves its own fabric, stitches finished garments, brands them, and sells them through its own stores. That vertical spread is its heritage strength and, as we'll see, the source of its margin puzzle. Five ideas unlock the rest of this primer.

The structural fact that shapes the sector: brand and retail are where the margin lives, not the loom

Across global apparel, the fat, durable profits accrue to brands and retailers — the companies that own the customer relationship and the shelf — not to the mills and factories that make the cloth and stitch the garment. A commodity fabric earns a few percent; a loved brand sold through a well-run store can earn many times that. Raymond's inheritance is the opposite way round: it was built as a manufacturer first — a superb fabric mill — and has been layering brand and retail on top for decades. The investment question, at its heart, is whether an engineering-and-manufacturing culture can finish the journey into a high-margin consumer-brand company. That is exactly the arc Indian peers like Trent and Vedant Fashions have already travelled, and it is why the market watches Raymond's apparel margins far more closely than its fabric volumes.

How the money flows, stage by stage

  • Make the cloth — Raymond spins wool and blends and weaves worsted suiting at plants in Vapi (Gujarat), Chhindwara (Madhya Pradesh) and Jalgaon (Maharashtra), with aggregate capacity of roughly 43 million metres. This is the branded-textile engine.
  • Turn cloth into garments — some fabric becomes Raymond's own branded apparel; a separate garmenting arm stitches to order for third-party global brands.
  • Brand it — Park Avenue, ColorPlus, Parx, Raymond Ready-to-Wear and Ethnix each target a different wallet and occasion.
  • Sell it — through The Raymond Shop franchise network, exclusive brand outlets, department stores, and increasingly online — with a distinctive reach into India's smaller towns.
02

How one Raymond became three

The demerger that created this listco — and why the split is itself the thesis.

For most of its life "Raymond" was a single, sprawling, confusing company: fabric and suits sat under the same roof as engineering tools, aerospace components, an FMCG arm and a very valuable slab of Mumbai-region land. Investors could never cleanly value it. Between 2024 and 2025 the group finally unbundled itself into three separately listed companies, and Raymond Lifestyle is the pure apparel-and-fabric piece that fell out of that split.

Three tickers now carry the Raymond name. Confuse them and you can badly mis-read a price chart: Raymond Ltd's share fell sharply on the days value was spun out to the other two — an accounting event, not a collapse.

Raymond Lifestyle itself listed on the NSE and BSE on 5 September 2024, distributed to the shareholders of the parent. (The listed shell used for it had, confusingly, been called Raymond Consumer Care before being renamed.) A separate scheme then hived off Raymond Realty, which listed in 2025 — the day the realty value left the parent, Raymond Ltd's quoted price dropped by roughly two-thirds, purely because a big chunk of the company had walked out of the door into a new ticker. None of that is a Lifestyle event, but it is exactly the kind of thing that trips up a first-time reader scanning "Raymond" charts.

Why a demerger can create value out of thin air

A conglomerate often trades at a discount to the sum of its parts, because different investors want different things and none wants the whole muddle. A fabric-and-fashion investor doesn't want to underwrite a land bank; a real-estate investor doesn't want an aerospace-components cycle bolted on. Splitting the group lets each business be valued on its own merits, gives each its own management focus and capital, and lets shareholders own exactly the piece they want. For Raymond Lifestyle the pitch is clean: a debt-free, cash-generative consumer-and-fabric company, no longer subsidising or subsidised by unrelated divisions, free to be judged as a branded-apparel play. Whether that clarity earns a higher multiple depends entirely on the margins the next few sections dig into — a clean structure is a necessary start, not a finish.

03

The four businesses under one share

A guided tour of the segments — because they behave nothing alike.

Raymond Lifestyle reports across four operating segments, and the most important thing to grasp is how different they are — in size, in growth, and above all in profitability. Read the revenue split first; then, in section 05, watch how unevenly the profit is spread across it.

Segment 1 · Branded Textile — the world-class core

This is the historic heart: worsted suiting fabric sold to tailors, to The Raymond Shop network and to institutional buyers, plus the Raymond brand's own famous cloth. Raymond is the domestic market leader and the largest integrated worsted-suiting maker in the world, with about 43 million metres of capacity. It is a real manufacturing edge built over decades, it earns a respectable margin, and it throws off cash — but it grows at the pace of India's suiting and wedding-fabric demand, which is steady rather than explosive.

Segment 2 · Branded Apparel — the growth bet

Ready-to-wear garments under Park Avenue (workwear/formal), ColorPlus (premium casual), Parx (youth casual) and Raymond Ready-to-Wear. This is where management wants the future to be — the segment grew double digits in FY26 to ~₹1,812 Cr — but its margins are still thin as it invests in stores, marketing and the shift from selling fabric to selling finished, branded garments. It is the single most important swing factor in the whole earnings story.

Segment 3 · Garmenting — the export engine

A large B2B business that stitches shirts, trousers and jackets for global brands out of Indian and overseas facilities. Revenue was roughly flat in FY26 at ~₹1,066 Cr, but it turned sharply higher late in the year: Q4 garmenting revenue jumped about 38% year-on-year as orders recovered, helped by an India–US trade deal easing tariffs. It is low-margin and utilisation-sensitive, but it is the segment most geared to the "China+1" global sourcing shift (section 08).

Segment 4 · High-Value Cotton Shirting — the quiet specialist

The smallest segment by revenue (~₹575 Cr) and the one almost no casual observer has heard of: premium, fine-count cotton shirting fabric made as a B2B supplier. Hold onto it — because per rupee of sales it is, remarkably, the most profitable thing Raymond does. That inversion is the subject of section 05.

The mental model for reading these four

Two of Raymond's segments are fabric businesses (Branded Textile and Cotton Shirting) and two are garment businesses (Branded Apparel and Garmenting) — but that is not the line that matters for profit. The line that matters is brand power versus commodity. The two high-earning lines (suiting fabric, specialty cotton shirting) are ones where Raymond's product is differentiated and hard to substitute. The two low-earning lines (own-brand apparel, and export garmenting) are, today, either still building brand strength or competing largely on cost and capacity. Raymond's whole strategic task is to move more of its revenue from the second kind of business to the first — to make its apparel as differentiated as its fabric already is.

04

The company, the Singhanias, and who controls it

A hundred-year-old family business, a promoter with a headline history, and a clean balance sheet.

Raymond is one of India's oldest and most recognised industrial-family businesses, controlled for generations by the Singhania family. Its heritage is a genuine asset — few consumer names carry a century of trust into small-town India — but the promoter story also carries governance texture that a serious reader should understand rather than skip.

  • 1980s–2010s

  • 2025

  • FY25

    Record year: revenue ~₹6,888 Cr (+11.5%), EBITDA ~₹804 Cr (+23%), PAT ~₹46 Cr; ends the year debt-free with ~₹179 Cr net cash and a maiden dividend.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter: the Singhania family, through group holding entities, controls roughly 59.5% of Raymond Lifestyle (promoter and promoter-group holding was ~59.52% in mid-2026). The balance is public float, including domestic and foreign institutions. Confirm the exact figure in the latest shareholding filing.
  • Chairman & managing figure: Gautam Hari Singhania, the third-generation family head who drove the modern brand expansion and the demerger blueprint. His leadership is inseparable from the group's strategy.
  • Balance sheet: the demerger left Lifestyle debt-free, closing FY26 with a net-cash surplus of ~₹179 Cr — a meaningful strength for a consumer business planning heavy store expansion, and a contrast with the working-capital-heavy reputation the old conglomerate carried.
Governance, represented plainly: Raymond's promoter has been the subject of prominent public disputes — most visibly a bitter, widely-reported family and marital dispute involving Gautam Singhania in 2023–24 that spilled into questions about family settlements, board composition and related-party arrangements. None of that changes the operating businesses described here, and the group has emphasised professionalising its boards through the demerger. But it is a real part of the story: a controlled company whose strategy rests heavily on one family means minority investors should keep watching board independence, related-party dealings across the three Raymond entities, and capital-allocation discipline. The heritage is an asset; the concentration of control is a risk to price with eyes open.
05

The margin ladder: where the profit actually hides

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is counter-intuitive.

Most people would guess Raymond makes its money on the famous suit — the branded apparel, the shop, the wedding season. That guess is only half right, and the wrong half is instructive. Line the four segments up not by revenue but by EBITDA margin — profit per rupee of sales — and the order almost inverts. The smallest business earns the most per rupee; the glamorous branded-apparel line earns almost nothing per rupee today.

Sit with that picture. Raymond's largest revenue engines — Branded Apparel (~₹1,812 Cr) and Garmenting (~₹1,066 Cr) — together are nearly ₹2,900 Cr of sales that, at present, contribute only a sliver of EBITDA. Meanwhile the ~₹575 Cr cotton-shirting arm and the suiting-fabric core carry most of the profit. The consolidated business earns ~11% EBITDA margin only because a couple of high-margin lines are averaging up a couple of near-breakeven ones. That is the real anatomy of the company.

The device that only fits Raymond Lifestyle: the profit is upside-down against the revenue

Rank Raymond's four segments by size and you get: fabric, apparel, garmenting, cotton shirting. Rank them by margin and you get almost the reverse: cotton shirting, fabric, apparel, garmenting. The company's biggest and most famous growth engine — branded apparel — is today one of its least profitable, while an anonymous specialty-fabric line a fifth its size out-earns it per rupee many times over. So the entire equity story reduces to a single question you can hold in one hand: can the big, low-margin engines be dragged up the ladder toward the margins the small, high-margin ones already prove are achievable inside this same company? Raymond doesn't need to invent profitability — it already runs a 33%-margin fabric business and a mid-teens suiting one. It needs to make its apparel and garment volumes earn something closer to that. Every rupee of margin improvement in apparel drops almost straight to a profit line that is currently only ₹46 Cr — which is why small operational gains can move the earnings dramatically.

This also explains why the reported net profit is so small. FY26's ~₹804 Cr of EBITDA has to clear depreciation on the mills and stores, finance and lease costs, tax, and the operating drag of segments still investing to scale — and by the time it does, only ~₹46 Cr is left. A tiny shift in apparel or garmenting profitability, or in the finance and depreciation load, changes that residual by a large percentage. The bottom line is a lever with enormous mechanical advantage — which cuts both ways.

06

The small-town retail moat: The Raymond Shop

The distribution asset rivals can't quickly copy — and the genuine surprise in the story.

Here is the fact that surprises anyone who pictures Raymond as a metro premium brand: its deepest competitive advantage is geographic reach into small-town India, built over decades through a franchise model that most modern apparel brands have never bothered to replicate. The Raymond Shop (TRS) — a franchised store that sells suiting fabric and apparel together — sits in district towns and mofussil markets where a Zara or a Westside will never open, and where the trusted "Raymond" name still anchors a man's wedding or job-interview purchase.

The model is deliberately capital-light: many TRS stores are owned and stocked by franchisees, so Raymond gets distribution reach and brand presence without pouring its own balance sheet into real estate and inventory in every town. That is why a debt-free company can credibly plan ~900 new outlets — it is largely other people's capital opening the doors, with Raymond supplying the brand, the fabric and the apparel.

Why small-town reach is worth more than it looks

India's apparel growth story is often told as a big-city, mall-and-e-commerce narrative. But a very large share of aspirational, occasion-driven clothing spend — weddings, festivals, a first formal suit — happens in towns that the fashionable brands find too small, too logistically awkward, or too low-density to serve. Building trust and a physical shelf in hundreds of those towns takes decades and thousands of franchisee relationships; it cannot be bought quickly with a marketing budget. Raymond already has it. The question is whether it can layer higher-margin branded apparel onto that trusted fabric-selling footprint — using the reach it already owns to sell a ColorPlus shirt or a Raymond ready-made suit, not just a length of cloth. If it can, the distribution moat and the margin-ladder problem solve each other. If the small-town customer keeps buying only the low-margin fabric, the reach is real but under-monetised.

The surprise to hold onto: Raymond's edge is not fashion — plenty of brands out-design it — it is where it already stands. A distribution network reaching 380-plus towns, funded substantially by franchisees, is a structural asset that competitors with slicker products simply cannot conjure at speed. The strategic prize is turning that fabric-era footprint into a channel that sells finished, branded, higher-margin garments — converting reach into margin.
07

Weddings, premiumisation, and Ethnix

The demand tailwinds management is leaning into — occasion, aspiration, and ethnic wear.

Beyond the segment mechanics, Raymond is riding a few genuine consumer tailwinds. India's clothing spend is premiumising as incomes rise; the vast wedding-and-occasion market is structurally huge and largely branded-light; and men's ethnic wear is one of the fastest-growing, highest-margin corners of apparel. Raymond has products aimed squarely at all three.

The wedding and occasion market

India hosts millions of weddings a year, and the groom's and family's occasion wardrobe — suits, sherwanis, premium shirting — is exactly Raymond's home turf. Occasion buying is relatively price-insensitive, brand-conscious, and repeatable across a customer's life. It plays to Raymond's twin strengths: premium fabric and trusted small-town retail, precisely where much wedding shopping still happens.

Ethnix by Raymond — the ethnic-wear push

Ethnix is Raymond's dedicated men's ethnic-wear brand (kurtas, sherwanis, Indo-western occasion wear), sold through its own stores and the wider network. It targets the same fast-growing, higher-margin ethnic-wear demand that has made Vedant Fashions (Manyavar) one of the most profitable apparel companies in India. For Raymond, Ethnix is both a growth line and a strategic statement: an attempt to earn Manyavar-like margins by pairing occasion-wear product with reach Raymond already owns.

Why premiumisation matters more here than for an average brand

Recall the margin ladder. Raymond's problem isn't demand — it sells plenty — it is that too much of what it sells is low-margin fabric and commodity garmenting. Premiumisation is the cure that fits the disease: every shift of a customer from buying a length of suiting toward buying a finished Raymond suit, a ColorPlus premium-casual outfit, or an Ethnix sherwani moves a rupee up the margin ladder. The wedding and ethnic-wear pushes aren't just "more sales" — they are better-quality sales, the kind that could lift the thin branded-apparel margin toward where peers already sit. That is why management talks about mix and premiumisation as much as about volume: in this company, mix is margin.

The brand architecture is built for this laddering: Parx catches the value-conscious young buyer, Park Avenue the office wardrobe, ColorPlus the premium-casual customer, Raymond Ready-to-Wear the formal and occasion buyer, and Ethnix the wedding-and-festival wardrobe — a spread of price points designed to grow with the customer and capture more of a rising Indian clothing wallet.

08

Garmenting and the China+1 export opportunity

The lowest-margin segment — but the one geared to a global sourcing shift.

Raymond's garmenting arm — stitching shirts, trousers and jackets to order for international brands — is the least profitable segment today, but it sits in front of one of the biggest structural shifts in global apparel: the move of sourcing away from China toward India, Bangladesh, Vietnam and elsewhere, as brands diversify their supply chains. If India captures even a modest slice of that reallocation, integrated Indian garment makers with scale and capacity stand to gain volume.

FY26 gave a live glimpse. Garmenting revenue was flat for most of the year, then Q4 revenue jumped roughly 38% year-on-year as orders recovered — a swing management linked to improving global demand and an India–US trade deal that eased tariff friction on Indian apparel exports. That volatility is the segment in a nutshell: it swings with global brands' order books and with trade policy, neither of which Raymond controls.

The balanced read on garmenting

It would be easy to over-sell "China+1" as a guaranteed windfall, and easy to dismiss garmenting as a margin-dilutive drag. The truth sits between. This is a genuinely cyclical, low-margin, capacity-driven business whose good years depend on demand and trade winds Raymond does not set — the FY26 Q4 surge shows how quickly it can turn, in both directions. But it is also real scale in exactly the category global brands are re-sourcing, and even a low single-digit margin on a much fuller order book adds absolute profit and utilisation to the group. The right frame is optionality: garmenting is unlikely to be the profit hero, but if the sourcing shift and trade access hold, it can stop being a drag and start contributing — turning a near-breakeven ₹1,000 Cr of revenue into a positive line.

09

The rivals: a company that competes on four fronts

Because it has four businesses, Raymond faces four different competitive sets.

Raymond Lifestyle is unusual in having no single "peer." It competes with fabric makers in one segment, branded-apparel giants in another, ethnic-wear specialists in a third, and global-sourcing garment exporters in a fourth. Its scorecard against each set differs — dominant in fabric, a challenger in apparel.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Raymond's competitive position is strongest exactly where the industry's margins are thinnest (fabric manufacturing) and weakest exactly where the margins are richest (branded, retail-led apparel). That is the strategic irony of the whole company. Peers like Trent and Vedant Fashions have shown that Indian apparel can earn mid-teens EBITDA margins and command premium valuations — but they got there by being retail-and-brand-first, not mill-first. Raymond is trying to make the harder journey in the other direction: from a manufacturing champion into a consumer-brand company, carrying a distribution moat none of those peers can match. Whether that heritage-and-reach advantage outweighs their brand-and-margin head start is the real competitive question, and it will be answered in apparel-segment margins, not fabric volumes.

10

Financials & valuation

A record top line, a thin bottom line, and a multiple that only makes sense as a bet on margin.

FY26 was Raymond Lifestyle's best year yet on revenue and EBITDA — but the profit remained slim, and the valuation only makes sense once you see it as a wager on future margin rather than a claim on current earnings. At roughly ₹3,980 Cr of market value on ~₹46 Cr of net profit, the trailing P/E of ~27× looks expensive against thin earnings; the case is that those earnings are depressed, not that the business is dear.

The one number that settles the thesis: Branded Apparel EBITDA margin

At ~27× depressed earnings, Raymond is not cheap on today's numbers and doesn't pretend to be — the case is entirely that the earnings are artificially thin and set to widen. So the metric that proves or breaks it is the Branded Apparel segment's EBITDA margin, tracked quarter by quarter. It sits near low single digits today against apparel peers in the low-to-mid teens and ethnic peers far higher. Raymond has ~₹1,800 Cr of apparel revenue; drag that margin from ~2% toward even 10% and you add well over ₹100 Cr of EBITDA that drops almost straight through to a net profit currently only ₹46 Cr — the earnings could multiply without a single extra suit sold. Watch it alongside two companions: consolidated EBITDA margin (is the blend marching toward the mid-teens?) and same-store growth (is the retail footprint selling more per store, or just adding stores?). If apparel margin climbs as the store base premiumises, the high multiple resolves into ordinary growth. If it stays stuck near breakeven, FY26's thin profit is the honest run-rate and the valuation has nothing to stand on.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A debt-free icon with a distribution moat, asked whether it can finally earn like a brand.

Raymond Lifestyle enters FY27 clean: no debt, net cash, a dominant fabric franchise, a trusted name reaching 380-plus towns, and a stated plan to add ~900 largely franchisee-funded stores. What it has yet to prove is the one thing the valuation rests on — that its big, low-margin apparel and garmenting engines can earn like the branded consumer business it is trying to become, rather than the manufacturer it grew up as. That tension frames both cases.

The bull case

  • Margin runway inside its own walls — a 33%-margin cotton-shirting line and mid-teens fabric core prove Raymond can earn; dragging apparel up the ladder could multiply a tiny profit base.
  • An un-copyable distribution moat — 1,100+ stores across 380+ towns, expanding capital-light on franchisee money, reaching where rivals won't go.
  • Clean, debt-free balance sheet — ~₹179 Cr net cash funds store growth and premiumisation without dilution or leverage.
  • Real demand tailwinds — wedding/occasion wear, premiumisation, and Ethnix's push into high-margin ethnic wear all shift mix upward.
  • China+1 optionality — a scaled garmenting arm geared to global re-sourcing and easing India–US trade friction, as the Q4 FY26 surge hinted.

The bear case

  • Expensive on thin earnings — ~27× a ₹46 Cr profit; if apparel margins don't widen, there is little to support the multiple.
  • Apparel margin is unproven — closing a double-digit gap to peers is hard; brand-building against ABFRL, Arvind, Trent and Vedant is costly and slow.
  • Cyclical, low-margin garmenting — swings with global demand and trade policy; a demand air-pocket quickly turns it back into a drag.
  • Promoter concentration & governance — a family-controlled group with a headline dispute history; related-party dealings across three Raymond entities need watching.
  • Slow-growth fabric core — the profitable base grows at India's steady suiting-demand pace, so the whole re-rating leans on the harder apparel journey.
The bottom line

Raymond Lifestyle is a hundred-year-old brand handed a clean second start: debt-free, focused, and sitting on a distribution reach that money cannot quickly buy. But the demerger only clarified the company — it did not fix the thing that matters, which is that too much of Raymond's revenue still earns a manufacturer's margin, not a brand's. The equity is priced for the fix, not the status quo. The next test is concrete and datable: whether the Branded Apparel segment's EBITDA margin climbs out of low single digits toward double digits across the FY27 quarters as the store base premiumises and Ethnix scales, with same-store growth confirming the reach is being monetised rather than just extended. Deliver that, and a ₹46 Cr profit becomes a multiple of itself against an unchanged share count and a moat rivals envy. Fail to, and the market will conclude the loom, not the label, is still where Raymond really lives — and value it accordingly.
