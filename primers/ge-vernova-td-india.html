00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

GE Vernova T&D India Limited (ticker GVT&D) makes the heavy electrical equipment that builds and stabilises the power-transmission grid — the high-voltage gas-insulated switchgear (GIS) and air-insulated switchgear that connect and protect the network, power and instrument transformers that change voltage, circuit breakers, and — its crown jewels — the HVDC and FACTS systems that move bulk electricity over long distances and hold a renewable-heavy grid steady. It is the listed Indian arm of GE Vernova's Grid Solutions business, and one of only a tiny handful of companies on earth that can build the very highest-voltage, most complex parts of a grid.

Three facts frame everything:

  • An old company having a spectacular second act. This business has been in Indian transmission for over 100 years (through the Alstom and GE lineages), but for most of the last decade it was order-starved and barely profitable. As India's grid cycle turned, it transformed: FY26 (year ended March 2026) revenue was ₹6,206 Cr (+45%), EBITDA margin 27.1% (up from ~19% a year earlier and low-single-digits before that), and net profit ₹1,233 Cr — more than double the prior year. It is effectively net cash (only ₹24 Cr of debt) and earns a stunning ~76% ROCE.
  • The order book is the forward story. FY26 order intake was ₹14,776 Cr (+37%), with the March quarter alone booking ~₹8,614 Cr (+188% YoY) on the back of large HVDC and GIS wins. The order backlog reached ₹21,456 Cr (+70%) — more than three years of revenue already contracted. For an equipment-and-projects company, backlog is the single most important number.
  • It rides India's transmission "supercycle." To plug in a target of ~500 GW of non-fossil power and meet surging demand (data centres, EVs, electrification), India plans to spend ~₹9.15 lakh crore expanding its grid to 2032, including nine new HVDC links. GE Vernova T&D sits at the high-technology centre of that build-out — which is exactly why the market pays a valuation of close to 100× earnings for it, the single most contested feature of the stock.
In plain terms

Think of the power grid as a national plumbing system for electricity. Power is made at one end (a solar park in Gujarat, a coal plant in the east) and has to travel hundreds of kilometres to a city, factory or data centre. Along the way it passes through transformers (which change voltage), switchgear and substations (the junctions, taps and safety valves), and increasingly HVDC links (ultra-efficient long-distance "electricity superhighways"). GE Vernova T&D doesn't sell the electricity — it sells the pipes, valves and pumps of the grid, and it specialises in the hardest, highest-voltage parts. For years the plumbing business was slow and low-margin. Then India decided to rebuild the entire system at once — and this company suddenly had more work than it could handle, at much better prices.

01

The grid, in plain terms — and why "T&D" matters

No engineering degree required — just the shape of how electricity gets from a solar park to your socket.

Electricity is unusual: it must be consumed the instant it is made, and it is generated far from where it is used. The system that bridges that gap is the transmission & distribution (T&D) grid — and the "T" (transmission) is GE Vernova T&D's home turf. Understanding four ideas explains almost the whole business.

GE Vernova T&D lives mainly in Stages 2–3 — the high-voltage transmission backbone, not the low-voltage last mile. The further into the high-voltage/HVDC end you go, the fewer players can compete and the fatter the margins.

Four ideas that explain the whole business

  • Voltage is a highway. To send power a long way with little loss, you raise it to very high voltage (up to 1200 kV) for the journey, then step it back down near the user. Transformers do the raising and lowering; the higher the voltage, the more specialised and valuable the equipment.
  • Switchgear is the plumbing's valves and fuses. Substations must be able to connect, disconnect and instantly cut off faults. Gas-insulated switchgear (GIS) does this in a fraction of the space of the old open-air kind — vital in cities and increasingly the standard at high voltages.
  • AC vs DC — two ways to move power. The grid runs on alternating current (AC), but for very long distances or undersea/underground links, converting to direct current (HVDC) loses far less energy. HVDC needs giant converter stations at each end — extremely high-tech, few-supplier kit.
  • A renewable grid needs "stabilisers." Solar and wind are variable and don't naturally steady the grid the way spinning coal turbines do. FACTS devices (Flexible AC Transmission Systems) inject or absorb power electronically to keep voltage and flow stable — the more renewables you add, the more you need them.
Why this is a great business right now

Two of those four ideas — HVDC and FACTS — are exactly what a grid built around solar and wind needs most, and they are precisely the products only a few global firms can make. As India shifts from a coal-anchored grid to a renewable-heavy one, demand tilts toward the very equipment that has the fewest competitors and the highest margins. GE Vernova T&D isn't just riding more volume; it's riding a mix shift toward its most defensible products.

Products vs projects — why the mix matters

Selling a transformer off a production line is a product sale — repeatable, factory-driven. Designing and commissioning a complete 800 kV HVDC converter station is a project — a multi-year, engineered contract worth hundreds or thousands of crore. Projects bring the eye-catching order wins and the long visibility; products bring steadier factory throughput. GE Vernova T&D does both, and increasingly the project wins (HVDC, big GIS packages) are what drive the headline order-intake numbers and the technology story.

03

The GE Vernova rename — Alstom to GE to a pure-play

The name changed twice in a decade — and the latest change plugged it into a freshly-listed global energy giant.

If you looked this company up a few years ago it had a different name — and that lineage is worth understanding, because it explains both the "over 100 years in India" heritage and the fresh parent-technology angle. This is the same operating business under three successive owners' badges.

How it got here

    The transmission business traces its Indian roots back over a century, through the switchgear/transformer operations later consolidated under Alstom.
  • 2010s

  • Nov 2024

  • FY26

Why the GE Vernova parentage is a real asset

The rename isn't cosmetic. GE Vernova is one of the world's few holders of the most advanced HVDC and grid-stabilisation technology. Being the listed Indian arm of that group gives GVT&D three things: (1) access to cutting-edge HVDC/FACTS technology and reference credentials it could never build alone; (2) a role as a low-cost manufacturing and engineering hub for the parent's global orders (an export tailwind); and (3) a global sourcing/supply-chain that helps it bid the biggest, hardest projects at home. The trade-off is that a 51%-owner parent controls strategy, transfer pricing and dividend policy — minority holders ride along.

The company and who controls it

A century-old franchise, now a 51%-owned arm of a global energy pure-play.

GE Vernova T&D India is a subsidiary of the GE Vernova group, run for the long term as the group's Indian grid-equipment platform. Control is clear, the balance sheet is pristine, and the shareholder register has shifted markedly toward institutions in the last year.

Ownership & balance sheet

  • Promoter (GE Vernova group): ~51% as of March 2026 — held via Grid Equipments Pvt Ltd (~68.5% of the promoter block) and GE Grid Alliance BV (~6.5%), reduced from ~75% after the Nov 2024 offer-for-sale. Control and technology flow from the global parent.
  • Institutions have piled in. Foreign institutional holding roughly doubled to ~20% over the year to Mar 2026; domestic institutions hold ~21%. That re-rating of ownership mirrors the re-rating of the stock.
  • Effectively net cash. Total borrowings were just ₹24 Cr at FY26 — negligible against ₹1,233 Cr of annual profit. The heavy negative working-capital dynamics of an advance-funded project business help keep it cash-rich.
  • Elite return ratios. ROCE of ~76% and ROE in the 40–57% range reflect an asset-light-ish, advance-funded model earning fat margins on a small equity base — a hallmark of a well-run capital-goods franchise at the top of its cycle.
The parent-control lens: as a majority-owned MNC subsidiary, GVT&D's fortunes are tied to GE Vernova's global strategy — how much technology and export work it routes through India, how it prices inter-company transactions, and how it treats minority shareholders on dividends and related-party terms. So far the alignment has been strongly positive (technology access, export orders, a rising dividend), but it is a permanent governance consideration for any minority investor.
05

The transmission capex supercycle

The single biggest force behind the numbers — and it is a multi-year, policy-backed structural wave, not a blip.

Everything good in GVT&D's recent results traces back to one thing: India is rebuilding its transmission grid at a scale and speed it has never attempted before. This is the company's "China section" — the defining external driver. And crucially, it is structural and policy-mandated, which is why it can support years of order flow.

Why the grid suddenly needs a decade of heavy spend

For most of the 2010s, India built lots of generation but under-invested in the wires that carry the power. Now three forces collide at once:

  • Renewables must be evacuated. Solar and wind are built where the sun and wind are (Rajasthan, Gujarat, the coasts) — far from the cities that use the power. Integrating a target of ~500 GW of non-fossil capacity requires thousands of kilometres of new high-voltage lines, HVDC corridors and reactors to carry and stabilise that power.
  • Demand is surging from new sources. Data centres, EV charging, rising air-conditioning load and broad electrification are pushing peak demand up sharply — and every new load needs grid capacity to reach it.
  • The plan is on paper and funded. The Ministry of Power's National Electricity Plan (Transmission) commits to roughly ₹9.15 lakh crore of transmission investment over 2023–2032, adding ~1,91,000 circuit-km of lines, 1,270 GVA of transformation capacity, and nine new HVDC links (~33 GW) — nearly doubling India's HVDC capacity.

Why "supercycle" is the right word

An ordinary upcycle lasts a few good quarters. A supercycle is a decade-long structural surge driven by something that has to happen — here, decarbonisation forces a wholesale rewiring of the grid. Because the spending is mandated by policy, tied to legally-committed renewable targets, and spread across the whole 2020s, the order flow is unusually visible and durable. That durability is what lets a grid-equipment maker's backlog build to three-plus years of revenue — and what lets the market pay up for years of growth rather than one good year.

06

HVDC & FACTS — the technology moat

The products that only a handful of firms on earth can build — and GVT&D's specific edge within them.

If the supercycle is the tide, HVDC and FACTS are the deep water where GVT&D has the fewest competitors and the strongest position. These are not commodity equipment; they are reliability-critical, physics-heavy systems that sit behind a wall of engineering, patents, references and safety approvals. Understanding what they do — and why they're so hard — is understanding the moat.

HVDC, in plain English

Most of the grid runs on alternating current (AC). But over very long distances, or through undersea/underground cables, AC "leaks" too much energy. HVDC converts the power to direct current for the journey, then back to AC at the far end. Doing that needs enormous converter stations stuffed with power electronics — the most sophisticated kit in the entire grid. India's plan for nine new HVDC links means a wave of these stations, each worth hundreds to thousands of crore.

FACTS, in plain English

A grid built on coal has heavy spinning turbines that naturally steady voltage and frequency. Swap that for solar panels and wind turbines — which don't spin and vary minute-to-minute — and the grid becomes twitchy. FACTS devices (things like SVCs and STATCOMs) are electronic "shock absorbers" that inject or soak up reactive power in milliseconds to keep the grid stable. The more renewables you add, the more FACTS you need — so demand rises exactly as the energy transition accelerates.

Why this is a fortress, not just a product

Four things make HVDC/FACTS a genuine moat. (1) Technology — the converter know-how sits with a tiny club of global players (GE Vernova, Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy); a new entrant can't simply build it. (2) Reliability stakes — a converter station failing can black out a region, so utilities buy only from proven names with a track record. (3) References — you can't win the next project without having delivered the last one, which locks out newcomers. (4) Parent access — GVT&D can draw on GE Vernova's global HVDC portfolio and references, something a domestic-only rival cannot. The result: as the grid tilts toward HVDC/FACTS, competition narrows to two or three firms, and pricing power improves.

The competitive read: analysts expect the coming 12–18 months of large Indian HVDC awards to be contested largely between GE Vernova T&D and Hitachi Energy — a two-horse race at the very top voltages. That is a remarkably good place to sit in a market about to order nine HVDC links.

The turnaround and operating leverage

How a thin-margin, order-starved company became a 27%-margin cash machine — the mechanism, explained.

The most striking thing in the numbers is not the growth — it's the margin transformation. GVT&D's operating margin went from low-single-digits (and outright losses in the lean years) to ~19% in FY25 and 27.1% in FY26. That leap is the biggest single driver of the profit explosion (PAT up 103% in FY26 on revenue up 45%), and it is the classic story of operating leverage in a project business. Here's the mechanism.

Operating leverage — the core idea

A transmission-equipment plant has largely fixed costs — factories, skilled engineers, design teams — that must be paid whether it runs at 50% or 90% capacity. In the lean years, the company had too few orders to cover those fixed costs, so margins were razor-thin. Now the same factories are full, running high-value HVDC/GIS work, and every extra rupee of revenue lands on the same fixed base — so a huge share of it drops straight to profit. On top of that, three things compound the effect: (1) a richer mix (more high-margin HVDC/FACTS/automation, less low-margin commodity gear); (2) pricing power in a supply-tight market where buyers compete for scarce capacity; and (3) better project selection — the company can now cherry-pick profitable orders rather than chase revenue at any price. Stack those, and margins can rise faster than revenue for several years.

This is why the same backlog that drives the top line is also driving the bottom line even harder — and why bulls argue there is more margin to come as the highest-value orders in the book execute. The bear counterpoint: margins near 27% are historically peak for grid equipment, and a supply/demand normalisation (or a few badly-priced projects) could pull them back down as fast as they rose.

But three-plus years of visibility, tilted toward high-value work, is about as strong a forward position as a capital-goods company can have.

The export / global-sourcing engine

Because it belongs to GE Vernova, GVT&D can be a low-cost manufacturing and engineering hub for the parent's worldwide grid orders — India already makes an estimated ~80% of the world's T&D products by volume, and Indian engineering is cost-competitive. The industry and the big three

A narrow, high-barrier oligopoly at the top voltages — and GVT&D's specific place in it.

India's high-voltage grid-equipment market is concentrated by design: the technology, reliability stakes and reference requirements shut out most players at the top end. Three MNC-backed listed pure-plays dominate the highest voltages — and GVT&D is one of them, with a distinct HVDC/FACTS lean.

How to position GVT&D against its peers

All three big pure-plays ride the same supercycle, but they aren't identical. Siemens Energy India is the broadest — it also makes power-generation turbines, so it's a "grid + generation" play. Hitachi Energy India is the largest by revenue and GVT&D's direct HVDC rival. GE Vernova T&D is the purest, highest-margin transmission play with the sharpest HVDC/FACTS technology lean and the best return ratios — but also, currently, the richest valuation relative to its size. In a market about to award nine HVDC links, being one of only two credible top-end HVDC bidders is the position that matters most — and that is GVT&D's.

The structural point: because the highest voltages are a 2–3 firm oligopoly, this isn't a market where a price war erodes everyone's margins. The scarce resource is capacity and proven capability, not customers — which is why all three players are expanding factories and all three trade at premium multiples. The competition is for the best projects, not for survival.

How to think about a ~100× multiple

At a market cap of about ₹1.22 lakh crore on ₹1,233 Cr of FY26 profit, GVT&D trades near 95–100× trailing earnings — one of the priciest capital-goods names in India (the stock is up roughly 28-fold in three years). That only makes sense if you believe several years of the supercycle, margin gains and backlog execution are still ahead — i.e. the market is paying for FY28–FY30 earnings, not FY26. The bull view: a scarce, HVDC-levered, net-cash, ~76%-ROCE franchise with three-plus years of visibility deserves a scarcity premium, and forward earnings could grow into the multiple. The bear view: 100× leaves zero room for error — it capitalises peak margins and peak order flow into perpetuity; any margin normalisation, order-flow pause, or execution stumble, and a 100× stock can de-rate violently. Both can be right at different points in the cycle — which is why margins, order intake and backlog are the numbers to watch each quarter.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely excellent business at the top of its cycle — priced for the cycle to keep going.

GVT&D is one of the cleanest ways to own India's grid build-out: a technology-rich, net-cash, high-margin transmission pure-play with a full order book and a powerful global parent. The investment debate is almost entirely about valuation and cyclicality — how long the supercycle and today's peak margins last — not about business quality.

The bull case

  • Supercycle tailwind — ₹9-lakh-crore, decade-long grid plan with nine HVDC links; multi-year, policy-backed demand.
  • HVDC/FACTS moat — a 2–3 firm oligopoly at the top voltages; GVT&D is one of only two credible big-HVDC bidders in India.
  • Margin & operating leverage — 27% EBITDA with a case for more as high-value backlog executes.
  • Balance sheet & returns — net cash, ~76% ROCE, self-funded capacity expansion, rising dividend.
  • GE Vernova parent — technology access + an export channel that diversifies demand and keeps factories full.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — ~95–100× earnings capitalises peak margins and order flow; huge de-rating risk on any wobble.
  • Cyclicality — order flow is lumpy and the supercycle will eventually mature; grid capex is not a straight line.
  • Peak margins — 27% is historically high for grid equipment; supply/demand normalisation could compress it.
  • Execution risk — big HVDC projects can face delays, cost overruns or client deferrals.
  • MNC-subsidiary governance — 51% parent control means dependence on GE Vernova's strategy, transfer pricing and dividend policy.
The bottom line

GE Vernova T&D India is a structurally advantaged, technology-rich transmission pure-play perfectly positioned for India's grid supercycle — net cash, high-margin, HVDC-levered, and backed by a world-class parent. The business risk is genuinely low. The valuation and cyclicality risk is genuinely high. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter: order intake (is the supercycle still feeding the backlog?), EBITDA margin (are peak margins holding?), and execution (is the backlog converting to profit on schedule?). Get those right and the compounding can grow into even a ~100× multiple; stumble on any of them, and a stock priced for perfection has a long way to fall.
