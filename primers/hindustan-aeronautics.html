00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Hindustan Aeronautics Limited (HAL) is India's flagship defence-aerospace company — a state-owned "Maharatna" public-sector undertaking under the Ministry of Defence that designs, builds, repairs and overhauls military aircraft, helicopters and aero-engines for the Indian armed forces. It is, in practice, the country's near-monopoly supplier of indigenous combat aircraft: if India wants a fighter, a trainer or a military helicopter built at home, it is almost always built by HAL. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) it did ~₹33,050 crore of revenue and earned ₹9,116 crore of net profit at a ~30% operating margin — extraordinary profitability for a manufacturer of complex machines.

Four facts frame everything:

  • It runs on an order book, not a catalogue. HAL doesn't sell off-the-shelf products; it wins enormous, multi-year contracts to build and maintain aircraft fleets. At 31 March 2026 its order book stood at ₹2,54,538 crore — roughly 7–8 years of revenue already contracted. This is the master KPI; everything else flows from it (see §02).
  • It is, in practice, a near-monopoly with guaranteed demand. HAL is the default Indian builder of military aircraft, and its biggest customer — the Indian Air Force — is flying only ~31 fighter squadrons against a sanctioned 42. That structural shortfall plus the national push to "buy Indian" makes HAL's demand close to guaranteed for a generation.
  • It is cash-rich and debt-free. HAL carries essentially no borrowings (~₹11 crore) and sits on a vast pile of cash and bank deposits (~₹42,400 crore), helped by customer advances on big contracts. The balance sheet is a fortress (see §09).
  • The government owns it — and is also its biggest customer. The Government of India holds ~71.6%; the Ministry of Defence and the armed forces provide the overwhelming majority of revenue. That alignment is both HAL's moat and its central risk.
In plain terms

Think of HAL as India's national aircraft factory. When the Air Force needs a home-built fighter jet, when the Army needs an attack helicopter, or when any of the thousands of military aircraft already in Indian service needs its engine stripped down and rebuilt, the work very often goes to HAL — sometimes by tender, very often by nomination because it is the trusted, security-cleared national supplier. Because it builds high-value, hard-to-replicate machines for a captive customer (the Indian state) with almost no domestic rival, it earns fat margins and gets paid advances up front — so it is flush with cash. The key insight is that a defence-aerospace builder's future is unusually visible: the order book tells you what it will build for the better part of a decade. So the questions are not really "will the orders come" (they will — the air force is desperately short of aircraft) but "can HAL build them on time" — and here a single delayed American engine has become the whole story.

01

What HAL actually does — and how it earns

No engineering background required — just how an aircraft house turns a contract into profit.

HAL is, financially, a project-and-product manufacturer plus a maintenance house for one dominant customer set: the Indian armed forces. It earns in two distinct ways that are worth separating because they behave very differently. First, manufacturing — building new aircraft, helicopters and engines under multi-year contracts, booking revenue as machines are completed and delivered. Second, and bigger, Repair & Overhaul (ROH) — the recurring, high-margin work of maintaining, upgrading and rebuilding the fleets HAL (and others) already supplied. Understand four ideas and the model clicks.

Why the margins are high — the opposite of a normal contractor

A road-builder earns pennies on the rupee because anyone can pour concrete and clients pick the cheapest bid. HAL is the reverse. It builds combat aircraft and aero-engines that essentially no other Indian company can produce, for a customer that prizes sovereign capability, security clearance and indigenous content over rock-bottom price — and on most programmes HAL is the nominated producer, not one bidder among many. That scarcity is pricing power. Layer on the second engine: the ROH business is a near-captive annuity — once an aircraft or engine is in Indian service, it must be overhauled every few years for decades, and HAL is the natural place to do it. New-build wins the headlines; overhaul quietly pays the bills at very fat margins. The result is a manufacturer that earns like a moated franchise — ~30% operating margins and ~25% return on equity — rare for a government-owned factory.

Where the risk lives

  • Customer concentration. The overwhelming majority of revenue comes from the Indian Ministry of Defence and the armed forces. One customer, one budget cycle, one set of priorities.
  • Execution & the supply chain. HAL's single biggest practical problem is building on time. Critical items — above all the GE F404 jet engine for the Tejas — are imported, and a supplier slip can stall a whole production line (see §08). This is THE key risk.
  • Lumpy ordering. Mega-programmes (a ₹62,000-crore fighter or helicopter deal) land unevenly — order inflow can swing hugely from one year to the next depending on when a contract is signed.
  • Q4 back-loading. Defence billing clusters in the March quarter as the government closes its year — so revenue is seasonally skewed, and the first three quarters look soft against a heavy fourth.
02

The order book and the squadron shortage

If you watch only one metric for HAL, watch this one — and here's the demand behind it.

For a consumer company you watch sales; for a bank, loans. For a defence-aerospace builder, you watch the order book — because it is the next several years' revenue, already contracted by the government. The flow runs in one direction: orders are won, sit in the backlog, convert into revenue as aircraft are built and delivered, and finally turn into cash. HAL's whole financial engine is this conveyor belt, with the unusual feature that the customer often pays a large advance up front. At ₹2,54,538 crore, the backlog is roughly 7.7× a single year's revenue — visibility almost no listed manufacturer anywhere can match.

The twist vs. a normal manufacturer: the government frequently pays large advances against big aircraft orders, so cash often arrives before HAL spends it — the source of its negative working capital and ₹42,000-plus-crore deposit hoard (see §09).

Why the demand is close to guaranteed: the squadron shortage

Here is the structural fact that underwrites everything. The Indian Air Force is authorised ("sanctioned") to operate 42 fighter squadrons — the strength its planners judge necessary to handle a simultaneous two-front threat from China and Pakistan. It is actually flying only about 29–31 squadrons, and that number is set to fall further as ageing Soviet-era jets (MiG-21s and the like) retire. In other words, the air force is structurally short of aircraft and getting shorter — and indigenous policy means most of the replacements must be built in India, by HAL. This is not a cyclical, "if the economy is good" demand; it is a national-security gap that must be filled.

Why the order book is the bull case in one number — and what to watch

A large backlog does two jobs at once. It guarantees revenue: ₹2.55 lakh crore of contracted work means the next ~7–8 years of building are largely locked in regardless of the economy. And it signals demand: a record ₹97,028 crore of fresh orders in FY26 says the government keeps handing HAL programmes. Behind the signed book sits an even larger pipeline: a further Tejas Mk1A tranche, the ~₹1.5-lakh-crore-class Tejas Mk2 production order, future engine deals, and the fifth-generation AMCA. With the demand all but assured by the squadron gap, the danger sign is not "no orders" — it is the opposite of most companies: HAL's risk is that it cannot convert the book to revenue fast enough, because the backlog grows faster than the factory (and its engine suppliers) can deliver. That is why analysts now obsess less over order inflow and more over execution (see §08).

03

The company and who controls it

An 85-year-old state enterprise — owned by the very government it supplies.

HAL traces its origins to 1940 in Bangalore, when industrialist Walchand Hirachand founded Hindustan Aircraft to build aeroplanes; the government took control during the Second World War, and the modern HAL took shape in 1964 through a merger of the Bangalore aircraft works with Aeronautics India. Over eight decades it became India's core military-aircraft builder, earning the government's top-tier "Maharatna" autonomy status in 2024. Its defining feature is ownership: it is a public-sector undertaking (PSU) whose controlling shareholder, the Government of India, is simultaneously its dominant customer. That dual role is the heart of both its strength and its risk.

Ownership & governance

  • Government-controlled (~71.6%). The Government of India holds roughly 71.6% as promoter (as of March 2026). This majority cannot be diluted away without a deliberate policy decision, so control is permanent and stable — and gives the government room to sell further stakes over time (a periodic overhang).
  • Institutionally held float. The remaining ~28% is widely owned — foreign portfolio investors (~10%), domestic institutions and mutual funds (~10%), and a large retail base. HAL is a Nifty heavyweight and a core "defence theme" holding.
  • Maharatna autonomy. The Maharatna tag gives HAL's board freedom to invest, form joint ventures and commit very large capital sums without case-by-case government approval — more nimble than a typical PSU.
  • What this means: a permanent anchor shareholder and a captive customer give extraordinary stability and visibility — but the same government sets the defence budget, can push for lower prices or higher dividends, can change procurement policy, and can sell shares into the market. HAL's fate is tied to the state on both the demand and the ownership side.

Why "platform integrator with a lifetime tail" is the key phrase

HAL doesn't just sell an aircraft and walk away. It builds the platform and then owns its entire service life — supplying spares, upgrades and, above all, periodic deep overhauls for twenty, thirty, even forty years. That makes every new-build win the start of a decades-long annuity, not a one-off sale. It also makes HAL's position extraordinarily sticky: once a fleet of Su-30s or Tejas is in Indian service, the only practical place to overhaul them is HAL, which holds the drawings, the tooling, the licences and the security clearance. A rival could perhaps assemble a component, but standing up a certified, sovereign builder-and-maintainer of combat aircraft is a different game entirely. This is the moat in one idea — HAL sells sovereign aircraft capability, and then maintains it forever.

05

The Tejas programme — and the engine that holds it hostage

HAL's flagship — its biggest opportunity, and the source of its most painful problem.

If one programme defines HAL, it is the Tejas Light Combat Aircraft (LCA) — India's home-designed single-engine fighter, decades in the making, now the intended backbone of the air force's combat fleet. The order book around it is huge: an 83-jet Mk1A contract signed in 2021 (~₹48,000 crore), followed by a 97-jet Mk1A follow-on signed in FY26 (₹62,370 crore) — together taking the planned Mk1A fleet toward ~180 aircraft. This is exactly the kind of multi-year, advance-funded mega-programme that drives HAL's backlog and margins. But the programme also illustrates HAL's single greatest vulnerability with painful clarity.

The GE F404 engine — one imported part, one stalled assembly line

A fighter is useless without its engine, and the Tejas Mk1A's engine — the GE F404-IN20 — is imported from GE Aerospace in the United States. HAL ordered 99 of these engines in August 2021. As of early 2026, it had received only about six. GE's supply ran roughly two years behind schedule, and the consequence is stark: HAL has reportedly built, flown and tested around 30 Mk1A airframes that are simply sitting on the ground, awaiting engines before they can be formally handed to the air force. HAL had targeted delivering ~24 Mk1A jets by March 2026; the actual figure fell far short, and management now expects the induction of fresh engine consignments to resume only around August–September 2026.

Why this matters — the demand is certain, the schedule is not

The Tejas story crystallises HAL's whole investment case and its whole risk in a single picture. On the one hand, demand is about as certain as it gets: the air force is critically short of fighters, the Tejas is the chosen home-built answer, and HAL has the orders signed and the airframes physically built. On the other hand, HAL cannot deliver — and therefore cannot bill the bulk of the revenue — until an American company ships engines it doesn't control. HAL has responded by invoking penalty clauses against GE and by accelerating its own engine ambitions (the Mk2's GE F414 comes with a full transfer of technology, and India is pursuing an indigenous engine for the future). But the lesson is blunt: HAL's near-term earnings are hostage to a supply chain that runs through a single foreign supplier. When the engines arrive, a wall of built-but-undelivered aircraft converts to revenue quickly; until they do, the revenue line disappoints even as the order book swells.

Watch this above all else: the cadence of F404 engine deliveries from GE is, in the near term, the most important operating variable for HAL. A resumption to steady monthly deliveries lets the backlog of finished airframes flush through to revenue; another slip pushes recognition further right. It is the rare case where one imported component dictates a ₹3-lakh-crore company's quarterly numbers.
06

Mk2, AMCA and the fighter roadmap

The pipeline behind Tejas — where the multi-decade growth is being written.

Behind the current Tejas Mk1A lies a ladder of ever-more-capable aircraft that, if executed, keeps HAL building combat jets well into the 2040s. Two programmes matter most: the Tejas Mk2, a bigger and more powerful fighter that bridges the gap to imported jets, and the AMCA, India's first attempt at a home-grown fifth-generation stealth fighter.

Tejas Mk2 — the near-term step up

The Tejas Mk2 (Medium Weight Fighter) is a larger, longer-range, more heavily armed evolution of the Tejas, powered by the more powerful GE F414 engine — crucially, with a 100% transfer-of-technology agreement concluded in 2026, so a version of that engine can eventually be made in India. The prototype completed its formal rollout in early 2026 and is approaching its maiden flight (targeted mid-2026). A production order, when it lands, could run to well over ₹1 lakh crore across a planned fleet — a future backbone order for HAL once flight-testing and certification are done.

AMCA — the fifth-generation prize, with a twist

The Advanced Medium Combat Aircraft (AMCA) is India's planned twin-engine stealth fighter — the country's bid to join the small club of nations that build fifth-generation jets. The programme is funded and moving: prototype rollout is targeted for late 2026 / early 2027, first flight around 2028, and induction around 2034. Engine talks (the GE F414 for early jets, an indigenous engine later) are underway.

The important nuance — AMCA is not automatically HAL's: in a notable break from the past, the government opened AMCA's development-and-build partner selection to competition rather than handing it to HAL by default. In early 2026, consortia led by Tata (TASL), Bharat Forge and L&T were reported as the shortlisted contenders, with HAL's bid not among the selected integrators. HAL remains central to Indian military aviation and will likely participate in AMCA's ecosystem, but the days of automatic nomination on every flagship programme are ending — a genuine shift in HAL's competitive moat worth watching.
In plain terms — a long runway, but no longer a guaranteed monopoly on the future

The roadmap is what gives HAL a growth story that stretches decades, not years: finish Mk1A, ramp Mk2, then AMCA — a near-continuous pipeline of combat-aircraft work, each programme worth tens of thousands of crores and each spawning a multi-decade overhaul annuity behind it. That is the bull case for HAL as a compounder, not just a backlog. The honest caveat is the AMCA twist: by inviting private players (Tata, L&T, Bharat Forge) to compete for the crown-jewel fifth-gen programme, the government has signalled that HAL's monopoly on India's future fighters is no longer guaranteed. HAL still has the fleets, the overhaul base and the Tejas line — but the competitive landscape a decade out looks more contested than its past suggests.

07

The overhaul annuity — HAL's quiet profit engine

The least glamorous part of HAL — and arguably the most valuable.

New fighters make headlines, but the bigger and steadier slice of HAL's revenue comes from Repair & Overhaul (ROH) — the recurring work of maintaining, upgrading and rebuilding the aircraft and engines already in service. Every military aircraft must be periodically stripped down to its components, inspected, rebuilt and re-certified; engines must be overhauled on a fixed cycle. For the hundreds of Su-30MKIs, Jaguars, MiGs, Mirages, Hawks, ALH Dhruvs and (increasingly) Tejas in Indian service, the natural — often the only — place to do that work is HAL. In FY26, ROH made up roughly two-thirds of revenue and is the most stable, high-margin part of the business.

Why the overhaul business is so valuable

Three features make ROH the jewel in HAL's model. First, it is recurring and predictable: aircraft and engines come back for deep maintenance on fixed cycles, so the work arrives like clockwork regardless of whether new orders are flowing — it smooths out the lumpiness of the build business. Second, it is near-captive and high-margin: HAL holds the drawings, tooling, licences and clearances, so it faces almost no competition on Indian military fleets and can price the work richly. Third, and most powerfully, it is self-reinforcing: every new aircraft HAL builds today becomes an overhaul customer for the next thirty-plus years, so the manufacturing backlog is quietly seeding decades of future ROH revenue. In effect, HAL is laying down an ever-larger installed base that it alone can service — the closest thing in heavy industry to a subscription business. Importantly, while engine-supply delays can stall new builds, they don't stop the existing fleet from needing overhaul — so the ROH annuity keeps the lights on even when the Tejas line is jammed.

08

Execution and delivery delay — the key risk

For most companies the risk is "will the orders come?" For HAL it is "can it deliver?"

This is the section that matters most, because HAL's risk profile is the inverse of a typical company. Demand is essentially assured — the air force is desperately short of aircraft, the orders are signed, and the backlog is enormous. The whole bear case therefore lives in one word: execution. Can HAL actually build and deliver the ₹2.55 lakh crore of contracted work on schedule, and convert it into recognised revenue and cash? Recent history says: only partly, and often late.

Why aircraft slip — the anatomy of a delay

  • The engine bottleneck. The most visible cause — the GE F404 shortage (§05) — has left ~30 finished Tejas airframes grounded and the Mk1A schedule running roughly two years late. One imported component can paralyse a whole line.
  • Complex global supply chains. Modern fighters and helicopters use thousands of parts from dozens of countries; a delay in avionics, seekers, castings or sub-systems anywhere ripples through to a missed delivery.
  • Sheer programme complexity. First-of-type aircraft (Mk1A, Mk2, AMCA) involve flight-testing, certification and design changes that routinely run longer than planned — defence aerospace is hard everywhere, not just in India.
  • Revenue recognition lags the build. Because HAL books much of its revenue on delivery/acceptance, airframes built but not handed over (awaiting engines) don't show up in the income statement — so the order book can swell while revenue growth looks pedestrian (FY26 revenue grew just ~7% despite a record backlog).
The bear's core argument: a giant order book is only worth what you can convert. If HAL keeps adding orders faster than it can deliver them — held back by engines, supply chains and complexity — then the backlog becomes a promise the market has already paid for but that arrives years late. The gap between the ₹2.55-lakh-crore book and ~₹33,000-crore-a-year of actual delivery is the central tension in the stock: bulls see locked-in growth, bears see a conversion problem.
How to weigh it — the optimistic and the realistic read

The optimistic read: delivery delays are largely a timing problem, not a demand or quality problem. The aircraft are built and flying; the moment engines arrive, a wall of finished jets converts to revenue, and the missed deliveries are recovered rather than lost. On this view, slippage defers HAL's earnings but doesn't destroy them, and the very long backlog gives plenty of runway to catch up. The realistic read: delays have real costs — they push revenue and cash recognition years to the right, they invite government and air-force displeasure (the Ministry of Defence has publicly warned HAL over missed Tejas timelines), they tie up working capital in built-but-unbilled inventory, and at a premium valuation the market punishes any quarter where the conversion stalls. The truth sits in between: HAL's growth is high-quality and contracted, but its pace is genuinely uncertain — and for a stock priced for execution, pace is what moves the price.

09

Cash-rich PSU economics — and exports

The quiet reason HAL is so valuable — plus a small, strategic second engine.

Most manufacturers must fund their work: buy materials and pay staff now, get paid later, and borrow to bridge the gap. HAL frequently runs the opposite way. On large defence contracts the customer pays advances — money up front, before HAL has built much — and that, combined with milestone payments, can leave HAL holding the customer's cash. The result is a balance sheet most industrials can only dream of: near-zero debt, a vast net-cash pile, and at times negative working capital.

Why customer advances change everything

Imagine a builder who is paid before he starts the job. He doesn't need a bank loan to buy materials — the client's money funds the work — and any cash he hasn't spent yet sits in his account earning interest. That is HAL's "negative working capital" in plain terms: defence advances and progress payments mean cash often arrives ahead of spending. The consequences are profound. HAL needs no debt, so it pays no interest and is immune to rate cycles; it earns substantial interest income on its ₹42,000-plus-crore deposit hoard, which materially flatters profits; and it can self-fund its R&D, capacity expansion and a generous dividend — all without diluting shareholders or borrowing. For a government that values both sovereign capability and a steady dividend stream, this is close to an ideal asset: HAL paid total dividends of ₹45 per share for FY26 (₹35 interim + ₹10 final).

The honest wrinkle: the same engine-delay dynamic that hurts revenue also ties up cash — built-but-undelivered aircraft sit in inventory as work-in-progress, and as HAL scales its self-financed build programmes, working-capital days can creep up and free cash flow can become lumpy. It remains hugely cash-generative and debt-free, but the days of permanently negative working capital may ease as the build mix grows — worth watching in the cash-flow statement, not just the profit line.

Exports — a small base, a strategic ambition

HAL is overwhelmingly a domestic supplier, but exports are a deliberate, growing second engine. It sells aircraft, helicopters, structures and overhaul services abroad — recent examples include Do-228 utility aircraft (e.g. to Guyana) and ALH Dhruv helicopters — and it builds aerospace structures and components for global majors. Exports are still a small fraction of revenue, but they matter for two reasons: they diversify HAL beyond the Indian government, and they align with India's national defence-export ambition (a stated target running into the tens of thousands of crores). The bull case treats exports as a free option on top of the captive domestic engine; the realist notes they remain tiny and that global markets are far more competitive than HAL's cosy home niche.

10

Record profit, fortress balance sheet — at a premium, defence-theme multiple.

The valuation debate is whether a ~32× earnings multiple already prices in years of indigenisation-driven optimism, or leaves room for the backlog to convert.

How to think about the ~32× multiple

At roughly ₹2.93 lakh crore market cap on ~₹9,116 Cr of FY26 profit, HAL trades around 32× trailing earnings — rich in absolute terms and above its own history, though more moderate than peer Bharat Electronics (~50×). The multiple is paying for four things: (1) visibility — a ₹2.55-lakh-crore order book locking in ~7–8 years of revenue; (2) quality — ~25% ROE, ~30% margins, a debt-free balance sheet stuffed with cash; (3) the indigenisation supercycle — a multi-year, policy-and-shortage-driven demand story with Mk2 and AMCA still ahead; and (4) scarcity — there are few pure-play, large-cap ways to own India's aircraft build-out. Bulls argue a near-monopoly compounder with a decade of contracted work deserves a premium. Bears counter that the order book is racing ahead of HAL's ability to convert it (revenue grew only ~7%), that one foreign engine supplier holds the deliveries hostage, and that the AMCA competition chips at the long-run moat — so the multiple leaves thin cushion for execution stumbles.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A national champion with a generation of demand — priced for it to deliver.

HAL is one of the cleanest structural stories in Indian large-caps: the near-monopoly indigenous builder of military aircraft, with demand all but guaranteed by a chronically under-strength air force and a national "buy Indian" mandate, a record backlog, fat margins, and a fortress balance sheet. Unusually, the debate is barely about whether the orders come — they will. It is about execution, supply-chain dependence, the competitive shift on future programmes, and how much optimism is already in the price.

The bull case

  • Generational demand visibility — ₹2.55 L Cr order book = ~7–8 years of revenue locked in; the IAF's ~11-squadron shortfall must be filled at home.
  • Near-monopoly on built fleets — the default builder of indigenous fighters and helicopters, and the only practical overhaul house for India's military fleets.
  • The ROH annuity — ~two-thirds of revenue is recurring, high-margin overhaul that grows with every aircraft delivered; smooths the lumpy build cycle.
  • Fortress balance sheet — debt-free, ~₹42,000 Cr of cash, customer-advance-funded; ~25% ROE; self-funds R&D, capex and a ₹45 dividend.
  • Decade-long roadmap — Mk1A → Mk2 → AMCA keeps the build line full into the 2040s, each programme seeding new overhaul revenue.

The bear case

  • Execution is the whole story — a giant book is only worth what HAL can deliver; revenue grew just ~7% as deliveries lagged the backlog.
  • Hostage to one engine supplier — ~30 Tejas built but grounded awaiting GE F404 engines that ran ~2 years late; near-term revenue depends on a foreign firm.
  • The moat on future jets is contested — HAL was not selected as the AMCA development integrator; Tata/L&T/Bharat Forge now compete for the fifth-gen crown.
  • One customer, one budget — overwhelmingly dependent on the Indian government for demand, pricing and (as ~71.6% owner) any stake-sale overhang.
  • Full valuation & seasonality — ~32× earnings prices in conversion; Q4-heavy billing and lumpy orders leave little room for a delivery stumble.
The next chapter for HAL is not about whether the orders come — an air force flying 31 squadrons against a sanctioned 42 all but guarantees them — but about whether India's national aircraft factory can build fast enough to convert a generational backlog into delivered jets and cash.
The bottom line

HAL is a structurally advantaged national champion — the default builder of India's military aircraft, with demand underwritten by a chronic fighter shortage, a record backlog, rich margins, and a balance sheet that funds its own growth and dividend. The demand risk is unusually low; the open questions are execution, supply-chain dependence, the competitive shift on next-generation programmes, and valuation. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers: deliveries (do GE's engines arrive, and does the wall of built-but-undelivered Tejas finally convert to revenue?), revenue conversion (does growth re-accelerate toward the order book's promise?), and the AMCA outcome (does HAL keep a central role in India's fighter future, or cede it to private rivals?). Get deliveries flowing and the backlog compounds into years of high-quality growth; let the engine bottleneck and execution drag persist, and a ~32× multiple has room to give back.
