Astra Microwave, the RF plumbing inside India's radars and missiles

A complete walk through the Hyderabad firm that makes the radio-frequency and microwave hardware — the transmit/receive modules, antennas and subsystems — that generate, beam and read the high-frequency signals at the heart of radar, missile seekers and electronic warfare. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Astra Microwave Products designs and builds radio-frequency (RF) and microwave hardware — the electronics that generate, transmit, receive and process the very high-frequency signals used by radar, missiles, electronic-warfare systems and defence communications. It does not build the finished radar or the finished missile. It builds the RF guts that go inside them — the transmit/receive (T/R) modules, antennas, converters, amplifiers and subsystems — and hands them to the organisations that integrate everything into a working weapon: chiefly DRDO (India's defence R&D labs) and Bharat Electronics (BEL). It is one of the specialist suppliers that make the parts of India's defence-electronics machine that almost nobody outside the industry has heard of, yet without which the machine does not switch on.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is an order-book business, and the order book is what you watch. Astra exited FY26 with a ~₹2,610 Cr consolidated order book — a little over two years of revenue already contracted. Defence programmes are lumpy and slow: an award can be worth years of work, and a dry spell between awards can make one year look weak and the next look brilliant even though nothing about the business changed. The backlog, not any single quarter, is the true gauge of health.
  • It is climbing from cheap parts to expensive systems. Astra started life selling components — the lowest-value rung. Its whole strategy is to move up: from a bare RF component, to a populated subsystem, to a complete subsystem or system it delivers itself, plus a growing slug of exports. Same underlying RF know-how, several times the price captured. FY26 EBITDA margin of ~28.7% — high for a hardware maker — is the early evidence that the climb is working.
  • It rides India's defence indigenisation, at a steep price. Make-in-India and import-substitution in defence electronics give Astra a multi-year demand backdrop; FY26 revenue rose ~11% to ₹1,163 Cr and PAT ~26% to ₹193 Cr. But the market has already paid up hard — the stock trades near 90 times earnings on a ~₹16,300 Cr market cap, pricing in years of order wins and margin gains that still have to show up.
In plain terms

Every radar and every radar-guided missile has, buried inside it, a bank of small electronic modules that do the actual radio work — pushing out a microwave pulse, catching the faint echo that bounces back off an aircraft, and turning that echo into a signal a computer can read. Astra Microwave is one of the few Indian firms qualified to make those modules and the boxes around them. It is a parts-and-subsystems supplier to India's defence programmes, not a household weapons name — and its value is precisely that it is hard to replace: once its hardware is designed into a radar or a missile programme, it tends to stay there for the life of that programme. The investment question is whether the order book keeps growing, the mix keeps shifting toward higher-value systems and exports, and the margin holds — enough to justify one of the richest multiples in Indian defence.

01

RF & microwave 101: the eyes and ears of a weapon

No physics degree needed — just the handful of ideas that make this business make sense.

A radar works by shouting and listening. It sends out a burst of high-frequency radio energy — a microwave pulse — into the sky, and then it listens for the faint echo that bounces back off anything solid: an aircraft, a drone, an incoming missile. From the delay and the shift in that echo it works out how far away the object is, how fast it's moving and in which direction. A radar-guided missile does the same thing in miniature, carrying a small radar (a "seeker") in its nose to home in on the target. An electronic-warfare system does the opposite job — it listens for an enemy's radar signals and either warns of them or blasts out noise to jam them. Every one of these jobs is, at heart, generating, beaming and reading radio-frequency and microwave signals — and that is exactly the hardware Astra makes.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
RF / microwaveVery high-frequency radio energy — the physical carrier of a radar pulse or a communications link. "RF hardware" is the electronics that make, shape and read those signals. Astra's entire product world lives here.
T/R module"Transmit/Receive module" — a small brick of electronics that, on command, either transmits a slice of the radar pulse or receives the returning echo. This is Astra's crown-jewel product; a single modern radar face uses many hundreds to thousands of them.
AESA radar"Active Electronically Scanned Array" — a modern radar whose antenna is a flat panel tiled with T/R modules. Instead of a physically spinning dish, it steers its beam electronically. It is the reason T/R-module demand has exploded — an AESA face is, in effect, thousands of tiny radars.
SeekerThe little radar (or RF sensor) in a missile's nose that finds and tracks the target in the final seconds. Built from the same RF building blocks — amplifiers, antennas, converters — that Astra supplies.
EW"Electronic Warfare" — detecting, intercepting and jamming enemy signals. Needs highly sensitive RF receivers and high-power RF transmitters, another slice of Astra's catalogue.
SubsystemAn assembly one level up from a component — e.g. a complete transmitter, receiver or antenna array, ready to be integrated. Moving from components to subsystems (and to whole systems) is Astra's value-chain climb.
The T/R module is the atom of this whole story

Hold on to one image. Old radars used a single big transmitter and a mechanically spinning dish. A modern AESA radar throws that out: it uses a flat panel covered in hundreds to thousands of small T/R modules, each one a miniature transmitter-and-receiver, all working together to steer a beam by pure electronics — no moving parts. That design shift is the single most important tailwind Astra has, because it multiplied the number of RF modules inside every radar by an order of magnitude. Where a legacy radar needed one transmitter, an AESA face needs a wall of them. Astra makes exactly that wall. Every AESA radar, seeker and phased-array antenna the country builds is, from Astra's point of view, a bulk order for its most valuable product.

Where this hardware ends up

  • Radar — ground, naval, airborne and weapon-locating radars that detect and track aircraft, drones, ships and incoming projectiles. The biggest single end-use.
  • Missile systems — seekers, RF front-ends and onboard electronics for surface-to-air and other guided-weapon programmes (the Akash / QRSAM / air-defence family and beyond).
  • Electronic warfare — jammers, radar-warning receivers and signal-intelligence gear that detect and defeat hostile emissions.
  • Communications — secure high-frequency and satellite communication links for the armed forces.
  • Space, meteorology & civil — satcom hardware, weather-radar and sounding systems for the space agency and the met department, plus some civil/telecom work. (This slice is being demerged — see §08.)
02

What Astra actually makes: components, subsystems, systems

One RF skill-set, sold at three very different price points.

Astra's catalogue looks bewildering — amplifiers, converters, oscillators, antennas, phased arrays, transceivers, T/R modules, complete radar and EW subsystems — but it resolves into one simple idea. It is all RF and microwave hardware, and Astra sells it at three levels of completeness. The higher the level, the more of the value Astra keeps.

The physics barely changes from rung to rung — it is the same RF engineering. What changes is how much of the finished product Astra delivers, and therefore how much of the price it keeps. The whole equity story is the drift rightward along this row.

The product lines, decoded

  • RF & microwave components — the raw building blocks: amplifiers that boost weak signals, converters that shift frequencies, oscillators that generate them, filters and switches that clean and route them. Individually cheap, made in volume, and the foundation Astra's reputation was built on.
  • T/R modules & antenna arrays — the highest-volume high-value product. A single AESA radar consumes hundreds to thousands of T/R modules, so one radar programme translates into a very large, repeatable module order. Astra's ability to make these to spec, at yield, is its hardest-to-copy asset.
  • Subsystems & systems — complete transmitters, receivers, radar front-ends, EW assemblies and, increasingly, whole systems. Here Astra is not just a parts vendor; it is a design partner, which means stickier relationships and better margins. This is where management wants the revenue mix to go.
The one construct that only fits Astra: same module, three prices

Picture a single T/R module — one small brick of RF electronics. Astra can sell that brick three ways. It can ship the bare module to an integrator, and capture the price of a part. It can build that module into a populated antenna array or transmitter subsystem and deliver the assembly, capturing several times as much. Or it can wrap it inside a complete radar or EW system it delivers and supports itself, capturing a large multiple more — and often the follow-on service too. The underlying RF engineering is nearly identical at each step; the difference in what Astra pockets is enormous. That is why the phrase "order book grew" is not enough to judge this company. You have to ask what rung the new orders sit on. A billion rupees of bare-component orders and a billion rupees of system orders are the same headline and a completely different business.

03

The company, the history, and who controls it

A Hyderabad defence-electronics house — and a genuinely unusual ownership story.

Astra Microwave Products was founded in 1991 in Hyderabad by a group of engineers — several with roots in India's own defence and space labs — to build RF and microwave hardware indigenously at a time when almost all of it was imported. That origin matters: Astra grew up next to DRDO, ISRO and the DRDL/RCI missile complex in Hyderabad, and its business has always been to make the RF pieces those organisations could not, or preferred not to, build themselves. Over three decades it moved from a small component shop into a broad defence-electronics supplier with multiple facilities in and around Hyderabad.

Ownership & structure — read this twice

  • Almost no promoter left. The founder-promoter group holds only around 6–7% of Astra — extraordinarily low for an Indian defence company, where a controlling family or the government usually dominates. The bulk of the register is domestic and foreign institutions plus the public. Astra is, in effect, a professionally-run, widely-held company rather than a promoter's fiefdom.
  • What that means. There is no deep-pocketed promoter to backstop the balance sheet or force a strategy, and no single owner whose interests dominate — the board and professional management run it, and the market, not a family, sets its direction. It also means there is no promoter pledge or family-succession overhang, but equally no anchor shareholder to lean on if a capital raise or a crisis ever comes.
  • Balance sheet. Astra runs lightly geared, effectively net-cash at the operating level, funded by internal accruals and its equity base. That matters in a business where government customers pay slowly and working capital (inventory and receivables tied up in long defence contracts) is heavy — a clean balance sheet is what lets it carry that working capital without strain.
A genuine surprise: most people assume an Indian "defence champion" is either government-owned (like BEL or HAL) or tightly held by a founding family (like several newer defence names). Astra is neither. The founders have diluted to a single-digit stake, and the company is essentially owned by institutions and the public. It is one of the few pure-play defence-electronics firms in India that is genuinely ownerless in the promoter sense — run by professional management, answerable to a diffuse shareholder base.
04

Supplier to the integrators: where Astra sits in the food chain

Understand this one relationship and the whole customer list makes sense.

India's defence-electronics world has a clear hierarchy. At the top sit the integrators — organisations that design and assemble complete systems and answer to the armed forces: DRDO's laboratories (which develop radars, missiles and EW systems) and Bharat Electronics (BEL) (the state-owned giant that mass-produces defence electronics). Below them sits a tier of specialist suppliers who make the pieces the integrators design in but do not build themselves. Astra lives in that supplier tier — and being there is both its strength and its ceiling.

Why "qualified supplier" is a moat, not a weakness

It is tempting to see a parts supplier to a few big buyers as a fragile position — a vendor squeezed by powerful customers. In defence RF, the opposite is closer to the truth. Before a T/R module or a subsystem can go into a radar or a missile, it has to be qualified — tested and certified to survive vibration, heat, shock and the exacting reliability standards of a weapon that must work first time. That qualification takes years and money, and once Astra's hardware is designed into a programme, ripping it out to re-qualify a rival is expensive and slow. So the relationship is sticky in Astra's favour: the integrator depends on the qualified supplier at least as much as the supplier depends on the integrator. The flip side is real and covered in the risks — a few large government buyers still hold the purse strings, and a programme delay or budget shift lands squarely on Astra.

05

The order-book engine: why the backlog is the business

The single most important idea in the case — and the reason quarter-watching misleads.

Astra is not a business you can judge by a single quarter, because its revenue is the tail-end of a process that started years earlier. A defence programme is awarded, Astra qualifies and builds hardware over a long cycle, and revenue is recognised as it delivers — often lumpily, sometimes bunched into one quarter, sometimes stalled by a customer's programme slippage. The number that actually tells you whether the machine is healthy is the order book, read alongside how fast new orders are replacing delivered ones (book-to-bill).

A book worth ~2.2× annual revenue gives multi-year visibility — but visibility is not the same as timing. When those orders convert to revenue depends on programme schedules Astra only partly controls, which is exactly why results swing year to year.

This is why Astra's reported numbers can look erratic even when the underlying business is fine. A quarter can be soft simply because a customer's programme slipped a milestone and a delivery moved a few months; the next quarter can jump because two deliveries land together. Reading those swings as "the business is deteriorating / accelerating" is the classic mistake with a lumpy defence supplier. The steady signals are the order book (is the pipeline growing?), book-to-bill (are fresh awards outpacing deliveries?), and the mix and margin of what's in the book.

The mechanism: one AESA programme = a wall of modules = years of Astra work

Here is how a single award becomes years of revenue. Suppose a radar programme calls for AESA faces, each tiled with a couple of thousand T/R modules, across a fleet of platforms. That one programme can translate into tens of thousands of modules Astra must qualify, build and deliver over several years — plus the antennas and transmit/receive subsystems around them. So an order-book number is really a stack of these multi-year commitments layered on top of each other. When you see the backlog rise, you are watching future years being pre-loaded; when you see it flatten, you are watching a gap open between programmes. The book-to-bill ratio is the early-warning gauge — above 1.0, the future is being refilled faster than the present is being consumed; below 1.0 for long, and a revenue air-pocket is coming even if today's quarter looks fine.

06

Climbing the value chain: from parts to systems to exports

The strategic spine — and the reason margins can keep rising.

Everything in Astra's plan points in one direction: capture more of the finished product. A firm that only sells components is a price-taker at the bottom of the food chain, forever squeezed on cost. A firm that delivers complete subsystems and systems — and sells them abroad — designs itself into the customer's product, earns design-led margins, and builds relationships that outlast any single order. Astra has been walking up that ladder for years, and the FY26 margin is the scoreboard telling you it's working.

Illustrative only — the point is the shape, not the exact widths. Each rung up the ladder captures more of the finished value from broadly the same RF know-how, which is how a hardware maker sustains a ~28–29% EBITDA margin rather than the low-teens of a commodity parts vendor.

Three levers push Astra up the ladder at once. Product depth — as it masters more of the signal chain, it can offer complete transmit/receive assemblies and radar front-ends rather than loose parts. Design-in — the deeper it goes, the more it co-develops with DRDO and BEL, which locks it into programmes. And exports — selling RF hardware to foreign OEMs both diversifies the customer base away from Indian government timing and validates Astra's quality on a world stage, which in turn strengthens its hand at home.

Why exports are the tell to watch

Domestic defence demand can be explained away — a captive market, a government that has to buy local, a tailwind that flatters everyone in the sector. Exports cannot be explained away. When a foreign defence OEM chooses Astra's RF hardware, it is doing so in an open contest against global suppliers, on merit, with no indigenisation mandate helping. That is why the export share of the order book is the cleanest single read on whether Astra is genuinely climbing the value chain or merely riding India's budget. A rising export mix says the RF engineering is world-class; a stagnant one says the company is still, at heart, a domestic-programme vendor. It is the difference between a supplier the world wants and a supplier India is obliged to use.

07

The indigenisation wave that lifts the whole sector

The structural tailwind — real, multi-year, and not unique to Astra.

Astra's demand backdrop is one of the strongest in Indian industry right now, and it comes from policy as much as from geopolitics. For decades India imported the bulk of its defence electronics. A deliberate national push — Make-in-India, import-substitution lists, offset rules, and a preference for indigenous content in defence procurement — has been steadily forcing that spend onshore. For a qualified domestic RF supplier, that is a tide that lifts the order book without Astra having to win share from anyone.

The honest caveat about a policy tailwind

A tailwind that lifts everyone lifts your rivals too. Indigenisation is real and durable, but it is not proprietary to Astra — every listed defence-electronics name is riding the same wave, and the market knows it, which is precisely why the whole sector trades at rich multiples. The tailwind explains why the sector is exciting; it does not explain why Astra specifically should win. That answer has to come from the things only Astra controls: its qualified position inside specific programmes, its climb toward higher-value systems, and its export traction. When judging Astra, separate the part of the story that is "India is spending on defence" (true of the whole peer group) from the part that is "Astra is capturing more value per programme and selling abroad" (the part that could actually justify a premium within the group).

The tailwind also has limits worth naming. Defence budgets are set annually and can be reprioritised; large programmes slip for reasons that have nothing to do with the supplier; and payment from government buyers is famously slow, which ties up working capital even when order flow is strong. The wave is real, but it arrives in irregular sets, not a steady swell — another reason the order book, not the quarter, is the gauge that matters.

08

The space demerger: splitting off the slower rocket

A structural change FY26 shareholders need to understand.

In FY26 Astra's board approved a significant corporate action: demerging its Space, Meteorology & Hydrology business into a separately listed company (proposed as Astra Space Technologies), with existing Astra shareholders receiving shares in the new entity on a 1:1 basis. In plain terms, the group is being cut into two focused pieces — a core defence-RF company, and a standalone space/met/hydrology company — and shareholders will end up owning both directly rather than one blended whole.

The demerged business is the smaller slice: roughly ₹150–160 Cr of FY26 revenue (around an eighth of the group) with its own order backlog of a few hundred crore. It covers satcom hardware, weather-radar and sounding/hydrology systems — genuinely different customers (the space agency, the met department) and a different growth rhythm from the defence core.

Why split a working business in two?

The logic of a demerger like this is focus and visibility. Blended inside one company, a fast, high-margin defence-RF engine and a steadier space/met business get valued as a lumpy average — investors who want one may not want the other, and neither is seen clearly. Split apart, each can be run, funded and valued on its own terms: the defence core as a pure indigenisation-and-exports play, the space/met arm as a distinct government-technology business that may attract its own following as India's space activity grows. For a shareholder, the practical effect is that the ~₹16,000-crore figure you see quoted for "Astra" today is really pricing a company that is about to become two — so as the demerger completes, judge the defence core on its own numbers, not on the combined entity's history.

Watch the mechanics: demergers change the share count, the reported financials and the comparison base. Once the split is effective, Astra's "revenue" and "order book" will refer to the slimmer defence-focused company, and the historical figures in this primer (which are the combined group's FY26 numbers) will not be like-for-like with post-demerger reporting. Track the record date, the scheme's approval milestones, and how the order book and margin are restated for the continuing business.

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, richly-valued defence-electronics peer group — with one giant in the middle.

Astra competes in a defence-electronics landscape that has, in a few short years, gone from sleepy to feverishly popular with investors. The key relationships to hold in mind: Astra is a supplier to BEL as much as a rival to it, and it sits alongside a cluster of newer listed private defence names that the market lumps together — sometimes unfairly, because they do quite different things.

The competitive tension to hold in mind: BEL is both the customer and the threat

The most important relationship on that list is also the most double-edged. BEL is Astra's largest kind of customer — the integrator that designs Astra's modules into systems and buys them in volume. But BEL is a vast, well-funded organisation with every incentive to make more of its own RF content in-house over time. So the same entity that drives a big chunk of Astra's order book could, in principle, become the reason a future programme buys fewer Astra modules. This is the structural knife-edge under the whole thesis: Astra's climb to higher-value subsystems and systems is not just about earning more margin — it is about staying indispensable enough that the integrators keep designing it in rather than replacing it. A supplier that only sells commoditised parts is the easiest to in-source; a supplier that co-develops complete subsystems is far harder to dislodge. The value-chain climb and the survival strategy are the same move.

Financials & valuation

Two features stand out. First, margin quality: an EBITDA margin near 29% is unusually high for a hardware manufacturer, and it has been expanding — direct evidence that the value-chain climb is showing up in the P&L, not just the strategy deck. Second, balance-sheet safety: with a lightly-geared, effectively net-cash position, Astra can carry the large inventory and slow government receivables that defence contracting demands without financial strain — a quiet but real advantage in a working-capital-heavy trade.

Against that sits the valuation. At roughly 90 times trailing earnings on a ~₹16,300 Cr market cap for a company earning ~₹193 Cr, Astra is priced not for what it did but for years of what it is expected to do — a growing order book, a richer mix, and rising exports, all delivered without a stumble. Management's own near-term guidance is more measured than the multiple (revenue growth in the low-double-digits, not a moonshot), which sharpens the gap between price and near-term fundamentals.

The metric that settles the thesis: order book × mix × margin

There is no single ratio that decides Astra, but there is a single compound. The case is proven or broken by the trajectory of three linked numbers, read together each year: the order book and book-to-bill (is the multi-year pipeline still growing faster than it is being consumed?), the share of higher-value system/subsystem and export revenue (is the company climbing the ladder, or stuck selling parts?), and the EBITDA margin (is the climb actually converting into economics?). If all three keep improving — book growing, mix richening, margin holding near or above the high-20s — then a premium multiple is arguable, because Astra is becoming a genuinely differentiated systems-and-exports company rather than a component vendor. If the order book flattens, the mix stalls at modules, or margin slips back toward the low-20s, then a ~90× stock is holding a valuation the business is no longer earning. Everything else — a soft quarter, a strong quarter, a delivery that slipped a month — is noise around those three lines.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality niche supplier in a real boom — asked to grow into an extraordinary price.

Astra Microwave enters FY27 with a strong hand: a record order book, expanding margins, a clean balance sheet, a structural indigenisation tailwind, and a demerger that will sharpen its identity as a defence-RF pure-play. What it does not have is any margin of comfort in its valuation. The whole debate reduces to whether a qualified RF supplier climbing toward systems and exports can keep compounding fast enough to earn a multiple that already assumes it will.

The bull case

  • Indispensable qualified position — RF hardware designed into radar, missile and EW programmes is sticky; re-qualifying a rival is slow and costly, so Astra tends to stay in once it's in.
  • Structural indigenisation demand — import-substitution, rising defence capex and the AESA shift multiply RF content per platform for years.
  • Mix & margin climbing — the move from components to subsystems, systems and exports is already visible in a ~29% EBITDA margin that keeps expanding.
  • Export validation — winning foreign OEM work on merit diversifies the customer base and proves world-class RF capability.
  • Fortress balance sheet — effectively net-cash, able to carry heavy defence working capital without strain, no promoter pledge overhang.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — near 90× earnings prices in years of flawless execution; even good results can disappoint a price this high.
  • Customer concentration & government dependence — a handful of buyers (DRDO, BEL, MoD); a programme delay or budget reshuffle hits revenue hard.
  • Order lumpiness & slow payment — defence orders convert unevenly and government customers pay slowly, swinging results and tying up working capital.
  • BEL in-sourcing — the largest customer can build more RF content itself over time, eroding Astra's addressable demand.
  • Qualification, technology & export-clearance risk — new programmes need fresh qualification; export deals face clearance hurdles; competition from Data Patterns, Paras, Zen and others for the same investor rupee.
The bottom line

Astra Microwave is a rare thing: a genuinely capable, ownerless RF specialist embedded inside India's most important radar and missile programmes, with a margin profile most hardware makers never see and a balance sheet that can carry the trade's heavy working capital. The business is not the question. The price is. At near 90 times earnings the stock has already been paid for a decade of climbing the value chain — and the way to check whether that payment was justified is not to watch the next quarter's revenue print, but to trace three lines across the next several years: the order book and book-to-bill, the export-and-systems share of that book, and the EBITDA margin. If those three keep rising as the space demerger completes and the defence core stands on its own, Astra grows into the story its shareholders have already bought. If any of them stall, the market will re-price a component-and-subsystem supplier back toward what a component-and-subsystem supplier is worth. The order-award cadence over the coming financial years — not the tape between now and then — is where this gets settled.
