Ramkrishna Forgings, where American freight demand sets a Jamshedpur order book

India's second-largest forging company makes the load-bearing steel bones of heavy trucks — axle beams, crankshafts, knuckles, differential cases — and ships roughly a third of them overseas, much of it into the North American Class-8 truck market. This is a walk through how hot steel becomes a safety-critical part, why the money is made in the machining that follows the hammer, and what a 20-year, ₹12,227 crore order to forge 15.4 lakh railway wheels does to a company built on the truck cycle.

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Ramkrishna Forgings (RKFL) takes bars of alloy steel, heats them until they glow, and squeezes them between dies under thousands of tonnes of force until they take the shape of a truck part. It is India's second-largest forging company after Bharat Forge, run from Kolkata with its manufacturing cluster in and around Jamshedpur, Jharkhand. Its products are the pieces of a heavy vehicle that absolutely must not break: front axle beams, crankshafts, steering knuckles, differential cases, gear blanks, hubs and shafts. Increasingly it doesn't just forge them — it also machines them to finished tolerance, which is where the better money lives.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Roughly a third of revenue leaves India — and a majority of that lands in North America. In FY26 domestic sales were 68.4% of revenue, exports the remaining ~31.6%. Within exports, management has described North America at roughly 55–60% and Europe at 30–40%. That means the single biggest external swing factor on this Indian company's order book is the health of the US Class-8 heavy-truck market — a market whose build rates routinely move 30–40% from one year to the next.
  • FY26 was a year of operating repair and bottom-line pain at the same time. Revenue grew a modest 5% to ₹4,238 Cr, but EBITDA rose 15% to ₹642.70 Cr — and consolidated PAT was only about ₹71.8 Cr. Depreciation on a newly expanded asset base, interest on roughly ₹2,000 Cr of borrowings (long-term debt alone was ₹1,082.69 Cr at 31 March 2026) and tax consumed almost the entire operating profit. The fourth quarter was the turn: revenue ₹1,216.78 Cr (+28% YoY) and EBITDA ₹208.19 Cr (+111% YoY) at a 17.1% margin.
  • There is now a second, non-automotive company growing inside the first. The RKFL–Titagarh consortium holds a Ministry of Railways contract to manufacture and supply 15.4 lakh forged railway wheels over about 20 years, valued at ~₹12,227 Cr. Railways already moved from 4.6% to 7.5% of FY26 revenue, and wheel-plant production was slated to begin in Q1 FY27. A truck-cycle business is deliberately growing a government-contract annuity underneath itself.
In plain terms

A forging is a piece of steel that has been beaten into shape rather than poured into a mould. That distinction is not cosmetic — it is the entire reason this industry exists, and section 01 explains why. RKFL's business is to sell tonnes of that beaten steel, and its profitability depends far less on how many tonnes it ships than on how much value it has wrapped around each tonne before it leaves the gate. A raw forging is a commodity. A forging that has been heat-treated, machined to microns, inspected and shipped as a bolt-on finished part is a component — and it earns several times more per kilogram. FY26 shows what happens when that ladder is only half-climbed while the capex bill has already arrived in full.

01

Forging 101: why the strongest parts are beaten, not poured

No metallurgy degree needed — just one idea about how steel behaves.

There are three broad ways to turn raw steel into a shaped part, and they are not equivalent. You can cast it — melt the metal and pour it into a mould. You can machine it — start with a solid block or bar and cut material away until the shape emerges. Or you can forge it — heat the metal until it is soft but still solid, then hammer or press it between shaped dies until it deforms into the shape you want. Forging is the oldest of the three; it is also, for load-bearing parts, decisively the strongest.

The reason is grain flow. Steel is not a uniform jelly; at the microscopic level it has an internal grain structure, rather like wood. When you cast a part, the grain solidifies randomly and the piece is riddled with tiny voids and porosity where gas escaped. When you machine a part from bar stock, you cut through the grain lines — like sawing across the grain of a plank, you leave weak cross-sections exactly where the geometry changes. When you forge, the metal is squeezed while plastic, so the grain flows continuously around the contours of the part, following the shape instead of being interrupted by it. The result is a component with far better fatigue strength and impact resistance for the same weight of steel — and, critically, one that fails gradually and detectably rather than shattering.

Why this decides what RKFL sells

Now apply that to a loaded 40-tonne truck going over a pothole at 80 km/h. The front axle beam carries the whole weight of the cab and engine and absorbs every shock the road delivers, millions of cycles over a working life. A crankshaft converts thousands of combustion explosions per minute into rotation. A steering knuckle is the single joint between the wheel and the vehicle. If any of these fails, the truck does not limp — it crashes. Regulators, OEMs and insurers therefore treat these as safety-critical, load-bearing parts, and industry practice is overwhelmingly to forge them. That is the demand RKFL exists to serve: not "steel parts" generally, but specifically the handful of parts on a heavy vehicle where casting is not an acceptable answer.

One more structural feature matters. Forging is capital-heavy and energy-heavy: presses, furnaces, heat-treatment lines and machining cells cost hundreds of crores and depreciate whether or not they run. That gives the business enormous operating leverage — at high utilisation the incremental tonne is very profitable, and at low utilisation fixed costs devour the margin. It is the single reason a 5% revenue year and a 28% revenue quarter can produce such wildly different margins inside the same twelve months.

02

What Ramkrishna Forgings actually makes

The parts list, the customers, and the shape of the value chain.

RKFL's catalogue is best understood by walking down a heavy truck. Under the cab sits the front axle beam — a long I-section forging that is one of the heaviest single forged parts on the vehicle and a signature RKFL product. Inside the engine is the crankshaft. At each front corner is a steering knuckle. Along the driveline are differential cases, gear blanks, pinions, spiders, yokes, shafts and hubs. Beyond trucks, the same presses make parts for tractors and farm equipment, off-highway and construction machines, passenger vehicles, and — increasingly — industrial, oil-and-gas and railway applications.

Who buys these parts

  • Global commercial-vehicle OEMs and Tier-1s — the export book. These are truck makers and driveline specialists in North America and Europe. Qualification takes years: audits, sample runs, fatigue testing, PPAP-style approvals. Once through, the part number is effectively sole-sourced for the life of the platform, which is why a single win can be quoted as a multi-year order — RKFL announced a $220 million North America order in February 2024, for example.
  • Indian CV makers — Tata Motors, Ashok Leyland, and the driveline and axle suppliers around them, plus tractor and off-highway manufacturers. This is the domestic core: closer, faster-cycling, lower-realisation but steadier.
  • Indian Railways, via the consortium — the newest and structurally most different customer. One buyer, a 20-year horizon, an administered price, and volumes fixed by contract rather than by a freight cycle.
  • Industrial, oil & gas and other engineering buyers — a smaller diversification bucket that uses the same presses when automotive demand dips.
Why qualification is the real moat

Nobody buys a front axle beam on price alone. Before a global truck maker will let an Indian forging shop supply a part that holds up its vehicle, it must audit the plant, approve the steel source, validate the die design, run fatigue tests on sample parts, and sign off the entire process. That takes years and costs both sides money. The payoff for RKFL is asymmetric: switching away from an approved supplier is painful enough that OEMs rarely do it for a few percent of price. The cost is equally real — RKFL cannot quickly redirect capacity to a new customer when an existing one cuts build rates, because the new customer's approval clock has not started. That is precisely the trap a cyclical exporter falls into when the American truck market turns down.

03

Tonnes × realisation: the equation this company is

The one arithmetic frame that explains RKFL's good years and its bad ones.

Strip away everything else and RKFL's revenue line is a two-term product: tonnes shipped × realisation per tonne. Both terms move, and they move for completely different reasons. Tonnes move with the truck cycle, plant commissioning and utilisation. Realisation per tonne moves with steel prices (which pass through, up and down), with currency, and — the term that actually matters to shareholders — with how much processing has been added to each tonne before it ships.

That third driver is the whole strategic argument. Two companies can ship the same 10,000 tonnes of steel and book wildly different revenue and profit, because one shipped rough forgings and the other shipped inspected, machined, ready-to-bolt components. The difference does not show up in tonnage at all. It shows up entirely in realisation per tonne.

What is company-specific and real is the direction of travel: RKFL has spent a decade adding machining capacity, adjacent aluminium forging, casting and — through acquisitions — component businesses precisely so that more tonnes leave the gate at rung 3 rather than rung 1.
The number to actually track

Take reported revenue for a quarter and divide it by reported tonnage for the same quarter. That single ratio — revenue per tonne shipped — is the most informative number RKFL publishes, because it silently answers three separate questions at once: is the product mix moving up the ladder, is pricing holding against OEM pressure, and is the export share (higher realisation) rising or falling? Volume growth without realisation growth means RKFL is running its presses harder to sell the same commodity. Realisation growth with flat volumes means the value-add strategy is working and the operating leverage will follow.

Why the same tonne is not the same tonne

  • Export tonnes usually out-realise domestic tonnes. Overseas customers typically buy heavier, more complex, more heavily machined parts and pay in hard currency. This is why the export share is not just a diversification statistic — it is a margin statistic.
  • Steel pass-through inflates and deflates realisation without touching profit. When alloy-steel prices rise, contractual indexation pushes realisation per tonne up and revenue with it, but the extra rupees flow straight through to the steel mill. A rise in realisation driven by steel is cosmetic; a rise driven by machining mix is real. Distinguishing them is the analytical work.
  • Railway wheels are a different tonne entirely. A forged wheel is a large, heavy, single-piece forging on a fixed multi-decade price. It will land in the tonnage figure and in realisation, but its economics are governed by a contract, not by a cycle.
  • Castings dilute realisation and that is fine. The 16,602 tonnes of FY26 castings sell for less per tonne than machined forgings. They exist to widen the quote basket and use the customer relationship, not to lift the average.
Tonnage tells you whether the plants are busy. Realisation per tonne tells you whether the last ten years of capex were worth doing.
04

The Class-8 truck cycle: an American variable on an Indian P&L

The exposure most Indian auto-ancillaries simply do not have.

Here is the fact that surprises people about Ramkrishna Forgings. A meaningful slice of its earnings is determined not by Indian GDP, not by Indian truck sales, and not by the monsoon — but by how much freight is moving on American highways. Roughly 31.6% of FY26 revenue was exported, and North America has been described as roughly 55–60% of that export book. Somewhere approaching a fifth of the company's revenue is therefore tied to the build rate of Class-8 trucks — the largest category of American heavy trucks, the eighteen-wheelers that haul freight between cities.

Class-8 is a famously violent cycle. Fleet operators buy trucks when freight rates are high and their existing tractors are aging; they stop buying, abruptly and almost completely, when freight rates soften. North American Class-8 build rates have historically swung by tens of percent year to year — far more than passenger-car production ever does. On top of that sits a distinctly American overlay: emissions-regulation pre-buys, where fleets rush to purchase trucks ahead of a new emissions standard that will make the next model year more expensive, pulling demand forward and leaving an air pocket behind it. Layer on trade policy — tariff actions on imported components can change the landed economics of an Indian-made axle beam overnight — and you have an exposure with three independent sources of volatility.

The surprise, stated plainly

An investor buying RKFL because they like the Indian commercial-vehicle story is, without necessarily realising it, also taking a position on US freight demand, US emissions-regulation timing and US trade policy. There is no comparable Indian auto-component company where an American cyclical variable sits this close to the top of the risk list. Management's own FY27 framing leans on it directly: growth in North America driven by Class-8 truck volumes, which the company has indicated it expects to stay strong until around Q3 CY27. That is not a throwaway line — it is a schedule, and the market will mark the company against it.

What the cycle does to the financials

Because forging is fixed-cost heavy, an export downturn hits twice. First, the lost tonnes: presses that were running for a North American programme fall idle, and the fixed depreciation and interest do not fall with them. Second, the lost mix: export tonnes are typically the higher-realisation, more-machined tonnes, so losing them drags realisation per tonne down even if domestic volumes hold. The reverse is equally powerful on the way up, which is exactly what Q4 FY26's 111% EBITDA jump on 28% revenue growth demonstrates — the same plants, the same people, a better order flow, and operating profit more than doubles.

The hedges that exist — and the one that doesn't

  • Europe partly offsets North America. The two truck markets do not move in perfect lockstep; European build rates respond to different freight, regulatory and economic conditions. A 30–40% Europe share within exports softens, but does not remove, the North American beat.
  • The domestic book is a genuine counterweight. At 68.4% of revenue, India is the larger half. Indian CV demand runs on infrastructure spending, replacement cycles and freight rates that have very little to do with Ohio.
  • Railways is the deliberate structural answer. A 20-year contract with fixed annual wheel volumes is close to the opposite of a truck cycle. This is the point of section 06.
  • What is not hedged is the currency-plus-tariff combination. A rupee move helps or hurts translated export realisation, and a tariff action changes the landed cost for the customer. Neither is within the company's control, and neither is fully offset by the domestic book.
05

The domestic book: two-thirds of the company, quietly

Indian commercial vehicles, tractors, and everything the presses fill up with.

Exports get the attention; India pays most of the bills. At 68.4% of FY26 revenue, the domestic business is the ballast — and it is deliberately broader than it was five years ago. It spans medium and heavy commercial vehicles, light commercial vehicles, passenger vehicles, tractors and farm equipment, off-highway and construction machinery, industrial applications, and now railways.

Indian CV demand has its own rhythm. Heavy-truck sales track infrastructure spending, mining and cement despatch, freight rates, financing availability and replacement cycles. Regulatory events matter here too — axle-load norms, scrappage policy, emission-standard transitions and the shift toward higher-tonnage vehicles all change both the number of trucks built and, importantly for a forger, the weight of forgings per truck. A heavier-rated axle needs a heavier beam. That is content-per-vehicle growth arriving without any market-share gain at all.

Where a forger's domestic growth really comes from

There are three distinct ways RKFL can grow its Indian revenue without the Indian truck market growing at all. It can win a new part number on an existing platform, taking share from another supplier or from an OEM's own in-house forge shop. It can move up the ladder on parts it already supplies — from selling a rough forging to selling a machined one, doubling revenue on the identical tonne. Or it can serve a heavier vehicle mix, where each truck simply consumes more forged steel. Only the fourth route — more trucks — depends on the market itself. This is why domestic revenue can grind higher through a flat CV year, and why a flat headline revenue number does not necessarily mean a flat business.

The non-automotive widening

RKFL has consistently pushed toward non-automotive work, and FY26 gave a visible marker: Q4 order intake of about ₹594 Cr was split roughly 56% automotive and 44% non-automotive. For a company historically defined by trucks, a near-even split in new orders is a meaningful directional signal. The non-auto bucket includes railways, industrial forgings, and applications where the same presses and heat-treatment lines can be redirected when automotive demand softens — which is the practical definition of a diversified fixed-cost base.

06

The railway-wheel bet: 15.4 lakh wheels, twenty years

The single largest strategic decision in the company's history.

In 2023 a consortium of Ramkrishna Forgings and Titagarh Rail Systems won a Ministry of Railways contract to manufacture and supply 15.4 lakh (1.54 million) forged railway wheels, valued at approximately ₹12,226.5 crore, under the Aatmanirbhar Bharat programme. The contract runs about 20 years, with a delivery ramp of roughly 40,000 wheels in year one, 60,000 in year two, and 80,000 per year thereafter, against a plant designed to build toward about 200,000 wheels a year at full capability. The joint venture vehicle, Ramkrishna Titagarh Rail Wheels, contracted its production line from the European machine-tool group FFG, and production was scheduled to begin in Q1 FY27 — with the first wheel targeted around May 2026.

Why India is buying forged wheels at all

A railway wheel takes brutal punishment: it carries tonnes of axle load, transmits traction and braking through a contact patch the size of a coin, and heats and cools thousands of times. Cast wheels are cheaper; forged wheels handle higher speeds and loads with better fatigue behaviour, which is why they are specified for LHB coaches, WAG-9 electric locomotives and Vande Bharat trainsets. India has historically had to import a large share of its forged wheels, which is precisely the dependency the Ministry of Railways set out to end. That is the policy logic behind handing a two-decade, ₹12,000 crore-plus contract to a domestic consortium rather than continuing to buy abroad.

What this actually changes for RKFL

  • It converts a cyclical revenue base into a partly contracted one. Wheel volumes are set by a delivery schedule, not by freight rates. That is a structurally different revenue quality from anything else RKFL sells, and it is the deliberate answer to the Class-8 problem.
  • It has a very long tail. Twenty years of specified volumes, plus the replacement demand that follows every wheel ever supplied, plus the credibility to bid for further railway work.
  • It is already visible in the numbers. Railways rose from 4.6% of FY25 revenue to 7.5% in FY26 — before serial wheel production even started. The base business already supplies railway forgings; the wheel plant is an order-of-magnitude extension of it.
  • It is capital-hungry and execution-hard. A greenfield wheel plant with an imported production line has to be built, commissioned, qualified against railway specification, and then run at yield. Every one of those steps has a slippage risk, and none of them earns revenue until the last one is cleared.
  • The customer is a government. Indian Railways is creditworthy but it is also a single, administratively driven buyer with its own procurement calendar and payment rhythm. Working-capital timing on a contract this size is not a footnote.

The machining ladder and the acquisition sprawl

How RKFL has been buying and building its way up the value chain — and what that costs.

Over the last several years RKFL has done something more ambitious than adding presses. It has assembled adjacent capabilities — machining, casting, aluminium forging, electric-drivetrain components, an overseas machining footprint — partly by building and partly by acquiring. The strategic logic is consistent throughout: capture more of the value that sits on top of each forged tonne, and be able to answer more of a customer's shopping list from one relationship.

The bill for all of this arrives before the benefit does

Every item in that table is an asset that begins depreciating and, if debt-funded, begins accruing interest, on the day it is capitalised — while the revenue it enables arrives only after ramp-up, qualification and customer approval. That timing mismatch is FY26's profit-and-loss statement. The plants are largely built; the utilisation, the qualification and the mix improvement are still arriving. Management's FY27 framing is exactly this: push utilisation to 80–85% across roughly 3.5 lakh tonnes of combined forging and casting capacity, hold capex to a maximum of about ₹400 Cr, and repay ₹400–500 Cr of debt. That is the language of a company that has finished spending and now has to prove the spending worked.

Integration is the underrated risk

Bolting acquired component businesses onto a forging company is not the same as buying more presses. Each acquired unit brings its own customers, its own margin profile, its own working-capital habits and its own management. Consolidated revenue rises immediately; consolidated return on capital only rises if the acquired operations are genuinely improved. For an outside reader, the practical test is simple: does group EBITDA margin move up as the acquired revenue is absorbed, or does the group average drift down toward the weakest unit? FY26's 15.2% full-year margin against a 17.1% exit quarter suggests the answer is still being written.

08

The company, the family, and the plants

A Kolkata head office, a Jharkhand manufacturing belt, and a promoter group holding a shade over 43%.

Ramkrishna Forgings was incorporated on 12 November 1981 and is registered at 23 Circus Avenue, Kolkata. Its manufacturing heart, however, is in and around Jamshedpur, Jharkhand — India's original steel town, which is not a coincidence: a forging company wants to sit next to its steel supply, its skilled metallurgical labour pool and its customers' plants. The company is promoted and run by the Jalan family, with Mahabir Prasad Jalan as chairman and Naresh Jalan as managing director, alongside a long-tenured professional board.

Why the Jamshedpur cluster matters more than it sounds

Forging is one of the few manufacturing activities where geography is still a hard constraint. The input is heavy (alloy steel bar), the process is energy-intensive, and the workforce needs genuine metallurgical skill that takes years to build and cannot be hired in a quarter. Sitting inside India's oldest steel-manufacturing ecosystem gives RKFL short inbound freight, a deep local supplier base for dies and tooling, and access to trained people — advantages that a well-funded new entrant cannot simply buy. It is also, in the same breath, a concentration risk: a large share of the group's productive capacity sits in one district of one state, exposed to the same power supply, the same labour environment and the same local disruptions.

Steel, freight, FX and the limits of "pass-through"

The input economics, and why calling steel a pass-through is only three-quarters true.

Alloy steel is by far RKFL's largest raw material. Contracts with major OEMs typically include some form of price indexation, so a rise or fall in steel prices is largely passed on to customers with a lag. This is genuinely how the industry works, and it is why a forging company's gross margin is more stable than its revenue would suggest. But "pass-through" is a convenient shorthand that conceals three real exposures.

  • The lag itself is a cost. Indexation clauses usually reset quarterly or on an agreed formula. In a quarter when steel spikes, RKFL absorbs the increase and recovers it later — squeezing margin in the interim and consuming working capital.
  • Not every customer indexes equally. The largest OEMs negotiate the tightest terms in both directions. Smaller customers and spot business carry more of the raw-material risk on RKFL's side of the table.
  • Energy and consumables are not indexed at all. Furnaces, presses and heat-treatment lines consume power and gas. Dies wear out. These costs sit squarely with the manufacturer.
Freight and currency: the export tax nobody prices

Shipping a container of forged axle beams from eastern India to a truck plant in the American Midwest is genuinely expensive, and ocean freight rates are volatile in ways no contract fully covers. That physical cost is one reason the Mexico machining facility matters strategically well beyond its size — a North American footprint shortens the distance between the last operation and the customer's line, and it changes the tariff classification of what actually crosses the border. Layered on top, roughly a third of revenue is invoiced in foreign currency while nearly all costs are in rupees. A stronger rupee compresses realisation on every export tonne without a single order being lost. Currency is not a rounding error for this company; it is a line item.

The OEM pricing squeeze

The other permanent pressure comes from the customers themselves. Vehicle OEMs run structured annual cost-reduction programmes and expect their suppliers to deliver productivity savings every year. For a component supplier this means realisation per part tends to drift downward in real terms over a platform's life, and the only durable defences are (a) moving up the value ladder so the part being priced is a bigger, more complex one, and (b) becoming hard enough to replace — through qualification, tooling and capability — that the negotiation stays civil. RKFL is pursuing both. Neither eliminates the annual conversation.

The comparison that actually matters

The most useful benchmark for RKFL is not a valuation multiple against Bharat Forge — it is EBITDA per tonne and return on capital employed against Happy Forgings and MM Forgings. Those two numbers cut straight through the differences in scale and product mix and answer the only question that matters for a capital-intensive manufacturer: for every rupee of press, furnace and machining line put into the ground, how much operating profit comes back out each year? A forger that grows tonnage while its return on capital falls is buying revenue, not building a business. FY26's combination of record capacity and ₹71.8 Cr of net profit is exactly the configuration that makes this comparison urgent rather than academic.

Financials & valuation

What the numbers say, what they cost, and the one metric that settles the argument.

FY26 was a year with two faces. The full-year picture was subdued: revenue up 5% to ₹4,238 Cr, EBITDA up 15% to ₹642.70 Cr (a ~15.2% margin), and consolidated PAT of roughly ₹71.8 Cr after a first half that included a 78% profit fall in Q1 and margin pressure in Q2. The exit was a different company: Q4 revenue ₹1,216.78 Cr (+28% YoY), EBITDA ₹208.19 Cr (+111% YoY), a 17.1% margin, and a Re 1 dividend declared.

The market's price on all this

As of mid-July 2026 the shares traded around ₹568, for a market capitalisation of roughly ₹10,276 Cr and a trailing P/E in the region of 143× (approx., as reported by market data providers on 19 July 2026). That headline multiple should not be read as a growth-stock valuation. It is arithmetic: a ₹10,276 Cr equity value divided by a depressed ₹71.8 Cr trailing profit produces an enormous number, and trailing profit was depressed precisely because depreciation and interest on newly built capacity landed before the utilisation did. Add roughly ₹2,000 Cr of debt and the enterprise value against ₹642.70 Cr of EBITDA sits somewhere near 19× (approx., derived) — expensive for a cyclical forger, but a different order of expensive from 143×.

How to think about a cyclical at a peak multiple. For an asset-heavy cyclical, the P/E is at its highest exactly when earnings are at their lowest, and at its lowest exactly when earnings peak. Annualising the Q4 FY26 run-rate — ₹208 Cr of quarterly EBITDA, roughly ₹830 Cr annualised — against the same debt and depreciation base produces a dramatically different earnings figure and therefore a dramatically different multiple. Whether that annualisation is legitimate is the entire debate, and it turns on one question: was Q4 the start of a recovery or a good quarter in a flat year?
The metric that settles this

Revenue per tonne shipped. Not tonnage, not headline revenue, not the order book. RKFL's whole case rests on the claim that it has moved from selling steel shapes to selling engineered components, and that claim is falsifiable in a single ratio published every quarter. If realisation per tonne rises while steel prices are flat or falling, the machining-and-mix strategy is working and the margin recovery is structural. If realisation is flat and only tonnage grows, the company has spent a decade of capital buying volume in a commodity — and the 17.1% quarter was cyclical weather, not climate. Three numbers corroborate the verdict: the railway/non-automotive share of revenue (is the diversification real?), net debt against the guided ₹400–500 Cr of FY27 repayment (is the cash actually arriving?), and return on capital employed (is the enlarged asset base earning?).

What management has put its name to

  • Utilisation of 80–85% in FY27 across roughly 3.5 lakh tonnes of combined forging and casting capacity.
  • Capex capped at about ₹400 Cr — a sharp step down from the build-out years, and the precondition for deleveraging.
  • Debt reduction of ₹400–500 Cr during FY27.
  • Mix of roughly 65% domestic / 35% exports, implying export growth outpacing domestic.
  • Rail-wheel JV production starting in Q1 FY27, with North American Class-8 volumes expected to hold up until around Q3 CY27.

Each of these is checkable within twelve months, which is unusually helpful. A company that guides to utilisation, capex, debt and mix simultaneously has given the market four independent scoreboards, and missing any one of them will be visible immediately.

12

Future, risks, and the two-sided case

What has to go right, what can go wrong, and where the argument actually lives.

RKFL enters FY27 having finished the expensive part. The presses are installed, the aluminium line is commissioned, the casting business is ramping, the Mexico machining facility is progressing, and the railway-wheel plant is at the threshold of production. What remains is the unglamorous work of filling that capacity, climbing the machining ladder, and paying down the debt that built it all.

The risks, set out plainly

  • The North American Class-8 cycle is the dominant risk, and it recurs. Roughly a fifth of revenue depends on American truck build rates, which historically swing violently, are distorted by emissions pre-buys, and can be reshaped overnight by tariff action on imported components. This risk never goes away; it only takes turns being quiet.
  • Customer concentration and structural pricing pressure. A small number of large OEMs account for a large share of the book, and every one of them runs an annual cost-down programme. Losing a platform is rare but expensive; conceding price is routine.
  • Input costs, currency and freight. Steel indexation covers most but not all of the raw-material risk, and covers none of the energy, die and consumable cost. Roughly a third of revenue is invoiced in foreign currency against a rupee cost base. Ocean freight rates are outside anyone's control.
  • Railway-wheel execution. A greenfield plant, an imported production line, a new metallurgical specification and a government customer — commissioning slippage, qualification delays and payment timing are all live risks. Q1 FY27 is a date, not a certainty, and the ramp starts at 40,000 wheels.
  • Leverage and the capex hangover. Long-term debt of ₹1,082.69 Cr, total debt around ₹2,000 Cr and 3.12× debt/EBITDA leave less room than a cyclical business ideally wants going into an uncertain export year. Interest and depreciation consumed most of FY26's operating profit; if the recovery is slower than guided, they will do so again.
  • Acquisition integration. Several bolt-on businesses have to be absorbed without dragging group margins or return on capital down. Consolidated revenue flatters this; consolidated ROCE does not.
  • Valuation. A cyclical exporter carrying real leverage, on an optically extreme trailing multiple, whose enterprise value already discounts a substantial recovery in both utilisation and mix.

The case for

  • The exit quarter was genuinely strong. +28% revenue and +111% EBITDA at a 17.1% margin, on the same asset base — evidence that the operating leverage is real and the fixed costs are now being covered.
  • The capex cycle is ending. Guidance of ≤₹400 Cr capex against ₹400–500 Cr of debt repayment means free cash flow should start showing up rather than disappearing into the ground.
  • Railways is a structural, contracted revenue stream. 15.4 lakh wheels over ~20 years is the opposite of a truck cycle, and railways is already 7.5% of revenue before the wheel plant runs.
  • The value-add ladder is a real, self-funded margin lever. Machining, aluminium, casting and the Mexico footprint all raise realisation per tonne without needing the market to grow.
  • Class-8 visibility into CY27. Management expects North American volumes to hold through roughly Q3 CY27, and has guided the export share back up to ~35%.
  • Depressed trailing earnings flatter the apparent multiple. On normalised utilisation the enterprise value looks materially less demanding than 143× trailing suggests.

The case against

  • FY26 revenue grew 5%. Against a large new capacity base, that is weak absorption — and one strong quarter does not establish a trend.
  • ₹643 Cr of EBITDA produced ₹72 Cr of profit. Nearly 89% of operating profit was consumed below the line. Until that ratio improves, capacity growth does not reach shareholders.
  • 3.12× debt/EBITDA into a cyclical export year is leverage carried into precisely the wrong kind of uncertainty.
  • The North American exposure is unhedgeable. A US freight downturn or a tariff action hits volume and mix simultaneously, and the domestic book cannot absorb redirected capacity quickly because of qualification lead times.
  • The wheel contract is long, shared and back-loaded. ₹12,227 Cr across ~20 years and two partners is a de-risking of the 2030s, not an FY27 earnings event — and the plant still has to qualify and yield.
  • Integration and ROCE. Multiple acquired businesses have yet to prove they lift group returns rather than dilute them.
What to watch, in order

First, revenue per tonne shipped each quarter, read against steel prices — the proof or disproof of the entire value-add thesis. Second, the wheel plant: whether the JV clears qualification and delivers against the year-one 40,000-wheel schedule, and how quickly Indian Railways pays. Third, net debt at each half-year against the ₹400–500 Cr FY27 repayment commitment. Fourth, North American Class-8 order intake and build rates, which are published monthly by industry trackers and will tell you what RKFL's export book looks like a quarter before RKFL does. Fifth, group EBITDA margin — whether the 17.1% exit quarter holds through a full year or reverts toward the 15.2% average.

The bottom line

Ramkrishna Forgings has spent a decade converting itself from a company that sold hot-pressed steel shapes into one that sells engineered components — adding machining, aluminium, castings, a North American footprint and, most consequentially, a two-decade contract to forge the wheels under India's trains. FY26 was the year the bill for that conversion arrived in full while only part of the benefit had landed: record capacity, ₹643 Cr of EBITDA, and ₹72 Cr of profit. The fourth quarter offered the first serious evidence that the two are about to meet.

The next twelve months have unusually specific markers. A forged railway wheel has to come off the JV's line and pass Indian Railways' qualification. Forty thousand of them have to be delivered in year one. Utilisation has to reach 80–85% across three and a half lakh tonnes of steel and casting capacity. And ₹400–500 crore of debt has to actually leave the balance sheet. None of those is a matter of narrative or sentiment — each is a date, a count, or a rupee figure that will either be reported or not. For a company whose fortunes have long been decided by freight rates on a continent it does not operate in, that is a refreshingly checkable list.
