The Anup Engineering, exotic metallurgy, made to order

A ground-up walk through one of India's most respected makers of critical process equipment — the heat exchangers, pressure vessels, reactors and towers that live inside a refinery or a chemical plant — how a company that welds titanium and zirconium to spec earns fat margins on made-to-order steel, why the whole story turns on India's process-industry capex cycle, and how a fabricator carved out of a denim group is climbing toward cryogenic, hydrogen and nuclear work.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

The Anup Engineering makes static process equipment — the large steel-and-alloy vessels that do the physical work inside a process plant. Its flagship product is the heat exchanger (the equipment that transfers heat between two fluids), alongside pressure vessels, reactors, columns and towers, and industrial centrifuges. These are not shelf products. Each is engineered and fabricated to order against a customer's exact specification — a specific size, a specific pressure, and very often a specific exotic metal — for refineries, petrochemical complexes, fertiliser plants, chemical makers, and pharma. It is Ahmedabad-based, and it was demerged from Arvind Ltd (the textiles group) and separately listed in 2019.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The value is in the metallurgy, not the tonnage. Anyone can cut and weld carbon steel. Anup's reputation is for building equipment in difficult materials — titanium, zirconium, tantalum, nickel alloys, duplex stainless, clad plate — to tolerances that most fabricators cannot hold. That is why it earns a ~20%+ EBITDA margin and ~20% return on capital on what is, on paper, a metal-bending business. The steel is a small part of the price; the engineering, the welding qualifications and the testing are the rest.
  • It is a capex-cycle business, and the cycle is turning up. Anup only gets an order when someone builds or expands a plant. India's process industries are in a spending upswing — refinery expansions, a specialty-chemicals and China+1 capex wave, fertiliser revamps — and export demand adds to it. That is what filled the order book to roughly ₹770 Cr entering FY27, on top of an enquiry pipeline management pegs near ₹1,200 Cr.
  • It just doubled its own capacity and moved up-spec. A new greenfield plant at Kheda takes group capacity to roughly 20,000 tonnes a year — enough, at full utilisation, to support well over ₹1,200 Cr of revenue versus the ~₹822 Cr done in FY26. And it is pushing into higher-complexity work — cryogenic, hydrogen-ready, and its first nuclear equipment order — that a bulk fabricator cannot touch.
In plain terms

Anup is a bespoke tailor working in steel. A plant owner arrives with a blueprint and a nasty requirement — "this vessel has to hold a corrosive acid at high pressure for twenty years, so it must be made of titanium" — and Anup designs, welds, X-rays, pressure-tests and ships the finished piece. Two things make it a good business rather than a job-shop: the customer cannot afford a leak in a live plant, so they pay for a maker they trust; and the hard materials keep low-cost rivals out. The catch is that Anup does not control its own demand — it rises and falls with how much India's refiners and chemical makers decide to spend on new plants, and a made-to-order book is only worth as much as the factory's ability to execute it on time and on margin.

01

Process equipment 101: the machines inside the plant

No engineering degree needed — just what these vessels do and why they are hard to make.

Walk into any refinery, fertiliser plant or chemical complex and most of what you see is not moving parts — it is static equipment: big steel drums, towers and coils that hold, heat, cool, mix and separate fluids under pressure. Anup makes exactly this class of hardware. Four product families cover almost the whole business, and it is worth understanding what each one does.

The equipment, in plain English
ProductWhat it does (and why it is hard)
Heat exchangerTransfers heat from one fluid to another without mixing them — e.g. using a hot process stream to pre-heat a cold feed, saving energy. The common "shell-and-tube" design packs hundreds of thin tubes into a shell; "air-fin coolers" reject heat to the air. Getting hundreds of tube-to-tubesheet welds leak-free is the craft. Anup's single largest product — over half of revenue.
Pressure vesselA sealed container built to hold gas or liquid safely at high pressure — a rupture in a live plant is catastrophic, so these are code-built, X-rayed and pressure-tested. The metallurgy and weld quality are the whole game.
ReactorThe vessel where the chemical reaction actually happens — often at high temperature, high pressure and in corrosive conditions, which is why reactors frequently demand exotic alloys or metal-clad construction.
Column / towerTall vertical vessels used to separate a mixture into its parts — the distillation towers that split crude oil into fractions, for example. Large, heavy, and fabricated in sections.
CentrifugeA rotating machine that separates solids from liquids by spinning — used heavily in chemicals and pharma. One of the few products Anup makes as a more standardised, repeat item.
Why "static equipment" is a genuinely good place to sit

This equipment has three features that make it attractive to manufacture. First, it is mission-critical and safety-critical — a plant owner will not gamble on the cheapest bidder for a vessel that could fail and shut the whole complex, so track record and certifications win the order, not price alone. Second, it is custom — each piece is designed for one plant, so there is no commodity spot market beating the price down. Third, it is certification-gated — to supply an oil major or an overseas client you need approvals (ASME "U" stamps, client vendor lists, welding qualifications for exotic metals) that take years to earn and act as a moat. Anup has spent decades collecting exactly those approvals.

Who buys this equipment

  • Oil & gas, refining & petrochemicals — the biggest buyer class: refinery expansions and new petrochemical trains need columns, exchangers and vessels by the hundred.
  • Chemicals & specialty chemicals — the fast-growing driver, as India builds out specialty-chemical capacity on the back of the China+1 shift.
  • Fertilisers — ammonia and urea plants are heat-exchanger- and vessel-heavy, and a wave of revamps has kept fertiliser demand alive.
  • Pharma, power, water, paper & pulp — a long tail of process industries, each needing the same core kit in smaller volumes.
  • New frontiers — cryogenic (LNG / industrial gases), hydrogen, aerospace and now nuclear and defence — higher-spec, higher-value work Anup is deliberately moving into.
02

What Anup actually builds — and how an order flows

From a customer's spec to a fabricated vessel on a truck — the model in one picture.

Anup is not an EPC contractor that builds whole plants, and it is not a bulk steel fabricator selling by the tonne. It sits in a specific, defensible spot: it takes a customer's design specification and turns it into a finished, tested, certified piece of equipment. Understanding the flow of a single order explains why the margins look the way they do.

Every stage is order-specific. There is no standard product coming off a line — which is why capacity is measured in tonnes of steel processed, not units, and why execution skill decides the margin on each job.

Because each order is engineered from scratch, the revenue mix skews to a handful of product families rather than dozens of SKUs. Heat exchangers dominate, with pressure vessels, reactors, columns and centrifuges filling out the rest.

Why made-to-order beats a standard product here

It sounds like a weakness that Anup builds everything bespoke — no economies of a production line, no shelf inventory to ship on demand. In this market it is the opposite. A standardised product invites price competition and cheap copies; a made-to-order vessel, engineered for one plant's exact duty and metallurgy, has no spot price to be undercut on and locks Anup into the customer's project at the design stage. The customer is not buying a catalogue item — they are buying Anup's engineering judgment on a piece they cannot afford to get wrong. That is why the diversification across oil & gas, chemicals, fertiliser and pharma is real protection: a soft patch in one sector's project pipeline can be offset by a live cycle in another, and no single standardised SKU can be commoditised out from under the company.

03

Out of a textile house: the demerger and who owns it

A ninety-year-old workshop hidden inside a denim group — until 2019.

Here is the fact that surprises almost everyone who meets this company: a maker of titanium heat exchangers spent most of its life as a side-business of a garment company. Anup Engineering traces its roots to the Lalbhai family's Arvind group — the Ahmedabad textile house best known for denim and shirting. The engineering unit was a small, quiet, cash-generative corner of a textiles empire for decades, and only in 2019 was it demerged and listed as a standalone company so investors could own the process-equipment business on its own.

Timeline

  • 1962
    The engineering workshop is set up under the Arvind / Lalbhai group in Ahmedabad, building fabricated equipment for local industry.
  • 1990s–2000s
    Grows into a specialist in heat exchangers and pressure vessels, earning international codes and approvals (ASME stamps, oil-major vendor listings) and a reputation for exotic-metal work.
  • 2019
    Demerged from Arvind Ltd and separately listed on NSE & BSE — the process-equipment business becomes a pure-play company that investors can value on its own merits.
  • 2020–2023
    Steady margin-led growth; the Odhav (Ahmedabad) plant runs near its ceiling, and management commits to a large greenfield site to break the capacity constraint.
  • FY24–FY25
    The Kheda plant comes on stream; revenue crosses ₹700 Cr; return ratios stay high; the order book and export mix build with the capex cycle.
  • FY26
    Kheda phase-2 lifts group capacity to ~20,000 MT/yr; first nuclear-equipment order booked; revenue ~₹822 Cr (+~12%) but PAT eases to ~₹110 Cr on margin/mix — a scale-up year with a profit wobble.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Lalbhai family and associated Arvind-group entities remain the controlling shareholders after the demerger, holding a large majority stake. This is a professionally run, long-established industrial house rather than a founder-operator startup — continuity of management and customer relationships is part of the asset.
  • What it is not: do not confuse Anup with the parent Arvind Ltd (textiles) or Arvind Fashions / Arvind SmartSpaces — different listed companies from the same family tree. Anup is the standalone engineering business only.
  • Balance sheet: conservatively financed. Borrowings are modest relative to a ~₹2,700 Cr net worth base, and the business is structurally cash-generative — high return on capital funds most of its own growth, with the Kheda capex the main recent call on cash. This is a low-leverage, high-ROCE profile, not a debt-fuelled expansion.
What the demerger actually changed

Sitting inside Arvind, the engineering business was invisible — its profits were a rounding line in a textile company's accounts, and no textile investor was buying the stock for heat exchangers. The 2019 demerger did two useful things. It let the market value the business on its own economics — a high-return, low-debt equipment maker deserves a very different multiple from a capital-intensive textile mill, and only a standalone listing could reveal that. And it gave the unit its own balance sheet and capital-allocation freedom, which is precisely what funded the Kheda plant. A business that spent decades as someone else's footnote could finally raise capital and invest as itself.

Why the parentage still matters: being carved out of a disciplined, old-economy industrial family shaped Anup's culture — patient, quality-obsessed, allergic to over-leverage. That is a genuine asset in a business where one botched weld on a live-plant vessel can cost a client a reputation and Anup a decade of approvals. But it also means Anup grows at the deliberate pace of a made-to-order engineering shop, not the explosive pace a market sometimes wants to pay for.
04

The metallurgy moat: why the steel is the cheap part

The single idea that separates Anup from a job-shop — and it is measurable.

The most useful way to understand Anup is to ask a blunt question: if two companies both weld steel into vessels, why does one earn a 20%+ margin and 20% return on capital while a generic fabricator scrapes single digits? The answer is that Anup barely sells steel at all. It sells the ability to build in metals that most fabricators cannot weld — and that ability shows up directly in how much revenue it extracts from each tonne of metal it processes.

At ~₹822 Cr of FY26 revenue against ~20,000 MT of capacity, a fully utilised Anup realises on the order of ₹6 lakh per tonne — a large multiple of what a carbon-steel job-shop earns per tonne, because the price reflects design, exotic-metal welding and testing, not the weight of the metal.

Run the same question through a single job: on a titanium reactor, the raw titanium plate might be a serious cost, but the reason the customer pays a premium is that only a handful of shops in the country can weld titanium to code, X-ray every seam, and warrant it for twenty years of corrosive service. That skill — not the metal — is what is scarce, and scarcity is what margins are made of. A generic fabricator competing on carbon-steel tonnage is selling a commodity; Anup is selling a certified capability.

The moat, made concrete

Three things fence out competition. Metallurgy: qualifying welders and procedures for titanium, zirconium, tantalum, nickel alloys and clad plate takes years and destructive testing — you cannot fake it, and a client will not let you learn on their reactor. Approvals: to bid for an oil major or an export order you must already be on approved vendor lists and hold the right code stamps, a paperwork moat that a new entrant cannot leap. Track record: in equipment where failure is unacceptable, a decades-long clean delivery history is itself the product. Together these mean Anup competes against a short list of credible names, not a crowd — which is why a metal-bending business earns software-like returns on capital.

There is a ladder implicit in all this. At the bottom sit commodity carbon-steel vessels that anyone can weld; in the middle, the stainless heat exchangers that are Anup's bread and butter; above them, exotic-alloy and clad reactors; and on the top rungs the frontier work — cryogenic, hydrogen-ready, and now nuclear and defence. Every rung up raises the price per tonne and shrinks the list of qualified rivals, and Anup's whole strategy is to keep migrating its mix rightward. The Kheda clean-room and that first nuclear order are the most recent steps onto the higher rungs.

A genuine surprise: in FY26 Anup grew revenue by roughly 12% yet its net profit slipped, from about ₹118 Cr to ~₹110 Cr. For a company this profitable that is a tell worth sitting with — a made-to-order shop's margin in any given year depends on the mix of jobs it happened to execute and how cleanly they ran, not just on how busy it was. Record revenue with softer profit is the signature of a scale-up year where lower-margin work and start-up costs at a new plant crowded in. It is a reminder that "order book up" and "profit up" are not the same sentence here.
05

The order book and the capex cycle that fills it

Anup does not control its demand — so watch what its customers are spending.

A made-to-order equipment maker has no demand of its own; it borrows the demand of everyone building a plant. That makes the process-industry capital-expenditure cycle the single most important thing happening outside Anup's factory gates. When refiners, chemical makers and fertiliser companies commit capital to new units, enquiries land on Anup's desk; when they pause, the order book thins. Right now the cycle is working in Anup's favour on several fronts at once.

Why the enquiry pipeline is the number to watch, not just the order book

The order book tells you what is already won; it is a lagging comfort. The enquiry pipeline — the value of live bids Anup is quoting on — is the forward signal, because it reflects how much plant-building is being planned right now. If the pipeline keeps widening, the order book refills as jobs are delivered and the capex cycle is intact. If the pipeline shrinks, it is the early warning that customers are deferring projects — the thing that would eventually starve the factory. For a business with no demand of its own, that funnel is the closest thing to a demand forecast you will get.

06

Kheda: breaking the capacity ceiling

The growth bet that has to earn its keep — a doubled factory looking for orders.

For years Anup's growth was capped by a very physical constraint: its original Odhav plant, hemmed in inside Ahmedabad's urban sprawl, had little room to add capacity or build the very large, very heavy vessels the market increasingly wanted. The answer was a large greenfield site at Kheda, and it is the most important capital decision Anup has made since listing.

Approximate split. The Kheda phase-2 expansion (adding roughly 8,000 MT) roughly doubles group capacity to ~20,000 MT/yr. On management's own arithmetic, that fully-utilised capacity can support well over ₹1,200 Cr of revenue — versus ~₹822 Cr delivered in FY26.

Kheda is not just more of the same shed. It was built to make the equipment Odhav couldn't — larger and heavier vessels, with better logistics for moving oversized pieces, and a dedicated clean-room facility for the high-purity fabrication that pharma, electronics and frontier applications demand. In other words, the new plant simultaneously adds volume and pushes Anup up the complexity ladder — the two levers that drive this company's revenue and margin at the same time.

The clean-room is the quiet tell

The easy way to read Kheda is "bigger shed, more tonnes." The more revealing detail is the clean-room. Building a controlled, high-purity fabrication facility is not what you do to churn out more carbon-steel drums — it is what you do to make equipment for pharma, electronics, hydrogen and the frontier applications where contamination is unacceptable. In other words, the same capital expansion that doubles volume also equips Anup to sell up the ladder. A management team that spends on a clean-room while doubling capacity is telling you it intends to grow revenue and mix at the same time, not just fabricate more of the same.

The gap between what the factory did and what it could do — roughly ₹822 Cr of FY26 revenue against a capacity that could support ₹1,200 Cr-plus — is the growth runway and the risk in one line. Idle capacity is expensive; the whole Kheda bet rests on the capex cycle delivering enough orders to fill it. Utilisation, not capacity, is the number that turns this plant from a cost into a compounding asset.

The real tension in the Kheda bet: doubling capacity is only value-creating if orders arrive to fill it — a half-empty specialist plant carries fixed costs that eat the very margins that make Anup special. FY26's revenue-up-profit-down pattern is partly the fingerprint of a plant ramping ahead of its order load. The bet is sound if the capex cycle runs for years; it is a drag if the cycle stalls before utilisation catches up. That is why the enquiry pipeline (§5) and this plant's utilisation are two halves of the same question.
07

Exports and the climb into higher-spec work

Half the book is overseas — and the frontier segments are the optionality.

Two things make Anup more than a domestic capex proxy. First, roughly half its order book is exports — equipment shipped to overseas plant owners and EPC contractors who value certified Indian fabrication at competitive cost. Second, Anup is deliberately steering its mix toward higher-spec, harder-to-make equipment where the price per tonne and the barriers to entry are both highest.

The precise split moves quarter to quarter, but Anup consistently targets that 50–60% export band. That overseas demand partly de-couples the company from the timing of India's own capex cycle — a slow domestic year can be cushioned by export orders, and a busy one runs both engines at once. It also validates the quality claim: an overseas plant owner choosing certified Indian fabrication over a local or Western OEM is a harder customer to win than a domestic one, and winning them repeatedly is its own proof of Anup's standing.

The move up-spec is where the optionality lives. Anup is building qualifications and reference orders in a set of frontier applications, each of which is small today but sits on the most defensible, highest-value rungs of the ladder:

  • Cryogenic & industrial gases — equipment for LNG and air-separation duty, where sub-zero service demands specialist metallurgy and welding. A natural extension of Anup's exotic-metal expertise.
  • Hydrogen-ready equipment — as green-hydrogen and related capex builds, the electrolyser and processing chains need vessels and exchangers rated for hydrogen service — a market that barely existed a few years ago.
  • Nuclear — Anup booked its first nuclear-equipment order (columns and vessels) from a leading EPC player in FY26. Nuclear is the toughest qualification in the industry — long-cycle, heavily audited, and correspondingly hard for rivals to enter.
  • Defence & aerospace — specialised fabrication for defence and aerospace supply chains, another high-barrier, indigenisation-driven pool.
How to hold the frontier segments in your head

Cryogenic, hydrogen, nuclear and defence are not yet the earnings story — they are small slivers of today's revenue. Their value is different: each is a qualification Anup is banking now that could open a large, high-margin market later, and each one a low-cost rival cannot follow it into. The correct way to weigh them is as a portfolio of hard-won entry tickets, not as a forecast — most may stay small, but the ones that scale would scale precisely because they are so hard to enter. Judge them by the reference orders and approvals Anup accumulates, not by revenue they haven't yet produced.

08

The industry and the rivals

A short list of credible names — not a commodity scrum.

Because the barriers are metallurgy, approvals and track record, process-equipment fabrication in India is a concentrated field of a few reputable players, not a fragmented commodity market. Anup competes against a mix of focused specialists and the equipment arms of large engineering houses — and, in one adjacent niche, a technology it deliberately does not chase.

The margin gap between the tiers is the whole point. A focused, exotic-metal specialist like Anup earns a mid-to-high-teens-to-20s EBITDA margin — roughly double a diversified engineering house and several times a commodity fabricator — because it competes on capability, not on the price of a tonne of steel. That is the financial fingerprint of where a company sits in the pecking order, and it is why Anup guards its niche rather than chasing volume down-market.

The competitive shape to remember

Two features define the field. First, this is a reputation oligopoly: in equipment where failure is unacceptable, buyers stick to a short approved list, so a rising capex cycle tends to lift the credible specialists together rather than trigger a price war. Second, Anup's real edge over the giants is focus — a dedicated process-equipment shop can chase the mid-sized, high-metallurgy jobs that are too fiddly for an L&T-scale package and too demanding for a commodity fabricator. Its edge over the commodity crowd is simply that they cannot make what it makes. The competitive risk is not a new low-cost entrant; it is a direct specialist like Godrej out-executing it, or the capex cycle shrinking the pie for everyone.

Financials & valuation

A high-return niche compounder — priced as one, with a profit wobble to explain.

Anup's financial signature is the opposite of its heavy-industrial appearance: modest debt, high returns on capital, and cash generation that funds most of its own growth. FY26 was a scale-up year — revenue grew but profit dipped — so the valuation question is whether the market is paying for the through-cycle quality or for a straight-line ramp that FY26 just complicated.

The one number that settles the thesis: Kheda utilisation and the margin it carries

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A quality specialist with a doubled factory — asked to prove the cycle will fill it.

Anup enters FY27 as one of the best-run names in its niche: high returns, low debt, a respected metallurgy franchise, half its book overseas, a new plant that can carry far more revenue, and a foot in the door of nuclear, cryogenic and hydrogen work. The open question is no longer about quality — it is whether the process-industry capex cycle delivers enough of the right orders to fill the capacity it has just built, at the margins that make Anup, Anup.

The bull case

  • Capex-cycle tailwind — refinery, specialty-chemical/China+1 and fertiliser spending, plus exports, feed a ~₹1,200 Cr enquiry pipeline.
  • Capacity headroom — Kheda phase-2 roughly doubles capacity to ~20,000 MT/yr and can support ₹1,200 Cr-plus of revenue as it fills.
  • Metallurgy moat & high returns — exotic-metal capability and code approvals underpin ~20% margins and ~20% ROCE that bulk rivals cannot match.
  • Move up-spec — cryogenic, hydrogen and a first nuclear order add high-barrier, high-value optionality with a growing clean-room base.
  • Balance-sheet quality — low debt and self-funded growth; a disciplined, old-industrial-house culture built for reliability.

The bear case

  • No demand of its own — a made-to-order shop is hostage to the capex cycle; a downturn in client spending thins the book directly.
  • Utilisation risk — a doubled plant carries fixed costs; if orders lag, the very margins that define Anup get squeezed (FY26 hinted at this).
  • Lumpy, mix-driven profit — profit swings with the jobs executed and start-up costs; revenue growth need not mean profit growth, as FY26 showed.
  • Premium valuation — ~33× earnings prices in continued high-quality compounding; a soft cycle or margin slip can de-rate it sharply.
  • Concentration & competition — heavy reliance on heat exchangers; a capable direct peer in Godrej and the scale of L&T at the top end.
The bottom line

Anup Engineering is a quietly excellent business doing a hard thing well: turning difficult metals into critical equipment that plant owners cannot afford to have fail, and earning specialist returns for it. What it has just done — double its capacity at Kheda and step onto the nuclear and cryogenic rungs — widens the runway but raises the stakes, because a specialist plant only compounds if it stays full of the high-value work it was built for. FY26's record revenue and softer profit is the honest preview of that tension. The next few years answer it in one checkable place: watch Kheda's utilisation and the revenue Anup wrings from each tonne of steel — if both climb as the enquiry pipeline converts, the capex cycle is doing its job and the multiple is earned; if the plant runs light or the mix drifts to commodity work, the return ratios tell you before the share price does.
