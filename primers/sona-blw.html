Sona BLW Precision Forgings, the EV-content compounder

A complete walk through Sona Comstar — the Indian precision-components maker that sells differential gears, driveline assemblies and, increasingly, EV traction motors and controllers to the world's biggest carmakers. How an auto-parts business actually earns money, why "content per vehicle" is the whole story, how a ₹23,700-crore order book that is ~70% electric gives it rare revenue visibility, why an electric car often needs more Sona value than a petrol one — and why one of India's cleanest listed ways to play the global EV shift trades at ~60× earnings.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Sona BLW Precision Forgings — the market knows it as Sona Comstar — is a high-growth, high-margin auto-components maker that is one of the cleanest listed ways to bet on the global switch to electric vehicles. It does not make cars. It makes the precision-engineered guts of the drivetrain — differential gears and assemblies, driveline systems, and increasingly EV-specific traction motors, motor controllers and e-axles — and sells them to the world's biggest automakers in North America, Europe, China and India.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is unusually electric for an auto-parts firm. In Q4 FY26, 39% of its automotive product revenue came from battery-electric vehicles (BEVs) (~35% for the full year) — a share most legacy suppliers can only dream of. Its ₹23,700-crore net order book is ~70% linked to EV programmes. So Sona is a rare thing: a profitable, dividend-paying way to own the EV transition through content, not through a loss-making carmaker.
  • It grows fast and earns fat margins. FY26 revenue was ₹4,449 Cr (+26% YoY) at a ~24.7% EBITDA margin, with ₹629 Cr net profit and a near debt-free balance sheet. Over five years, revenue and profit have both compounded at ~23–24% a year — a sector-leading combination of growth and profitability.
  • The market pays a premium price for it. At a ~₹41,200 Cr market cap on ₹629 Cr of profit, the stock trades at roughly 60× trailing earnings — a rich "EV compounder" multiple that assumes the order book converts on schedule. That, plus a founder-chairman's sudden death in 2025 and a family succession fight, are the tensions in the story.
In plain terms

Think of every car — petrol or electric — as needing a set of gears that let the two driven wheels turn at different speeds when the car corners (that's what a "differential" does). Sona is one of the top makers of those gears in the world. The clever part of the investment case is this: when a car goes electric, it doesn't stop needing a differential — and it often needs Sona's motors and controllers on top. So the shift to EVs, which terrifies makers of pistons and fuel injectors, actually increases the value of Sona's parts inside each vehicle. Sona has spent a decade turning that insight into a huge forward order book dominated by electric programmes — which is why the market treats it less like an auto-parts firm and more like an EV growth stock.

01

Content per vehicle 101: how an auto-parts firm earns

No engineering degree required — just three ideas that drive the whole business.

An automotive components company doesn't sell cars; it sells parts that go inside other people's cars. Its revenue is, at heart, one simple product: the number of vehicles built × the value of Sona's content in each one. Understand those two levers — volume and content-per-vehicle — and you understand almost the whole model.

The three ideas that explain the business
IdeaWhat it means (and why it matters for Sona)
Tier-1 supplierSona sells directly to the carmaker (the "OEM" — Original Equipment Manufacturer), not to another parts firm. Being a "Tier-1" means it wins multi-year contracts to supply a specific vehicle model or platform — and gets designed-in early, which is sticky and hard to displace.
Content per vehicleThe rupee (or dollar) value of Sona parts in one car. A petrol car might carry Sona's differential gears; an EV can carry the differential plus a traction motor plus a controller — several times the content. This is the single most important concept in the whole thesis.
Order book (backlog)The total value of business already awarded but not yet delivered, spread over future years. Because auto programmes run 5–8 years, a big order book gives revenue visibility — you can see years of sales coming before they show up in the accounts.
Why "content per vehicle" is the magic phrase

Most auto-parts companies live or die on how many cars the world builds this year. Sona's edge is that it can grow even if car volumes are flat, simply by putting more of its parts into each vehicle. The EV transition is the great enabler: an internal-combustion car needs a differential (Sona's traditional product); a battery-electric car needs that same differential-and-gear set plus the electric traction motor that actually drives the wheels plus the power electronics that control it. Sona makes all three. So every petrol car that becomes an electric one is, potentially, a multiplier on Sona's content — the opposite of the existential threat EVs pose to piston-ring and fuel-system makers. That mechanism is why the same EV wave that scares half the auto-parts industry is Sona's core growth engine.

The two ways Sona grows

  • Win share on existing products — take a bigger slice of the global market for differential gears (where it has roughly doubled its share since 2019). More of the world's cars carry a Sona gear.
  • Add new products up the value chain — move from just gears, to gears-plus-assemblies, to motors, to controllers, to full "e-axles" and sensors. Each step raises content per vehicle and pulls Sona from being a component supplier toward being an integrated systems supplier.
02

What Sona actually makes

From a forged gear to a complete electric drivetrain — the product ladder.

Sona's portfolio is best understood as a ladder climbing from mechanical precision toward electrification. It started at the bottom rung — precision-forged gears — and has been climbing steadily into motors, power electronics and software, adding value (and content per vehicle) at each step.

The further right you climb, the more electric and software-rich the product — and the more Sona value sits inside each vehicle. This is the strategic direction of the whole company.

The clinical menu, decoded

  • Differential gears & assemblies — the core. A differential is the gearbox that lets a car's two driven wheels spin at different speeds when it turns a corner (the outer wheel travels further). Sona is a global top-tier maker: it has produced 500 million differential gears and 10 million assemblies since inception, with an estimated global market share of ~8.7% in differential gears (CY2025), up from ~4.5% in 2019 — a near-doubling in six years.
  • Traction motors (BLDC & PMSM) — the electric motors that propel an EV. Sona is one of India's largest makers of EV traction motors, having sold 180,000+ indigenous traction motors since starting production at its Chennai plant in November 2020.
  • Motor controllers & power electronics — the "brains" that manage how the motor draws power and delivers torque. Sold especially into India's electric two- and three-wheeler market, and increasingly bundled with motors as complete e-drive systems.
  • Starter motors, BSG/ISG & hybrid parts — from the Comstar business: starter motors and belt/integrated starter-generators for conventional and hybrid vehicles — the "legacy" cash engine that funds the electric push.
  • Sensors & software (Novelic) — mmWave radar and perception systems for driver-assistance (ADAS), a newer "sensors and software" pillar.
  • Railway components (Escorts Kubota division) — engineered components and systems for Indian Railways, a fresh non-automotive diversification (see §07).
Why Sona is unusual among global suppliers

Very few companies in the world sit at the crossroads of mechanical drivetrain engineering, electric-motor manufacturing and gearing all under one roof. Most suppliers are either "metal" (gears, forgings) or "electric" (motors, electronics) — Sona does both, and increasingly sells them together as integrated e-drive systems. That combination puts it in rare company globally (the likes of BorgWarner and Magna), and it is precisely what lets Sona pitch a single, higher-value package to a carmaker designing its next EV platform — rather than being one of a dozen commodity gear vendors competing on price.

03

The company and who controls it

A Blackstone-forged platform — now navigating a founder's death and a family fight.

Sona Comstar as it exists today was assembled by private equity. The Sona Group's forgings business was combined with the US-origin Comstar motors business under a Blackstone-backed platform in 2019, listed in a blockbuster 2021 IPO, and grew into a global EV-components champion — before its chairman's sudden death in 2025 opened a difficult chapter of governance and succession.

  • 2025
    Completes acquisition of Escorts Kubota's railway equipment division (₹1,600 Cr) — a new non-auto growth pillar.
  • FY26

Ownership & management

  • Promoter holding: ~28% (Mar 2026). Relatively modest for an Indian company — a legacy of Blackstone's ownership and staged exit, which leaves a large free float in institutional and public hands. The rest of the register is heavy with domestic and foreign institutions.
  • Management: Vivek Vikram Singh is Managing Director & Group CEO — the operational architect of the EV pivot and the global-customer strategy. The company is professionally run, not owner-managed in the traditional Indian promoter sense.
  • The succession overhang: the death of chairman Sunjay Kapur in June 2025 triggered a family dispute over the promoter stake and board control. It is a genuine governance uncertainty to watch, even though day-to-day operations sit with a professional management team.
  • Balance sheet: essentially net cash / near debt-free, funding capacity expansion (India, Mexico) largely internally.
The governance caveat: a low promoter stake plus an unresolved family succession fight is an unusual combination for a stock trading at a premium multiple. The professional management gives comfort on execution, but corporate-governance and control questions are a live part of the Sona story in a way they are not for most family-controlled peers.
04

The EV pure-play question

The single reason Sona commands a premium — and the single biggest risk.

Sona's defining metric is not revenue or margin; it is how electric it is. Almost no listed auto-components maker has as high a share of revenue tied to battery-electric vehicles, and the share is rising. That is why the market treats Sona as an EV growth stock rather than an ordinary parts supplier — and why the EV cycle is the axis the whole thesis turns on.

The forward book (~70% EV) is even more electric than current revenue (~35%) — meaning the mix is set to keep shifting toward EVs as the order book converts into sales.
Why an EV needs MORE Sona value, not less

Here is the mechanism that makes Sona unusual. A petrol car has an engine and gearbox from other suppliers; Sona's content is mainly the differential gears. An electric car still needs a differential — the two driven wheels still turn at different speeds around a corner — so that content survives the transition intact. But an EV also needs an electric traction motor to drive the wheels and a controller to manage the power. Sona makes those too. So on an EV programme where Sona supplies the driveline and the motor and the electronics, its content per vehicle can be several times what it earns on a comparable petrol car. This is why Sona benefits from electrification while engine-part makers are threatened by it — and why "how fast do EVs sell?" is the most important external question for the stock.

The other edge of the same sword: being EV-levered cuts both ways. If global EV demand slows — as it has wobbled in the US and Europe — or if a big EV customer's volumes disappoint, Sona feels it directly. Its premium valuation is built on the assumption that the electric mix keeps rising; a prolonged EV air-pocket is precisely the risk the bears point to. The very concentration in EVs that earns Sona its multiple is also what makes it vulnerable to the EV cycle.
05

The order book engine

₹23,700 crore of awarded business — the visibility that underwrites the story.

The most reassuring number in the whole Sona story is its net order book: ₹23,700 crore — roughly 5.3× a single year's revenue, and ~70% linked to EV programmes. Because automotive contracts run for years, this backlog is a window into sales that are already won but not yet delivered — a rare degree of forward visibility for a fast-growing company.

How the order book actually works

  • Programmes, not orders. A carmaker designing a new vehicle "awards" Sona the business for a specific part on that model — often before the car even launches. Sona then supplies it for the model's whole 5–8 year production life.
  • "Net" order book is the value still to be delivered — it grows as Sona wins new programmes and shrinks as it delivers existing ones. A rising net book means new wins are outpacing deliveries: the pipeline is filling faster than it empties.
  • The 70% EV skew is the tell. Because the forward book is far more electric (~70%) than today's revenue (~35% BEV), it signals that Sona's future mix is set to keep shifting toward EVs. The order book is, in effect, a pre-paid ticket to a more electric revenue base.

Why the order book is the bull case in one number

For most companies, next year's sales are a forecast. For Sona, a large chunk is already contracted — sitting in that ₹23,700 Cr book, waiting to be built and shipped as customers' vehicle programmes ramp. That converts the abstract promise of "EV growth" into something concrete and countable: business Sona has already been chosen to supply. The catch is that an order book is a maximum, not a guarantee — it delivers only if the underlying vehicle programmes actually sell in the volumes assumed. If a marquee EV model flops, the slice of the book tied to it converts into far less revenue than the headline suggests. So watch two things together: the book growing (new wins), and the customers' vehicles actually selling (conversion).

06

Global customers and the geography of demand

An export-led business selling to the world — with the concentration risk that implies.

Sona is unusual among Indian auto-component firms in that it is fundamentally a global, export-led business. Its largest single end-market is North America, and it sells to a roster of global OEMs across the US, Europe, China and India. That worldwide reach is a strength — but it also concentrates demand on a handful of large customers and specific EV programmes.

The market-share story

Sona's rise is a genuine share-gain story, not just a rising-tide story. In differential gears — its founding product — its estimated global market share climbed from ~4.5% in 2019 to ~8.7% in CY2025. In a mature, slow-growing product category, doubling global share in six years is the clearest evidence that Sona is winning business from established Western and Japanese incumbents on engineering and cost, not merely riding volume growth.

The customer-concentration question: a large share of the EV order book has historically been tied to a small number of big programmes — including a major North American EV maker. Management is explicit that it caps any single customer's share, precisely because over-dependence would threaten either growth or margins. But the honest risk remains: if one marquee EV customer's volumes stumble, Sona's near-term numbers feel it. The FY26 cancellation of a supply arrangement with China's BYD was a reminder that even awarded EV business can evaporate — one reason diversification (below) matters so much.
07

Diversification: railways, sensors and the China question

Three deliberate bets to reduce dependence on any single EV programme.

Sona knows its greatest strength — deep EV exposure — is also its greatest concentration risk. So it has been deliberately broadening the base: into railways, into sensors and software, and (cautiously) into China. Each is an attempt to add a growth pillar that does not depend on the same handful of global EV programmes.

Reading the diversification honestly

These pillars are the right instinct — a company this levered to a few EV programmes should build other engines. Railways in particular gives Sona a large domestic market with an entirely different cycle. But it is early: autos (and EVs within autos) still dominate revenue by a wide margin, so the diversification is a multi-year project rather than a today-fact. And the China JV going on hold is a useful reality check — the single biggest EV market on earth is, for now, largely closed to Sona on geopolitical grounds. The bull reads diversification as reducing risk over time; the bear notes that, for now, the EV-programme concentration is still the dominant feature.

08

The industry and the rivals

Competing against the giants of global driveline — on engineering, not just price.

Sona plays in a global oligopoly of driveline and electric-drive suppliers, up against some of the largest and best-resourced component makers in the world. Its competitive claim is not scale — many rivals dwarf it — but a combination of precision engineering, cost, agility and the rare ability to offer both mechanical and electrical drivetrain content together.

Where Sona's moat actually is

Sona is not going to out-spend BorgWarner or ZF. Its defensible ground is threefold. (1) Designed-in stickiness: once Sona's part is engineered into a multi-year vehicle programme, it is expensive and risky for the OEM to swap it out mid-cycle. (2) The mechanical-plus-electrical combination: few suppliers can offer gears, motors and controllers together — a package Sona can pitch to an OEM building a new EV platform. (3) India-cost precision: world-class forging and machining at an Indian cost base, which is how it has taken global share on differentials. The counter-risk: its biggest rivals are enormous, deeply capitalised and equally determined to own the EV drivetrain — so Sona has to keep out-engineering and out-executing companies many times its size.

The structural backdrop: the entire global auto-supply base is being reshuffled by electrification. Winners are the suppliers whose content grows on an EV (motors, power electronics, batteries, e-drives); losers are those whose content disappears (exhausts, fuel systems, multi-speed gearboxes). Sona has deliberately positioned itself on the winning side of that line — but so has every large incumbent, which is why the competition for EV-drivetrain content is intense and global.
09

Financials and the margin premium

Rare growth-and-margin combination — with margins easing as the mix shifts.

Sona's financial signature is what makes it stand out from the Indian auto-ancillary pack: high growth and high margins at the same time. Most parts makers offer one or the other. Sona has compounded revenue and profit at ~23–24% a year for five years while holding EBITDA margins in the mid-20s% — territory more typical of a specialty-chemicals or branded-consumer business than an auto supplier.

Reading the FY26 numbers honestly

Two nuances matter. First, the margin drifted down. EBITDA margin eased from ~28% in FY24 to ~24.7% in FY26. That is not deterioration so much as mix: EV and motor products, and newer geographies/plants (Mexico) in their ramp phase, carry lower margins than the mature gear business — so fast EV growth dilutes the blended margin even as absolute profit rises. Second, profit grew slower than revenue. FY26 revenue rose 26% but reported PAT only ~5% (₹600→₹629 Cr), reflecting that margin drift plus higher depreciation and start-up costs from the expansion. (Some releases quote an adjusted PAT nearer ₹670 Cr.) The bull frames this as investment ahead of the order-book ramp; the bear notes that return ratios (ROCE ~14%, ROE ~11%) have softened as capital is deployed ahead of the revenue it will earn.

10

Valuation — the ₹41,000-crore question

A premium "EV compounder" multiple — and whether it's deserved.

There is no way around it: Sona is expensive. At a ~₹41,200 Cr market cap on ₹629 Cr of FY26 profit, the stock trades at roughly 60× trailing earnings and ~9× book value — a multiple that assumes years of high growth still to come. The business quality is not really in dispute; the debate is entirely about the price.

How to think about a ~60× multiple

A 60× earnings multiple only makes sense if you believe the ₹23,700 Cr order book converts into years of 20%+ growth, that the EV mix keeps rising, and that Sona keeps taking global share. Bulls argue that is exactly what a debt-free, high-ROCE, structurally-advantaged EV-content leader with rare growth-and-margin economics should deliver — and that scarcity (few clean listed EV-content plays exist) justifies a premium. Bears counter that the multiple leaves no room for error: a global EV slowdown, a key customer's volumes disappointing, margin drift continuing, the China route staying blocked, or the succession dispute damaging governance — any one could trigger a sharp de-rating from a 60× base. Both can be right at different points in the cycle, which is why the order-book conversion and the EV mix are the numbers to track each quarter.

Honest read on the price: Sona is a high-quality business at a demanding valuation. The premium is earned by the growth, margins and EV positioning — but it is also fragile, because so much of it is priced on the order book converting smoothly and the EV cycle staying supportive. A patient owner is buying a genuine EV-content compounder; they are also buying meaningful de-rating risk if any part of that story slips.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine EV-content winner — priced for near-flawless execution.

Sona Comstar is one of the cleaner ways in the Indian market to own the global EV transition through profitable, dividend-paying content rather than a cash-burning carmaker. The business is high-quality; the investment debate is about valuation, EV-cycle sensitivity and governance, not about whether the strategy is sound.

The bull case

  • EV-content leverage — an EV needs Sona's differential plus motor plus controller: electrification raises content per vehicle rather than destroying it.
  • Order-book visibility — ₹23,700 Cr (~5.3× revenue), ~70% EV — years of contracted growth already won.
  • Global share gains — differential-gear share doubled to ~8.7% since 2019; winning from entrenched Western/Japanese incumbents.
  • Rare economics — mid-20s% EBITDA margins, high ROCE, near debt-free, ~24% five-year profit CAGR.
  • Diversification optionality — railways, ADAS sensors and integrated e-drives broaden the base over time.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~60× earnings leaves no margin for error; vulnerable to sharp de-rating on any stumble.
  • EV-cycle & customer concentration — heavy reliance on a few big EV programmes; a global EV slowdown or a key customer's volumes hurt directly (cf. the BYD deal cancellation).
  • Margin drift — EBITDA margin fell from ~28% to ~24.7% as mix shifts to lower-margin EV/motor and new plants; PAT growth lagged revenue.
  • Governance & succession — low ~28% promoter stake plus an unresolved family control dispute after the chairman's 2025 death.
  • Forex, geopolitics & competition — export-led (currency risk), the China route on hold, and giant rivals (Bosch, ZF, BorgWarner) chasing the same EV content.
The bottom line

Sona Comstar is a structurally advantaged EV-content compounder: it has turned the insight that electric cars need more of its parts into a huge, mostly-EV order book, rare growth-and-margin economics and a rising global share — a genuinely differentiated way to play electrification through content. The business risk is comparatively low; the valuation and cycle risks are real. The story over the coming years will be written in three numbers each quarter — the EV/BEV revenue mix (is it still rising?), the order book (growing and converting?), and the margin (stabilising or still drifting?). Get those right and the ~60× multiple compounds into it; stumble on any, and a premium EV stock has a long way to fall.
