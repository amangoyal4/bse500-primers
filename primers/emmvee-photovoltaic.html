Emmvee Photovoltaic Power, the panel factory the state protects

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Emmvee Photovoltaic Power makes solar panels — and, increasingly, the light-catching cells that go inside them. It is a factory business: it buys silicon wafers, turns them into solar cells, and assembles those cells into finished modules (the blue-black panels you see on rooftops and in fields), which it sells to the companies that actually build power plants. It does not produce electricity itself. By manufacturing capacity, Emmvee is among India's larger names and one of only a handful of pure-play integrated cell-and-module makers in the country.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Backward integration is the whole story. In FY24 Emmvee did ₹952 Cr of revenue but only ₹29 Cr of profit — because it was assembling modules from cells it had to buy. It then switched on its own 2.94 GW TOPCon solar-cell line. FY25 revenue jumped to ₹2,336 Cr and profit to ₹369 Cr; FY26 doubled again to ₹5,049 Cr revenue and ₹1,082 Cr PAT. Same panels — but now Emmvee keeps the cell margin instead of paying it away.
  • A 2025 IPO with an expansion plan attached. Emmvee listed in November 2025 at ₹217 a share (a ~₹2,900 Cr issue), raising fresh money largely to expand. It is racing module capacity toward 16.3 GW and cell capacity toward 8.9 GW by FY27, and has committed to a ~₹6,300 Cr push even further upstream — into the ingots and wafers it currently imports. Order book at end-FY26: 9.4 GW.
  • The demand is real, but the moat is written in policy. Global solar-module prices have collapsed under Chinese overcapacity. What lets an Indian factory earn a fat margin anyway is a wall of government protection — an Approved List (ALMM) that effectively bars cheap Chinese modules from subsidised projects, a coming version of that list for cells, and steep import duties. Emmvee's profits are a function of that wall as much as of the sun.
In plain terms

Emmvee is, stripped down, a hardware factory that stamps out a commodity — a watt of solar module — whose global price keeps falling. On its own, that is a brutal business: China can make the same watt for less. Two things rescue it. First, Emmvee moved up the assembly line, making the cell as well as the panel, so it captures a slice of value it used to hand to a supplier. Second — and this is the part outsiders miss — the Indian government has drawn a legal line around its own market: for a large share of projects, the panel (and soon the cell) must be Indian-made. That single rule is what turns a low-margin assembler into a company earning 34% EBITDA margins. The bet you are being asked to make is on both the factory and the fence around it holding.

01

Solar manufacturing 101: from sand to a working panel

Five steps turn quartz into electricity — and Emmvee sits in the middle two.

A finished solar panel is the end of a long, capital-heavy chain. You don't need any physics to follow it — just the order of the steps and, crucially, where the money and the difficulty concentrate. Each stage is a distinct industry with its own factories, its own margins, and its own dominant players (overwhelmingly Chinese at the top of the chain).

→

Value and technical difficulty rise as you move left (upstream). Module assembly (Stage 4) is the easiest to enter and the most commoditised; cell-making (Stage 3) is harder and scarcer. Emmvee's whole strategy is to keep climbing left — from module-only, to cell-plus-module, and next toward wafers.

Why "module vs cell" is the distinction that matters

  • A module (panel) is an assembly job — lay cells in a grid, wire them, laminate glass and a frame. Real, but not hard to copy: India has ~50 module makers and roughly 190+ GW of approved module capacity, far above demand. So module-only margins are thin and getting thinner.
  • A cell is where the actual photovoltaic magic is made — doping and coating a wafer so it converts sunlight to current. It needs cleaner processes, costlier equipment and know-how. India has far less cell capacity (~30 GW approved) than module capacity, so the cell is the bottleneck — and the bottleneck earns the margin.
  • Wafers & polysilicon are further upstream still — heavy, energy-intensive, almost entirely Chinese. India barely makes them. Whoever cracks domestic wafers next captures the last big slice of imported value; Emmvee has said it intends to.
The one idea to hold onto

In solar, the further upstream you go, the fewer competitors and the fatter the margin — but the harder and more capital-hungry the plant. Anyone can bolt together modules; making cells is a real barrier; making wafers is a national-scale industrial project. Emmvee's entire investment case is a single sentence: it is walking up that chain, one expensive step at a time, and pocketing the margin at each rung the moment its own capacity replaces an import. Keep this "climb the chain" picture in mind and every later section clicks.

02

The business model: capacity, utilisation, and the spread per watt

A factory earns on four numbers — and one of them isn't revenue.

Emmvee is a classic manufacturing business, so it lives and dies by factory arithmetic rather than the "occupancy" or "same-store" logic of services. Four levers explain almost everything, and management is unusually blunt that the headline (revenue) can mislead — what matters is the margin captured on each watt produced.

The factory levers
LeverWhat it means (and Emmvee's number)
Capacity (GW)The nameplate ceiling on annual output. Emmvee: 10.3 GW of module lines, 2.94 GW of cell lines (Mar 2026), rising fast.
Utilisation (%)How much of that ceiling is actually running. FY26: module lines just ~43% (new lines had only partial availability); cell lines ~70%, hitting ~85% by May 2026. Under-used lines carry full fixed cost.
ASP per watt (₹/W)The selling price of a watt of module. Volatile and globally falling; Indian DCR-grade modules command a large premium over Chinese. This is the top line of the spread.
EBITDA per watt (₹/W)The profit kept on each watt after cell and input costs — the number management says to watch instead of revenue. Backward integration widens it.
Why "EBITDA per watt", not revenue, is the honest gauge

A module maker can grow revenue simply by pushing more panels out of the door at a wafer-thin margin — impressive-looking sales, negligible profit. That was essentially Emmvee in FY24: nearly ₹1,000 Cr of revenue, a ~3% net margin. The quality question is how many rupees of gross profit stick to each watt sold. When Emmvee makes the cell itself, it stops paying a supplier's mark-up and keeps that spread; when it merely assembles bought-in cells, the spread is thin. So the company (rightly) tells investors to track absolute EBITDA and EBITDA/watt, because a quarter where it sells fewer but more integrated watts can be worth more than a bigger, thinner one. Revenue is the vanity number; the per-watt spread is the sanity number.

The two product grades — and why one earns triple

  • DCR modules — panels built with Indian-made cells, eligible for "Domestic Content Requirement" projects (government-subsidised rooftop, PM-KUSUM, PSU tenders). Scarce, protected, and premium-priced.
  • Non-DCR modules — panels that may use imported cells; sold to the open market, where they compete much more directly on price with imports.
  • The point of integration: owning a cell line lets Emmvee build DCR-eligible panels and keep the cell margin — the difference between being a price-taker and a price-maker in the same factory.
03

The company and who controls it

From solar water-heaters in 1992 to a listed gigawatt-scale panel maker.

Emmvee is a founder-controlled Bengaluru business, not a private-equity roll-up. Its roots go back to solar water heaters in 1992; it moved into photovoltaic modules in the late 2000s and has since ridden — and helped build — India's solar-manufacturing boom. The promoter family retains a commanding ~80% stake after the IPO, so the people running the factory own most of it.

Timeline

  • 1992
    The Emmvee group is founded in Bengaluru by D.V. Manjunatha, initially in solar water heaters — a foothold in solar thermal long before PV was viable in India.
  • 2007
    Emmvee Photovoltaic Power Limited is incorporated to make PV modules, entering the panel business as India's solar market begins to stir.
  • 2010s
    Scales module capacity through the National Solar Mission era; builds a domestic customer base among developers and EPC contractors.
  • FY24–25
    Commissions its own TOPCon solar-cell manufacturing — the pivotal backward-integration step. PAT leaps from ₹29 Cr (FY24) to ₹369 Cr (FY25) as the cell margin is captured in-house.
  • Nov 2025
    IPO — ₹2,900 Cr issue (₹2,144 Cr fresh + ₹756 Cr offer-for-sale) at ₹217/share; lists flat on NSE & BSE on 18 Nov 2025. Fresh proceeds earmarked largely for capacity and debt reduction.
  • FY26
    Module capacity reaches 10.3 GW, cell 2.94 GW; revenue ₹5,049 Cr, PAT ₹1,082 Cr; order book 9.4 GW; announces a ~₹6,300 Cr ingot-and-wafer backward-integration plan.

Ownership & leadership

  • Promoter: D.V. Manjunatha (Chairman & Managing Director) and family, holding roughly 80% post-listing. High promoter ownership means aligned incentives — but also a small public float and concentrated control.
  • Base: Bengaluru, Karnataka, with manufacturing clustered in and around the region — convenient to South-India power markets and ports for wafer imports.
  • Balance sheet: after the fresh IPO money, Emmvee ended FY26 in a net-cash position (net debt-to-equity of about −0.06×), a marked change for a capital-hungry manufacturer and the fuel for its expansion.
A quiet flag: a ~34-year-old family business that spent most of its life below the radar has, in three years, become a multi-thousand-crore listed company with a wall of expansion to execute. The commercial track record is real; the public-company track record — governance under scrutiny, capital allocation across a much bigger balance sheet — is only just beginning.

That is not operating leverage in the usual sense — it is value-chain capture: the same factory keeping money it used to pay away. The ingot-wafer plant is a bet that the trick works one rung higher.

05

ASP, China, and the spread that is the whole business

Module prices have crashed globally — so why is Emmvee's margin rising?

To understand the risk and the opportunity, you have to hold two facts at once. Globally, solar-module prices have collapsed — Chinese factories, drowning in overcapacity, have driven module prices toward ~$0.10–0.12 per watt, with polysilicon at multi-year lows and inventories equal to hundreds of gigawatts of latent supply. And yet Emmvee's margins have expanded. Resolving that apparent contradiction is the key to the company.

Two prices for (almost) the same thing

A functionally similar solar cell sells for roughly three times as much when it qualifies for India's domestic-content projects. That wedge — not the physics of the cell — is where an Indian integrated maker earns its return. It exists only because policy forbids the cheap import in protected tenders.
The surprise most newcomers miss

You would assume a "solar" company wins when solar hardware gets cheaper and demand explodes. For a developer like ACME Solar, that's true — falling panel prices are pure fuel. For a manufacturer like Emmvee, falling global prices are the enemy: they compress what it can charge. What rescues Emmvee is the opposite of a free market — a protected one. The Indian cell that goes into a domestic-content panel fetches roughly 3× the price of the identical-looking Chinese cell, purely because a government approved-list makes the cheap one ineligible for a big chunk of Indian projects. So Emmvee's fortunes hinge less on how much sun India gets, or even on global solar demand, and more on how high and how durable New Delhi keeps the fence around its own market. A solar manufacturer is, in large part, a policy-arbitrage business wearing an engineering uniform.

Emmvee sells a commodity whose world price only falls — and grows its margin anyway, because the margin is manufactured by an approved-list, not by the panel.
The double-edge: the same import glut that props up Emmvee's protected pricing threatens its unprotected pricing. On non-DCR, open-market sales — and on any export ambition — Emmvee competes head-on with ultra-cheap Chinese supply. Cheap imports are simultaneously the reason India built the fence and the flood pressing against it.
06

The policy wall: ALMM, DCR, duties, PLI

The four-part fence that turns a commodity factory into a profitable one — and the section that matters most.

If you read only one section, read this. India has spent five years constructing a deliberate industrial fence to build a domestic solar-manufacturing base from scratch — and Emmvee is a direct beneficiary of every plank of it. The fence has four parts, and they are being raised, one stage of the value chain at a time.

The four planks of the fence
PlankWhat it doesEffect on Emmvee
ALMM List-I (modules)An "Approved List of Models & Manufacturers." Only listed makers' modules can be used in government / subsidised projects. Effectively excludes Chinese panels.Emmvee is on the list; guarantees access to the protected slice of demand.
ALMM List-II (cells)The same idea, extended to cells. From 1 June 2026, approved modules for non-exempt government projects must use Indian-made, listed cells.The big one. Protects cell-makers directly. Emmvee's captive TOPCon cells are on List-II — turning its integration bet into a licence to sell the scarce input.
Import duties (BCD)A ~40% basic customs duty on imported modules and ~25% on imported cells, taxing the Chinese alternative.Widens the price umbrella under which Indian hardware sells profitably.
PLI schemeProduction-Linked Incentives rewarding domestic, integrated manufacturing (bonus for going upstream to cells/wafers).Subsidises exactly the backward-integration ladder Emmvee is climbing.

The sequence is the tell. First the state protected modules (ALMM List-I) — which is why India now has ~190+ GW of module capacity, far above demand. That over-built the easy end. So the protection is now marching upstream: the 1 June 2026 cell mandate (List-II) does for cells what List-I did for modules, and manufacturers are already lobbying for the same treatment for wafers. Each time the fence moves a stage upstream, it mints a scarcity — and a margin — for whoever has capacity at that stage first.

Where the protection has reached — and where it's heading

~30 GW

The scarcity — and therefore the margin — sits wherever domestic capacity is thinnest relative to the demand the fence redirects to it. Today that's cells; tomorrow, if policy extends, wafers. Emmvee is positioning at each rung just ahead of the mandate.
The dependence cuts both ways: this fence is Emmvee's moat, and Emmvee's single largest risk. Every plank is a policy choice that can be softened, delayed or reversed — an ALMM exemption here, a duty rollback there, a mandate deferral (India has deferred ALMM before). The company's margins are, to an unusual degree, an administrative asset. That is a very different risk profile from a business whose moat is a brand or a patent.
07

Who buys the panels: the 500 GW demand pull

Behind the policy is a genuinely enormous, decade-long build-out.

Protection would be worthless without demand to protect. Here the tailwind is real and large: India has targeted roughly 500 GW of non-fossil power capacity by 2030, of which solar is the biggest chunk — a multi-hundred-gigawatt build-out that needs an ocean of panels, most of which policy insists be Indian-made. Emmvee sells into that pull.

Emmvee's customer mix — who actually places the orders

  • Independent Power Producers (IPPs) — utility-scale developers (the ACME Solars of the world) building large solar farms under long government contracts. The biggest, chunkiest orders; roughly 40% of Emmvee's mix.
  • Commercial & Industrial (C&I) — factories, campuses and businesses installing solar to cut power bills; ~30%. Higher-value, often DCR-linked.
  • Others — EPC contractors, distributors, rooftop and government-scheme demand (PM-KUSUM, PM Surya Ghar rooftop) — much of it DCR-mandated, i.e. must-be-Indian.

The subtle risk hiding inside the tailwind

A 9.4 GW order book sounds like years of visibility, and it is reassuring. But two cautions belong next to it. First, solar orders are priced at prevailing ASPs — an order booked today ships at tomorrow's module price, so a strong book doesn't lock in today's margins if prices keep falling. Second, Emmvee's demand is heavily domestic and policy-linked (exports were essentially zero in FY26). That concentration is comfortable while the fence stands and India builds — and uncomfortable if a policy pause slows domestic tendering, because there is little export cushion to fall back on. The demand is huge; it is also unusually correlated to a single government's follow-through.

08

The competitive set

A domestic land-grab — with the world's cheapest supplier waiting outside the fence.

Emmvee competes on two fronts at once. Inside the fence, it races a growing field of Indian integrated makers all adding cell and module capacity at the same time. Outside it, Chinese majors remain the low-cost benchmark — the reason the fence exists, and the permanent threat if it weakens. The domestic contest is the one that decides Emmvee's near-term margins.

The tension to watch inside the fence

Protection guarantees a market for Indian hardware — but it does not guarantee it for Emmvee specifically. The same incentives pulling Emmvee up the value chain are pulling everyone up it: Waaree, Premier, Adani, Vikram, ReNew and a dozen others are all racing to add cell (and eventually wafer) capacity behind the same ALMM wall. India is over-built at the module stage already; the worry is that the industry over-builds the cell stage too, and the scarcity premium that makes today's integration so lucrative erodes as domestic cell capacity swells past ~30 GW toward the demand line. Emmvee's edge is timing and focus — it committed to cells early and stayed a pure-play manufacturer — but the window in which cells are scarce is finite, and every rival is building toward closing it.

The one number that settles the thesis

At ~21× trailing earnings, Emmvee is not priced like a fragile story — the market is paying a fair-to-full multiple for a real profit base, unlike some richer solar peers. So the question isn't "is it cheap?" — it's "how durable is the FY26 margin?". And that reduces to one specific gauge: the EBITDA/watt spread on captive-cell modules — cell-line utilisation times the DCR/ALMM cell premium Emmvee can hold as domestic cell capacity floods in. If cell utilisation stays high (it reached ~85% by May 2026) and the protected cell premium holds while Emmvee's own cell capacity closes the gap with its modules, ~34% margins can persist and the earnings base grows into a larger, more integrated company. If the domestic cell glut compresses that premium faster than Emmvee climbs to wafers, the margin — and the multiple — normalise together. Watch captive-cell utilisation and realised per-watt spread, quarter by quarter; they are the vital signs.

10

The growth runway

Three rungs still to climb — cells, wafers, and (maybe) exports.

Emmvee's growth levers are unusually legible because they are physical and pre-announced: capacity being built, a value chain being deepened, and a demand wall that keeps rising. The near-term runway is about closing its own internal gap — making enough cells to feed its own modules — before chasing the next rung.

  • Rung 1 — cells catch modules. Cell capacity from 2.94 GW toward ~8.9 GW by FY27 shrinks the reliance on bought-in cells, lifting the share of high-margin, fully-integrated DCR panels. This is the most valuable near-term lever and needs no new demand — just execution.
  • Rung 2 — the wafer plant. The ~₹6,300 Cr, ~9 GW ingot-and-wafer project (first phase ~FY29) attacks the largest remaining imported input. If it works, it captures another margin layer and positions Emmvee for a future ALMM-for-wafers, the way its cells were positioned for List-II.
  • Rung 3 — export optionality. Exports were essentially zero in FY26 — management treats them as upside, not core. US and other protected Western markets (which also penalise Chinese supply) are a possible future outlet that would reduce reliance on the Indian policy cycle.
The execution reality: each rung is harder and more capital-intensive than the last. Modules were straightforward; cells took real process engineering (and imported German equipment); wafers are a genuinely different industrial discipline that even large Indian players have found daunting. The runway is clear — but it is a climb, not a stroll, and a slipped wafer-plant timeline or a cell-ramp stumble would show up directly in the per-watt spread.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A real, profitable manufacturer — riding a genuine boom on a policy-built margin.

Emmvee is one of the more credible plays on India building its own solar-manufacturing base: a founder-run, now net-cash integrated maker with explosive recent profits, a full order book, and a clear ladder to climb. The core debate is whether the FY26 margin is a new normal or a policy-and-scarcity high-water mark — and whether execution keeps pace with a crowded, capacity-adding field.

The bull case

  • Value-chain capture proven — the FY24→FY25 profit leap shows integration works; cells catching modules extends it.
  • Policy tailwind rising, not fading — the June 2026 cell mandate directly rewards early integrated cell-makers like Emmvee.
  • Net-cash after IPO — expansion funded without the leverage that burdens developers; ROE ~51%, ROCE ~38%.
  • Full order book into a 500 GW build-out — 9.4 GW booked, orders getting larger, demand structural.
  • Aligned owner — ~80% promoter holding; a 30-year operator, not a financial sponsor flipping the asset.

The bear case

  • Margin is policy-made — an ALMM exemption, duty rollback or mandate deferral could compress the very spread the thesis rests on.
  • Domestic cell glut — everyone is adding cell capacity behind the same fence; the scarcity premium is a wasting asset.
  • Global price crash — Chinese overcapacity keeps world module/cell prices falling, pressuring non-DCR and export pricing.
  • Execution & concentration risk — a hard wafer-plant build, near-zero exports, and demand tied to one government's tendering cadence.
  • Young public company — thin float, short listed history, first big capital-allocation cycle under scrutiny.
The bottom line

Emmvee is a real factory turning a genuine profit inside a genuine boom — but its extraordinary FY26 margin is the joint product of a scarce, protected cell and a demand wall the government keeps raising. The investable question is whether Emmvee can climb the chain faster than the protection it depends on gets crowded or diluted — filling its module lines with its own cells, then reaching wafers before the cell premium thins. The two milestones that will tell you are concrete and dated: whether the FY27-end cell capacity actually closes the gap with its modules, and whether the ~₹6,300 Cr ingot-wafer plant lands on schedule around FY29. Get through those with the per-watt spread intact, and Emmvee graduates from policy beneficiary to structural cost-leader; fall behind the field on either, and it becomes just another Indian module line waiting for the next tariff decision.
