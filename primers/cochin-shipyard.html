Cochin Shipyard, the yard that built India's aircraft carrier

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Cochin Shipyard Limited (CSL) is a Government-of-India defence PSU under the Ministry of Ports, Shipping & Waterways, and India's largest shipbuilding and ship-repair yard. It is best known for building INS Vikrant — the country's first Indigenous Aircraft Carrier (IAC-1), commissioned in 2022. It does three things: it builds warships and commercial ships, and it repairs and refits ships — the last being a steadier, higher-margin annuity that offsets the lumpiness of newbuild orders.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A cash-rich, order-backed defence yard. Revenue was ~₹5,022 Cr in FY26 with profit (PAT) of ~₹717 Cr. The Government of India owns ~67.9%. The balance sheet carries ~₹10,600 Cr of cash & investments — much of it customer advances — against modest borrowings, so it is effectively cash-rich. A ~₹21,100 Cr order book (Jun 2025) gives multi-year revenue visibility.
  • Defence is the engine; repair is the ballast. Roughly 65% of the order book is defence (anti-submarine craft, next-generation missile vessels, and the potential second carrier), with commercial newbuild and a fast-growing ship-repair business. Repair earns far fatter margins than newbuild — one quarter's repair EBIT margin ran above 40% versus ~10–12% for shipbuilding.
  • The story is a naval super-cycle meeting new capacity. India's "Atmanirbhar Bharat" naval build-up plus CSL's brand-new 310-metre dry dock and International Ship Repair Facility at Kochi mean it can now build and repair much bigger ships. That runway is exactly what the rich ~55× P/E is paying for — and the execution/lumpiness risk is exactly what could puncture it.
In plain terms

Think of Cochin Shipyard as India's national ship factory-and-garage, owned by the government. On the "factory" side it builds ships — mostly warships for the Navy and Coast Guard, some commercial vessels for global buyers. Warships are giant, multi-year, one-off projects: cash comes in lumps as milestones are hit, and a good year followed by a gap year is normal. On the "garage" side it repairs and overhauls other people's ships — smaller jobs, but frequent and much more profitable, which smooths the ride. Because it's the yard that pulled off the aircraft carrier, it sits first in line for the Navy's next big builds — and the whole investment case is a bet that India keeps ordering more warships for a decade while CSL's new, larger docks let it take on bigger jobs.

01

Shipyard economics 101: how a yard earns

No engineering degree required — just how money moves through a shipbuilding order.

A shipyard is not like a factory that stamps out identical widgets. It is a project business: each ship is a bespoke, multi-year construction contract worth hundreds or thousands of crores, built to order against a signed contract. Understanding four ideas explains almost the whole financial picture.

The ideas you actually need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters at CSL)
Order book / backlogThe value of signed, not-yet-completed contracts. It is a shipyard's most important number — it is future revenue you can already see. CSL's stood at ~₹21,100 Cr (Jun 2025), several years of sales.
Milestone (percentage-completion) revenueRevenue is booked as a ship is built, not when it's delivered — as each construction stage ("milestone") is certified, a slice of the contract is recognised. So revenue tracks physical progress on the slipway.
Customer advancesBuyers (especially the Navy) pay large sums upfront and along the way. That cash sits on CSL's balance sheet and funds the build — which is why the yard is cash-rich without borrowing.
Lumpiness / gestationBig ships take 3–8 years. A quarter can swing sharply depending on which projects are mid-build. Repair revenue and a deep order book are what smooth this out.
Why the order book is everything

For most companies you forecast next year's sales; for a shipyard you can partly read them off the order book. A ₹21,000 Cr backlog against ~₹5,000 Cr of annual revenue means roughly four years of work already booked — that visibility is the whole appeal of defence shipbuilders. But there is a catch built into the same mechanism: because each ship is lumpy and milestone-based, revenue and profit can be jumpy quarter to quarter, and a thin patch between the end of one big programme and the ramp-up of the next (an "order-book air-pocket") can dent a year's numbers even when the long-term pipeline is full. That is the tension every shipyard analyst lives with.

Newbuild vs repair — two very different engines

  • Shipbuilding (newbuild) — large, lumpy, lower-margin (guided ~10–12% for CSL). This is where the aircraft carrier, warships and export vessels sit. It dominates the order book.
  • Ship repair & refit — many smaller jobs, fast turnaround, and structurally much higher margin (one recent quarter's repair EBIT margin was ~44%). Lower headline revenue, but a steady, capital-light annuity that stabilises earnings and returns.
02

The business model: build, repair, and get paid in advance

Two segments, one strategic customer, and a balance sheet funded by its clients.

CSL reports through two segments — Shipbuilding and Ship Repair — but the strategic logic is a three-legged stool: defence newbuild for scale and visibility, commercial/export newbuild for diversification, and ship repair for steady, high-margin cash. The customer that matters most is the Indian Navy, and the way it pays — big advances against milestones — is why CSL barely needs debt.

The value chain — from steel plate to commissioned warship

A ship moves through the yard as a sequence of stages, and CSL earns revenue at each. The further the ship progresses, the more of the contract is recognised — and the more advance cash has already been received.

Why "paid in advance" is the quiet superpower

Defence and export shipbuilding contracts come with staged advance payments: the customer pays a chunk at signing, more as milestones are cleared, and holds back a final slice on delivery. That advance cash sits on CSL's books and pays for the steel, engines and labour before the ship is finished. The consequence is a balance sheet with ~₹10,600 Cr of cash and investments and only modest borrowings — the company is effectively net-cash. It earns interest ("other income") on that float, which is a real and recurring part of profit. The flip side: much of that cash is spoken for (it belongs to unfinished orders), and the reported profit leans partly on treasury income, not just operations — something to watch when the operating margin wobbles.

03

The company and who controls it

A 1972 Government yard that grew into the maker of a nation's aircraft carrier.

Cochin Shipyard was set up by the Government of India in 1972 as a greenfield yard at Kochi, Kerala. Over five decades it moved from building bulk carriers to servicing the world's ships to, ultimately, constructing INS Vikrant — a feat that put it in a very small global club of yards that can build an aircraft carrier. It listed in 2017 and remains majority state-owned.

  • FY26
    Revenue ~₹5,022 Cr; ~₹21,100 Cr order book; ship-repair revenue surging; management targets doubling revenue by FY31; Navratna-status review under way.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter: the President of India / Government of India, holding ~67.9%. CSL is a "Mini-Ratna" central PSU under the Ministry of Ports, Shipping & Waterways, with a review under way for the higher "Navratna" status (more financial autonomy).
  • Subsidiaries / JVs: Udupi Cochin Shipyard (an acquired west-coast yard adding commercial capacity) and Hooghly Cochin Shipyard (a Kolkata-region JV building smaller vessels and green ferries) extend the footprint beyond Kochi.
  • Balance sheet: effectively net cash — ~₹10,600 Cr of cash & investments (much of it customer advances) against ~₹1,672 Cr of borrowings; reserves ~₹5,741 Cr; book value ~₹223/share.
  • Why state ownership matters: as a government yard, CSL is strategically favoured for the Navy's most sensitive builds (the carrier, warships) — a durable advantage — but it also carries PSU realities: government as customer and owner, policy dependence, and dividend/capital decisions influenced by the state.
04

Defence: the yard that built the carrier

The engine of the story — and India's chosen builder for its most complex warships.

Defence shipbuilding is CSL's largest and most strategic business, ~65% of the order book. Building INS Vikrant — a ~45,000-tonne aircraft carrier, one of the hardest engineering projects a nation can attempt — proved CSL can do work almost no other Indian yard can. That reputation is a moat: for the Navy's biggest, most sensitive programmes, CSL sits at the front of the queue.

Why the carrier credential is worth so much

Only a handful of countries can build an aircraft carrier, and within India, CSL is the only yard that has actually done it. That matters because the Navy plans its future around two operating carriers, and the ageing INS Vikramaditya will eventually need a replacement — the widely-discussed IAC-2. If and when that programme is sanctioned, CSL is the natural builder: it has the dock, the workforce that built Vikrant, and the only living Indian experience of the task. A single carrier is a ~₹40,000–50,000 Cr, decade-long programme — a transformational order for a company CSL's size. The carrier is therefore both a completed job and a call option on the single biggest naval contract India can place. The honest caveat: IAC-2 is not yet formally sanctioned, and defence procurement timelines routinely slip by years.

The concentration reality: being the Navy's carrier-builder is a moat, but it also means CSL's fortunes are tied to a single, cyclical, politically-driven customer. Order flow depends on defence budgets, procurement decisions and geopolitics — none of which CSL controls. Diversification into commercial newbuild and repair is precisely the hedge against that dependence.
05

Ship repair: the quiet, high-margin stabiliser

Smaller than shipbuilding, but the part that smooths the ride and earns the fattest margins.

If shipbuilding is the lumpy, headline-grabbing business, ship repair is the annuity underneath it. It means dry-docking existing vessels — naval ships, offshore rigs, commercial ships — for overhaul, refit and life-extension. The jobs are smaller and faster than a newbuild, but they recur, they need far less capital, and crucially they earn much higher margins. This is the segment CSL is deliberately scaling.

Why repair earns so much more

A newbuild is a giant, fixed-price, multi-year contract where CSL bears the risk of steel prices, delays and cost overruns for years — so margins are thin. Repair is different: the yard sells scarce dock time, skilled labour and fast turnaround to a ship-owner who is losing money every day the vessel is idle. That urgency, plus limited repair capacity on India's coast, gives CSL strong pricing power on jobs that finish in weeks, not years. Less capital tied up, less risk, faster cash, richer margin. As CSL grows repair — using its new International Ship Repair Facility — it lifts blended profitability and reduces the earnings swings caused by lumpy newbuild. Management does flag that repair's share of the total will fall as shipbuilding scales, but its absolute revenue keeps growing (repair guided at ~₹1,500 Cr for FY26).

06

Commercial & green vessels: the export & future-fuel angle

The diversification leg — and a genuine early lead in the ships the world is trying to decarbonise.

Beyond defence, CSL builds commercial vessels for domestic and global buyers — and here it has quietly become a leader in one of the most interesting niches in world shipping: green, zero-emission and autonomous ships. This is both a diversification away from the single defence customer and a bet on where global shipbuilding demand is heading.

The commercial & green portfolio (jargon decoded)

  • Autonomous zero-emission cargo ships — CSL delivered the world's first fully autonomous, zero-emission ships to Norway's ASKO: crewless, electric cargo vessels. A genuine global first that showcases capability far beyond low-cost hulls.
  • Hydrogen fuel-cell ferries — India's first indigenous hydrogen fuel-cell passenger ferry, launched in 2025; plus hydrogen-powered feeder ships for European owners (Samskip's SeaShuttle green corridor).
  • Hybrid-electric ferries & SOVs — battery-hybrid catamaran ferries for the Inland Waterways Authority, and a hybrid-electric Service Operation Vessel (an offshore-wind support ship) for a UK owner — laid down in late 2025.
  • Conventional commercial newbuild — tankers, bulk carriers, multi-purpose and feeder vessels for export; CSL has exported ~45 ships historically.
Why "green vessels" is a real edge, not just PR

Global shipping must decarbonise, and that means a coming wave of demand for ships powered by hydrogen, ammonia, batteries and fuel cells — vessels most yards have never built. By taking on autonomous and zero-emission projects now, CSL is building the design and integration know-how early, while margins on these novel, complex ships are better than on commodity hulls. If the world's fleet-replacement cycle tilts green over the next decade, a yard with a track record of actually delivering hydrogen ferries and autonomous cargo ships is well placed to win export work — a way to grow that doesn't depend on the Indian Navy's budget. The honest limits: these are still small orders today, global commercial shipbuilding is fiercely price-competitive (dominated by Korean, Chinese and Japanese mega-yards), and green-ship demand could ramp slower than the hype suggests.

07

Bigger docks, bigger ships, bigger orders

The single most important physical fact about CSL's future — capacity just stepped up.

A shipyard can only build what its docks can physically hold. That is why CSL's roughly ₹2,768 Cr capacity expansion at Kochi — a giant new dry dock plus the International Ship Repair Facility — is so consequential. It doesn't just add capacity; it changes the class of ship CSL can build and repair, which changes the size of order it can win.

The two new facilities at Kochi

New Dry Dock310 m long × 75/60 m wide × 13 m deep; 600-tonne gantry crane; 600 t/m load capacityBig enough for a ~70,000-tonne aircraft carrier. A bigger dock = bigger ships = bigger, higher-value orders — including a potential larger IAC-2.

Why a bigger dock is a bigger business

Ship contracts scale with size: a larger, more capable vessel is worth far more than a small one, and only a yard with a dock big enough to hold it can bid. Before the new dry dock, CSL's largest-ship ceiling limited which tenders it could chase. Now, with a 310-metre dock rated for a 70,000-tonne carrier, CSL can pursue the very largest naval and commercial projects — and repair the largest ships that previously had to sail abroad for dry-docking. In effect, the expansion raises CSL's addressable market at both ends: bigger newbuilds and bigger repairs. The catch is the usual one for capacity-led growth — the docks are a large fixed cost, and the returns only show up if the orders arrive to fill them. Empty world-class docks are expensive.

Capacity is destiny for a shipyard: you cannot win the order for a ship you cannot physically fit in your dock.
08

The naval super-cycle & Atmanirbhar Bharat

The structural force that defines the whole investment case — and CSL sits at its centre.

Every defence PSU rides government spending, but Indian naval shipbuilding is in something more specific: a multi-decade build-up driven by geopolitics and self-reliance policy. India wants a much larger navy to counter a rising Chinese fleet in the Indian Ocean; and under "Atmanirbhar Bharat" (self-reliant India), it wants those ships built at home. For a domestic yard with carrier credentials, that is the tailwind of a generation — and it is exactly what the market's premium valuation is capitalising.

These are qualitative drivers, not sized forecasts. The point: for the first time, fleet growth, a domestic-build mandate and geopolitics all pull in the same direction — toward more orders for Indian yards.

Put simply: India is committed to expanding its navy over the next 10–15 years, and policy insists those warships be built domestically. That is a structural, multi-year order pipeline flowing to a small set of Indian yards — of which CSL, as the carrier-builder, is one of the most strategically important. The order book (~₹21,100 Cr, ~65% defence) is the visible tip of that pipeline; the potential IAC-2 is the giant, not-yet-sanctioned prize sitting on top.

Why this is powerful — but not a guarantee

The bull case is genuinely strong: a rising fleet + a build-at-home mandate + a favoured national builder = years of visible orders and a reason for the market to pay up. But there are three honest counterweights. (1) Timing is government-controlled. Defence procurement is famously slow — sanctions, tenders and negotiations slip by years, so the pipeline can be real yet arrive later than hoped, leaving order-book "air-pockets". (2) The orders are shared. CSL competes with Mazagon Dock and GRSE for naval work; the carrier is CSL's edge, but many programmes are contested. (3) Execution risk on mega-projects. Building a carrier is extraordinarily hard; delays and cost pressures on such programmes directly hit margins (as FY26's softer operating margin showed). The super-cycle is the reason to own the sector; execution and timing are why it doesn't compound in a straight line.

The honest tension: almost all of CSL's premium rests on the expectation of sustained naval ordering, culminating in a possible IAC-2. If procurement stalls or a big programme slips, revenue can flat-line for a year or two even though the long-term case is intact — and a stock priced for smooth growth reacts badly to that. Watch fresh order inflows and any IAC-2 sanction news above all else.
09

The industry and the rivals

A small club of state-owned yards, each with its own speciality.

Indian defence shipbuilding is dominated by a handful of government-owned yards, each historically specialising in different vessel types. They compete for naval tenders but are rarely head-to-head across the board — the Navy spreads work to keep multiple yards viable. Globally, commercial shipbuilding is a different, brutally competitive world ruled by Asian mega-yards.

How to read the peer set

Two things stand out. First, on pure returns, MDL and GRSE screen better than CSL — their ROEs (~37–40%) dwarf CSL's ~12.5%, partly because CSL's big carrier/complex builds are lower-margin and its balance sheet carries a large low-yielding cash pile. So CSL is not automatically the "best" defence shipbuilder on financials. Second, CSL's differentiation is capability and repair, not headline margin: it is the carrier-builder with the biggest docks and India's leading repair business. The investment question is whether that unique capability (and the IAC-2 optionality) justifies a valuation that, on current returns, sits richer than more profitable peers. That is a live debate — some brokerages are lukewarm on CSL specifically because peers convert capital better.

The policy backdrop: the government is CSL's owner, its biggest customer and its regulator. Defence-indigenisation policy and shipbuilding subsidies help; but the same state control means order flow, pricing and even capital-return decisions are policy-driven. A potential Navratna upgrade would give CSL more autonomy to invest and partner — a modest structural positive to watch.

How to think about the ~55× multiple

At ~₹39,600 Cr market cap on ~₹717 Cr of FY26 profit, CSL trades around 55× trailing earnings — a premium even among defence PSUs, and richer than more-profitable peers on a returns basis. That price makes sense only if you believe the naval super-cycle delivers years of rising orders, the new docks fill with bigger contracts, IAC-2 eventually lands, and margins recover as execution matures. Bulls argue a debt-free, cash-rich, strategically-irreplaceable carrier-builder with four years of visible backlog deserves a scarcity premium. Bears point to the FY26 profit fall, a ~12.5% ROE well below peers, treasury income flattering earnings, and a 55× multiple that leaves no room for a procurement slip or a soft-margin year. Both can be right at different points — which is why order inflows and operating margin are the numbers to watch each quarter, not the headline backlog alone.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A strategically vital, cash-rich yard — priced for the super-cycle to keep delivering.

Cochin Shipyard is one of the cleaner ways to own India's naval build-up: the state-favoured carrier-builder, cash-rich, with a deep order book and brand-new capacity, targeting a doubling of revenue by FY31. The debate is not whether the business matters — it plainly does — but whether the current price already assumes flawless execution of a government-timed pipeline.

The bull case

  • Strategic moat — the only Indian yard to build an aircraft carrier; first in line for the Navy's biggest, most complex programmes.
  • Visible backlog — ~₹21,100 Cr order book (~65% defence) = ~4 years of revenue already booked.
  • Cash-rich, self-funding — effectively net cash (~₹10,600 Cr); customer advances fund the build; no debt strain.
  • New capacity — 310 m dry dock (carrier-capable) + doubled ship-repair capacity unlock bigger, higher-value orders.
  • Structural tailwind — naval expansion + Atmanirbhar Bharat + IAC-2 optionality + a growing green/export niche.
  • High-margin repair annuity — a fast-growing, ~40%-margin stabiliser smoothing newbuild lumpiness.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~55× earnings, a premium to peers and to its own ~12.5% ROE; little room for error.
  • Lumpy, mix-driven margins — FY26 profit fell ~13% despite higher revenue; operating margin compressed.
  • Government dependence — order flow, pricing and capital returns hinge on defence budgets and PSU policy CSL can't control.
  • Procurement timing risk — IAC-2 unsanctioned; big programmes slip by years, risking order-book "air-pockets".
  • Lower returns than peers — MDL/GRSE convert capital far better; a big low-yield cash pile dilutes CSL's ROE.
  • Execution & competition — mega-builds are hard; private yards (L&T) and global commercial rivals encroach over time.
The bottom line

Cochin Shipyard is a strategically irreplaceable, cash-rich national asset riding a genuine multi-decade naval super-cycle, with new docks that expand what it can build and a high-margin repair business underneath. The business risk is modest; the valuation and timing risk is real. The next few years will be written in three numbers — fresh order inflows (is the pipeline converting to signed contracts?), operating margin (is execution improving after FY26's dip?), and any IAC-2 sanction (the giant optionality). Get those right and the ~55× multiple can be grown into; stall on procurement or margins, and a richly-priced PSU has a long way to fall.
