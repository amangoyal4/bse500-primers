Triveni Engineering, sugar's cash, ethanol's engine

A full walk through one of India's largest integrated sugar-and-ethanol makers — the UP-cane conglomerate that also builds industrial gears and treats the country's dirty water. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Triveni Engineering & Industries Limited (TEIL) is a diversified company anchored in sugar. It is one of India's largest integrated sugar producers, crushing cane at a cluster of mills across Uttar Pradesh and turning it into sugar, and — increasingly — into ethanol and industrial alcohol at a fast-growing set of distilleries. Bolted onto that farm-and-fermentation core are two very different engineering businesses: a market-leading maker of high-speed industrial gears and gearboxes (power transmission), and a water & wastewater treatment engineering-and-services arm. It is run by the Sawhney family's Triveni Group — and it is a completely separate company from Triveni Turbine, the steam-turbine maker that was hived off years ago.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The core is a regulated, cyclical sugar business — the growth is the distillery. Sugar is where most of the revenue and the balance sheet sit, but it is hostage to cane prices the state fixes, sugar prices the state influences, and a monsoon nobody controls. The distillery — fermenting cane juice, syrup, molasses and grain into ethanol — is where the margin and the momentum now live, riding India's national programme to blend ethanol into petrol. In FY26 alcohol output hit a record ~23.7 crore litres, up ~18%, and the distillery was explicitly the year's growth driver.
  • Two engineering arms sit alongside as higher-margin ballast — and one is leaving. The gears business earns roughly a 35% operating margin — a different universe from sugar's high-single-digits — while the water business carries a large multi-year order book (~₹1,600 Cr, much of it long O&M contracts). In a landmark move, TEIL is demerging the entire power-transmission (gears) business into a separately listed company, Triveni Power Transmission Ltd, on a 1:3 ratio (record date 22 July 2026). After the split, TEIL is a sugar + ethanol + water company; the gears trade on their own.
  • FY26 was a solid, distillery-led year. Consolidated revenue rose ~10.6% to ~₹6,291 Cr and net profit ~12.3% to ~₹269 Cr, even as cane crushing fell ~9% on a poor UP crop. The stock trades around 24× earnings — a multiple that says the market is paying for the ethanol growth and the value the demerger is meant to surface, not for the sugar cycle alone.
In plain terms

Triveni is really three companies wearing one ticker. The biggest and oldest crushes Uttar Pradesh sugarcane into sugar — a heavily regulated, weather-driven, low-margin cyclical whose selling and buying prices are both shaped by government. The fastest-growing takes the same cane's sugars and ferments them into ethanol for petrol, a policy-blessed business with far better economics. And a pair of engineering units — precision gears and water treatment — earn steadier, richer margins that have long subsidised the volatility of the sugar cycle. The 2026 story is that the family has decided the gears deserve their own stock-market identity, and is spinning them out. What remains is a bet that sugarcane is worth more converted into fuel than sold as sugar.

01

Many businesses under one share — the map

Before the mechanics, get the shape: four segments, wildly different economics, about to become three.

The single most confusing thing about Triveni to a newcomer is that "the company" is not one thing. It is a portfolio of businesses with almost nothing in common operationally — a sugar mill and a gearbox factory share no customer, no input and no cycle. What binds them is a balance sheet and a controlling family. To read the stock you first have to see the parts and how much each contributes.

Why the mix is the whole investment question

A pure sugar mill is valued like a commodity cyclical — cheaply, and nervously. A pure ethanol play is valued like a policy-backed growth story — richly. A precision-gears maker is valued like a niche capital-goods leader — richly again, on very different grounds. Triveni is all of these bundled together, which means the market has always struggled to price it: value the whole thing on sugar and you underrate the ethanol and engineering; value it on ethanol and you overrate the sugar. The bespoke tension in this stock is not "is sugar good this year" — it is "how do you fairly price a low-margin cyclical stapled to three higher-margin businesses?" Everything that follows is an attempt to answer that.

02

Sugar 101 and the cane bargain that sets the floor

The regulated, cyclical core — and the single input price the company does not set.

To understand Triveni you have to understand how an Indian sugar mill actually makes money, because it is nothing like a normal factory. A mill buys one raw material — sugarcane — at a price fixed by the government, from farmers it is legally obliged to buy from, and sells sugar into a market whose price the government also leans on. Both ends of the P&L are politically managed. That is the box the whole sugar business lives in.

The only sugar terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
FRP / SAPThe cane price. The Centre sets a Fair & Remunerative Price (FRP); states like Uttar Pradesh set their own, higher State Advised Price (SAP). A mill must pay it regardless of what sugar sells for — so cane cost is fixed before the mill knows its selling price. This is the crux of sugar's margin risk.
Recovery rateHow much sugar you extract per tonne of cane, as a %. A tenth of a percent matters across millions of tonnes. Cane variety, weather and pest all move it — FY26's recovery in western UP was hurt by red-rot disease and poorer cane.
Sugar MSP & quotasThe government sets a minimum selling price for sugar and a monthly release quota capping how much each mill can sell — controlling the market to protect both farmers and consumers. So a mill can't simply sell all its sugar when prices are high.
Molasses & bagasseThe two by-products. Molasses (and B-heavy molasses, and now diverted syrup/juice) is the feedstock for the distillery. Bagasse — the crushed fibre — is burnt to cogenerate power that runs the mill and sometimes feeds the grid. Nothing is wasted; the by-products are where the extra value hides.
Cane arrearsMoney a mill still owes farmers for cane already delivered. Because mills pay a fixed cane price whatever sugar does, a bad year builds arrears — a politically charged, cash-draining overhang unique to this industry.
The structural squeeze that defines a sugar mill

Here is the geometry that makes sugar hard. The mill's biggest cost — cane — is fixed by government before the season, and it must buy all the cane farmers in its command area grow. Its biggest revenue — sugar — is capped and controlled by minimum prices and release quotas. So the mill is squeezed between an administered cost it cannot lower and an administered price it cannot freely raise, on a crop whose yield swings with the weather. In a good cane year with firm sugar prices it earns a modest spread; in a bad one it can crush at a loss and pile up arrears to farmers. That asymmetry is exactly why the industry has spent a decade trying to escape sugar into ethanol — a use of the same cane that the government actively wants to grow.

What it does buy is a large, captive stream of molasses, syrup and juice — the raw material for the far more interesting business next door.

03

The juice fork: sugar or fuel?

The defining operating decision of a modern Indian sugar company — and it is unique to this industry.

Once cane is crushed, its sugars can go two ways, and the mill chooses the split every season. Path one: crystallise them into sugar and sell it into a capped, cyclical market. Path two: divert that same sucrose — as cane juice, syrup or B-heavy molasses — into the distillery to be fermented into ethanol, sold to oil companies at a policy-set price. This choice — call it the juice fork — is the single most important lever a company like Triveni pulls, and it exists in no other industry.

Diverting sucrose to ethanol lowers the sugar you make (which is why FY26 production held flat on lower cane — some cane went to fuel) but often earns a better, more certain return per unit of sugar sacrificed. The government encourages the diversion to hit its blending target and to keep sugar surpluses from crashing prices.

Why is the fork such a gift? Because it lets a company convert its worst problem into its best opportunity. A sugar surplus normally crushes sugar prices and builds unsold stock; diverting that surplus into ethanol drains it into a guaranteed-offtake, policy-priced market instead. The government gets its fuel-blending target; the mill gets steadier cash and shorter working-capital cycles (oil companies pay faster than sugar drains through quotas); the farmer keeps getting paid. It is the rare policy where the mill, the state and the farmer are broadly aligned — which is exactly why the ethanol programme has been durable.

The device that only a cane-plus-distillery company has: the sacrifice ratio

The number that runs this business is not the price of sugar or the price of ethanol on their own — it is the relative return on a tonne of sucrose sent to sugar versus the same tonne fermented into ethanol, after allowing for the sugar you give up. Each season Triveni effectively re-solves that equation: if ethanol pays better per unit of sugar sacrificed, divert more (lean to B-heavy and syrup); if sugar prices spike, crystallise more. No pure sugar mill and no pure distiller faces this choice — only an integrated cane-to-ethanol company can arbitrage its own raw sugar between two end markets. That optionality is a real, structural advantage, and it is why the same falling-cane year (FY26) could still be a rising-profit year: management pushed the fork toward fuel and let record ethanol volumes carry the flat sugar.

04

The distillery engine: where the growth and the margin now live

Ethanol blending, a capacity build, and multi-feed flexibility — the reason to own this stock.

If sugar is the cyclical floor, the distillery is the growth story, and it exists because of one national policy: India's push to blend ethanol into petrol to cut its crushing oil-import bill and its carbon. The country raced from single-digit blending to a 20% (E20) target, and it needs enormous volumes of ethanol to get there. Companies like Triveni that can ferment their own cane sugars into fuel are the direct beneficiaries — and Triveni has spent heavily to be one of the biggest.

Capacity went from 320 to 860 KLPD in four years — and Triveni continues to add multi-feed and grain-based lines (e.g. a new 160 KLPD grain distillery and further molasses-based units), backed by a stated ₹1,000 Cr-plus expansion plan. This is where the capital is going.

Three features make Triveni's distillery leg genuinely strong, not just big:

  • Multi-feed flexibility. The plants can run on cane juice, syrup, B-heavy or C-heavy molasses and grain (maize). That means when cane sugars are scarce or expensive — as in FY26 — the distillery can swing to grain and keep running. This flexibility is why FY26 alcohol volumes hit a record even as cane crushing fell; falling maize prices actually helped margins.
  • Ethanol-heavy sales. Ethanol for petrol blending is now the overwhelming majority of alcohol sales (~90%+), with the balance in extra-neutral alcohol (ENA) for potable/industrial use. The offtake is contracted to oil marketing companies at administered prices, giving far more revenue certainty than the sugar quota system.
  • Better, steadier economics. The distillery earns a materially higher and less volatile margin than raw sugar, and the government's periodic revision of ethanol procurement prices provides a policy floor. In FY26 the distillery was, in the company's own framing, the key driver of the year's growth.

Why the distillery re-rates the whole company

The distillery does something subtle to Triveni beyond adding profit: it changes what kind of company this is. A pure sugar mill is a price-taker on both sides, valued as a nervous cyclical. Graft on a large, growing, contracted-offtake ethanol business and a chunk of the earnings becomes policy-backed and far more predictable — the sort of stream the market pays a higher multiple for. That is why Triveni trades nearer a capital-goods multiple than a sugar-mill one. The risk sits in the same place as the reward: the ethanol economics rest on government procurement prices and the blending mandate, and a squeeze on either — or a swing in grain/molasses costs — hits the engine directly. But for now, the policy wind is firmly at its back.

05

The family, the group, and who controls it

A Sawhney-family conglomerate with a long UP-sugar lineage — and a habit of unbundling.

Triveni Engineering is the flagship of the Triveni Group, controlled by the Sawhney family. It is a founder-run industrial house with a distinctive pattern: build several unrelated businesses under one roof, then, when one grows valuable enough to stand alone, spin it off as its own listed company. Understanding that pattern explains both the company's shape today and the demerger unfolding right now.

Timeline

  • 1930s–1990s
    The Triveni sugar business takes root in Uttar Pradesh; the group builds up into sugar, engineering (gears, turbines) and later water — a classic Indian diversified industrial house.
  • 2006
    Triveni Engineering & Industries in its modern listed form is consolidated (BSE code 532356; NSE: TRIVENI), housing sugar, distillery, gears, turbines and water under one company.
  • ~2010–11
    Triveni Turbine demerged — the steam-turbine business is hived off into a separate listed company, Triveni Turbine Ltd. (This is why the two names are constantly confused: they share a heritage and a family, but are now distinct companies with distinct shares.)
  • FY19–FY22
    The ethanol-blending programme accelerates nationally; Triveni pivots hard into distillery capacity, converting more of its cane sugars into fuel.
  • FY23–FY25
    Distillation capacity vaults from 320 to 860 KLPD; multi-feed and grain lines added. The distillery grows into the profit engine; engineering margins stay rich.
  • 2026
    Power-transmission (gears) demerger announced and executed — record date 22 July 2026, 1:3 ratio, into Triveni Power Transmission Ltd; a parallel restructuring folds in sugar subsidiary Sir Shaadi Lal Enterprises. FY26 delivered record distillery volumes and ~₹269 Cr PAT.

Ownership & control

  • Promoter (Sawhney family / Triveni Group): holds roughly 60–61% of the company — a firm controlling stake, with no disclosed pledge/encumbrance on promoter shares (a governance positive). The balance is public float, including institutions. Confirm the exact figure in the latest shareholding filing.
  • Leadership: the business is run by the Sawhney family, with Tarun Sawhney as Vice-Chairman & Managing Director a prominent public face — including as an active voice on ethanol-blending policy. Founder-family stewardship brings long-termism and capital-allocation continuity; it also concentrates strategic decisions in one house.
  • The unbundling instinct: having already spun out Triveni Turbine, the group is now spinning out Triveni Power Transmission. The consistent logic: a diversified conglomerate's parts are worth more separately listed than buried inside a sugar company the market prices as a cyclical.
Why the family pattern matters to a minority investor: Triveni's controllers clearly believe in "value through separation," and they have twice acted on it. That can be genuinely shareholder-friendly — a demerger hands you a direct, tradable share in the hidden high-margin business rather than leaving it obscured. But serial restructuring (turbine spin-off, gears spin-off, subsidiary mergers) also demands that you watch the terms: ratios, related-party dealings and how debt and central costs are split between the pieces. The direction of travel has favoured minorities so far; the discipline is to keep checking that each new deal does too.
06

The demerger of the gears: a 35%-margin business leaves home

The live, defining corporate event of 2026 — and the fact that surprises everyone who assumes "sugar company."

Here is what almost nobody expects on first meeting Triveni: buried inside this Uttar Pradesh sugar company is a world-class industrial-gears business earning roughly a 35% operating margin — five times the margin of the sugar it sits next to. It makes high-speed and niche precision gears and gearboxes for industry, plus marine and defence applications, and it is a market leader in its slice. In 2026 the family decided that business no longer belongs hidden inside a cyclical, and moved to demerge it into a separately listed company: Triveni Power Transmission Ltd (TPTL).

The mechanics are precise and already in motion. TEIL is transferring the entire power-transmission business — its assets (~₹373 Cr), liabilities (~₹114 Cr), contracts, employees and intellectual property — into TPTL, and giving existing Triveni shareholders one TPTL share for every three TEIL shares held (a 1:3 ratio). The record date was 22 July 2026, with TPTL expected to list separately shortly after. Post-listing, an investor who held Triveni now holds two shares: the sugar-ethanol-water company, and a standalone precision-gears company.

The genuine surprise to sit with

The instinct is to file Triveni under "UP sugar mill" and move on. But the most valuable business it owns per rupee of sales is a precision-gears maker with a ~35% margin and a growing order book — a business with more in common with a specialist capital-goods company than with a sugar refinery. It has been sitting inside a sugar wrapper for years, its quality masked by a low-margin, cyclical core and a valuation to match. The 2026 demerger is essentially management saying out loud: this hidden jewel is worth more in the open. The gears' order book had grown ~25% (to the high-₹400-Cr range) and capacity is being scaled from ~₹400 Cr toward ~₹700 Cr of potential revenue — so it walks out the door not as a tired appendage but as a growing, high-return franchise. That a sugar company's crown asset makes gearboxes is the kind of fact that reframes how you read the whole business.

What the demerger means for the remaining company: after the split, TEIL is a cleaner sugar + ethanol + water business — it loses the highest-margin segment (a dilution to blended group margin) but gains a simpler story the market can price on its own terms. TPTL, meanwhile, becomes a pure-play high-margin gears company. The bet behind the move is that "sugar-ethanol-water" and "precision gears" are each worth more with their own valuation than the two were lumped together and priced as a conglomerate. Whether that value actually shows up is the near-term scoreboard: watch where TPTL trades once listed, and what multiple the slimmed-down TEIL holds.
07

Water and the engineering ballast

The quiet, asset-light annuity that steadies a weather-driven company.

The least-discussed Triveni business may be the most useful for smoothing the ride: water and wastewater treatment. It designs, builds and — crucially — operates and maintains water treatment, effluent-treatment, common-effluent and sewage plants for municipal and industrial clients. Where sugar swings with the monsoon and ethanol with policy, water throws off steadier, contract-backed revenue that helps carry the company through the cycle.

Two things make the water arm valuable out of proportion to its size:

  • A long, visible order book. The water business carries a multi-year order book in the region of ~₹1,600 Cr, a large share of it long-dated O&M contracts (roughly ₹1,100 Cr) rather than one-off builds. O&M revenue is annuity-like — it recurs for years after a plant is commissioned — which gives Triveni a slice of predictable, higher-quality income that the sugar business simply cannot.
  • Asset-light and policy-tailwinded. Unlike sugar's cane-and-capital intensity, water is an engineering-and-services business that ties up relatively little of its own capital. It also rides real structural demand: India's tightening environmental norms and municipal water investment mean more treatment plants and more contracts to run them.
Why the ballast businesses earn their place

For most of Triveni's life, the engineering arms — gears and water — did an unglamorous but vital job: they earned steady, higher-quality profit that cushioned the lurches of the sugar cycle, so the whole company never lived or died on one cane crop. With the gears now demerging, the water business inherits that role alone within TEIL: it is the counter-cyclical, contract-backed, asset-light stream that keeps the remaining company from being a pure play on sugarcane and ethanol policy. It will never be the headline — but a treatment plant under a 15-year O&M contract keeps paying whether or not the monsoon shows up, and that is worth more than its modest revenue suggests.

After the power-transmission demerger, water becomes TEIL's only remaining engineering segment — so its order-book conversion and O&M renewals matter more to the remaining company than they did when the gears were also inside. It is small, but it is the diversification that is left.
08

Adding up the parts

Why a sum-of-the-parts lens fits Triveni better than any single multiple — and how the demerger uses it.

Because Triveni's segments have such different economics, no single P/E or EV/EBITDA describes it well. The honest way to value it is a sum-of-the-parts: price each business on its own kind of multiple, add them up, and compare to the market cap. That is precisely the logic the family is monetising by demerging — turning an implicit sum-of-parts into two actual, separately traded prices.

Held this way, the ordering flips from the revenue chart in section 01. Post-demerger, the ethanol/distillery leg is arguably the largest value contributor despite sugar's far larger revenue — because a rupee of policy-backed, growing ethanol profit earns a higher multiple than a rupee of capped, cyclical sugar profit. The water annuity adds a modest, steady increment on top; the sugar core, for all its bulk, contributes the least per rupee of sales. The separately-listed gears (TPTL) now sit outside this box entirely — priced by the market directly.

The demerger is a live experiment in whether the market will pay for the parts. Before it, an investor buying Triveni got the gears "for free" inside a sugar valuation — the ~35%-margin business was there, but the share price rarely credited it. By listing TPTL separately, the family forces the market to put an explicit price on the gears, and lets the remaining sugar-ethanol-water company be judged on its own merits. If the combined value of the two post-split shares exceeds the pre-split single share, the conglomerate discount was real and has been unlocked. That is the whole rationale — and the near-term test of it.

The valuation trap to avoid with a company like this

The mistake is to slap one multiple on the whole thing. Value Triveni entirely on sugar and you badly underrate the ethanol growth and the engineering quality; value it entirely on ethanol and you overrate the cyclical sugar bulk. Neither number is right because the company is not one business. The discipline is to price the ethanol stream as growth, the sugar as a cyclical, and the water as an annuity — three different lenses — and only then add them. The demerger is management doing exactly this in public: separating a business the blended multiple could never fairly capture, so each can be priced for what it truly is.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded sugar-and-ethanol field with distinct leaders — and a gears business with almost no listed peer.

Triveni competes in two utterly different arenas. In sugar and ethanol it is one of a handful of large integrated players — a scale game of cane command areas, distillery capacity and balance-sheet strength. In gears (soon to be TPTL) it competes in a niche where few listed Indian rivals exist at all. The two competitive maps could not look less alike.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

In sugar and ethanol, competition is less about winning individual customers than about cane command area, recovery efficiency, distillery capacity and balance-sheet room to keep expanding ethanol — the lowest-cost, best-integrated, best-capitalised player rides the policy tailwind hardest, and Balrampur is usually held up as that benchmark. Triveni's differentiator against these pure sugar names is precisely its diversification: the engineering arms have long given it a quality-of-earnings edge that a pure mill lacks. The irony of 2026 is that it is now handing one of those differentiators — the gears — to its own shareholders as a separate share, betting that clarity is worth more than conglomerate cover.

10

Financials & valuation

A distillery-led FY26, a mid-20s multiple, and a demerger the price is already partly discounting.

FY26 was a good, distillery-carried year: revenue up ~10.6% to ~₹6,291 Cr and net profit up ~12.3% to ~₹269 Cr, achieved despite a ~9% fall in cane crushing — proof that the ethanol engine can now offset a weak sugar crop. Unlike a pure sugar cyclical trading in the low teens, Triveni carries a mid-20s earnings multiple, reflecting the ethanol growth, the engineering quality, and the value the demerger is meant to release. The debate is not whether the year was good; it is what the slimmed-down company is worth once the gears have left.

The one number that settles the thesis: ethanol volume growth against a firm procurement price

At ~24× earnings, Triveni is not priced as a cheap sugar cyclical — it is priced for the ethanol engine to keep growing. So the metric that proves or breaks the remaining company is distillery volume growth (the ~23.7 crore litres of FY26, and the ramp of the new multi-feed/grain capacity toward it), read against the government's ethanol procurement price and the blending mandate that underwrite the economics. If volumes keep climbing on capacity already built and the procurement price holds, the mid-20s multiple is earned and the sugar cyclicality matters less each year. If the mandate stalls, procurement prices are cut, or grain/molasses costs spike, the engine sputters and the stock re-rates back toward the sugar cyclical it grew out of. Litres of ethanol times a policy-set price — that, far more than the price of sugar, is the scoreboard now.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A diversified cane company mid-transformation — into a cleaner sugar-ethanol-water play plus a spun-out gears jewel.

Triveni enters FY27 in the middle of reinventing itself: a record distillery year behind it, a near-tripled ethanol capacity to fill, a water order book to convert, and its highest-margin business — the gears — being handed to shareholders as a separate listed company. The bet is that sugarcane converted to fuel, plus an engineering annuity, plus an unbundled precision-gears franchise, is worth more than the old lumped-together conglomerate. What it cannot control is the policy and weather the whole edifice still rests on.

The bull case

  • Ethanol engine with a policy tailwind — E20 blending needs volume; Triveni's near-tripled, multi-feed distillery capacity is built to supply it at better, steadier margins than sugar.
  • The juice-fork optionality — being able to swing its own cane sugars between sugar and fuel (and its distillery between cane and grain) let it grow profit in FY26 even as crushing fell.
  • Value unlock via demerger — separately listing the ~35%-margin gears (TPTL) forces the market to price a jewel the sugar valuation had buried.
  • Diversification & ballast — the water annuity and (until now) engineering smoothed the sugar cycle; the group has a proven, minority-friendly unbundling playbook.
  • Founder stewardship — a long-term family owner with ~60% skin in the game, no pledged shares, and a clear "value through separation" strategy.

The bear case

  • Sugar is still the bulk — and still regulated — fixed cane prices (SAP), capped sugar prices, quotas and cane arrears make the core a low-margin, politically exposed cyclical.
  • Ethanol economics are policy-dependent — the whole growth case rests on the blending mandate and government procurement prices; a cut or a stall hits the engine directly.
  • Input & weather risk — a weak monsoon or cane disease (as in FY26) cuts crushing; swings in grain/molasses costs move distillery margins.
  • Loses its best margin — after the demerger, TEIL sheds the 35%-margin gears, diluting blended profitability to sugar-ethanol-water economics.
  • Not cheap — a mid-20s multiple already discounts a lot of the ethanol growth and demerger value; execution has to deliver to justify it.
The bottom line

Triveni Engineering is a cane company that has spent a decade turning itself from a sugar mill into an ethanol producer with engineering on the side — and in 2026 it is completing the reinvention by handing its highest-margin business to shareholders as a separate share. What remains is a cleaner wager: that Uttar Pradesh sugarcane is worth more fermented into fuel than crystallised into sugar, backed by a policy that wants exactly that, and steadied by a small water annuity. The core is still a regulated, weather-driven cyclical, and the ethanol growth still leans on Delhi's mandate and its procurement price. The next concrete tests are datable: where Triveni Power Transmission trades once it lists, whether the slimmed-down company holds its multiple without the gears, and whether distillery volumes keep climbing into FY27 as the new multi-feed capacity fills. Get the ethanol ramp and a firm procurement price, and the transformation looks vindicated; lose the mandate or the monsoon, and the sugar cycle reasserts itself over everything else.
