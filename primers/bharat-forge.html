Bharat Forge, from forge to firepower

A complete walk through the Kalyani group's flagship — one of the world's largest forging companies, transforming from a cyclical auto-forgings exporter into a diversified defence, aerospace and industrials engineering house. This primer explains what forging actually is and why scale + metallurgy are a real moat; how the same furnaces that shape truck crankshafts now build 155mm artillery guns; why the ₹11,000-crore defence order book is re-rating the whole company; how a US-truck-cycle export book and troubled European subsidiaries are the drag on the other side; and why a re-rated defence-and-diversification story trades on a rich multiple with a depressed earnings base.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Bharat Forge is, at its core, a metal-shaping company — one of the largest forging businesses on earth. Forging means shaping metal under enormous pressure to make parts that are exceptionally strong: crankshafts, front axles, connecting rods and chassis components for trucks and cars; drilling parts for oil rigs; components for mining machines, trains, power turbines and aircraft. It is the flagship of the Pune-based Kalyani group, run for over five decades by Babasaheb "Baba" Kalyani, and by tonnage it ranks among the world's top forging companies (long described as second only to Germany's thyssenkrupp).

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • It is two companies in one — and mid-transformation. The legacy business is cyclical auto forgings — a big export book of engine and chassis parts sold to global truck and car makers, dangerously sensitive to the US Class-8 (heavy truck) cycle. The new Bharat Forge is a diversified defence + aerospace + industrials + e-mobility engineering house. FY26 (year ended March 2026) delivered ₹16,812 Cr of consolidated revenue and ₹1,089 Cr of net profit — but standalone auto exports were soft while defence and industrials carried the story.
  • Defence is the re-rating engine. Through its subsidiary Kalyani Strategic Systems (KSSL), Bharat Forge builds 155mm artillery guns (the ATAGS howitzer), armoured vehicles, ammunition and defence exports. FY26 defence revenue was ~₹1,562 Cr, sitting on a ~₹10,961 Cr order book — multi-year, high-margin, high-visibility work that is exactly why a cyclical forging company now trades like a defence stock.
  • The two drags are the overseas subsidiaries and the auto cycle. The European/US forging operations have historically been lower-margin and loss-making; FY26 carried a ₹450 Cr impairment on the e-mobility (Tork Motors) bet and the start of a European steel restructuring. Meanwhile US heavy-truck exports were down sharply for much of the year. Those one-offs depressed profit and are why the trailing P/E screens optically enormous.
In plain terms

Picture a giant, century-honed metalworking workshop that has spent decades pounding red-hot steel into the strongest bits of the world's trucks. That skill — heating metal and squeezing it under thousands of tonnes of force so its internal grain aligns and it becomes far tougher than cast or machined metal — is the same skill you need to make an artillery gun barrel or an aircraft component. Bharat Forge's whole story is that it is redirecting that furnace: away from a boom-and-bust dependence on how many American trucks get built this year, and toward long, sticky, high-margin defence and aerospace contracts. Get that pivot right and a cyclical exporter becomes a structural growth compounder. The market has already started paying for the pivot — which is the entire investment debate.

01

What forging actually is — and why it's a business, not a commodity

No engineering degree required — just the one idea that explains the whole moat.

To understand Bharat Forge, you first have to understand forging — because it is the single capability that connects a truck axle, an oil-rig part and an artillery gun. Forging is the process of shaping metal by pressure — heating a billet of steel until it glows, then hammering or pressing it into shape between dies under thousands of tonnes of force. Unlike casting (pouring molten metal into a mould) or machining (cutting a part out of a solid block), forging reshapes the metal's internal grain structure, aligning it along the part's contours. The result is a component that is dramatically stronger, tougher and more fatigue-resistant than the same part made any other way.

That matters because forged parts are used precisely where failure is not an option — the crankshaft spinning thousands of times a minute inside an engine, the front axle beam holding up a fully-laden truck, the connecting rods, the landing-gear components on an aircraft, the barrel of a gun. These are "safety-critical" and "high-stress" parts. Nobody wants the cheapest crankshaft; they want one that will never crack.

Why this is a high-barrier engineering business

Anyone can buy a hydraulic press. What's hard is the combination that Bharat Forge has spent 60 years assembling: (1) scale — some of the world's largest forging presses and single forging facilities, which let it make very big parts and huge volumes at low unit cost; (2) metallurgy — deep expertise in alloys, heat treatment and die design, so the part comes out with exactly the right strength and fatigue life; and (3) qualification — global truck and car makers, oil majors and defence ministries only buy safety-critical parts from suppliers they have certified over years of testing. Winning a crankshaft programme for a global engine can take years of validation; once you're in, you're in for the life of the platform. That certification wall is the moat — and, crucially, it is dual-use: the same presses, metallurgists and quality systems that qualify you for a Daimler axle also qualify you for an aircraft forging or an artillery barrel.

Hold on to that last point — dual-use — because it is the hinge of the entire Bharat Forge story. A company that has already mastered making the toughest metal parts for the world's automakers is one of the few in India already equipped to make the toughest metal parts for the world's militaries. The pivot from trucks to guns is not a leap into an unrelated business; it is pointing an existing, world-class capability at a new, far more attractive customer.

02

The company and the Kalyanis

From a 1961 forge shop to a global engineering group — under one family for three generations.

Bharat Forge was founded in 1961 by Nilkanthrao "Neelkanth" Kalyani as a maker of forged components for India's fledgling automotive industry. The transformation into a global player is the work of his son, Babasaheb "Baba" Kalyani, an MIT-trained mechanical engineer who joined in 1972, started on the shop floor, and over five decades built the company from a small domestic forge into one of the world's largest forging groups and the flagship of the multi-billion-dollar Kalyani group.

  • 2010s
    Deliberate de-risking push begins — building the non-automotive / industrials book (oil & gas, power, rail, construction) and laying the groundwork for defence and aerospace.
  • 2016 onward
    Steps up the defence programme — artillery (ATAGS), protected vehicles, ammunition — building indigenous capability under the "Make in India" / defence-indigenisation push.
  • 2020–22
    Enters e-mobility (Tork Motors electric motorcycles, e-axles/components) and expands aerospace and aluminium light-weighting; buys US aluminium forging assets.
  • FY25–26
    Defence order book crosses ₹11,000 Cr (184 ATAGS howitzers, exports); consolidates the strategic business under Kalyani Strategic Systems; takes a ₹450 Cr e-mobility impairment and begins a European steel restructuring — pruning the drags while feeding the growth engines.

Ownership & control

  • Promoters: the Kalyani family holds roughly 44%, with Baba Kalyani as Chairman & Managing Director. A founder-family that has run the business for three generations means the strategic pivot into defence and diversification is driven by owners with a very long horizon.
  • Group context: Bharat Forge is the listed flagship of the Kalyani group (annual turnover over US$3 bn), which spans steel, forging, defence, renewables and speciality chemicals — giving the defence and industrial businesses a broader ecosystem to draw on.
  • Structure: the listed entity reports both a standalone business (the core Indian forging operation, the highest-margin part) and a larger consolidated group that folds in the overseas subsidiaries, the defence entities, castings (JS Autocast), aluminium and e-mobility — which is why standalone and consolidated numbers can move in opposite directions.
Standalone vs consolidated — read this once

Bharat Forge reports two sets of numbers and they tell different stories. Standalone is the crown-jewel Indian forging plant — a ~27% EBITDA-margin operation, but small and export-exposed (FY26 standalone revenue actually fell ~5% to ₹8,396 Cr as US truck exports slumped). Consolidated adds everything else — the overseas forging units, defence, castings, aluminium and e-mobility — and grew ~11% to ₹16,812 Cr as defence and industrials offset the auto weakness. The overseas subsidiaries are lower-margin, so consolidated margins are structurally thinner than standalone. When you hear "Bharat Forge does ~27% margins", that's the standalone gem; the group as a whole runs closer to ~17%. Watch both: standalone shows the health of the core, consolidated shows the whole machine.

The one mix-shift to watch

The single most important trend in the numbers is the rising share of defence + industrials and the falling share of auto exports. Each year the mix tilts further, the business becomes less cyclical and higher-margin — and, the bulls argue, deserves a higher multiple.

04

The forging moat — scale, metallurgy and the certification wall

The first of the three forces that truly define Bharat Forge.

Everything Bharat Forge does rests on one hard-won asset: it is genuinely world-class at forging. That sounds obvious, but in an industry of commodity metal-bashers it is rare — and it is what lets the company charge a premium, win multi-year platforms, and credibly walk into defence and aerospace. The moat has three layers, and they compound.

Layer 1 — Scale

Bharat Forge operates some of the largest forging presses and single forging facilities in the world. Scale does two things: it lets the company make very large forgings (big crankshafts, big industrial parts, gun components) that small forgers physically cannot; and it drives down unit cost, so it wins high-volume global programmes on price and capability. Capital-intensive plants like these are extraordinarily expensive to replicate — a would-be competitor needs hundreds of crores and years just to build the presses, before it has forged a single qualified part.

Layer 2 — Metallurgy & engineering

The real difficulty in forging isn't the press — it's knowing which alloy, which heat treatment, which die design will give a part exactly the strength and fatigue life a customer's engineering spec demands. This is decades of accumulated know-how, backed by in-house R&D, testing labs and increasingly by design capability — Bharat Forge doesn't just make to a drawing, it co-develops parts with customers. That engineering depth is what lets it move up the value chain, from raw forgings toward machined, assembled and even fully-engineered systems.

Layer 3 — The certification wall

This is the deepest moat. Global OEMs, oil majors and defence ministries buy safety-critical parts only from suppliers they have certified — a process that can take years of audits, sample testing and validation. Once a supplier is qualified onto a platform (an engine family, a truck programme, a weapon system), switching is costly and risky for the customer, so incumbents keep the business for the platform's whole life. Bharat Forge sits inside the qualified-supplier lists of a long roster of global truck and car makers — relationships that are, in effect, an annuity and a barrier to entry rolled into one.

Why the moat travels — the dual-use insight

Here's the point that makes Bharat Forge special. The three moat layers — scale, metallurgy, certification — were all built serving the auto industry. But they are not auto-specific. A press big enough for a heavy-truck crankshaft can forge an artillery component. The metallurgists who tune steel for a fatigue-critical axle can tune steel for a gun barrel that must survive thousands of firings. The quality systems that satisfy Daimler's audits are exactly what a defence ministry demands. So when Bharat Forge decided to enter defence and aerospace, it wasn't starting from zero — it was redeploying a world-class capability that very few companies anywhere possess. That is why the diversification is credible where, for a pure metal-basher, it would be fantasy.

05

The defence transformation and the order book

The second defining force — and the reason a forging company is re-rating like a defence stock.

If forging is Bharat Forge's foundation, defence is its future — and the single biggest reason the stock has re-rated. Through Kalyani Strategic Systems (KSSL), its defence subsidiary, Bharat Forge has built genuine, indigenous capability in the highest-value corner of defence manufacturing: artillery, armoured vehicles, ammunition and defence exports. In FY26 defence revenue reached ~₹1,562 Cr, sitting on a ~₹10,961 Cr order book — years of visible, high-margin work.

What KSSL actually builds

  • Artillery guns — the flagship. The ATAGS (Advanced Towed Artillery Gun System), a 155mm/52-calibre towed howitzer that can fire shells ~48 km, co-developed with India's DRDO and built by Bharat Forge and Tata Advanced Systems. In March 2025 the Ministry of Defence signed a landmark ~₹6,900 Cr order for 307 ATAGS guns; Bharat Forge/KSSL supplies 184 units (~60% of the order, worth ~₹4,000 Cr) — India's biggest indigenous artillery order.
  • Defence exports. KSSL has exported the Kalyani-42 and ATAGS-class 155mm systems — including orders from Armenia (6 ATAGS delivered, with intent for ~84 more) — proving the products can win in a global market, not just at home.
  • Armoured / protected vehicles. The Kalyani M4 mine-protected vehicle and related platforms.
  • Ammunition & systems. A growing ammunition line (incl. artillery shells and small-arms/carbine orders), plus naval systems and unmanned platforms — broadening beyond a single product.

Why defence re-rates a cyclical forger

Three reasons the market treats defence revenue as worth far more per rupee than auto revenue. (1) Visibility. A ₹11,000 Cr order book is contracted, multi-year work — you can see the revenue coming years out, unlike auto exports that swing with the US truck cycle every quarter. (2) Margin. Fully-engineered defence systems (a complete artillery gun, not a raw forging) carry structurally higher margins than commodity auto parts. (3) Structural tailwind. India is on a multi-year defence-indigenisation drive — the government wants weapons made at home, not imported — and Bharat Forge is one of the few private players with the metallurgy and scale to deliver artillery-grade hardware. Stack visible, high-margin, structurally-growing revenue on top of a cyclical forging base, and the market stops valuing the whole company like a cyclical — it starts paying a defence-and-growth multiple. That mix-shift is the re-rating.

The nuance to keep in mind: defence is powerful but still a minority of revenue today (~₹1,562 Cr of ~₹16,800 Cr consolidated). The order book promises a much bigger share over the next few years — but execution (delivering complex weapon systems on schedule and on budget) and order-timing (defence orders are lumpy and politically sensitive) are the real-world risks. The thesis is about the trajectory, not today's snapshot.
06

Diversifying away from cyclical auto

The third defining force — the de-risking story that upgrades both growth and margins.

For most of its history, Bharat Forge was the US truck cycle — a forging exporter whose fortunes rose and fell with how many heavy trucks got built in North America and Europe. The company's most important strategic decision has been a deliberate, decade-long push to break that dependence — spreading revenue across industrials, defence, aerospace and e-mobility so that no single cyclical end-market can sink the whole ship.

The de-risking, engine by engine

  • Industrials / non-auto — oil & gas (drilling and wellhead components), construction & mining equipment, power (turbine forgings), rail. Different demand drivers than autos, so they smooth the cycle. In FY26 this book grew even as auto fell — proof of concept.
  • Defence — the highest-value diversification (see §05): higher-margin, order-backed, structurally growing, and largely uncorrelated with the auto cycle.
  • Aerospace — forgings and components for aircraft; a high-barrier, high-margin market where Bharat Forge's metallurgy and certification pedigree translate directly. Small today but scaling.
  • E-mobility & light-weighting — the "powertrain-agnostic" hedge: whether vehicles run on diesel, petrol or electricity, they still need forged parts. Bharat Forge makes e-axles and EV components, owns EV-maker Tork Motors (Kratos electric motorcycles), and is expanding aluminium light-weighting (lighter forged parts for fuel efficiency and EV range).
Why "powertrain-agnostic" defuses the EV bomb

A big fear for any auto-parts maker is that the shift to electric vehicles destroys its product — no engine means no crankshaft, no connecting rod. Bharat Forge's answer is that forging is largely powertrain-agnostic: an EV still has axles, chassis, suspension and structural parts that must be forged, and it needs new forged parts too (e-axles, motor components, battery-tray structures, and lots of light-weighting — forged aluminium instead of heavy steel, to extend battery range). So while some engine-specific parts fade, the addressable content per vehicle doesn't collapse — it re-shapes. The catch, as FY26 showed, is that the direct EV bets (making whole electric vehicles via Tork) are a different, harder game than making forged EV components — and that's where the ₹450 Cr impairment landed (see §08).

The upgrade in one line: every rupee of revenue that moves from cyclical auto forgings into defence, aerospace or industrials makes Bharat Forge less volatile and higher-margin — which is the mechanical reason the diversification story deserves a re-rating, quite apart from the growth it adds. The de-risking and the re-rating are two sides of the same coin.
07

The auto cycle and the US-truck sensitivity

The risk side of the legacy — the boom-and-bust that the diversification is meant to escape.

To value Bharat Forge you have to respect its oldest vulnerability: a large chunk of the legacy business is tied to the North American and European heavy-truck (Class-8) cycle, which is one of the most violently cyclical markets in industry. When freight demand is strong, fleets order trucks in huge numbers and Bharat Forge's export order book booms; when freight softens or emission rules pause, orders collapse — and the export book collapses with them.

The same market swung from a deep destocking slump (exports down ~51% YoY) to a sharp rebound (US Class-8 net orders +159% YoY) within a year — the whipsaw that makes the auto export book so hard to forecast.

The FY26 story is exactly this cycle in action. For much of the year, US commercial-vehicle exports fell sharply — down ~51% year-on-year in Q3 FY26 — hit by fleet destocking (buyers running down inventory) and pauses in US emission policy (uncertainty over new rules made fleets delay orders), compounded by tariff uncertainty on parts entering the US. That is what dragged standalone revenue down ~5% for the year. Then, late in the year, the cycle began to turn — US Class-8 net orders surged ~159% YoY in February 2026 — setting up the recovery that underpins management's confident FY27 guidance.

Why the cycle matters less than it used to — but still matters

Two things to hold together. First, the auto export book is still large enough to move the whole company — a fresh US-truck downturn would hurt a meaningful slice of revenue, and FY26 proved the drag is real. Second, and this is the whole point of the diversification, defence + industrials are now big enough to partly offset an auto slump — which is precisely why consolidated revenue rose ~11% in FY26 even as standalone (auto-heavy) revenue fell. So the cycle hasn't gone away; it has been diluted. Each year the auto share shrinks, the company's earnings become less hostage to a chart of American trucking freight. But until that mix-shift is complete, "how are US truck orders trending?" remains a question every Bharat Forge investor has to ask.

08

The overseas drag and the FY26 clean-up

The other side of the ledger — the historically troubled subsidiaries, and the pruning under way.

Not everything Bharat Forge owns is a jewel. Its overseas forging subsidiaries — acquired over the years in Europe (Germany) and the US to sit close to customers — have historically been lower-margin and, at times, loss-making, a structural drag on consolidated returns. Alongside them, the e-mobility bet has proved harder than hoped. FY26 was, in large part, the year management got serious about cleaning up the drags — and the clean-up is exactly why reported profit looks depressed.

The two FY26 one-offs to understand

  • The ₹450 Cr e-mobility impairment. Bharat Forge wrote down ~₹450 Cr on its investment in Kalyani Powertrain's e-mobility division (including Tork Motors), as global EV adoption came in slower and more uneven than expected. Baba Kalyani called it "an acceptance of the need to take a fresh look at how we address the EV opportunity." It is not a full exit — the group is reorienting toward EV components and platforms (e-axles, LCV EV platforms) rather than trying to be a vehicle maker — but it is an admission that the direct-EV bet disappointed.
  • The European steel/forging restructuring. Bharat Forge began restructuring the steel business of CDP Bharat Forge in Europe, a process expected to run into end-2027, scaling down the European manufacturing footprint and pursuing alternative uses for it. This is the long-running "overseas subsidiary" problem being tackled head-on.
Read the profit optics carefully. Because of these exceptional items, FY26 reported profit is depressed relative to the underlying business, and the trailing P/E screens optically enormous (well over 80×). That is a distortion, not the true earnings power — the standalone core still earns ~27% EBITDA margins, and the exceptionals are (by design) one-time clean-ups. When judging valuation, look through to forward earnings on a normalised base, not the trailing multiple on a one-off-hit year (see §09–10).
Why the clean-up is arguably good news

It's tempting to read impairments and restructuring as pure bad news — and in the short term they hurt reported profit. But strategically, pruning the drags is the flip side of feeding the growth engines. Money and management attention tied up in a struggling European steel unit or a sub-scale EV venture is money and attention not going into the high-return defence, aerospace and industrials businesses. By taking the write-downs and restructuring now — in a year when auto was weak anyway — management is cleaning the deck so that FY27 onward the reported numbers better reflect the higher-quality, diversified business underneath. The bull reads FY26's messy profit as a reset year; the bear worries the overseas problems recur.

How to read the valuation without being fooled

The trailing P/E — well over 80× (some screens show ~125×) — looks absurd for a forging company. But it's an artefact of the depressed one-off-hit FY26 profit base (the ₹450 Cr impairment and a soft auto year). Analysts value Bharat Forge on forward earnings: at ~₹2,115 the stock trades around ~50× FY27 estimated earnings — still a rich, "priced-for-the-pivot" multiple, but a very different number from the trailing one. The stock has already re-rated hard (up ~60%+ over the prior year) as the market bought the defence-and-diversification story. So the debate isn't "is this cheap?" — it isn't — it's "does the defence/industrials growth and margin upgrade justify a multiple this far above a normal auto-parts maker?" That is the crux of §10.

10

The re-rating debate

Two ways to see the same company — and the multiple sits in the tension between them.

Bharat Forge is one of the most argued-over names on the exchange precisely because you can tell two entirely coherent, opposite stories about it — and the share price reflects the market currently siding with the first.

The whole investment case is a bet on the direction of the revenue mix — that each passing year, Bharat Forge looks less like an auto-parts cyclical and more like a diversified defence-and-engineering compounder.
The honest synthesis

Both stories are true at once — that's what makes the stock hard. Today, Bharat Forge is still majority-cyclical-auto, with real overseas drag; but the trajectory is unmistakably toward defence, aerospace and industrials, and management is actively pruning the drags to get there. The market has chosen to pay ahead of that transition, giving the stock a rich multiple on the promise. The bull is buying the destination; the bear is pointing at the current address and the price of the ticket. The way this resolves is simple to state and hard to predict: does defence + industrials growth (and the auto recovery) show up in the numbers fast enough to grow into the multiple — or does a stumble arrive first? Watch the defence order book, defence execution/deliveries, and the US truck cycle each quarter; those three tell you which story is winning.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A world-class capability mid-pivot — priced for the pivot to succeed.

Bharat Forge is a genuinely rare asset — a world-class forging capability being redirected from a cyclical commodity end-market toward structural, high-margin defence and engineering. The business transformation is real; the investment debate is almost entirely about pace and price: how fast the mix shifts, how cleanly the overseas drags are resolved, and whether the growth justifies a multiple already paying for success.

The bull case

  • Forging moat — top-tier global scale, deep metallurgy, and a certification wall that few can cross; genuinely dual-use across auto, industrial, aerospace and defence.
  • Defence re-rating engine — a ~₹11,000 Cr order book (184 ATAGS howitzers + exports), 30–40% guided growth, high-margin and multi-year — riding India's indigenisation wave.
  • De-risking mix-shift — industrials, aerospace and defence steadily displacing cyclical auto, lifting both growth and margins and lowering volatility.
  • Auto-cycle turning up — US Class-8 orders rebounding; ~25% FY27 growth guided for Indian manufacturing ops as the export drag reverses.
  • Aligned, long-horizon owners — the Kalyani family (~44%), three generations deep, driving the pivot.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~50× forward earnings prices in the pivot's success; little margin for error, vulnerable to de-rating.
  • Still majority cyclical-auto — defence is only ~9% of revenue today; a fresh US/Europe truck downturn still moves the whole company.
  • Overseas & EV drag — European subsidiaries have chronically disappointed; the FY26 ₹450 Cr e-mobility impairment shows the new bets can misfire.
  • Defence execution & lumpiness — turning a big order book into delivered, profitable systems on schedule is hard; defence orders are politically timed and uneven.
  • One-off-distorted earnings — trailing profit is depressed and messy; the "clean" earnings power is a management/analyst estimate, not yet a reported fact.
The bottom line

Bharat Forge is a high-quality engineering franchise in the middle of a genuine transformation — from a cyclical auto-forgings exporter into a diversified defence, aerospace and industrials company, powered by a dual-use forging moat that very few companies possess. The business risk is manageable and the strategic direction is compelling; the valuation risk is the real one, because the market has already paid up for the pivot to work. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter: the defence order book and its execution (is the growth engine delivering?), the revenue mix (is auto's share really shrinking?), and the US truck cycle (is the legacy drag turning to tailwind?). Get those right and a cyclical forger keeps re-rating into a defence-and-engineering compounder; stumble on any of them, and a richly-priced stock has room to fall.
