Refex Industries, the refrigerant firm that quit refrigerants

A complete walk through one of India's stranger small-cap diversifications — a Chennai company that was born selling cooling gas, quietly became the country's biggest hauler of fly-ash and coal for power stations, launched a fleet of electric cabs, and is now shutting the very refrigerant business its name is built on. How each of those unrelated engines actually earns money, why a low-tech logistics business — not the branded chemical — is where nearly all the profit lives, and what the coming break-up is meant to unlock.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Refex Industries Limited is a Chennai-based small-cap that has reinvented itself twice. It started in 2002 as Refex Refrigerants — importing, filling and branding the cooling gases (like R-134a) that go into air-conditioners and refrigeration. Today that legacy business is barely 1% of revenue and is being closed down entirely. What actually pays the bills now is something completely different: a large, unglamorous power-ancillary services business that handles fly-ash and coal for the country's thermal power plants. Bolted on around that are an electric-cab / green-mobility venture and a set of wind and solar power interests. It is less a company than a holding structure of loosely related bets, and management is now busy pruning it.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The profit engine is ash and coal handling, not chemicals. In FY26 the Ash & Coal Handling segment produced roughly 74% of revenue and about 91% of segment EBIT — it is the company in profit terms. Refex trucks fly-ash away from power stations and hauls coal to them under multi-year service contracts, running a fleet of 2,000-plus vehicles and moving ~65,000–70,000 tonnes a day. It is a logistics business dressed in an industrials name.
  • FY26 was a margin story, not a growth story. Consolidated revenue was roughly flat at ~₹2,277 Cr (standalone revenue actually fell ~10% as low-margin trading was culled), yet profit jumped: consolidated PAT rose to ~₹242 Cr and the ash-handling segment's EBIT margin nearly doubled, from about 9.8% to ~17.6%. The stock re-rated on that swing, and trades around ~14× earnings.
  • The company is deliberately breaking itself up. The Board has approved discontinuing the refrigerant-gas and power-trading lines and a composite scheme to hive the EV-cab business into a separately listed Refex Mobility Ltd. The stated end-state: a cleaner Refex Industries focused on ash & coal handling, with the capital-hungry mobility bet given its own listed home. Shareholders backed the scheme almost unanimously.
In plain terms

Read the name and you would expect a specialty-chemicals maker. Read the accounts and you find a haulage-and-disposal contractor for power stations, with a start-up cab company and some renewable-energy stakes attached, sitting under a promoter group that also controls a separate listed solar firm. The interesting tension is that the business the company is famous for (refrigerants) makes almost nothing and is being shut, while the business almost no one associates with the brand (moving ash and coal) earns nearly all the money. FY26 was the year the market noticed — margins in the ash business doubled, profit surged, and the shares re-rated. The open question is whether that margin is a durable feature of a consolidating market or a good moment in a thin-margin logistics trade.

01

Three engines on one odd chassis

A diversified group with almost nothing in common between its parts — and that is the point of understanding it.

Most diversified companies share something across segments — a technology, a customer, a distribution channel. Refex is unusual in that its businesses barely touch. A refrigerant filler, an ash hauler, an EV-cab operator and a solar developer have almost no shared supply chain or buyer. What links them is a promoter group's appetite for opportunistic, capital-light entries into infrastructure-adjacent niches. To follow the stock you have to hold four separate businesses in your head and weight them by profit, not by how prominent their name is.

Why weighting by profit — not prominence — changes the whole picture

If you value Refex as "a cooling-gas company with some side projects," you have the weighting exactly backwards. The refrigerant brand is a rounding error and closing; the EV cabs are a promising but loss-absorbing start-up; the renewables are early. The single thing keeping the lights on — and driving the FY26 profit surge — is a fleet of trucks and excavators moving ash and coal for power utilities. That is where an analyst spends 80% of their attention, because it earns roughly 90% of the money. Everything else is optionality layered on top of that one cash engine.

02

The name that outlived the business

How a company called "Refex" ended up exiting refrigerants — and why that isn't the failure it sounds like.

"Refex" is a contraction of Refrigerants Export. The company was incorporated in 2002 as Refex Refrigerants Limited to import bulk cooling gas, fill it into cylinders and cans, and sell it under its own brand to the air-conditioning and refrigeration trade — a genuinely useful niche in a country adding cooling load every year. For a decade that was the company. Then, in November 2013, it renamed itself Refex Industries — a deliberately generic banner that gave it licence to become almost anything. Over the following decade it did exactly that, and in January 2026 the Board approved discontinuing the refrigerant-gas business altogether, alongside the low-margin power-trading line.

Why walk away from your own origin? Two reasons, both revealing about how this management thinks. First, economics: branded refrigerant trading is a small, working-capital-consuming, import-and-FX-exposed business whose margins never scaled to matter beside the ash-handling engine. Second, regulation: the global phase-down of high-GWP (global-warming-potential) HFC gases under the Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol is steadily squeezing the traditional refrigerant slate. That shift is both a threat (older gases like R-134a face quota-driven decline) and an opportunity (a transition to lower-GWP HFO refrigerants) — but capturing the opportunity would need investment and technical commitment the group evidently preferred to spend elsewhere.

A company named after refrigerants now makes almost none — and has decided the smartest use of the "Refex" brand is to keep it while quietly retiring the thing it was named for.
The surprise worth sitting with

Here is the counter-intuitive fact that catches most first-time readers: the business the company is named for, was founded on, and is still widely associated with is the one it is shutting — and its disappearance improves the reported numbers, because it was dragging the group's margin and tying up cash. The refrigerant exit is not a wound; it is a pruning. It tells you that Refex, under its current promoters, treats its own portfolio ruthlessly and unsentimentally — a trait that cuts both ways for a shareholder. The upside is disciplined capital allocation toward what actually earns. The downside is that a management this willing to enter and exit businesses opportunistically is harder to underwrite: the Refex of 2030 may look as different from today's as today's does from the cooling-gas filler of 2012.

It is worth naming the environmental irony this creates, because it sits at the heart of the group's identity. The refrigerant business it is leaving was, at root, about managing a class of potent greenhouse gases. The business it is doubling down on — hauling coal and disposing of the fly-ash from burning it — is a service to India's most carbon-intensive industry. And the third engine it is nurturing is a fleet of zero-tailpipe electric cabs. Refex simultaneously cleans up after coal power and sells clean mobility. That is not a contradiction the company hides; it frames the ash work as environmental remediation (fly-ash that would otherwise pollute is instead moved to cement plants, road projects and mine-fills) and the EV fleet as decarbonising transport. Whether an investor finds that coherent or opportunistic is a genuine judgement call.

03

Ash & coal handling: the actual profit machine

The unglamorous logistics business that earns ~90% of the money — how it works and why the margin moved.

Every time a thermal power plant burns coal, it produces two problems Refex gets paid to solve. Going in, the plant needs coal delivered and handled. Coming out, combustion leaves behind vast quantities of fly-ash — a fine, powdery residue that must be removed, transported and disposed of or sold, because a plant that cannot clear its ash cannot keep running. Refex sits on both sides of that flow as an outsourced coal-and-ash logistics contractor, and it has quietly become one of India's largest organised players in the niche.

How the ash business actually earns

  • The contract: Refex signs multi-year service agreements with power utilities to lift fly-ash from the plant and move it to end-users — cement makers (who use fly-ash as a clinker substitute), road and highway projects (embankment fill), brick manufacturers and mine-void reclamation. It is paid per tonne handled, plus for coal procurement and transport where that is bundled in.
  • The asset base: a fleet of 2,000-plus owned or leased vehicles — tippers, bulkers, excavators — plus loading infrastructure at plant sites. Capital-light relative to owning power or chemical plants: the moat is operational reliability and scale, not a giant fixed asset.
  • The scale: Refex has serviced roughly 40+ thermal power plants across 14-plus states and handled tens of millions of tonnes of ash cumulatively. It currently moves around 65,000–70,000 tonnes of ash a day and carries an order book in the region of ₹1,500 Cr, estimated at roughly 30–35% of the organised market.
  • The demand driver: India's thermal fleet is still growing and running at high utilisation, and environmental rules increasingly mandate 100% fly-ash utilisation — plants cannot simply dump it. That regulatory push turns ash from a nuisance into a must-move commodity, and Refex is the outsourced hands that move it.

The economics used to be thin. This is fundamentally a haulage business, exposed to diesel prices, vehicle availability and competitive tendering, and for years it earned single-digit segment margins. What changed in FY26 is the crux of the whole investment case — and it deserves its own section (07). But the operational story is this: even as segment revenue dipped ~10% (from ~₹2,236 Cr to ~₹2,013 Cr, partly a deliberate exit from low-margin coal-trading volume), segment EBIT rose ~62% to ~₹354 Cr, because the company chased profitable tonnes over vanity tonnage. Management now guides toward a 90,000 tonnes-per-day run-rate by Q4 FY27 — the single operational number to track.

Why "boring logistics" is exactly the point

Investors often overlook a business like this precisely because it is unglamorous — trucks and ash, priced per tonne, no brand a consumer would recognise. But that plainness is its virtue. It needs relatively little capital, it rides a regulatory tailwind (mandated ash utilisation, a still-growing thermal fleet), and scale earns real advantages: route density, plant relationships, and the reliability that makes a utility renew rather than re-tender. Refex's edge is not a patent or a formula; it is being the largest organised operator in a fragmented, must-happen service. The risk, equally, is that nothing structurally stops a well-funded rival from bidding the per-tonne rate back down.

04

Refex Mobility: the EV bet and the coming demerger

A fast-growing, cash-hungry electric-cab venture that the group has decided should stand on its own.

The newest engine is green mobility — an electric-vehicle fleet business run through Refex Green Mobility, branded increasingly as Refex Mobility. It is a tech-enabled platform: electric cars plus trained drivers, sold as employee-transport services, corporate rentals and B2B aggregation, operating across Delhi NCR, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad and Chennai, with a fleet of roughly 1,750 vehicles. In FY26 it crossed ~₹100 Cr of income, up ~164% year-on-year — genuine traction from a small base.

Rapid growth off a tiny base — but still a fraction of group revenue, and running near break-even at the EBIT line as the fleet scales. Growth like this needs vehicles, and vehicles need cash.

Here is the tension a diversified holding structure creates. An EV-fleet business is capex-heavy and cash-absorbing in its scaling years — every new corporate contract needs more cars, chargers and drivers before it pays back. Sitting inside Refex Industries, that hunger competes for capital with the ash business and muddies how the market values either one: is Refex a steady logistics cash machine, or a speculative EV growth story? The answer "both" is precisely what depresses the multiple, because different investors want different things.

Management's solution is structural surgery. The Board has approved a composite scheme of arrangement: Refex Green Mobility (a wholly-owned subsidiary) is first merged into Refex Industries, and the green-mobility business is then demerged into a separately listed entity, Refex Mobility Ltd. Shareholders approved the scheme with near-unanimity (reported at roughly 99.999% at the court-convened meetings). Post-demerger, Refex Industries becomes a cleaner ash-&-coal-focused company, while Refex Mobility pursues clean-transport growth — and raises its own capital — on its own balance sheet and its own multiple.

What the demerger really tests

A demerger doesn't create value by itself — it relocates it. The bet is that a focused ash-handling company and a focused EV-mobility company are together worth more than one hybrid that neither logistics investors nor growth investors fully want. That can work: separate listings can each attract their natural shareholder, and the EV arm can raise growth capital without leaning on the parent. But it can also disappoint if the mobility business, once standing alone, turns out to need far more cash than the market will fund at a friendly price, or if the ash "RemainCo" is left carrying the group overhead. The proof will be in the post-listing economics of each piece — not in the announcement.

05

Wind, solar and the power businesses

The renewable-energy layer — real, but early, capital-heavy and entangled with the wider group.

Around the two headline engines sits a set of power interests. Refex operates wind-power assets that, in FY26, delivered roughly ₹233 Cr of revenue and turned their first positive segment EBIT (a ~7.3% margin) — a small but symbolically important milestone after years of drag. There is also a solar thread, part of a broader group renewable ambition that extends beyond the listed company into related entities. And there was a power-trading business — buying and selling electricity — which, like refrigerants, the Board decided to discontinue as a low-margin distraction.

The pieces

  • Wind power — generation from wind assets. Capital-intensive and weather-dependent, but FY26's first positive EBIT suggests the assets are finally covering their costs. Management guides to a modest ~5–6% net margin here.
  • Solar — solar generation and development, some of it through the wider Refex group's renewable vehicles (see section 06). A structural-growth space, but one where the group's interests are spread across more than one entity.
  • Power trading (exiting) — a thin-margin electricity-trading line the company is winding down, part of the same "cut the low-margin clutter" discipline that is closing refrigerants.
How to weight the renewable layer

The renewable businesses are best read as secondary to the ash engine for now: real revenue, thin or nascent profit, and heavy capital needs. Wind's move into positive EBIT is a genuine improvement, but at ~₹17 Cr it is a small fraction of group profit. The more important thing this layer reveals is the group's direction of travel — toward infrastructure and energy-transition niches — and the fact that the promoter's renewable ambitions live partly outside the listed company, which is exactly why an investor must understand the group structure before trusting any single segment number.

06

The promoter, the group web and governance

Who controls Refex, the related entities around it, and the watch-points a minority investor should hold in view.

Refex is a promoter-driven company built by Anil Jain, its Founder, Chairman and Managing Director. The promoter group holds roughly 56%, and control sits within a wider Refex group / Sherisha web that spans several related entities — including a separately listed renewables company and various solar special-purpose vehicles. That structure is the source both of the group's opportunistic energy and of the governance questions a careful investor should keep open.

Ownership & the group web

  • Promoter: the Anil Jain / Refex promoter group holds roughly 55.85% (confirm the exact figure and any changes in the latest shareholding filing). Sherisha Technologies sits as a promoter-group parent following the 2022 stake acquisition.
  • Related entities: the wider group includes a separately listed renewables company (Refex Renewables & Infrastructure) and multiple solar SPVs (e.g. Sherisha rooftop-solar vehicles). Renewable ambitions therefore straddle the listed Refex Industries and other group vehicles — a structure minorities should map carefully.
  • Promoter pledge: a meaningful slice of promoter holding — reported around ~28.8% of promoter shares in early 2026 — has been pledged. Pledged promoter stock is a standard governance watch-item: it can amplify downside if the shares fall, and its trajectory is worth tracking each quarter.
The governance texture, stated plainly: Refex offers a promoter with a genuine record of building an unglamorous but profitable business (ash handling) and of pruning weak segments unsentimentally. Against that, a minority investor must weigh a complex, multi-entity group in which related renewable interests sit both inside and outside the listed company, a promoter pledge to monitor, and an acquisitive, opportunistic style that makes the future portfolio hard to pin down. None of this is a red flag on its own; together they mean related-party dealings, capital allocation between group entities, and the terms of the demerger deserve close, ongoing scrutiny. Read the related-party notes, not just the P&L.
07

The margin re-rating: how a haulage business doubled its spread

The single most important thing that happened in FY26 — and the one number that decides whether it lasts.

The FY26 story is not that Refex sold more. On a consolidated basis revenue barely moved, and standalone revenue actually fell as the company deliberately walked away from low-margin coal-trading and shut the refrigerant and power-trading lines. The story is that the profit on what it did sell roughly doubled. In the ash & coal segment, EBIT margin jumped from about 9.8% to ~17.6%, and group EBITDA margin re-rated from the low-teens toward the mid-to-high teens. Understanding why is the crux of the entire investment case, because it tells you whether FY26 is a new floor or a favourable peak.

The whole FY26 profit surge is this shift — the same trucks, roughly the same tonnes, earning far more per tonne. Multiply a ~7-point margin gain across ~₹2,000 Cr of segment revenue and you get the ~₹136 Cr EBIT jump that powered the year.

So what drove the spread wider? A combination, and the durability of each is different:

  • Mix discipline (durable): deliberately dropping thin-margin coal-trading tonnage and pushing higher-value ash-handling and value-added work. This is a management choice and should stick.
  • Operating leverage & route density (durable-ish): as scale and contract density rise, fixed costs (depots, management, idle vehicle time) spread over more tonnes — a structural benefit of being the largest organised operator.
  • Culling loss-making clutter (durable): exiting refrigerants and power-trading removed drags on the consolidated margin.
  • Input & pricing environment (cyclical): diesel costs, contract-repricing timing and competitive tendering all flatter or squeeze the per-tonne margin, and none of these is permanently in Refex's control.
The device that fits only Refex: profit up while the top line stood still

Most companies grow profit by growing sales. Refex did the opposite in FY26 — it shrank standalone revenue by cutting the cheapest tonnes, and its profit went up because the tonnes it kept earned far more each. The whole year is captured in one line: the ash segment moved a similar quantum of material for less headline revenue, yet turned ~₹218 Cr of EBIT into ~₹354 Cr — a ~62% profit jump on ~10% less revenue. For a logistics business, that inversion is the tell. It means the FY26 leap was earned by quality-of-tonnage and cost discipline, not by a market that simply got bigger — which is a better base to build on than a volume spike, but only as durable as the pricing discipline that produced it.

That is why the two numbers to watch are the daily ash-handling run-rate (tonnes/day) and the ash segment EBIT margin, read together. Run-rate is the volume lever — management targets ~90,000 tpd by Q4 FY27 versus ~65,000–70,000 today. Margin is the quality lever — can ~17% hold as the company chases those extra tonnes, or does winning marginal volume mean shading price back toward the old single digits? A rising run-rate at a holding margin is the bull outcome; a rising run-rate bought with a falling margin is the bear's warning that FY26 was the good part of a cycle.

08

Financials & valuation

A cheap-ish small-cap that just re-rated on margins — the debate is durability and the demerger, not heroic growth.

FY26 was a strong profit year on a flat top line: consolidated revenue ~₹2,277 Cr, EBITDA ~₹362 Cr, and PAT ~₹242 Cr, with return ratios that flatter the light asset base — ROCE ~22.7% and ROE ~17.8%. The balance sheet is modestly geared (net debt is small relative to peers in heavier industries), and the stock trades around ~14× trailing earnings. That is not an expensive multiple; it reflects exactly what Refex is — a small-cap logistics-led group whose margin just doubled, whose portfolio is mid-restructuring, and whose durability the market wants proven before it pays more.

The valuation puzzle is the diversification itself. A pure ash-handling logistics company might earn a steady infrastructure-services multiple; a scaling EV-mobility company might earn a growth multiple; a renewable developer, something else again. Blended into one small-cap, the parts get a conglomerate discount — no single investor type is fully served, so the sum trades below what focused pieces might command. That discount is precisely what the demerger is meant to attack: separate the EV arm, clean up the RemainCo, and let each be priced on its own logic. Whether that unlocks value or simply reshuffles it is the central valuation question for FY27.

The one number that settles the thesis: ash EBIT margin, read against the run-rate

At ~14× earnings the case doesn't need a re-rating of the multiple — it needs the FY26 margin to prove durable while volume grows. So the metric that proves or breaks Refex is the ash & coal segment EBIT margin (the ~17.6% of FY26), tracked quarter by quarter against the daily handling run-rate (~65–70k tonnes today, targeted at 90k by Q4 FY27). Hold ~17% while the run-rate climbs and the earnings compound and the low multiple looks wrong. Let the margin slide back toward single digits as the company buys volume on price, and FY26 will read as a cyclical high rather than a new base. Watch it alongside the promoter pledge and the demerger terms — but the ash margin, per tonne moved, is the scoreboard.

Three different competitive arenas, because Refex is really three different companies.

Because its engines are unrelated, Refex has no single peer group — it competes in three arenas at once, and is a leader in only one of them. Any comparison has to be made segment by segment.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Refex's strength and weakness are the same fact: it is dominant where it matters (ash handling) and sub-scale everywhere else. In ash it enjoys the advantages of the largest organised operator — plant relationships, route density, the reliability that wins renewals — in a market with a regulatory tailwind and limited national competition. In EV mobility and renewables it is a minnow among whales, which is a large part of why the group wants to spin the mobility arm out to raise its own capital rather than starve it inside the parent. The investable question is whether the one arena Refex leads is deep and defensible enough to carry the whole story while the others find their feet or their own listings.

10

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A re-rating logistics business, mid-break-up, asked whether the margin and the focus can both hold.

Refex enters FY27 simplified and profitable: a dominant ash-handling core running at record margins, a fast-growing EV arm about to get its own listing, the loss-making clutter cut away, and early results (Q1 FY27 revenue reportedly up ~76%) suggesting momentum. What it cannot yet prove is the two things that decide the case — that the doubled ash margin survives the chase for volume, and that breaking the company in two creates value rather than merely relocating it.

The bull case

  • Dominant in a tailwind market — ~30–35% of an organised ash-handling market that regulation (mandated fly-ash utilisation) and a growing thermal fleet keep expanding.
  • Margin re-rating with room to run — ash EBIT margin doubled to ~17.6% on discipline and scale; a rising run-rate toward 90k tpd could compound earnings.
  • Portfolio clean-up — exiting refrigerants and power-trading, plus the mobility demerger, sharpens focus and may lift the conglomerate-discounted multiple.
  • Capital-light, cash-generative core — high ROCE (~22.7%) and modest debt for a business earning nine-figure profits.
  • Optionality — a separately listed, fast-growing EV-mobility business that can fund itself without draining the parent.

The bear case

  • Margin durability unproven — this is fundamentally per-tonne haulage; competitive tendering and diesel costs could drag the FY26 spread back toward single digits.
  • Concentration risk — ~90% of profit rests on one segment tied to thermal-power activity; a policy or utilisation shift hits the whole story.
  • EV cash burn — green mobility is capital-hungry and sub-scale against far larger rivals; the demerger may reveal a bigger funding need than the market will price kindly.
  • Governance & group complexity — a promoter pledge (~28.8% of promoter holding), a multi-entity Refex/Sherisha web, and an opportunistic, acquisitive style demand ongoing related-party scrutiny.
  • Reinvention risk — a management this willing to enter and exit businesses is hard to underwrite; the portfolio five years out is genuinely uncertain.
The bottom line

Refex Industries is not the cooling-gas company its name implies — it is a dominant ash-and-coal logistics operator that has learned to earn far more per tonne, with an EV-cab start-up and some renewable stakes attached, now being surgically separated. The FY26 leap was real and earned the right way: more profit on flat-to-lower revenue, by keeping only the tonnes worth moving. But the business underneath is still per-tonne haulage, and the group around it is complex and restless. The next tests are concrete and datable: whether the ash segment holds a ~17% EBIT margin as the daily run-rate climbs toward 90,000 tonnes through FY27, and whether the Refex Mobility demerger lists into value rather than a funding gap. Prove the margin durable while the trucks run harder, and land the break-up cleanly, and a ~14× small-cap looks under-priced. Let the margin bleed back to single digits as volume is bought on price, and FY26 will have been the good year that a thin-margin logistics trade is built to enjoy between the lean ones.
