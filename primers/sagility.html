00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Sagility India Limited is an Indian-listed company that runs the operational back-office of American health insurers. When a US insurance company — a "payer" — needs a member enrolled, a benefits question answered, an eligibility check run, a doctor's claim adjudicated and paid, an overpayment clawed back, or a treatment reviewed for medical necessity, Sagility does that work at scale from delivery centres in India, the Philippines and a few other low-cost hubs, blended with its own software and automation. Almost all of its revenue is earned in US dollars, from US healthcare, and roughly nine-tenths of it comes from insurers rather than hospitals.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A fast-growing, dollar-earning operations specialist. FY26 (year to March 2026) revenue was ~₹7,193 Cr (~US$814m), up ~29.1% in rupees (~23.6% in constant currency), at an ~25.3% adjusted EBITDA margin. Adjusted profit (PAT) rose ~39.5% to ~₹1,131 Cr. It serves 82 active client groups and employs roughly 46,860 people.
  • It is a payer-side pure-play — and that is the whole identity. Sagility does not treat patients, sell insurance, or write software for the mass market. It processes the administrative and clinical operations that sit behind a US health-insurance card. This is a distinct, recurring, deeply-embedded contract book — and a very different animal from provider-side firms that bill on behalf of doctors and hospitals.
  • The debate is concentration, automation and price. Sagility is ~100% exposed to US healthcare, heavily weighted to a handful of large payer clients, and freshly public after a November-2024 IPO out of an EQT-owned carve-out from Hinduja Global Solutions. Bulls see a structurally-growing, sticky, margin-expanding operator; bears see extreme concentration, US policy risk and the question of what AI does to a labour-heavy model.
In plain terms

Every time an American swipes a health-insurance card, an enormous amount of invisible paperwork follows: someone has to confirm the person is covered, decide whether the treatment is allowed, receive the doctor's bill, check it for errors, pay the correct amount, and field the phone calls when something goes wrong. US insurers employ armies of people to do this — and it is expensive, repetitive and rules-heavy. Sagility is the outside company those insurers hire to run much of that back-office for them, using trained staff in India and the Philippines plus automation, at a fraction of the US cost. It bills in dollars, signs multi-year contracts, and keeps clients for a long time because it becomes wired into how the insurer actually functions. The whole business is a bet on one idea: that American health insurers will keep handing more of their operational grunt-work to a specialist who can do it cheaper and cleaner.

01

What a health insurer actually does all day

Sagility's market is the payer's back-office — so start there.

To understand Sagility you have to understand what a US health insurer — a payer — is, and why it is drowning in operational work. A payer (UnitedHealthcare, a Blue Cross Blue Shield plan, Aetna, Humana, Centene and so on) collects premiums from members and employers, and in exchange pays members' medical bills. Between "premium in" and "claim paid" sits a vast, rules-bound administrative machine. That machine is Sagility's entire addressable market.

The payer's daily workload

  • Enrolment & member management. Signing members up, maintaining their records, handling plan changes, ID cards, and the annual open-enrolment surge. Get eligibility data wrong and every downstream claim is wrong.
  • Member & provider services. The phone lines and inboxes: "Is this doctor in-network?" "Why was I billed?" "Is this drug covered?" Millions of contacts a year, each needing an accurate answer under strict compliance rules.
  • Claims adjudication. A doctor or hospital submits a bill; the payer must decide — often in seconds, often automatically, sometimes by hand — whether it is valid, in-network, correctly coded, and how much to pay. This is the beating heart of a payer's operations.
  • Payment integrity. Catching the errors, duplicates, wrong codes and outright fraud before or after money leaves the door. On trillions of dollars of US medical spend, even a small leakage rate is enormous — so recovering or preventing it is high-value work.
  • Clinical & utilisation management. Nurses and clinicians reviewing whether a requested treatment is medically necessary (prior authorisation from the insurer's side), managing high-cost cases, and running care-management programmes.
  • Appeals & grievances. When a member or provider disputes a decision, a regulated process must handle it — accurately and on the clock.
Why insurers hand this to an outsider

A payer could staff all of this in-house in the United States — and the big ones partly do. But US labour is costly and hard to hire at seasonal peaks, the rules change constantly, and much of the work is repetitive process rather than core strategy. So payers hand large chunks of it to specialists who run it at scale, to tighter service levels, and far cheaper — largely from India and the Philippines, where a trained claims processor or member-services agent costs a fraction of a US one. That cost gap ("labour arbitrage") is the seed. But the reason a payer stays is that a good operations partner also hits accuracy and turnaround targets, absorbs volume spikes, and quietly improves the payer's own cost line — which, as Section 06 shows, a US insurer is under legal pressure to shrink.

Why this matters for the investment case: Sagility is not selling a discretionary product that vanishes in a downturn — it is embedded in how an insurer processes and pays claims, which does not stop. That makes revenue recurring and sticky. But it also ties Sagility almost entirely to one country's insurance system and its regulations. US administrative complexity is Sagility's oxygen; any reform that simplifies it, or any squeeze on payer economics, blows straight through the model.

02

The payer-operations model — how Sagility makes money

One operations engine, wrapped around an insurer's member-and-claims workflow.

Sagility describes itself as a pure-play healthcare business-process-management (BPM) provider. Stripped of jargon: it takes over defined slices of a payer's operations — a claims queue, a member-services line, a payment-integrity programme — and runs them under a service contract, measured on volume handled, accuracy, turnaround and cost saved. The further into the payer's workflow it reaches, the harder it is to remove.

Why the contract book is sticky

  • Multi-year, embedded contracts. A payer does not re-tender its claims-processing engine casually — the transition risk to its own cash flow and compliance is real. Once Sagility is inside the workflow, contracts renew and widen.
  • Land-and-expand. Sagility typically wins one process for one line of business, proves its accuracy and cost, then cross-sells adjacent work — member services, then payment integrity, then clinical — across the same client. FY26's revenue growth leaned heavily on existing clients expanding scope, not just new logos.
  • Regulated trust. Handling US patient data (under HIPAA) and paying claims correctly is a compliance-heavy responsibility. A payer that trusts a vendor with it does not swap lightly — the audit and onboarding burden alone is a moat.
The one-line business model

Sagility runs defined operations of US health insurers — claims, member services, payment integrity and clinical reviews — from lower-cost geographies, blended with automation, billed in US dollars on multi-year contracts. Its edge over an in-house US team is cost and scalable accuracy; its edge over a generic outsourcer is that it does only healthcare, with deep payer-domain expertise and clinical staff that a broad BPO cannot easily assemble. It grows three ways: win new payer clients, expand scope inside existing ones, and shift its mix toward higher-value work (payment integrity, clinical) where the price is tied to accuracy and recovered dollars rather than seats filled.

03

The company and who controls it

A carve-out from an Indian CX conglomerate, rebuilt by a European buyout fund.

Sagility's corporate story is unusual: it is not a founder's start-up but a carve-out. Its operations were, for years, the Healthcare Services division of Hinduja Global Solutions (HGS) — an Indian customer-experience and BPM company most Indians would recognise as a call-centre business. In 2021, HGS sold that healthcare arm to the private-equity firm Baring Private Equity Asia for an enterprise value of roughly US$1.2 billion. Baring then folded into EQT, the large Swedish/European buyout group, and the business was rebuilt and rebranded as Sagility. EQT ran it as a standalone company, made bolt-on acquisitions, and floated it in India in November 2024.

One name to get straight: the parent it was carved out of is Hinduja Global Solutions (HGS) — not the Hinduja group's other businesses. The financial owner is EQT (which acquired Baring Private Equity Asia); the promoter entity that holds the shares is Sagility B.V., an EQT vehicle. There is no operating "founder-promoter" family here — this is a PE-controlled company, which shapes both the selldown overhang and the professional-management structure.

Ownership & management

  • EQT-controlled, via Sagility B.V. Right after the November-2024 IPO the promoter held roughly 82% of the company (the IPO sold only a modest slice). Since then EQT has been selling down through block deals as lock-in periods expire. Treat the live promoter percentage as approximate and declining — it is a moving number and a genuine overhang (Section 09).
  • An offer-for-sale IPO. The listing raised no fresh money for the company — existing owners (EQT) sold shares to the public. The IPO was about giving the sponsor partial liquidity and a public currency, not funding growth; expansion is funded from the company's own cash flow.
  • Professionally managed, US-facing. As a payer-operations vendor, Sagility's leadership blends Indian and Philippine delivery management with US healthcare-industry executives who own the client relationships. There is no founder whose personal stake and vision anchor the story — the anchor is the contract book and EQT's stewardship until it exits.
  • Sector label vs reality. NSE classifies Sagility under Information Technology. That is a filing convenience: this is not a software or IT-services company in the Infosys sense — it is a healthcare-operations business whose raw material is trained people and process, not code licences. Read it as healthcare-BPM, not "IT".

The service portfolio, broken into parts

Payer operations dominate; a small provider tail rounds it out.

Sagility's revenue splits two ways at the top level — payer (insurers) and provider (hospitals/physician groups) — and the payer side is the overwhelming majority. Within payer, the work ranges from high-volume commodity processing up to specialised, clinically-informed, higher-priced services. The strategic direction is to keep tilting the mix upward.

What each pillar actually does

  • Claims management — receiving, validating and adjudicating the bills providers send to insurers, and paying the right amount. High volume, accuracy-critical, and the anchor process most client relationships start from.
  • Member & provider services — the contact centres and back-office that answer coverage, benefits and eligibility questions and maintain records. Labour-intensive and seasonal (open-enrolment peaks), and the natural home for conversational-AI automation.
  • Payment integrity — the higher-value work of catching billing errors, duplicates, coordination-of-benefits mistakes and fraud, and recovering overpayments. Because it is measured in dollars saved, it prices well above commodity processing and is harder to bring in-house.
  • Clinical & utilisation management — nurses and clinicians reviewing medical necessity, prior authorisation from the payer's side, and care-management support. Requires licensed clinical staff, which is a real barrier to entry and a mix-shift target.
  • Provider-side services (the ~11% tail) — revenue-cycle and support work for hospitals and physician groups. Real, but deliberately secondary; it is not where Sagility competes hardest.
Why payer-weighting is the whole personality of this company

It is tempting to lump every "healthcare BPM" name together, but the payer/provider distinction changes the business. Provider-side firms are paid to help doctors and hospitals get money in from insurers — their fortunes rise and fall with hospital revenue cycles. Sagility, being ~89% payer, is paid to help insurers process and control money going out — enrolment, adjudication, and especially catching leakage. That points its incentives at the insurer's cost line, plugs it into a smaller number of very large, very sticky clients, and exposes it to a different set of regulations (Medicare Advantage rates, the medical-loss-ratio rule, ACA) than a hospital-billing shop. The payer tilt is not a detail — it is the identity.

The delivery engine — people, places, and automation

How ~47,000 people across five countries turn US insurance admin into dollars.

Under the hood, Sagility is a labour-and-process machine spread across a handful of low-cost geographies, increasingly wrapped in automation. Its cost advantage rests on doing US-priced work with globally-priced staff — and its future rests on whether software can lift how much each of those staff can handle.

The geography of the cost advantage

Sagility delivers from India and the Philippines (the two big engines), with additional centres in Jamaica and Colombia and an onshore US presence for work that must stay stateside. India supplies scale and clinical/analytical depth; the Philippines is a large voice/contact-centre base; the near-shore Caribbean and Latin American sites offer time-zone and language coverage for US members. Sagility bills clients in US dollars while paying most of its salaries in rupees and pesos — the spread between the two is a large part of why a services firm can run at a ~25% adjusted-EBITDA margin.

The engine that decides the margin: arbitrage, then automation on top

At its base, Sagility exploits a simple gap — a claims processor or member-services agent in Chennai or Manila costs a fraction of a US equivalent, and the client pays in dollars. But pure arbitrage is a commodity any rival can copy, so Sagility layers automation over the top: rules engines and robotic process automation for repetitive claims steps, and — via its BirchAI acquisition — conversational AI that assists or deflects contact-centre calls. The design goal is to raise output per employee: handle more claims and calls with the same or fewer people, so growth outpaces headcount and margins drift up. That is exactly what FY26 showed — revenue up ~29% while the workforce grew far more slowly. The bull reading is that automation is a margin engine Sagility controls; the bear reading (Section 07) is that if automation gets good enough, the volume of billable human work it rests on starts to shrink.

The currency mechanics: revenue is in USD, a large share of costs are in INR and PHP, so a weaker rupee/peso helps reported margins and a stronger one hurts. It also means part of Sagility's rupee "growth" reflects the exchange rate rather than underlying volume — which is why the company reports a constant-currency growth number (~23.6% in FY26) alongside the ~29.1% rupee figure. Read the two together, not one alone.

06

The 15-cent squeeze — the regulation that pays Sagility's bills

The single mechanism a first-time reader most needs, and most rarely hears.

Here is the piece that makes Sagility's demand structural rather than cyclical, and it is a quirk of American law that most Indian investors have never heard of: the medical-loss-ratio (MLR) rule in the Affordable Care Act. It forces US health insurers to spend the large majority of every premium dollar on actual medical care — and, in doing so, it hands companies like Sagility a permanent reason to exist.

How the rule works

  • Under the ACA, a US health insurer must spend at least 85 cents of every premium dollar (in the large-group market; ~80 cents in individual/small-group) on medical claims and quality improvement.
  • That leaves the insurer only about 15 cents to cover everything else — administration, sales, overhead and its own profit. If it spends less than the mandated share on care, it must rebate the difference to customers.
  • So the payer's entire commercial engine — margin included — has to be run inside a legally-capped sliver of the premium. Every dollar of administrative cost it can cut is a dollar of profit protected.
Why this is Sagility's tailwind, not just trivia

Stack the pieces together. A US insurer is legally barred from keeping more than ~15 cents of each premium dollar for admin-plus-profit. The cheapest way to fit its whole operation inside that sliver — and thus to protect its profit — is to run claims, enrolment and member services at the lowest possible cost. Moving that work to Sagility in India and the Philippines does precisely that. In other words, the regulation designed to hold down insurers' overhead is the same regulation that pushes their overhead onto Sagility's rupee cost base. And it runs the other way too: payment-integrity work — catching wrongful payments — protects the 85-cent side of the ledger by making sure care dollars are not leaking to fraud and error. Sagility gets paid out of both halves of the same rule.

This is the non-obvious engine beneath the whole story. It is why payer-operations demand does not simply track the economic cycle: whether the US economy is hot or cold, insurers are permanently squeezed to run their 15 cents efficiently, and permanently motivated to protect their 85 cents from leakage. Both pressures point work toward a low-cost, accuracy-focused specialist.

But the same rule cuts the other way: because Sagility lives inside US healthcare policy, it is exposed to it. Changes to Medicare Advantage payment rates, to ACA rules, or to how insurers are allowed to operate can reshape payer budgets overnight — expanding or shrinking exactly the work Sagility depends on. A regulation-driven tailwind is, by definition, a regulation-driven risk. There is no way to own the upside of the 15-cent squeeze without owning the policy risk that comes with it.

07

The automation question — does AI lift Sagility or hollow it out?

The force that most divides how people value this stock.

Sagility is a labour-heavy business whose core tasks — reading claims, answering coverage questions, checking eligibility, drafting appeals — are exactly what automation and large language models are getting good at. So the same technology question that hangs over every offshore-services firm lands here with unusual force: does automation make Sagility do more per rupee, or does it erode the human volume its revenue rests on?

Sagility's own answer is to run the automation itself. It uses rules engines and robotic process automation across repetitive claims work, and its BirchAI acquisition put conversational-AI capability inside its contact-centre stack — assisting or deflecting calls so agents handle more, faster. Management frames automation as an efficiency lever it captures, not a competitor it fears; FY26's combination of ~29% revenue growth against much slower headcount growth is the evidence it points to.

The synthesis

The likeliest path runs between the extremes. Automation almost certainly compresses the value of the lowest-skill, high-volume tasks — simple claim steps, routine call handling — the very work labour arbitrage was built on. At the same time it expands the pool of admin work worth automating and rewards whoever owns the workflow and the accountability. So the real question is not "AI good or bad for Sagility?" — it is whether Sagility can shift its mix toward payment-integrity and clinical work faster than automation commoditises the routine processing at the bottom. A firm that turns automation into its own operating leverage while climbing toward judgement-heavy services is a net winner. A firm that stays a commodity claims-and-calls shop gets squeezed. Which one Sagility becomes is the central uncertainty in the stock.

The tell to watch: if Sagility keeps compounding revenue in the mid-20s-plus while holding or lifting its ~25% adjusted margin and growing headcount far more slowly, automation is working as leverage — the bull case proving out. If revenue growth cools and margins wobble even as automation matures, commoditisation is biting. The gap between revenue growth and headcount growth is the single most informative pair of numbers each year.

The industry and the rivals

A large, fragmented field — where Sagility's edge is payer focus and scale together.

Sagility competes in US healthcare BPM, a big and growing market with several kinds of rival. What sets it apart is being a pure-play weighted to the payer side at real scale — where most competitors are either broad IT/BPM firms with a healthcare line, provider-side specialists, or captives run by the payers themselves.

Where Sagility's moat is — and isn't

Sagility's defensible ground is payer specialisation at scale plus embeddedness: it runs critical, regulated processes for a small number of very large insurers, is wired into their claims and member operations, and carries clinical staff and payment-integrity know-how that a generic BPO cannot spin up overnight. That is why clients stay for years. Where it is not uniquely protected is the commodity layer — plenty of offshore rivals can process routine claims and staff a contact centre, and the payers themselves can always insource. The strategic contest is to move up toward payment integrity and clinical review, where judgement, licensure and measured dollar-savings make the work stickier and better-priced — and to keep proving that a focused specialist runs a payer's operations better than the payer's own captive can.

The regulatory backdrop: Sagility lives inside US healthcare rules — Medicare Advantage payment rates, ACA and the MLR rule, HIPAA data-privacy law, and any structural reform to how insurers operate. It also processes sensitive US patient data offshore, so data-security and compliance are permanent, non-negotiable costs and a constant audit surface. This is a business almost wholly exposed to one foreign country's healthcare regulation — a concentration with no easy hedge.

Decoding the two very different P/E numbers

At ~₹19,676 Cr market cap, Sagility trades at roughly 56× its reported (statutory) earnings but only about 17× its adjusted earnings of ~₹1,131 Cr. Why such a chasm? Because the company was assembled by acquisition — the carve-out from HGS and the bolt-ons — it carries a large stack of acquisition-related intangible assets, and the non-cash amortisation of those intangibles depresses statutory profit sharply below the cash the business actually generates. Add IPO/ESOP-related and financing costs and the reported bottom line looks small; strip out the non-cash purchase-accounting drag and the "adjusted" profit is roughly three times larger. Neither number is a trick — but you cannot judge the valuation on the headline reported P/E alone. The honest read is that this is a mid-to-high-teens multiple on cash earnings that grows into the low-double-digits if the growth continues, wearing a scary-looking reported P/E because of how it was built.

Naming the metric that settles it: the case rests on one composite gauge — constant-currency organic revenue growth staying in the low-20s-plus while the ~25% adjusted-EBITDA margin holds and top-client concentration keeps falling. If all three hold together, the adjusted multiple is defensible and the intangible amortisation runs off over time. If growth slows, or the margin cracks under automation-driven pricing, or a top client leaves before the book is diversified, the whole valuation resets. Watch those three together — not the reported P/E in isolation.

The next chapter is a race between two curves: how fast Sagility can broaden its client base and climb toward payment-integrity and clinical work, against how fast automation commoditises the routine processing underneath.

The runway itself is large. US healthcare administrative spend runs into the hundreds of billions of dollars, the payer back-office is only partly outsourced, and the regulatory squeeze of Section 06 keeps pushing work outward. Sagility's levers stack: win new payers, widen scope inside existing ones, tilt the mix toward higher-value services, and let automation lift output per head. The catch is that the same market draws in diversified BPM majors and the payers' own captives, so the diversification has to happen while the field is competitive — not at leisure.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely useful operator, freshly public, carrying a specific set of tail risks.

Sagility is a clean way to own a real structural theme: the relentless administrative load of US health insurance, run cheaply from India and the Philippines, growing fast with expanding margins. The debate is not about whether the business works — it plainly does — but about how much its concentration, its policy exposure and the automation question should temper the price.

The bull case

  • Structural demand — the MLR squeeze and rising complexity permanently push payer admin toward low-cost specialists; a multi-year tailwind.
  • Sticky, dollar-earning book — multi-year contracts embedded in insurers' claims and member operations; ~25% adjusted margins, ~29% growth.
  • Pure-play payer focus — scale plus clinical and payment-integrity depth that broad BPOs and provider-side rivals can't easily match.
  • Automation as leverage — revenue outgrowing headcount; BirchAI and RPA turning efficiency into margin.
  • De-risking in motion — top-3 concentration down from ~80% toward the low-60s and falling; 17 new clients in FY26.

The bear case

  • Extreme concentration — ~100% US healthcare and a heavy top-client skew; one client loss or policy shift lands hard.
  • US policy risk — Medicare Advantage rates, ACA and MLR rules can reshape payer budgets, and thus Sagility's work, overnight.
  • The automation question — if AI commoditises routine processing faster than the mix climbs, the labour model gets squeezed.
  • EQT selldown overhang — a PE-owned promoter trimming its ~82% stake via block deals is a standing supply of stock.
  • Optical valuation & FX — a ~56× reported P/E (even if ~17× adjusted) demands the growth continues; USD/INR swings move margins.
The bottom line

Sagility is a well-run, structurally-advantaged payer-operations specialist — sticky, profitable, dollar-earning, and pointed at a genuine and durable feature of the world's most expensive healthcare system. The business quality is not really in question. What is in question is whether it can diversify its client base and climb toward judgement-heavy work before either a top client, a US policy change, or automation-driven pricing catches up with it — all while EQT keeps selling stock into the market. The clean proof point is the one from Section 09: constant-currency growth in the low-20s-plus, the ~25% adjusted margin intact, and top-3 concentration continuing its march down from the low-60s. When Sagility can show a quarter where a new client sits among its largest — where the book that started at ~80% top-three has genuinely broadened — the concentration story will have turned from a risk into a track record. That milestone, more than any multiple, is the one to wait for.
