EPL Ltd, the tube behind the toothpaste

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

EPL Ltd — until 2020 called Essel Propack — makes the squeezable tube. Not the toothpaste, the tube: the flexible cylinder with a cap that holds toothpaste, face wash, hand cream, ointment or sauce. It is the world's largest manufacturer of laminated (and extruded) plastic tubes, running roughly 21 plants across 11 countries and turning out on the order of 8 billion tubes a year. Its customers are the giants of consumer goods — Colgate-Palmolive, Unilever, Procter & Gamble, GSK, Beiersdorf and hundreds of regional brands — who hand EPL their artwork and their fill specifications and take back printed, decorated, ready-to-fill tubes by the container-load.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a picks-and-shovels supplier to daily consumption. People brush their teeth and wash their faces in a recession, so EPL's volumes are unusually defensive — a component-maker riding the back of the world's most repeat-purchased products. The flip side of that comfort is customer concentration: a handful of multinational accounts, led by oral-care, drive a large share of the book, and EPL negotiates from the smaller side of the table.
  • The growth story is a mix-shift, not a volume story. Oral care — toothpaste tubes — is the legacy anchor at roughly 44% of FY26 revenue, but it is mature and grinds along at low-single-digit growth. The engine now is Beauty & Cosmetics: glossier, higher-decoration, higher-margin tubes for face creams, serums and colour cosmetics, growing ~30% and pulling group margins up as its share rises. Where the revenue comes from matters more than how much there is.
  • Its cap table has changed hands three times. Founded by the Essel (Zee) group's Goel family, sold to Blackstone in 2019, and now being partly sold on by Blackstone to Thailand's Indorama Ventures (a global petrochemicals and packaging major) at ₹240/share. FY26 revenue was ₹4,763 Cr with a ~20.4% EBITDA margin and ~₹394 Cr net profit; the stock trades near 19× earnings — priced as a steady compounder, not a turnaround.
In plain terms

EPL is a contract manufacturer of packaging that happens to dominate one narrow, sticky product: the laminated tube. It doesn't own a single consumer brand you'd recognise — it makes the container that carries them. That business has two speeds. The oral-care half is a slow, dependable annuity: billions of near-identical white toothpaste tubes at thin margins, protected by decades-long relationships and the sheer nuisance of switching a qualified packaging supplier. The beauty half is the opposite — smaller runs, fancier finishes, real design content, and margins two to three times better. The whole investment question is how fast EPL can tilt the sales mix from the first toward the second.

01

The tube business 101: why a tube is a precision product

No engineering degree needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

A tube looks trivial — a squeezy cylinder with a cap. It is not. A modern personal-care tube is a multi-layer engineered laminate: several thin films of plastic (and historically a hair-thin foil of aluminium) bonded together, printed with brand artwork, rolled into a cylinder, welded along a seam, capped with a moulded shoulder and closure, and manufactured to survive a two-year shelf life without the paste inside leaking, oxidising or reacting. Getting that right at a cost of a few rupees per unit, billions of times a year, is the entire skill. To follow EPL, you need five ideas.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)

Extruded tubeA tube made by extruding molten plastic directly into a seamless cylinder — favoured in cosmetics and pharma for a smoother, decoration-friendly wall. EPL makes these too; the growth categories lean on them.
Barrier layerThe thin middle layer that stops flavour, moisture and oxygen passing through the wall. Traditionally aluminium foil (ABL — aluminium-barrier laminate); the sustainability shift is about replacing it with plastic (PBL / mono-material) so the tube can be recycled.
DecorationHow the tube is printed and finished — matte, gloss, metallic, embossed, offset vs digital print. Cheap for a white toothpaste tube; elaborate (and far more valuable) for a premium cosmetic tube. Decoration is where the margin lives.

The economics that decide everything: value per tube

A tube business is deceptively simple to model: revenue = tubes sold × price per tube. The trap is that those two levers pull in opposite directions across the portfolio. A plain toothpaste tube sells for a few rupees at a wafer-thin margin, but EPL sells it by the billion — volume is everything. A premium cosmetic tube might sell for several times that, at a much richer margin, but in far smaller runs. So EPL can grow rupees two very different ways: crank out more commodity tubes (hard, low-margin, capacity-hungry) or shift the mix toward decorated, high-value tubes (the strategy). Every number in this primer is ultimately a story about which of those two levers is doing the work.

Why the customer almost never switches

The single most important structural fact about this business is stickiness. When a Colgate or a Unilever qualifies a tube supplier for a given product, it is approving the exact laminate, the exact printing, the seam, the shoulder and the plant — often integrated with the brand's own filling lines. Re-qualifying a new supplier means audits, trials, regulatory sign-off and the risk of a line stoppage on a product selling millions of units a week. Nobody does that to save a few paise. The result: EPL's relationships with its big accounts run for decades, and a competitor can rarely dislodge an incumbent tube — it can only compete for the next new product launch. That is the moat, and also the ceiling: the same inertia that protects EPL's book makes it slow to steal share from rivals.

Why "one tube, four categories" is the whole strategy

EPL cannot make toothpaste grow faster than people brush. What it can do is use the same factories, the same laminating and printing skill, and the same customer relationships to sell more of the expensive tubes. A cosmetics brand launching a new serum needs exactly what EPL already knows how to make — a decorated, barrier-protected tube — but will pay far more for it and reorder it in fashion-driven cycles. So EPL's growth plan isn't "build more capacity for toothpaste"; it's "point the existing skill at the categories that value it most." That is why a company with flat-ish volumes can still lift revenue and margin at the same time.

03

The oral-care anchor — and the concentration it brings

The defensive base that made EPL, and the dependency that caps it.

EPL was built on toothpaste. For most of its life it was, in effect, the tube division of the global oral-care industry — the supplier of choice to Colgate-Palmolive and the other paste majors, so deeply embedded that it grew wherever they grew: India, China, Egypt, the Americas, Europe. That heritage is the source of both its greatest strength and its most-discussed weakness.

The strength is defensiveness. Toothpaste is about as recession-proof as consumption gets — a small, non-discretionary, high-frequency purchase that people don't skip when times are hard. A supplier riding that demand has volumes that barely flinch through downturns, which is why EPL can carry debt, invest through cycles, and be valued as a stable business rather than a cyclical one. There is genuine comfort in being the picks-and-shovels vendor to a product the whole planet buys every month.

The dependency that defines the risk

The weakness is the mirror image: customer concentration. A large share of EPL's revenue sits with a small number of multinational FMCG accounts, oral care foremost among them, and Colgate-Palmolive is understood to be a very significant single customer. That is a razor-and-blade relationship in which EPL supplies the consumable blade — steady, repeat, defensive volume — but from a position of limited pricing power. When your customer is a global giant that buys billions of units, negotiates hard, and could in principle qualify a rival, you take price you can defend, not price you'd like. The stickiness of qualification protects the volume; it does far less to protect the margin. This is precisely why the beauty pivot matters so much — it is EPL's route to customers and products where it holds more of the pricing pen.

The tension in one line: the same oral-care dominance that makes EPL's revenue wonderfully predictable is what keeps its margin ordinary. Predictability and pricing power sit in different categories — and management is deliberately walking the mix from the first toward the second.
04

The beauty pivot: where the margin lives

The single most important trend in the company — and the reason it can re-rate.

If oral care is EPL's past, Beauty & Cosmetics is its declared future. This is not a new factory or a new product so much as a deliberate reweighting of the sales book toward tubes that are harder to make, prettier to look at, and much more profitable to sell. It is the closest thing this company has to a lever it can actually pull.

Beauty & Cosmetics grew several times faster than oral care in FY26 and carries a richer margin per tube. Because it is a growing share of a bigger pie, it lifts both the top line and the blended margin at once — the definition of good mix-shift.

Why is a beauty tube worth more? Three reasons stack up. Decoration — a premium cosmetic tube may be matte-finished, metallised, embossed, digitally printed in short runs and colour-matched to a brand's palette; each of those is a value-adding step a plain toothpaste tube skips. Innovation cadence — cosmetics launch and re-launch constantly, so a qualified beauty supplier gets a steady flow of new, high-value projects rather than one long commodity run. And willingness to pay — a brand selling a ₹1,200 serum cares far more about how the tube looks and feels than a brand selling ₹80 toothpaste, and prices the packaging accordingly.

The metric that will settle the thesis

Forget headline revenue for a moment. The number that actually decides whether EPL re-rates is Beauty & Cosmetics as a share of total sales, read together with the blended EBITDA margin. If that category keeps compounding at ~30% while oral care crawls, its weight in the mix rises every year, and each point of mix-shift drags the group margin up from the low-20s toward management's ambition. The bull case is not "EPL sells more tubes"; it is "EPL sells a richer kind of tube." Watch the beauty-mix percentage and the margin line move together — that pairing is the scoreboard, and it is specific to this company's re-rating.

A genuine surprise: most people assume EPL is a toothpaste-tube company through and through. It no longer is. Oral care has already slipped to roughly 44% of revenue, and Beauty & Cosmetics has quietly become the single most important growth category. The business the market is buying today is a personal-care and cosmetics packaging company that happens to have a large, stable toothpaste base underneath it.
05

Platina and the plastics paradox

EPL's sustainability edge — and the counter-intuitive reason it works.

The most distinctive thing EPL has built in the last decade is not a factory; it is a tube called Platina. To understand why it matters, you have to understand what makes an ordinary tube un-recyclable — and why EPL's answer runs against the instinct that "less plastic = greener."

A traditional high-barrier tube is a sandwich of different materials: layers of plastic film wrapped around a thin sheet of aluminium foil, which does the job of keeping flavour and oxygen out. That mix is exactly what recycling systems choke on — a recycler can process an all-plastic item or an all-metal item, but a bonded plastic-and-aluminium laminate is neither, so it goes to landfill. The barrier that protects the toothpaste is the very thing that dooms the tube.

The paradox worth sitting with

EPL's Platina answer is counter-intuitive: it makes the tube recyclable by replacing the aluminium barrier with an engineered plastic one, so the whole tube becomes essentially mono-material — all one plastic family — and can be recycled in existing streams. In other words, the sustainability win came not from using less plastic but from using plastic cleverly enough to drop the metal. A mono-material tube is one a recycling plant can actually accept. EPL followed it with r-Platina, which builds in post-consumer recycled content, closing the loop further. It is a rare case where "more thoughtful plastic engineering" is the green outcome, not the villain.

This is a real commercial edge, not a brochure line. Global FMCG brands have made public commitments to recyclable packaging, and toothpaste tubes — billions of un-recyclable units a year — are one of the most visible offenders. A supplier that can hand Colgate or Unilever a tube that looks and performs like the old one but ticks the recyclability box is selling a solution to a problem the customer has to fix. That is why EPL's partnership with Colgate-Palmolive to move to recyclable tubes mattered so much, and why Platina volumes crossed well over a billion tubes within a few years of launch — one of the fastest sustainable-packaging adoptions the sector has seen.

The global map: four regions, one product

A genuinely worldwide manufacturer — which is both a strength and a set of moving parts.

EPL is not an Indian company that exports; it is a multinational that happens to be listed in India. It manufactures close to its customers in each major region, because a tube is bulky, low-value-per-unit and expensive to ship empty across oceans — so the winning model is local plants serving local fills. Management reports the business across four geographies.

No single region dominates — AMESA (anchored by India) is the largest, but Europe, East Asia Pacific and the Americas each carry roughly a fifth to a quarter. That spread is a defensive asset and a currency-translation headache at the same time.

The geographic spread does three useful things. It diversifies demand — a soft patch in one region rarely coincides with all four. It puts EPL next to its multinational customers wherever they operate, which is exactly what a Colgate or a Unilever wants from a global packaging partner: one supplier who can serve them on three continents to a common standard. And it gives EPL a local cost base in each market rather than an export model exposed to freight and tariffs.

The cost of being everywhere

Being a true multinational is not free. EPL reports in rupees but earns in dollars, euros, yuan, pesos and more, so currency translation swings the reported line even when underlying volumes are steady. Some regions run structurally higher margins than others (the developed-market, beauty-heavy plants versus the commodity oral-care volume in emerging markets), so the regional mix quietly moves group profitability. And a global plant network means global fixed costs — 21 factories to keep utilised, upgrade and, occasionally, rationalise. The worldwide footprint is a moat against any single-country shock, but it is also a machine with a lot of moving parts, and reading EPL means watching which regions and which categories are pulling at any given time.

07

Three owners in six years

Family to private equity to strategic buyer — an unusually eventful cap table.

Few Indian large-caps have changed hands as completely, or as recently, as EPL. In little more than half a decade its controlling shareholder has gone from an Indian media family, to one of the world's largest private-equity firms, to a global petrochemicals and packaging major — three entirely different owner types, each with a different reason for wanting it.

  • 1990s–2010s

  • 2019

  • Oct 2020

Why the newest owner is the interesting one

Blackstone's playbook was the familiar PE one: buy a global leader, professionalise it under a strong management team (CEO Anand Kripalu came from consumer-goods pedigree), sharpen the mix toward beauty, and eventually sell. The twist is who it is selling to. Indorama Ventures is not another financial buyer looking to flip — it is one of the world's largest producers of PET and polymers, i.e. the very plastic feedstock that goes into EPL's tubes. A strategic buyer that sits upstream of EPL in the packaging chain is a fundamentally different owner from a PE fund: it is a vertical-integration move, not a trade. That single fact tells you the smart-money view of EPL — it is prized not as a quick turnaround but as a durable, integrable global platform in specialty packaging.

08

Margins and the resin pass-through

How a few-rupee product actually makes money — and what moves the margin line.

EPL's economics come down to a spread: the price it charges for a finished tube, less the cost of the plastic, foil, ink and energy that go into it. Because the biggest input is polymer — a petrochemical whose price swings with oil — the way EPL handles raw-material cost is central to how stable its margins really are.

The good news is that most of EPL's large contracts carry some form of cost pass-through: when resin prices rise, the tube price adjusts (with a lag), so EPL is not fully exposed to every gyration in petrochemical markets. The catch is in that word lag. Pass-through clauses reset periodically, not instantly, so a sharp spike in polymer costs can squeeze margins for a quarter or two before pricing catches up — and a sharp fall can flatter margins briefly before it gets competed away. The resin cycle is the main reason EPL's margin wobbles quarter to quarter even when volumes are calm. On ~₹4,763 Cr of FY26 revenue, EPL kept ~₹972 Cr as EBITDA and ~₹394 Cr as net profit; the gap between those two lines is depreciation on a 21-plant global asset base plus interest and tax — a reminder that this is a capital-intensive manufacturer, not an asset-light brand.

Where the margin actually improves

Two forces push EPL's margin up over time, and neither is "sell more toothpaste." The first is mix — every point of revenue that shifts from commodity oral care to decorated Beauty & Cosmetics lifts the blended margin, because the beauty tube simply earns more per unit. The second is operating leverage and efficiency — better plant utilisation, automation, and premium products like Platina that command a price for their engineering. Management's stated ambition is to push the EBITDA margin from the low-20s toward the mid-20s, and the path runs through those two levers, not through squeezing another paisa out of a toothpaste tube. If the margin line climbs while revenue grows, the mix strategy is working; if revenue grows but margin stalls, EPL is just making more commodity tubes.

The industry and the rivals

A consolidated global tube market where EPL is the scale leader.

Tube-making is a scale-and-qualification game, which naturally consolidates it. Because customers qualify suppliers slowly and buy in vast volumes, the market rewards players big enough to serve a multinational across regions to a common standard — and there are only a handful of those. EPL sits at the top of that short list.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, in the commodity oral-care base, EPL's edge is pure scale and incumbency — its share is defended less by technology than by the near-impossibility of dislodging a qualified tube on a billion-unit product. Second, in the premium beauty arena EPL is trying to grow into, the competition is real and sophisticated — Albéa and specialist decorators have long served the cosmetics houses, so EPL is the challenger there, not the incumbent. That asymmetry is the whole strategic tension: EPL is defending from strength in the low-margin category and attacking from a weaker position in the high-margin one. Its answer is to lead on the one axis brands now demand and can't easily source elsewhere at scale — recyclable, mono-material tubes.

Steady double-digit growth, improving margin, a reasonable multiple.

EPL's financials read like what it is: a global manufacturer of a defensive product, growing steadily rather than explosively, with a margin that inches up as the mix improves. The one number that settles the thesis

At ~19× earnings, EPL is priced as a dependable global compounder — not cheap enough to be a deep-value bet, not dear enough to demand heroics. 11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A defensive global leader trying to trade up — with a new owner watching.

EPL enters the next few years as the clear world leader in a small, sticky, defensive product, deliberately walking its sales mix up the value ladder from toothpaste toward beauty, armed with a genuinely differentiated recyclable tube, and midway through its third ownership change in six years. The debate is not whether the business survives — it plainly does — but how much the mix-shift and the sustainability edge can lift a fundamentally steady manufacturer.

The bull case

  • Defensive, repeat-purchase demand — tubes for products people buy every month; volumes barely flinch through downturns.
  • Mix-shift to beauty — a ~30%-growing, higher-margin category steadily lifting the blended margin as it gains share.
  • Platina moat — the leading recyclable, mono-material tube at a moment brands are pledging recyclable packaging; a switching cost EPL manufactures into its own relationships.
  • Global scale & incumbency — world's largest maker, qualified across regions with the biggest FMCG names; near-impossible to dislodge on existing products.
  • Strategic owner — Indorama's upstream polymer position implies feedstock synergies and a long-horizon owner, not a flip.

The bear case

  • Customer concentration — heavy reliance on a few multinational accounts (Colgate foremost) limits pricing power in the oral-care base.
  • Low structural growth — the core is a mature, single-digit-growth category; the whole re-rating leans on execution of the mix-shift.
  • Resin & currency — polymer-price swings squeeze margin with a lag; a truly global earnings base is exposed to translation.
  • Beauty is contested — EPL is the challenger, not the incumbent, against Albéa and specialist decorators in premium cosmetics.
  • Ownership overhang — the Blackstone→Indorama transition leaves an open question over ultimate control and terms to minorities.
The bottom line

EPL is a rare thing: a company most people have never heard of that touches almost everyone's daily life, several times a day, through the tube in their bathroom. Its base business — billions of toothpaste tubes for the world's FMCG giants — is about as defensive as manufacturing gets, but it is also mature and low-margin, and it hands most of the pricing power to a few very large customers. The interesting company is the one being built on top of that base: a higher-margin beauty-and-cosmetics packager with a recyclable-tube technology that global brands increasingly need rather than merely want. The next few years turn on one checkable pairing — whether the EBITDA margin climbs toward the mid-20s as Beauty & Cosmetics grows into a larger share of the mix. Watch that margin line and that mix percentage move together each year; and watch what Indorama, EPL's newest and most strategic owner, decides to do with a global platform it clearly values more than the last owner did.
