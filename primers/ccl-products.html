CCL Products, the coffee behind the coffee

The world's largest pure-play private-label instant-coffee maker sits in a small town in Andhra Pradesh — and quietly fills a big share of the "own-brand" coffee jars on supermarket shelves across ~90 countries. This is a walk through how it makes soluble coffee, why its profit tracks kilograms and a steady conversion spread rather than the wild price of coffee beans, and whether its new consumer brand can lift it up the value chain.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

CCL Products (India) Limited is a business-to-business coffee ingredient maker — not a consumer brand you'd recognise on a shelf, but the company that makes the coffee inside a great many jars you would. It takes raw green coffee beans, extracts and dries them into instant (soluble) coffee, and sells that powder and granule mostly in bulk to global food companies, supermarket private labels and distributors, who put their own name on the pack. It is the largest pure-play private-label soluble-coffee manufacturer in the world, run by the Challa family from a base in Guntur district, Andhra Pradesh, with plants in India, Vietnam and Switzerland.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • A record FY26 — but read it carefully. Consolidated revenue jumped ~43% to ~₹4,457 Cr, EBITDA rose 32% to ~₹741 Cr, and net profit grew 25% to ~₹388 Cr (FY26, year ended March 2026). Volumes, though, grew only ~18–20%. The gap between +43% revenue and +18% volume is coffee-bean price inflation flowing straight through the top line — a distinction that is the whole key to understanding this company.
  • Profit is a kilogram business. CCL earns a fairly stable conversion margin per kilo — around ₹135/kg of EBITDA in FY26 — and grows profit chiefly by pushing more kilograms through the plants. The price of green coffee is largely a pass-through: it inflates and deflates revenue and inventory but leaves the per-kg spread broadly intact.
  • Two live storylines. First, a relentless capacity build (new freeze-dried lines in Vietnam, new spray-dried lines in India) that feeds the volume engine. Second, a slow climb up the value chain via Continental Coffee, its own Indian retail brand — small today (~₹440 Cr of brand sales) but higher-margin and the source of most of the re-rating debate.
In plain terms

Picture the coffee aisle of a European supermarket: a dozen jars of "own-brand" or "value" instant coffee, each carrying the store's label, not a manufacturer's. A large share of that coffee was never made by the store — it was made by a single company in Andhra Pradesh and shipped in, ready to jar. That is CCL's core business: it is the contract manufacturer and blender the world's coffee sellers quietly rely on. Its skill is not marketing; it is chemistry and logistics at scale — buying beans anywhere, blending to any customer's exact taste and price point, and drying to any format, cheaply and consistently. The investment question is whether a low-profile, steady, volume-and-spread ingredient business can keep compounding — and whether its move into its own branded jars turns a reliable supplier into something worth a richer price.

01

Instant coffee, explained — and what CCL actually makes

No coffee-nerd knowledge assumed. Start from the bean and follow it to the jar.

Roughly speaking there are two ways coffee reaches a cup. Roast-and-ground coffee — beans you grind and brew yourself — and instant (soluble) coffee, a powder or granule that dissolves in hot water. CCL lives almost entirely in the second world, and specifically in the industrial version of it: making soluble coffee in bulk. Understanding the few production formats is enough to understand the whole business, because CCL's edge is being able to make all of them, to anyone's recipe.

From green bean to soluble powder

Soluble coffee is, in essence, brewed coffee with the water taken back out. The chain runs: buy green (unroasted) beans → roast them → grind and extract (brew a concentrated liquor) → dry that liquor back into a solid. The drying step is where the formats — and the price points — diverge:

  • Spray-dried. The coffee liquor is sprayed as a fine mist into hot air; the water flashes off and fine powder falls out. It is the cheapest, highest-volume format — the workhorse of value and mass-market instant coffee.
  • Agglomerated (granulated). Spray-dried powder is steamed and clumped into larger granules that look and pour better. A modest step up in appearance and price.
  • Freeze-dried. The liquor is frozen and the ice is drawn off under vacuum (sublimation), leaving crisp dark crystals. It is the premium format — better aroma retention, a "real coffee" look — and it commands the highest realisation and margin per kilo. Building freeze-dried capacity is how a soluble maker moves upmarket.
  • Liquid / specialty coffee. Concentrated coffee extract sold as a liquid, plus flavoured blends, cold-brew concentrates and single-serve formats — a growing, higher-value niche CCL is expanding into.
Why "blend to any taste and price" is the real product

A customer — say a UK supermarket chain — doesn't just want "instant coffee." It wants a specific cup profile at a specific landed cost: this much robusta for body and caffeine, that much arabica for aroma, a target acidity, a target price. CCL's genuine skill is matching any such brief by sourcing the right green beans from anywhere in the world and blending to hit the flavour and the price simultaneously. Because green beans are a global commodity available to everyone, the moat isn't the bean — it is the recipe library, the drying versatility, and the cost of conversion. That is what a rival can't quickly copy, and what keeps customers coming back year after year for the same reliable, repeatable jar.

So when you read that CCL sells "instant coffee," read it as: it operates large, flexible extraction-and-drying factories that can produce spray-dried, agglomerated, freeze-dried and liquid coffee to a customer's precise specification, at low cost, in export volumes. Everything else in this primer is downstream of that one capability.

02

The invisible maker: how private-label / B2B coffee works

The single idea that most separates CCL from a "coffee brand" like Nescafé or Bru.

Most people meet coffee as a brand. CCL is the opposite kind of business — the manufacturer behind the brand, and often behind no brand at all. Its output flows to three sorts of buyers, and in every case someone else's name ends up on the jar. Grasp this and you understand why CCL is an export and ingredient play, not a consumer story — at least, not yet.

CCL earns on the middle step — turning beans into finished soluble coffee. The brand, the shelf and the shopper relationship all belong to its customer. The exception is its own Continental Coffee (see §07), where CCL finally owns the last box too.

Who actually buys from CCL

  • Global FMCG brands that need extra soluble coffee capacity or specific blends they'd rather outsource than build a plant for.
  • Supermarket private labels — the "own-brand" and value ranges that now take a large slice of grocery coffee sales in Europe, the US and beyond. These are CCL's bread and butter.
  • Distributors and re-packers across ~90+ countries who buy in bulk, then pack and sell locally under regional names.
Why a company chooses to be "invisible"

Being the un-branded maker sounds like a weak position — you don't own the customer. But it comes with real advantages. There is no consumer marketing spend (a huge cost line for branded coffee), no fickle brand loyalty to defend, and demand that is stickier than it looks: once a supermarket has approved CCL's blend and built it into its supply chain, re-qualifying a new supplier is slow and risky, so contracts renew. The trade-off is pricing power — big B2B buyers negotiate hard, so the maker lives on volume and a disciplined conversion margin, not fat brand markups. CCL has essentially decided to win the unglamorous half of the coffee business globally, and only recently started reaching for the glamorous half at home.

A useful contrast: Nescafé (Nestlé) and Bru (HUL) are brands — they spend heavily to own the shopper and earn a brand margin. CCL is a maker — it spends nothing on the shopper and earns a conversion margin. Same cup of coffee; opposite business model. The interesting question is what happens when a maker tries to also become a brand.
03

The company and who controls it

A single-family coffee house that grew into a global supplier.

CCL is a founder-family enterprise, promoted and still run by the Challa family. It was incorporated in 1961, but its modern life began in the mid-1990s when it commissioned an instant-coffee plant at Duggirala, Guntur district, Andhra Pradesh, and set out to export soluble coffee to the world rather than sell it at home. That export-first, bulk-first DNA still defines the company three decades later.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter group: the Challa family holds roughly 46% of the company (approx.), with individual family members — including Ms Shantha Prasad Challa (~14%) — anchoring the block. This is a committed, generational owner, not a professional-management float.
  • Leadership: founder Challa Rajendra Prasad serves as Executive Chairman; the next generation, notably Challa Srishant (Managing Director & CEO), runs day-to-day operations and has driven both the Vietnam expansion and the Continental brand push.
  • Institutional base: the remainder is split between domestic and foreign institutions (~a third of the register) and public shareholders — a following built on CCL's long record as a steady, dividend-paying compounder rather than a momentum story.

The practical read: this is a tightly held, owner-operated business with deep coffee-processing expertise concentrated in one family and one region, now managing a genuinely global manufacturing footprint. That concentration is both a strength (aligned, patient capital; decades of accumulated know-how) and a governance factor a shareholder should keep in view.

04

Volume × the per-kg spread: the two numbers that make the profit

The single most important — and most counter-intuitive — mechanic in the whole company.

Here is the mental model that beats every other for CCL. Its profit is not really a function of the coffee price at all. It is, to a first approximation, kilograms sold × the conversion margin per kilogram. Green coffee beans — the biggest cost by far — are largely passed through to the customer. So when bean prices spike, CCL's revenue balloons but its spread barely moves; when they fall, revenue shrinks and the spread again holds. The company is far steadier than a "commodity coffee stock" sounds.

The number that rules CCL: EBITDA per kilogram

CCL and its analysts track one figure above all others: EBITDA per kilo — the operating profit earned on each kilogram of coffee converted. In FY26 it sat around ₹135/kg (roughly ₹138/kg in the March quarter), and management's stated aim is to hold it in the ₹135–140/kg band while volumes grow. If you know the per-kg spread and the tonnes shipped, you can rebuild the profit line without ever looking at the price of coffee. That is the opposite of how a commodity processor usually behaves, and it is the crux of the whole investment case.

Watch this year's own arithmetic

FY26 is the cleanest possible demonstration of the mechanic. Global green-coffee prices ran at multi-year highs through the year, and CCL's numbers show exactly what that does to a pass-through business:

Revenue grew more than twice as fast as volume. That excess is coffee-bean inflation moving through the price the customer pays — not extra units, and not extra spread. The per-kg margin held. That single divergence is the CCL business model on one chart.

Put the numbers together and the profit engine falls out with almost no reference to the coffee market. FY26 EBITDA of ~₹741 Cr at ~₹135/kg implies roughly 55,000 tonnes of coffee shipped (approx.). Grow that tonnage ~15% a year — management's guidance for FY27 — hold the spread near ₹135–140/kg, and EBITDA compounds by about the same 15%, whatever coffee does in the meantime. A doubling of the bean price would inflate the reported revenue dramatically and swell inventory, yet do little to that compounding.

How to read any CCL result: ignore the headline revenue growth (it mostly tracks the coffee price). Look instead at volume tonnes and EBITDA per kg. Rising tonnes with a stable ~₹135/kg spread is a good quarter; flat tonnes or a slipping spread is a poor one — regardless of what the top line does.
05

Three countries, one reason: why CCL makes coffee in India, Vietnam and Switzerland

A footprint that looks scattered but is really a single strategy: origin, tariffs and proximity.

A soluble-coffee maker could, in theory, run everything from one giant low-cost plant. CCL deliberately does not — it manufactures across India, Vietnam and Switzerland. The reason isn't sentimentality about locations; it is that where you make the coffee changes the tariff you pay and the customers you can reach. In a world of trade blocs and origin rules, a multi-country footprint is a competitive weapon.

Why the same coffee is worth more from a different address

Countries levy different import duties on coffee depending on where it was made, and trade agreements give some origins duty-free access that others lack. A jar of soluble coffee shipped into the EU or the US can face a very different tariff depending on whether it left India, Vietnam or a European plant — even if the coffee inside is identical. By manufacturing in more than one bloc, CCL can route each customer's order through the origin that lands cheapest for them, and sit physically closer to key markets. Vietnam also puts a plant right beside the planet's biggest robusta crop, trimming freight on the raw bean. The scattered map is, in effect, a tariff-and-logistics optimisation that a single-country rival simply cannot match.

This is also a quiet hedge. As global trade turns more protectionist and tariff lines shift, a maker locked into one country is exposed; CCL's spread of origins gives it options to re-route rather than absorb a new duty. It is the least-discussed and most durable part of the moat — harder to copy than a recipe, because it takes years and capital to build approved food plants in three jurisdictions at once.

06

The capacity engine: why CCL is always building a line

In a volume business, the factory is the growth plan.

If profit is tonnes × spread, and the spread is deliberately held steady, then growth has to come from tonnes — and tonnes come from new drying lines. CCL's history is a near-continuous cycle of adding capacity, filling it, then adding more. The current phase leans on two big additions: a 16,000-tonne freeze-dried line in Vietnam and a 16,000-tonne spray-dried line in India (approx.), which together lift group capacity meaningfully and, crucially, tilt the mix toward higher-value freeze-dried.

The whole model is this loop turning steadily. The risks are (a) a line that ramps slower than planned, leaving expensive capacity idle, and (b) the spread slipping if new capacity has to be filled with discounted volume. Utilisation is therefore the number to watch alongside EBITDA/kg.
The mix shift hiding inside "more capacity"

Not all new tonnes are equal. A fresh freeze-dried line doesn't just add volume — it adds the premium volume, at a higher realisation and a fatter per-kg margin than plain spray-dried powder. So as CCL loads more of its growth into freeze-dried and specialty/liquid formats, it can nudge the blended EBITDA/kg up even while promising to "hold" the spread — the reported ₹135 today is a mix of cheap spray and dear freeze-dried, and the mix is drifting the profitable way. This is the subtle upgrade underneath the plain-looking volume story: CCL is not only shipping more kilos, it is gradually shipping better kilos.

The flip side is capital intensity. Drying lines are expensive and take time to build, approve and fill, so CCL runs with meaningful debt and capex at any given moment (see §08 and §10). The bull case needs each new line to reach healthy utilisation reasonably quickly; a line sitting half-empty is idle capital and a drag on returns. FY26's guidance of ~15% volume growth into FY27 is really a statement that management expects the newest lines to fill on schedule.

07

Continental Coffee: the climb from maker to brand

Small today, and a genuinely different game — but the reason some investors pay up.

For its whole life CCL has sold coffee that carried other people's names. Continental Coffee is the exception — its own retail brand, sold to Indian consumers under CCL's own label. It is still a minor slice of the company, but it is the most-watched slice, because it is where a low-margin ingredient supplier reaches for the higher, stickier margins of a consumer brand.

Why branding is a different sport from manufacturing

Making coffee well and selling coffee well are unrelated skills. CCL is world-class at the first: chemistry, blending, cost, drying. The second — building a brand — means advertising, shelf-space fights, distribution muscle, and years of loss-leading spend to earn a place in the shopper's habit. It is marketing-heavy, slow, and pits CCL against entrenched giants (Nescafé, Bru) that have spent decades and fortunes owning the Indian coffee shelf. Continental's progress to ~₹440 Cr shows the effort is landing; but scaling a challenger brand is a fundamentally harder, riskier climb than adding a drying line, and success is far from guaranteed. The prize is worth the risk: a rupee of branded profit is worth more, and more durable, than a rupee of contract profit — which is exactly why the market watches Continental's growth so closely.

Why the branded push re-rates the whole story — if it works

  • Margin. Branded jars earn far more per kilo than bulk private label. Even a modest branded mix lifts blended profitability and the trajectory of EBITDA/kg.
  • Durability. A brand the shopper asks for by name is stickier than a B2B contract that gets re-tendered — it lowers the whole company's cyclicality over time.
  • Valuation. Markets pay a richer multiple for branded FMCG than for contract manufacturing. The more of CCL's profit that comes from Continental, the more of it earns the higher multiple — which is the core of the re-rating argument on the stock.
The tension worth naming: a large part of CCL's current valuation already assumes the branded climb keeps working. If Continental stalls — outspent on the shelf, or unable to widen beyond its base — CCL is left as an excellent but lower-multiple B2B processor. If it keeps compounding, CCL earns the branded-FMCG rating it is partly being given today. That gap is the single biggest swing factor in how the stock is priced.
08

Bean prices and the working-capital swing

The pass-through protects the margin — but not the balance sheet, and not always the timing.

§04 argued that CCL's profit is well insulated from coffee-bean prices. That is true of the margin — but a bean-price spike still lands somewhere, and where it lands is the balance sheet and short-term cash. This is the honest limit of the "pass-through" comfort, and it is worth understanding precisely.

The pass-through protects margin, not working capital

CCL buys green beans, holds them as inventory, processes them, ships the coffee, and gets paid on a delay. When bean prices double — as they broadly did into FY26 — every kilo of inventory and every unpaid invoice is suddenly worth far more in rupees, so the cash tied up in working capital swells even though volumes and the per-kg spread are unchanged. That extra cash has to be funded, usually with more short-term debt, which raises interest cost and can dent near-term cash flow and reported margins for a quarter or two. The spread survives; the cash cycle takes the strain. It reverses when prices ease — working capital releases cash back — but the swing is real and is the main way high coffee prices actually hurt CCL.

The other bean-price risks, in full

  • Timing lags. Pass-through is not instant. If costs jump faster than contracts re-price, a quarter's margin can compress before catching up — the FY26 quarters showed exactly this kind of near-term squeeze even as the full-year spread held.
  • Customer pushback at extremes. When shelf prices for coffee rise too far, some consumers trade down or buy less, and big B2B buyers lean harder on their suppliers — pressuring volumes or the spread at the margin.
  • Inventory risk. Holding large green-bean stocks means a sharp price fall can leave higher-cost inventory to work through, briefly narrowing the spread.

None of this breaks the model — FY26 delivered record profit through a high-price year, and net debt still fell by ~₹750 Cr to ~₹1,073 Cr as the company generated cash and paid down borrowings. But it is why "insulated from coffee prices" needs the footnote: insulated in margin, exposed in working capital and timing. A shareholder should watch the debt and the cash-conversion cycle in high-price years as closely as they watch the spread.

09

Who else makes the world's soluble coffee

CCL is the largest pure-play — but it is not alone, and the competition comes from several directions.

CCL's claim to be the world's largest pure-play private-label instant-coffee maker is real, but "pure-play" is doing work in that sentence. Bigger companies make more soluble coffee overall — they just do it mostly for their own brands. The competitive set spans four different kinds of rival, and CCL has to answer each.

How CCL keeps winning the bulk game

Against branded majors, CCL competes by being the outsource option they and everyone else turn to rather than build their own line. Against other B2B makers, its edge is the combination the table can't show: the widest blending flexibility, a multi-country tariff footprint, and decades of low-cost Indian conversion — a package that is hard to assemble all at once. Where it is genuinely exposed is at the commodity end: plain spray-dried powder from a Vietnamese or Brazilian plant next to the crop can undercut on pure cost. CCL's answer is to keep moving up — into freeze-dried, liquid, specialty and its own brand — where price is not the only thing that decides the order.

10

Financials & valuation

A steady compounder priced like a branded FMCG — and the one number that decides whether that's right.

FY26 was a record year on every headline line. The debate on the stock isn't about the quality of the business — it is dependable and well-run — but about the multiple: CCL trades at a premium rating (a P/E in the mid-50s in mid-2026, on a market cap around ₹15,900 Cr), the kind of valuation usually reserved for branded consumer companies rather than contract processors.

The metric that settles the thesis

Strip everything else away and CCL's entire case rests on one compound number: volume tonnes growing double digits at an EBITDA/kg held near ₹135–140 — with the branded (Continental) share of that profit steadily rising. If tonnes keep growing ~15% a year, the spread holds, and Continental keeps taking a bigger slice, then the premium multiple is a re-rating in progress and the compounding justifies it. 11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A quietly excellent supplier trying to become something the market will pay more for.

CCL has spent thirty years perfecting the unglamorous half of coffee: making it cheaply, flexibly and reliably for everyone else's label. The next chapter asks whether it can keep compounding tonnes at a stable spread and graduate into a branded business — turning a dependable processor into a genuine consumer franchise.

The bull case

  • Steady, understandable engine — profit is tonnes × a ~₹135/kg spread, largely immune to coffee-price swings; you can model it years out.
  • Relentless capacity — new freeze-dried and spray lines keep feeding double-digit volume growth (~15% guided for FY27).
  • Mix moving up — a tilt toward freeze-dried, liquid and specialty gradually lifts the blended spread.
  • Tariff-proof footprint — India + Vietnam + Switzerland lets it re-route orders through the cheapest origin, a hard-to-copy edge.
  • Continental optionality — a growing India brand (~₹440 Cr) that, if it scales, earns branded margins and a branded multiple.
  • Deleveraging — net debt cut ~₹750 Cr in FY26 to ~₹1,073 Cr; D/E down to ~0.5.

The bear case

  • Premium multiple — a mid-50s P/E on a business that is still mostly B2B contract manufacturing leaves little cushion if growth wobbles.
  • Working-capital swing — high bean prices bloat inventory and short-term debt, pressuring near-term cash even when the spread holds.
  • Customer concentration & pricing power — large private-label buyers negotiate hard and can squeeze the spread.
  • Branding is a different game — Continental must out-execute Nescafé and Bru on the shelf; challenger brands often stall.
  • Execution on capex — a line that ramps slowly is idle capital; utilisation must keep pace with the build.
  • Competition & trade risk — origin-country and ingredient-giant rivals at the commodity end; FX and tariff shifts for a global exporter.
The bottom line

CCL Products is one of the most quietly dominant businesses in Indian FMCG — the world's go-to private-label soluble-coffee maker, with a profit engine so structurally steady that the price of coffee barely touches its margin. The real question isn't whether the core works; it plainly does. It is whether the market is right to pay a branded-consumer price for it today. That will be decided not on a coffee-futures chart but on three concrete readings each year: volume tonnes (do the new Vietnam and India lines fill and keep double-digit growth going?), EBITDA per kilogram (does the spread hold at ₹135–140 as the mix shifts up?), and Continental's share of profit (does the brand keep climbing off ~₹440 Cr?). Watch those three — and the moment a new freeze-dried line reports full utilisation at an unbroken spread is the moment the premium rating starts to look earned rather than hoped-for.
