00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Infosys is an IT-services company: it supplies large global enterprises — mostly banks, manufacturers and retailers in the US and Europe — with armies of software engineers who build, run and modernise the technology those businesses depend on. It is India's second-largest IT-services firm after Tata Consultancy Services (TCS), with FY26 revenue of $20.2 billion (₹1.79 lakh crore) and roughly 329,000 employees.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A people business, sold by guidance. Infosys earns by putting skilled people on client projects and billing for their time — but what the market actually trades on is its annual revenue-growth guidance. Unlike TCS, Infosys issues and then repeatedly updates a constant-currency growth forecast; for FY26 it ultimately landed at +3.1%, and for FY27 it has guided a cautious 1.5%–3.5%. That number is the stock's master signal.
  • Margins are engineered, not given. Infosys runs a continuous cost-and-efficiency programme — "Project Maximus" — to defend its operating margin against wage inflation and pricing pressure. FY26 came in at a 21.0% adjusted operating margin, and FY27 is guided at 20%–22%. Holding that band while still growing is the central management discipline.
  • A cash machine with no controlling owner. Infosys has no promoter in control — the founders (the Murthy and Nilekani families and others) together hold only ~14%, with institutions owning the bulk. It converts almost all profit to cash and hands it back: FY26 saw over ₹37,500 crore returned via dividends (₹48/share) and an ₹18,000 crore buyback.
In plain terms

Think of Infosys as a global outsourcing partner that rents out brainpower. When a US bank or a European carmaker wants to rebuild its systems, move to the cloud, or now bolt AI onto its operations, it hires Infosys to supply the engineers and run the programme — paying either per hour worked or a fixed price per outcome. Because almost all the cost is salaries, the whole game is filling those people with billable work, keeping them productive, and not overpaying them. India's low-cost, English-speaking engineering talent is the historic edge. The two questions hanging over the model now: will Western clients keep spending (the demand cycle), and will AI let Infosys do more with fewer people — or let clients do it without Infosys at all?

01

IT-services economics 101: how Infosys earns

No engineering degree required — just the levers that turn people into profit.

An IT-services firm is, financially, a labour-arbitrage business: it employs a large pool of engineers at (largely Indian) cost, places them on client projects, and bills the client at a higher rate. Profit is the gap between what it pays its people and what it charges for them — multiplied across hundreds of thousands of employees. A handful of numbers drive the whole machine.

The only levers you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters)
HeadcountThe number of billable employees — the raw capacity. Infosys ended FY26 with ~328,600 people. More heads can mean more revenue, but only if they're billed.
UtilisationThe share of employees actually working on (billable) client projects vs sitting "on the bench". Higher utilisation = more revenue on the same payroll. A key margin lever.
Billing rate / pricingWhat the client pays per hour or per outcome. Rises with skill (cloud, AI, data) and falls when clients squeeze. The price side of the equation.
PyramidThe mix of cheap juniors to expensive seniors. A "fat" base of freshers under fewer senior architects keeps average cost low — managing the pyramid is core to margin.
AttritionHow fast people quit. High attrition means costly re-hiring and training; low, stable attrition (Infosys: 12.6% in FY26) protects both cost and delivery quality.
Constant currency — the phrase you'll see everywhere

Infosys earns in dollars, euros and pounds but reports in rupees, so currency swings can flatter or hurt headline growth. To strip that out, the industry quotes growth in "constant currency" (cc) — what growth would have been if exchange rates hadn't moved. That's why FY26 revenue rose 9.6% in rupees (helped by a weaker rupee) but only 3.1% in constant currency — and the cc number is the one analysts care about, because it measures real volume and pricing, not the currency tailwind. Always read the cc figure as the "true" growth.

Time-and-material vs fixed-price — two ways to bill

  • Time-and-material (T&M) — the client pays for hours worked at agreed rates. Lower risk for Infosys, but revenue is directly tied to bodies on the project.
  • Fixed-price / outcome-based — Infosys commits to deliver a defined outcome for a set fee. Higher margin if delivered efficiently (and the natural home for AI-driven productivity), but Infosys carries the execution risk. The mix is steadily shifting toward fixed-price as automation rises.
02

The guidance ritual — the number the market actually trades

The defining feature of the Infosys story, and what sets it apart from TCS.

Here is the single most important thing to understand about Infosys as a stock: it gives, and repeatedly revises, an explicit annual revenue-growth forecast — and the market lives and dies by it. TCS, the larger rival, deliberately does not give a number. Infosys does. So every quarter, the question isn't just "what were the results?" — it's "what did they do to the guidance?"

At the start of each financial year (with Q4 results), management sets a constant-currency revenue-growth range for the year ahead. Then at each of the next three quarters it narrows, raises or cuts that range as the year unfolds. A raise signals confidence and demand recovery; a cut signals trouble — and the share price moves hard on the change, often more than on the actual revenue or profit.

The FY27 range (1.5%–3.5%) is deliberately wide and cautious — a signal that management sees an uncertain demand environment, even as Q4 exited at a healthier +4.1% cc. Watch each quarter for whether the range is raised or cut.
Why a forecast can matter more than the result

Infosys's guidance is treated as a read on the whole Western IT-spending cycle, not just one company — because Infosys sees client budgets early, across banking, manufacturing and retail. So a guidance cut from Infosys is taken as a warning about everyone's demand, and the stock (and often the whole Indian IT index) reacts. The flip side: management knows this, so it tends to set the opening range conservatively and raise it through the year if it can — "under-promise, then upgrade". A wide low-starting band like FY27's 1.5%–3.5% says "we're being careful". The art of reading Infosys is reading the direction of the revisions, quarter by quarter.

What to watch: the most informative moments each year are (1) the opening FY guidance at Q4, (2) any mid-year raise or cut, and (3) the gap between guidance and the large-deal TCV pipeline. If deal wins are strong but guidance stays low, management is being cautious; if guidance is cut and deals slow, the demand cycle is genuinely turning.
03

The company and who owns it

A founder-built icon that, unusually, has no controlling owner at all.

Infosys was founded in 1981 by seven engineers — led by N. R. Narayana Murthy — with about $250 of borrowed capital. It became the symbol of India's IT boom, the first Indian company listed on a US exchange (Nasdaq, 1999), and a byword for corporate governance. Its most distinctive feature today: there is no promoter in control. The founders gradually diluted; the company is run by a professional board and management, with ownership spread across global and Indian institutions.

Timeline

  • 1981
    N. R. Narayana Murthy and six colleagues found Infosys in Pune with seed capital of about $250.
  • 1993
    IPO in India; pioneers employee stock options, creating a generation of "Infoscion" millionaires.
  • 1999
    First Indian company listed on Nasdaq; crosses $100 m revenue — a global validation of the India IT model.
  • 2014
    Hires Vishal Sikka (ex-SAP) as first non-founder CEO — a turbulent chapter that ends in 2017.
  • 2018
    Salil Parekh becomes CEO; stabilises strategy around digital, cloud and (later) AI, and restores steady growth.
  • 2023
    Launches Infosys Topaz (generative-AI services) and deepens Cobalt (cloud); AI moves to the centre of the pitch.
  • FY26
    Revenue crosses $20 billion (+3.1% cc); completes an ₹18,000 Cr buyback; sets a cautious FY27 guide of 1.5%–3.5%.

Ownership & control

  • No promoter control. The founder group (the families of N. R. Narayana Murthy, Nandan Nilekani and other co-founders) holds only about 14% combined and exerts no controlling stake — a genuine rarity among large Indian companies, most of which are promoter-dominated.
  • Institutions own the company. Foreign institutional investors hold roughly 28%, domestic institutions and mutual funds a further chunk, and retail investors around 14%. This widely-held structure makes the board, not a family, the locus of control.
  • Professional management. CEO Salil Parekh runs the firm under an independent board; co-founder Nandan Nilekani serves as non-executive Chairman, a stabilising founder presence without operational control.
  • Balance sheet: net cash, debt-free, with strong free cash flow funding both growth and the large buyback/dividend programme.

Verticals and geography — where the revenue comes from

A North-America-heavy, banking-led book — its proven strength and its cyclical risk.

Infosys sells to almost every major industry, but its revenue is concentrated in financial services and in North America. Those concentrations are its decades-deep relationships — and the channels through which a Western spending slowdown hits it first. The encouraging recent counter-note: Europe is growing faster than the US, broadening the base.

05

Margins and Project Maximus — the defence engine

Why operating margin is a managed number, not a fact of nature.

In a people business, the operating margin is under constant assault: wages rise every year, clients push for price cuts, and new hires sit idle before they're billable. Infosys's answer is "Project Maximus" — a structured, multi-lever programme to claw back margin and reinvest it. The FY26 result: a 21.0% adjusted operating margin (20.3% on a reported basis), with FY27 guided at 20%–22%. Holding that band is the central operating discipline.

In plain terms

Project Maximus is best understood as a permanent efficiency factory. Each year wage hikes and a softer pricing environment try to push the margin down; Maximus pushes it back up through dozens of small levers — squeezing the bench, hiring more juniors, automating repetitive delivery, and steering toward higher-value work. The point isn't just to lift margin for its own sake; the savings fund the things that drive future growth (more salespeople, AI capability, training). For investors, the FY27 20%–22% margin guidance is a promise that Infosys can absorb cost pressure and AI-related investment without the margin breaking — and a margin miss is treated almost as seriously as a revenue miss.

06

The generative-AI question

The defining force over the next decade — opportunity and existential threat at once.

No question matters more to Infosys's future than this: does generative AI help it or hollow it out? Infosys earns by selling human effort. AI can write code, test software and run processes — exactly the work it bills for. So AI is simultaneously a tailwind (clients hire Infosys to build their AI; productivity lifts margins) and a threat (if the same output needs fewer people, revenue-per-project — and the headcount model — could deflate).

Infosys's answer: Topaz (and Cobalt underneath)

  • Infosys Topaz — the firm's AI-first services brand: a stack of pre-built AI agents, models and services (including its own small language models and an "Agentic Foundry") that clients buy to embed AI into their operations. The pitch is that enterprises don't want raw models — they want trusted, deployed, industry-specific AI, which is a services sale.
  • Infosys Cobalt — the cloud foundation Topaz runs on; the plumbing that moves clients' data and workloads to the cloud so AI can be applied. Cloud migration remains a large, ongoing revenue stream in its own right.
  • The deal evidence — management says AI is increasingly embedded in deals rather than a separate line, and is a meaningful share of large-deal wins. FY26's $14.9 bn of large-deal TCV (55% net new) is pitched as AI-led demand for transformation and cost take-out.
The bull/bear of AI, in one frame

Bull: AI is the biggest enterprise-tech shift in a generation, and someone has to implement it — re-platform data, build the agents, integrate, secure and run them. That's services work, and Infosys is positioning Topaz to capture it and gain share, while AI also makes its own delivery cheaper (helping margins). Bear: if AI tools let a client achieve the same outcome with a fraction of the engineers, the industry's core "bill people by the hour" model shrinks — pricing and headcount could deflate faster than new AI work replaces it. The honest answer is that both are true; the question is the net and the timing. The first concrete signal to watch is whether revenue keeps growing even as headcount falls — which is exactly what happened in FY26 (headcount down ~8,400 in Q4 while revenue grew).

The signal to track: rising revenue on flat-or-falling headcount would mean AI is lifting productivity without deflating revenue — the dream outcome. Falling revenue-per-employee with shrinking deal sizes would mean the deflation thesis is winning. FY26's combination of modest growth and a headcount decline is an early data point worth watching closely.
07

Discretionary spend, BFSI & the US cycle

Why Infosys is, in part, a leveraged bet on Western corporate confidence.

IT-services demand splits into two moods. "Run the business" spend — keeping existing systems alive — is sticky and defensive. "Change the business" spend — new projects, transformations, the discretionary budget — is the growth engine, and it's the first thing clients cut when they get nervous. Infosys's growth rate is largely a function of how much discretionary spend its big US and European clients are willing to release.

Through FY24–FY26 that discretionary tap was tight. Higher interest rates and macro caution — especially among the US and UK banks that are Infosys's largest vertical — froze new project starts, and growth slowed to low single digits. The cautious FY27 guidance of 1.5%–3.5% reflects management's reluctance to assume a sharp thaw. The hopeful counter-signal: Q4 FY26 exited at a healthier +4.1% cc, and large-deal wins stayed strong, hinting at a pipeline waiting to convert.

In plain terms

When you buy Infosys you are, in part, buying a view on whether big Western companies — above all banks — feel confident enough to start new technology projects. In good times, discretionary budgets flow and Infosys compounds at double digits. In nervous times, those budgets are the first casualty, and growth stalls — even though the business itself hasn't deteriorated. That cyclicality is structural. The thing that can break the cycle to the upside is a genuine AI-driven spending wave (new transformation budgets); the thing that deepens it is a US recession or a banking shock. BFSI plus North America is the dial to watch.

08

#2 to TCS — the rivalry that defines the sector

A clear number two — and why that's both a benchmark and a burden.

Indian IT is, at the top, a duopoly of scale: TCS is the leader; Infosys is the established #2. TCS crossed $30 billion of FY26 revenue; Infosys is at $20.2 billion — roughly two-thirds the size. Infosys is constantly measured against TCS on growth, margin and deal wins, and the comparison shapes how the market judges it.

Why being #2 cuts both ways

Being the clear number two has advantages: Infosys is large enough to win the biggest global deals, carries a premium reputation for governance and digital/AI, and is often seen as the more "modern" of the two giants. But it also means it is perpetually compared — when TCS grows faster or holds a higher margin, Infosys is marked down for the gap; when Infosys's guidance disappoints, the whole sector wobbles. The structural reality: Infosys and TCS sell broadly the same thing to broadly the same clients, so the contest is fought on execution, margin discipline, and who wins the AI narrative — not on a fundamentally different product. Infosys's edge case is its sharper digital/AI positioning and its (sometimes double-edged) willingness to put a guidance number on the table.

09

Financials & valuation

Steady, cash-rich, modestly growing — at a more reasonable multiple than it once carried.

Infosys's financial profile is that of a mature, high-quality compounder going through a slow patch: dependable margins, enormous cash generation, almost no debt — but growth that has cooled with the demand cycle. The debate is whether the next leg is an AI-led re-acceleration or a structurally slower, AI-deflated future.

How to think about the ~14× multiple

At roughly ₹4.2 lakh crore market cap on about ₹29,440 crore of FY26 profit, Infosys trades around 14× trailing earnings — well below the ~25–30× multiples Indian IT enjoyed in the post-Covid euphoria, and a discount to its own history. That de-rating reflects the growth slowdown and AI uncertainty. Bulls argue you're buying a debt-free, ~21%-margin, cash-gushing franchise with optionality on an AI-spending recovery, at an undemanding price. Bears argue the multiple is fair, not cheap, because growth is structurally slower and AI could compress the very model that generates the cash. The swing factor is the guidance trajectory: a credible return to high-single-digit growth would re-rate the stock; further cuts would confirm the bears. (Note: the ~14× is a rough estimate from a ~₹1,037 share price on ~29 Jun 2026; market levels move daily — treat the multiple as indicative.)

10

The capital-return machine

What a low-growth, cash-rich business does with the money: gives nearly all of it back.

Because Infosys generates far more cash than it needs to reinvest (a people business is light on capital), the question becomes what to do with the cash — and the answer is a formal policy to return the vast majority of it to shareholders. FY26 alone saw over ₹37,500 crore handed back, through a rising dividend and a large buyback.

In plain terms

Infosys has a stated capital-allocation policy to return roughly 85% of free cash flow to shareholders over a period, via dividends plus buybacks. In a high-growth year that money would chase new projects; in a low-growth, cash-rich era like now, returning it is the disciplined choice — and it puts a floor under the stock through the dividend yield and the share-count shrinkage of buybacks. For a non-specialist: this is a business mature enough that it can't usefully spend all its profit on growth, so it acts a bit like a utility — compounding modestly, paying you to wait, and shrinking its own shares. The risk is the mirror image: heavy cash returns are also an admission that explosive growth is no longer the story.

A company that hands back nearly all its cash is telling you something true about its growth — and rewarding you honestly for owning it anyway.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality, cash-rich franchise priced for slow growth — with AI as the swing factor.

Infosys is one of the cleaner businesses in Indian large-caps: debt-free, ~21% margins, prodigious cash generation, world-class clients and governance. The investment debate is almost entirely about growth and AI — can it re-accelerate as Western spend recovers and AI demand builds, or has AI begun to erode the people-billing model that made it?

The bull case

  • AI tailwind — Topaz/Cobalt position Infosys to win enterprise-AI implementation and gain share as a new spending wave builds.
  • Margin discipline — Project Maximus has held a ~21% margin through a tough cycle; AI can lower delivery cost too.
  • Cash machine — debt-free, ~113% cash conversion, >₹37,500 Cr returned in FY26; a floor under the stock.
  • Cyclical recovery optionality — Q4 exited at +4.1% cc with strong deal wins; a BFSI/US thaw could lift growth back to high single digits.
  • Reasonable valuation — ~14× earnings, well below post-Covid peaks, with founder-grade governance and no debt.

The bear case

  • AI deflation risk — if AI lets clients do the same with fewer engineers, pricing and the headcount model could shrink faster than new AI work replaces them.
  • Cyclical demand — heavy BFSI/North-America exposure makes growth hostage to US/UK budget cycles and recession risk.
  • Cautious guidance — a wide, low 1.5–3.5% FY27 guide signals limited confidence in a near-term acceleration.
  • Structurally slower growth — the days of mid-teens compounding look over; heavy cash returns concede the point.
  • Always #2 — perpetually measured against a larger, higher-margin TCS; little room to disappoint on either growth or margin.
The bottom line

Infosys is a high-quality, cash-generative, well-governed franchise trading at an undemanding multiple — but it sits at the centre of the single biggest uncertainty in its own industry: whether generative AI is a growth engine or a slow solvent for the labour-arbitrage model. The story over the next few years will be told in three numbers each quarter — the guidance revision (is growth re-accelerating?), the margin band (is Maximus holding?), and revenue-per-employee (is AI lifting productivity or deflating the model?). Get those right and a cheap, cash-rich compounder re-rates; get them wrong and the discount is deserved.
