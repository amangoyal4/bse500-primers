Strides Pharma Science, the generics core, deleveraged

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Strides Pharma Science Limited makes finished generic medicines — the tablets, capsules and other everyday dosage forms a pharmacist hands over once a branded drug's patent expires — and sells them into the most tightly regulated markets on Earth: the United States, the United Kingdom and Europe, Australia, other regulated markets, and donor-funded institutional / African markets. It is a Bengaluru company founded by Arun Kumar, and in the pharma world it is best understood as a regulated-market generics pure-play — no branded blockbusters, no patented molecules of its own, just the disciplined, unglamorous business of getting cheap copies of off-patent drugs approved and onto shelves in countries where the approval bar is punishingly high.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It just made itself deliberately smaller — and that was the point. For years Strides was a sprawl of businesses: generics, plus a capital-hungry biologics/CDMO arm (Stelis), plus a soft-gelatin manufacturing operation. In FY25 it hived all of that off into a separately listed company, OneSource Specialty Pharma, handing OneSource shares straight to its own shareholders. What is left listed as "Strides" today is the leaner, cash-generative finished-dosage generics core — and shedding the loss-making, cash-absorbing pieces is precisely what let it deleverage and refocus.
  • The moat is approvals, not chemistry. Anyone can copy an off-patent molecule; the hard part is convincing the US FDA (and the UK's MHRA, Australia's TGA, and their peers) that your factory and your product are safe and identical, batch after batch. Strides holds a deep back-catalogue of US approvals — around 260 ANDAs filed, ~245 approved — and that regulatory library, plus the manufacturing quality behind it, is the actual asset. The permanent enemy is US generic price erosion: approved copies get cheaper every year as more rivals pile in.
  • FY26 was the recovery landing. Revenue rose ~6% to ~₹4,858.7 Cr, the EBITDA margin climbed to 19.0%, and operational net profit jumped ~50% to ~₹518 Cr — but almost all the growth came from outside America (ex-US up 21%; US up just 2%). Net debt fell to 1.55× EBITDA. The stock trades around 17–18× earnings — the multiple of a turnaround the market half-believes but wants to see repeated.
In plain terms

Strides is a factory-and-paperwork business. It makes generic pills cheaply and — far harder — keeps a library of regulatory approvals that let it sell those pills into countries where a single failed factory inspection can shut you out for years. A few years ago the company had loaded that steady core with two expensive bets: a biotech/contract-manufacturing arm and a soft-gel operation, both of which swallowed cash and piled on debt. So management performed surgery: it packaged those bets into a new listed company, OneSource, and let shareholders own that separately — leaving Strides as the clean, profitable generics engine. FY26 shows the surgery working: fatter margins, sharply higher profit, falling debt. The catch is that its biggest single market, the US, is barely growing, because American generic prices only ever fall. The whole future now depends on the rest of the world growing fast enough to outrun that.

01

Regulated-market generics 101: how the money is actually made

No pharma background needed — just what a generic is, why "regulated" is the whole game, and where the profit hides.

Every branded medicine eventually loses its patent. The day it does, other manufacturers are free to make an identical copy — a generic — and sell it far cheaper. Generics are how healthcare systems keep drug bills affordable: in the US, roughly nine of every ten prescriptions filled are generic. Strides lives entirely in this world. It does not discover drugs; it copies off-patent ones and sells them where copying is legal, cheap to distribute, and — crucially — hard to be allowed to do.

That last point is everything. There are two very different kinds of generics business, and Strides is firmly the second:

  • Branded / emerging-market generics — sold under a company's own brand to doctors and chemists in places like India, where a familiar name and a sales force do much of the work. Marketing-led.
  • Regulated-market generics — sold into the US, UK/EU, Australia and similar markets, where medicines are dispensed by molecule, not brand, and the gatekeeper is a regulator, not a doctor's habit. Here the competitive weapons are regulatory approvals, manufacturing quality, and cost. This is Strides.
The only generics terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
ANDAAbbreviated New Drug Application — the dossier a company files with the US FDA to sell a generic. "Abbreviated" because you prove your copy is equivalent to the original, not that the drug works from scratch. Each approved ANDA is a licence to sell one product in the US. Strides holds a stack of them — the core asset.
Finished dosage (FDF)The actual sellable form — a tablet, capsule or bottle — as opposed to the raw drug powder (the API). Strides makes finished dosage. It buys or makes the active ingredient and formulates it into the thing a patient swallows.
Price erosionThe steady annual fall in a generic's selling price as more approved competitors enter. In US generics this is relentless — often high-single-digit to double-digit percentage falls a year on a given product. The enemy every regulated-generics maker fights.
cGMP / inspectionCurrent Good Manufacturing Practice — the quality standard a factory must meet. Regulators inspect plants and issue verdicts (a clean "NAI", a minor "VAI", or a serious "OAI" that can block approvals). A bad inspection can freeze a company out of a market. Quality is the moat.
Regulated marketsCountries with strict, expensive approval regimes — US (FDA), UK/EU (MHRA/EMA), Australia (TGA), Canada, etc. High barriers keep competition rational; clearing them takes years. Strides' entire strategy is to be present in these, not in easy markets.
Why "hard to enter" is the good news, not the bad

It sounds backwards, but the difficulty of regulated markets is exactly why a company would choose them. In an unregulated market, anyone with a mixing tank can copy a drug and undercut you to zero. In a regulated market, you first have to spend years and millions clearing an FDA or MHRA bar — and that same bar keeps out the flood of low-cost competitors who never bother. So the profit in regulated generics does not come from a clever molecule; it comes from having already done the hard, boring, expensive work of getting approved and staying inspectable. Strides' library of ~245 US approvals and its clearances across the UK, Australia and beyond are, in effect, a toll gate it has already paid to cross. The catch, covered in section 06, is that once you and a handful of others are through the gate, you compete each other's prices down anyway.

Where a rupee of revenue comes from

  • The molecule and the formulation — Strides picks off-patent drugs to copy, develops a stable formulation, and files for approval. Choosing the right products (niche, hard-to-make, few competitors) is half the skill.
  • The factory — it manufactures the finished dosage at plants that must stay FDA/MHRA-inspectable. A lost inspection can idle an entire revenue stream.
  • The channel — in the US it sells to giant buying consortia and distributors; in institutional markets it supplies global health agencies and governments; in Africa it increasingly sells branded generics. Different channels, very different pricing dynamics.
02

The great slimming: how Strides shrank on purpose

The single most important thing to understand about this company — and the reason today's Strides is not the Strides of five years ago.

Most turnaround stories are about a company growing its way out of trouble. Strides did the opposite: it got out of trouble by giving pieces of itself away. To read the FY26 numbers correctly you have to know exactly what left the building — because a reader who assumes Strides still owns its famous soft-gel and biotech operations will misjudge the entire business.

Rewind to the early 2020s. Strides was carrying three very different things under one roof: (1) its finished-dosage generics business — steady, cash-generative, the historic core; (2) Stelis Biopharma — an ambitious biologics and complex-injectables contract-manufacturing (CDMO) arm that needed enormous, sustained capital investment before it could earn; and (3) a soft-gelatin capsule manufacturing operation. The generics core was throwing off cash. The biologics/CDMO bet was consuming it — and the debt to fund that ambition sat on the whole group's balance sheet, dragging down margins, returns and the share price together.

In September 2023 management announced the plan; the National Company Law Tribunal sanctioned it in November 2024 (with an effective date of 1 April 2024), and on 24 January 2025 OneSource Specialty Pharma listed on the NSE and BSE — built by folding Stelis' biologics CDMO, a complex-injectables business and Strides' own soft-gelatin operation into a single specialty-CDMO vehicle. Strides shareholders were handed OneSource shares directly. In one stroke, the capital-hungry, long-gestation businesses left the Strides balance sheet and became someone else's listed company — one that shareholders could choose to keep or sell on its own merits.

The device that only fits Strides: subtraction as strategy

Here is the construct that could not be lifted onto another primer. Strides did not fix its balance sheet by earning more; it fixed it by owning less. The equation was: (generics core cash flow) − (biologics/CDMO capital drain) − (attached debt) = a stock the market couldn't value. By cleaving off the middle and right terms into OneSource, the listed Strides became just the left term — a business whose cash flow no longer disappears into a build-out it may not live to see finished. The FY26 jump in margin and the fall in leverage are not mainly operational heroics; they are, in large part, the arithmetic of removal. Understand that and the recovery stops looking miraculous and starts looking structural.

The surprise most newcomers trip on: Strides was long celebrated for its soft-gelatin capsule capability — and it is easy to assume that is still a Strides strength. It is not: the soft-gel operation went into OneSource in the demerger. Today's listed Strides is overwhelmingly an oral-solids (tablets and hard capsules) and everyday finished-dosage generics maker. If you value Strides as a soft-gel specialist, you are valuing the wrong company — that capability now sits in the sister listing.
03

What Strides actually is today: the portfolio and the plants

A focused finished-dosage generics maker with an unusually global sales map.

With the CDMO and biotech gone, the listed Strides is easy to describe: it develops, manufactures and sells finished generic medicines — chiefly oral solids — into a spread of regulated and institutional markets, running a network of factories that must satisfy the world's toughest regulators. Its revenue is best understood not by product but by geography, because the same pill earns very different economics depending on where it is sold.

The manufacturing base

Strides runs finished-dosage facilities across multiple sites — its home base in and around Bengaluru and other locations in India, a US formulations facility at Chestnut Ridge, New York, plus a Singapore operation and a growing footprint serving Africa. The precise plant list and their individual regulatory statuses change with each inspection cycle, so the point to hold is structural rather than a headcount of buildings: every plant that serves the US or Europe must stay continuously FDA/MHRA-inspectable, and each one is both a revenue engine and a single point of regulatory risk. A serious adverse inspection at a key site can stall approvals and freeze a product line — which is why quality spending is not overhead here, it is the price of staying in business.

Why the product list matters less than the map

In a branded consumer company you track hero products. In regulated generics that instinct misleads, because most individual products are commodities whose price only falls. What actually moves Strides' profitability is the blend of markets its portfolio is sold into: a molecule sold into a crowded US category earns a thin, shrinking margin, while the same molecule sold into a less-contested "other regulated market" or a branded African channel can earn a fuller, steadier one. So the useful mental model is a map, not a shelf — and in FY26 that map tilted decisively away from the US and toward everywhere else, which is the story of section 05.

04

The company, the founder, and who controls it

A serial pharma builder's flagship — with a long history of building businesses and then selling or spinning them off.

Strides carries the fingerprints of one man: Arun Kumar, the founder-promoter, a figure known in Indian pharma for repeatedly building businesses and then monetising them. Understanding that pattern is essential, because the OneSource demerger is not a one-off — it is the latest move in a decades-long habit of assembling, listing and unlocking value from separate pharma vehicles.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter group: Arun Kumar and associated promoter entities (names such as Pronomz Ventures LLP and Karuna Business Solutions appear in the promoter block) hold roughly ~28% (approx.) of the company. The balance is a wide public and institutional float — including well-known domestic investors — which means Strides is not a tightly-held family fortress; public shareholders own the majority. Confirm the exact promoter percentage in the latest shareholding filing.
  • A pledge to watch: a meaningful portion of the promoters' own holding has historically been pledged (reported around a third of the promoter stake). Promoter pledging is a governance flag worth tracking — it can force selling in a downturn — even as it does not by itself impair the operating business.
  • Arun Kumar's role: he serves as Non-Executive Chairman, with a professional management team running operations day to day — a structure that leans on his strategic dealmaking while institutionalising the running of the generics engine.
Why the founder's track record cuts both ways: Arun Kumar's history of building-and-unlocking (Agila to Mylan, Solara, now OneSource) is a genuine value-creation skill — few Indian promoters have monetised businesses as repeatedly. But the same restlessness means minority investors must stay alert to related-party dealings, promoter pledging, and the possibility that the next value-unlock reshapes the company again. You are backing a builder-and-seller, not a steady-state operator — price that personality in.

The four markets — and the surprise inside them

Strides' revenue is a geography story, and FY26 flipped which leg is doing the work.

Strides sells the same kinds of pills into four very different worlds, and the split between them is the clearest window into the company. In FY26 something counter-intuitive happened: the market everyone thinks of first — the giant United States — was the slowest-growing leg, while the smaller "everywhere else" businesses did the heavy lifting.

The four legs, and how each earns

  • United States (~₹2,490 Cr, +2%) — the largest single market, sold through big buying consortia and distributors. It is where Strides' ANDA library lives (~72 commercialised products, top-3 rank in ~37 of them, ~70% of US revenue from those). But it is a treadmill: annual price erosion means simply standing still requires a steady drip of new launches. In FY26 it barely grew — a feature of US generics, not a Strides failing.
  • Other Regulated Markets (ORM) — the UK, Europe, Australia and the Nordics. Less crowded than the US, more contract-and-tender driven, and Strides' fastest-growing regulated leg: quarterly ORM revenue broke out of a long $40–43m range to exit FY26 near $52m a quarter. This is the leg quietly closing the gap on the US run-rate.
  • Africa (branded) — an "in Africa, for Africa" strategy selling branded generics through local channels; steadier, brand-influenced margins. A Sandoz branded-portfolio acquisition (Feb 2026) is set to roughly double Strides' branded African presence from H2 FY27.
  • Institutional / Access — supplying donor-funded and government health programmes (think global agencies buying anti-malarials, HIV and other essential medicines at scale). Lower-margin and lumpy, but high-volume and mission-aligned — and a genuine differentiator few pure US-focused generic makers have.
The genuine surprise: the "American generics company" is barely an American growth story

Ask a generalist to describe Strides and they will reach for "US generics exporter." FY26 quietly upends that. The US grew ~2%; everything else grew ~21%. Ex-US revenue (~₹2,240 Cr) has drawn almost level with US revenue (~₹2,490 Cr) and, on current trajectories, will overtake it. The company that clawed its way back to health did so not by winning America but by diversifying away from its dependence on it — turning a one-market treadmill into a four-market spread where a stumble in any single geography no longer decides the year. The US remains the biggest single number; it is no longer the growth engine. That reframing is the crux of the modern investment case.

06

US price erosion: the enemy that never sleeps

The structural headwind every regulated-generics maker fights — and why growth has to be manufactured, not assumed.

To understand why the US barely grows even for a healthy company, you have to understand the one force that defines American generics: price erosion. It is not a bad quarter or a cyclical dip — it is the permanent physics of the market, and it shapes everything Strides does.

The mechanism is simple and merciless. When a drug goes off-patent, the first generic entrant sells at a large discount to the brand but still earns well. Then a second approved competitor arrives, and a third, and a fifth — and because US pharmacies substitute by molecule and giant buyers run reverse auctions, the price of that generic ratchets down, year after year, often by high-single-digit to double-digit percentages annually. No brand loyalty slows it; no marketing rescues it. A product that earned handsomely at launch becomes a commodity earning cents within a few years.

What price erosion means for how Strides must run

Price erosion turns the US into a down escalator you have to keep climbing. Every year, the existing portfolio loses value on its own; to hold US revenue flat — let alone grow it — Strides must launch enough new approved products to replace what erosion takes away. That is precisely why the ANDA pipeline (section 08) is not a vanity metric but a survival mechanism: approvals are the fresh steps that offset the ground lost beneath. It also explains the strategic logic of the whole company: if the US is a treadmill that eats its own gains, the rational response is to (a) pick niche, harder-to-copy products where fewer rivals crowd in, and (b) grow the non-US markets where erosion is gentler. Strides is doing both.

This is why FY26's ~2% US growth should be read as success at standing still, not stagnation — the company launched enough to more than offset erosion in a brutal market. It is also why the ex-US pivot is not a nice-to-have but a necessity: a maker overexposed to US generics is running up a down escalator forever, and the only way off is a broader map. The permanent question for a Strides investor is therefore not "is the US growing?" (it structurally won't, much) but "is the rest of the portfolio growing fast enough to outrun US erosion — and is the margin holding while it does?"

US generic pricing is set in a highly consolidated buyer market and can move sharply with competitor entries, supply disruptions or channel consolidation. The ~2% FY26 US growth reflects new launches offsetting ongoing erosion; a wave of new competition on Strides' larger products, or a supply/quality setback, could turn that number negative in any given period. This is the single biggest swing factor in the US leg.

08

Approvals and the FDA: the pipeline that is the moat

Why a stack of dossiers and a clean inspection record are worth more than any single product.

If price erosion is the enemy, the approval pipeline is the weapon — and the inspection record is the fortress wall. For a regulated-generics maker, the asset base is not really the factories or the products; it is the accumulated right to sell, market by market, and the manufacturing quality that keeps that right alive. Strides has spent decades building both.

Two things follow from that funnel. First, an approved-but-not-launched ANDA is a real option: Strides can bring it to market when a competitor exits, a shortage opens up, or pricing turns attractive — optionality a company without the approval simply does not have. Second, the fact that ~37 top-ranked products drive ~70% of US revenue means the US business, for all its breadth, is concentrated in a manageable number of winners — good for focus, but a reminder that a competitive or regulatory hit to a few key products matters disproportionately.

Why the inspection record is the part that can end a company

The approvals are worthless if the factory that makes them loses the regulator's confidence. FDA and MHRA inspectors visit plants and grade them; a benign verdict (a clean "NAI" or a minor "VAI") lets business continue, while a serious "OAI" or a warning letter can freeze new approvals and stall shipments from that site — a potentially existential event for the products it makes. Strides' facilities have generally cleared their inspection cycles (its US formulations site at Chestnut Ridge, New York, secured an inspection closure report after a late-2025 FDA visit, for instance), which is exactly the boring good news a regulated-generics investor should want to keep reading. The day the news turns is the day the thesis needs re-examining — quality is the one risk here that is binary rather than gradual.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded field of Indian generics exporters — where Strides is a focused mid-cap, not a giant.

India is the world's pharmacy for generics, and Strides competes in a well-populated field of listed exporters — most of them larger, several of them far larger. It does not try to out-scale the giants; it tries to be a focused, well-run, diversified-geography operator with a lean balance sheet. Placing it against its peers clarifies both its modest size and its particular tilt.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two features stand out. First, Strides cannot win this industry on scale — the majors dwarf it, and in a business where cost and breadth matter, that is a real constraint. Its edge has to come from selection and spread: picking niche products where a giant's scale is less decisive, and being present across four market types so no single-geography price war is fatal. Second, the industry's own history is a warning and a comfort at once — a warning because US price erosion and periodic FDA crackdowns have humbled far bigger names, and a comfort because Strides has already lived through its own crisis and emerged lighter. In a field where the graveyard is full of over-levered, over-US-exposed generic makers, having deliberately fixed both of those weaknesses is the differentiator.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A repaired, diversified generics maker asked whether it can keep outrunning the US treadmill.

Strides enters FY27 in the best shape it has been in for years: the loss-making bets gone into OneSource, margins at 19%, debt down to 1.55× EBITDA, an ex-US engine growing 21%, and a Sandoz-backed push to deepen Africa. What it cannot promise is the one thing that decides the case — that the rest of the world keeps growing fast enough, and cleanly enough through the regulators, to more than offset an American market built to erode. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • The demerger de-risked it — the capital-hungry CDMO/biotech is gone; what remains is a cash-generative core that can deleverage and pay dividends.
  • Ex-US is compounding — ORM growing ~18% and breaking out to ~$52m/quarter, Africa set to nearly double on the Sandoz portfolio; the growth engine is no longer US-dependent.
  • Margin & returns recovering together — 19% EBITDA, 15.8% RoCE, a CARE A+ upgrade; a structurally healthier business than the pre-restructuring Strides.
  • An approval library as optionality — ~245 approved ANDAs, only ~72 commercialised, means launch-when-attractive upside without new regulatory spend.
  • Undemanding multiple — ~17–18× earnings for a de-levered, diversifying generics maker that just grew profit ~50%; little is priced in.

The bear case

  • US price erosion is permanent — the largest single market grows ~2% at best; a pricing shock or lost product could turn it negative and drag the whole.
  • Binary regulatory risk — a serious FDA/MHRA inspection failure at a key plant can freeze approvals and shipments overnight; quality is a one-strike game.
  • Concentration & scale — ~37 products drive ~70% of US revenue, and Strides is a fraction of the majors' size in a cost-and-breadth industry.
  • Residual debt & FX — net debt is lower but not gone; a dollar-earning, rupee-reporting exporter also carries currency swing in both revenue and cost.
  • Promoter/governance texture — a serial builder-and-seller founder, promoter pledging, and a history of restructurings mean minorities must watch capital allocation closely.
The bottom line

Strides Pharma Science is a company that saved itself by subtraction — cleaving its capital-draining ambitions into OneSource and keeping the disciplined generics core that always paid its way. FY26 shows the surgery worked: margins, profit, returns and leverage all moved the right way at once. But the business it kept lives on a fundamental tension — its biggest market erodes by design, so it must manufacture growth everywhere else faster than America takes it away. The next concrete test is datable and specific: whether, through the FY27 quarters, ex-US revenue keeps growing near 20% and lifts the group even as the US idles, while the EBITDA margin defends 19% and net debt/EBITDA edges toward ~1×. Deliver that spread of growth-over-erosion at a held margin, and a cheap, de-levered mid-cap quietly compounds. Let ex-US momentum fade or a plant lose the FDA's confidence, and FY26 will stand as the good year the model was rebuilt to reach — not the floor it hoped to build from.
