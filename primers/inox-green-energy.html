00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Inox Green Energy Services does not manufacture wind turbines. It maintains them. It is India's only listed pure-play renewable operations-and-maintenance (O&M) company — the firm a wind-farm owner pays a fixed annual fee to keep every machine cleaned, serviced, monitored and, above all, available to generate whenever the wind blows. Where its parent Inox Wind lives on lumpy, one-time turbine sales, Inox Green lives on the opposite: a small recurring cheque per megawatt that arrives every year for the 20-plus-year life of the asset, and that grows each time another turbine is switched on somewhere in India. It is the caretaker, not the builder.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The business is an annuity, not a project. A turbine sale happens once; the O&M contract on that same turbine pays a fee every year for two decades. Inox Green services roughly 13 GWp of wind and solar assets after the Wind World acquisition — an installed base that mechanically only grows, because a turbine put up in a boom still needs servicing through the following bust. Revenue visibility, not order-winning drama, is the point.
  • The margin story is a contract-mix story. Much of the legacy book is old fixed-price O&M contracts signed cheaply at the time the turbine was sold — thin, sometimes loss-making. The value creation is rolling those into richer comprehensive contracts (where Inox Green also supplies spares and consumables and charges accordingly) and winning higher-value third-party work. FY26 revenue of ~₹281 Cr looks small against a 13 GW fleet precisely because so much of it is still on legacy pricing.
  • It just scaled by buying a bankrupt rival. Rather than wait for its parent to build new turbines, in early 2026 Inox Green won the NCLT insolvency auction for Wind World India's ~4.5 GW wind O&M portfolio — instantly adding a fleet, and blue-chip third-party clients like Greenko, Tata, ReNew and Hindustan Zinc, that would have taken a decade to build organically. The stock trades near 60× earnings on a bet that this scale converts into a real, thick annuity.
In plain terms

A wind farm is a field of expensive machines that earn money only when they are running. Someone has to climb the towers, swap the gearbox oil, replace worn blades, watch the sensors and guarantee the fleet is available — and the owner would rather pay a specialist a flat annual fee than staff that itself. Inox Green is that specialist for renewables. Its revenue does not spike and collapse with the equipment cycle the way turbine-making does; it drips in steadily and compounds with every megawatt added to the national grid. The catch is that a great deal of its fleet is still serviced under cheap old contracts, so the fee it actually collects per megawatt is far below what the same machines could pay — and the entire investment case rests on closing that gap.

01

Renewable O&M 101: getting paid to keep the lights on

No engineering degree needed — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

Once a wind or solar farm is built and switched on, the exciting part is over and the boring, valuable part begins: keeping it running for the next 20–25 years. That is operations & maintenance. It is unglamorous, low-drama, and — for the company that does it well — a beautifully predictable business, because the customer cannot simply stop maintaining a ₹500-crore asset. To follow Inox Green, you need five terms.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
O&M"Operations & Maintenance" — the long-term service contract to keep a renewable asset running: inspections, servicing, spare-part changes, remote monitoring, breakdown repair. Inox Green's entire business. Paid as a recurring fee, not a one-off sale.
AvailabilityThe share of the time a turbine is able to generate (i.e. not shut for a fault or service). This is the number an O&M contract is written around — Inox Green runs ~96.5% machine availability. Every point of availability is real electricity the owner can sell.
GW / GWpGigawatts (peak, for solar) of capacity under management — the size of the fleet Inox Green services. ~13.3 GWp is the scale metric that matters most; revenue should, over time, track it.
Fixed-price vs comprehensiveTwo flavours of O&M contract. Fixed-price: a flat fee, with the owner buying the spares — cheap, thin margin. Comprehensive: Inox Green also supplies spares and consumables and guarantees availability — a much bigger fee. The shift between the two is the whole margin story.
Captive vs third-partyWhether the turbines being serviced were made and sold by parent Inox Wind (captive) or by someone else (third-party). Third-party work — like the Wind World fleet — is the growth frontier and proves the business can stand on its own.
The economics that decide everything: fee per MW × years

An O&M company's value is almost embarrassingly simple to write down: fee per megawatt × megawatts under management × contract years, minus the cost of the technicians and spares to honour it. The megawatts are sticky (a 20-year contract), so the two levers that actually move the needle are the fee per megawatt and the cost to serve. A legacy fixed-price contract might pay a fraction of what a comprehensive contract on the same machine pays, because the owner is buying the spares separately. Inox Green's core project is to lift that fee-per-MW — through comprehensive conversions and higher-value third-party wins — across a fleet that keeps getting bigger. Everything else in this primer is a variation on that one sentence.

Who pays for O&M

  • The turbine buyer, by default — when Inox Wind sells a turbine, an O&M contract is usually bundled in, and it lands in Inox Green's lap. This is the captive pipeline.
  • Independent power producers & utilities — big renewable owners (Greenko, ReNew, Tata Power, Apraava) who would rather outsource maintenance to a specialist than build an in-house crew for every scattered wind site.
  • C&I and corporate owners — factories and groups that bought captive wind or solar to green their power and now need someone to keep it running for decades. A growing customer class as corporate renewable buying accelerates.
02

What Inox Green actually sells: one fee, three tiers of value

The service model — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Inox Green sells uptime. A wind-farm owner hands over a field of turbines and, for an annual fee, gets back a guarantee that those machines are inspected, serviced, monitored around the clock and kept available to generate. The same core service is sold at three very different levels of richness — and moving a contract up the tiers is exactly how Inox Green grows revenue without adding a single new megawatt.

The same turbine can travel up this ladder over its life. Every step up is more revenue and more margin from a machine Inox Green already services — growth that needs no new installation to occur.

The revenue lines, decoded

  • Core wind O&M — the backbone. Long-duration contracts to service wind turbines: scheduled maintenance, blade and gearbox care, breakdown response, and a 24×7 remote monitoring centre that watches the fleet's sensors and dispatches technicians. Whether a given contract is fixed-price or comprehensive is the single biggest driver of how much this line earns.
  • Solar O&M — the diversification. Inox Green has pushed beyond wind into servicing solar plants (module cleaning, inverter maintenance, performance monitoring), lifting its portfolio toward a blended ~3 GWp of solar alongside the wind fleet. Solar O&M is lower-touch than wind but broadens the addressable market and smooths the technology mix.
  • Common infrastructure & ancillary — the long tail. Wind farms share roads, internal lines, substations and power-evacuation infrastructure; maintaining these common facilities is a recurring charge layered on the same sites. (In 2025–26 the group carved the dedicated power-evacuation business out via a demerger, sharpening Inox Green as a cleaner O&M pure-play.)
Why "recurring" is worth explaining

The prize in this business is switching cost. Once Inox Green holds the O&M contract on a wind farm, it knows the machines, holds the spares, staffs the site and runs the monitoring — and the owner faces real risk and disruption in prying that contract away to move it elsewhere. So contracts renew, fees inch up, and the same fleet keeps paying for decades. That stickiness is why an O&M annuity is worth far more per rupee of revenue than a one-time equipment sale: it is visible, repeatable and defended. The flip side, and the real tension in this specific company, is that a contract signed cheaply at Tier 1 stays cheap until it is actively renegotiated up — stickiness cuts both ways, and a fleet locked into thin legacy pricing is slow to re-rate.

03

The company, the parent, and who controls it

A GFL-group company — and the O&M half of the Inox wind story.

Inox Green Energy Services began life as the operations-and-maintenance subsidiary of Inox Wind, both part of the Inox / GFL group controlled by the Jain family (the same industrial house behind Gujarat Fluorochemicals). It was carved out and separately listed so that the steady, annuity-like service business could be valued on its own terms, distinct from its parent's cyclical turbine-manufacturing swings.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Inox / GFL group (via Inox Wind and associated Jain-family entities) holds roughly 56% (approx.). Inox Green remains a listed subsidiary of Inox Wind — the parent both controls it and feeds it turbines to service.
  • The group map (keep it straight): Inox Wind (INOXWIND) makes and installs the turbines; Inox Green (INOXGREEN) — this company — maintains them. The Wind World IPP assets (~600 MW of operating wind farms) went to a separate group vehicle (Inox Clean Energy / Inox Neo Energies), while the O&M portfolio came to Inox Green — the two halves of the same insolvency deal landing in different pockets. Do not confuse any of this with the unrelated Inox India (cryogenic tanks) or INOX Leisure (cinemas).
  • Balance sheet: IPO proceeds cut the debt inherited from its captive years; net debt has fallen sharply (borrowings down toward ~₹90 Cr) and the receivables backlog that once choked cash has been worked down — the enabling cleanup covered in §7.
The parent relationship cuts both ways: being Inox Wind's O&M arm hands Inox Green a captive pipeline — most turbines the parent sells arrive with an O&M contract attached. But it also ties Inox Green's captive growth to the parent's ability to actually commission turbines on schedule, and it means related-party pricing and receivables between the two must be watched. A subsidiary that depends on its parent for its pipeline is only as reliable as the parent's execution.
04

The contract-tenor engine: why the same turbine can pay double

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — and it is specific to this company.

Here is the puzzle that defines Inox Green. It services roughly 13 GWp of assets — an enormous fleet — yet booked only ~₹281 Cr of revenue in FY26. Do the arithmetic and the implied fee per megawatt looks strikingly low. That is not a mistake; it is the whole opportunity. A large share of that fleet sits on legacy fixed-price contracts signed years ago, cheaply, when the turbine was sold. The entire value-creation plan is dragging those contracts up the tiers — and the same physical machine can pay Inox Green roughly double once it moves from a fixed-price to a comprehensive contract.

Fleet size sets the ceiling; contract mix sets how much of that ceiling is actually billed.

This is the company in one idea. The 13 GWp headline is the potential revenue pool. What Inox Green actually earns depends on where each contract sits on the ladder — and today far too much of the fleet is stuck at the bottom rung. So the metric that genuinely matters is not "how many gigawatts?" but "what share of those gigawatts is on comprehensive rather than fixed-price contracts?" — because that share, multiplied out, is the difference between ₹281 Cr of revenue and something several times larger on the very same machines.

The device that only fits Inox Green: fleet size vs contract mix

Most service companies you can size with one number — revenue per customer, say. Inox Green needs two that pull in opposite directions: a huge and growing fleet (13 GWp, sticky, 20-year contracts) sitting on an under-monetised contract book (much of it thin legacy pricing). The gap between the two is the entire thesis. If comprehensive-mix climbs — legacy contracts renegotiated up, new turbines signed comprehensive from day one, third-party wins landed at market rates — revenue and margin re-rate hard off a small base, with the fleet already captured. If the mix stays stuck, the company is a giant caretaker collecting pocket-money fees, and a 60× stock is valuing a fleet it isn't being paid properly to service. The megawatts tell you the size of the prize; only the contract mix tells you how much of it Inox Green will bank.

05

The Wind World deal: growing by buying a bankrupt rival's fleet

The move that changed the company's scale overnight — and the genuine surprise in the story.

The most striking fact about Inox Green is how it grew big. An O&M company's obvious growth path is to wait for its parent to build and sell new turbines, then service them. Inox Green did something far more interesting: in early 2026 it won the NCLT insolvency auction for the O&M business of Wind World India — the former Indian arm of German turbine pioneer Enercon, once a top-two Indian wind name, that had collapsed into bankruptcy. In one stroke, Inox Green picked up the servicing rights to a ~4.5 GW installed fleet and a roster of blue-chip clients it had never served before.

Why buy a bankrupt rival's maintenance book rather than compete for the same contracts one by one? Because in O&M, the installed base is the moat, and building it organically takes decades. Wind World's ~4.5 GW of turbines were already up, already generating, already needing service — and the insolvency process let Inox Green acquire the servicing relationships in a single court-approved transaction rather than winning them tender by tender over ten years. It converts a competitor's misfortune directly into Inox Green's annuity, and the group has framed the added capacity as roughly ₹600 Cr of long-term annuity income.

A genuine surprise: the single biggest driver of Inox Green's growth is not its parent building more turbines — it is the failure of a competitor. India's wind industry is littered with the wreckage of once-great names (Enercon/Wind World, Suzlon's near-death, others), and every distressed fleet still has to be maintained by someone. Inox Green has, in effect, discovered that an O&M consolidator can grow fastest not in a boom but in the aftermath of a bust — buying the servicing rights to fleets whose original makers no longer exist. That is a counter-cyclical growth engine hiding inside an annuity business.
Why the blue-chip clients matter more than the megawatts

The Wind World book came with names Inox Green could not have won on its own reputation quickly: Greenko, Tata, Apraava, ReNew, Hindustan Zinc — some of the largest and most demanding renewable owners in the country. Winning them via acquisition does two things. It proves the company can service anybody's turbines, not just its parent's, which is the whole argument for third-party O&M. And it plants Inox Green inside blue-chip accounts that own far more capacity than the acquired fleet — a foot in the door to service their future projects too. The 4.5 GW is the headline; the client list is the option value.

06

Captive vs third-party: two very different ways to grow

The tension between depending on the parent and standing on its own.

Every megawatt Inox Green services arrives by one of two routes, and the balance between them tells you how healthy the business really is. Captive work comes from parent Inox Wind: sell a turbine, attach an O&M contract, hand it to the subsidiary. Third-party work is won against competitors on somebody else's turbines. Captive is easy but tethered; third-party is hard-won but proves the company can survive on its own merits.

The captive channel is a real advantage. Inox Wind carries a ~3.1 GW turbine order book, and as those machines get commissioned, most arrive with an O&M contract that flows straight to Inox Green — a built-in, multi-year pipeline of new fleet requiring no sales effort. In a wind up-cycle, that captive feed alone can grow the serviced base steadily for years. The risk is symmetry: if the parent's commissioning slips (as Inox Wind's has, historically), the captive pipeline slips with it.

Why third-party is the tell

A captive O&M arm servicing only its parent's turbines is really just a cost centre wearing a listco's clothes — its "growth" is an accounting artefact of the parent's sales. What makes Inox Green interesting is the deliberate push beyond the parent: solar O&M, and above all the Wind World fleet of turbines Inox Wind never built. Every third-party megawatt is evidence that customers will pay Inox Green for competence, not just because they bought an Inox turbine. Watch the third-party share of the fleet: as it rises, Inox Green stops being an appendage of a turbine-maker and becomes a genuine renewable-services platform that would have value even if the parent stumbled. That transition — from captive dependant to independent consolidator — is what the equity story is really about.

The nuance to hold onto: because Inox Green is a listed subsidiary of Inox Wind, related-party transactions — the pricing of captive O&M contracts, receivables owed between parent and subsidiary — sit at the heart of its accounts. Minority shareholders of Inox Green should read those related-party disclosures carefully: the same group that controls the parent controls the fee the subsidiary charges it.
07

The post-IPO cleanup: debt and receivables

The unglamorous repair that had to happen before the annuity could shine.

When Inox Green listed in November 2022, it did not arrive as a pristine annuity machine. It carried the scars of its captive years — debt and a bloated receivables book, much of it owed by or entangled with a parent that was itself cash-starved at the time. A big reason for the IPO was simply to fix that: a large part of the ~₹740 Cr raised went to repaying borrowings. The cleanup is the least exciting part of the story and the one that had to come first.

First, treat the headline profit with care: a big slice of FY26's ₹103 Cr PAT is not the recurring O&M business earning its keep — it is balance-sheet income that will not repeat at the same scale. Second, the low core operating margin is precisely the setup the bull case needs: it is a business earning thin margins on a fleet it is under-charging, which is what makes the comprehensive-conversion upside credible rather than fanciful. A company already earning fat margins would have little room to re-rate; one earning ~8% core on 13 GWp has a long runway if it executes the mix shift.

Why the receivables cleanup is the permission slip

An O&M business should be a cash machine — fees collected steadily against modest costs. That only works if customers actually pay on time. Inox Green's early problem was the opposite: cash tied up in a large receivables book, much of it related to the group, throttling the very working capital it needed to serve and grow. Using IPO proceeds to repay debt and grinding the receivables down turns the annuity from a paper stream into real collected cash — the difference between a business that reports recurring revenue and one that can actually fund its expansion (and, eventually, integrate a 4.5 GW acquired fleet). It doesn't guarantee the margin re-rating, but it removes the reason the annuity was leaking before.

08

Why the renewable-O&M market keeps growing

The structural tailwind — and why it looks different from owning the assets.

Inox Green's addressable market has a lovely property: it grows every year India installs renewables, and it never shrinks, because a turbine put up today needs servicing for the next two decades regardless of what happens to new installations. The O&M pool is the running total of everything ever built and still spinning — a cumulative curve, not an annual one. Several forces are steepening it.

The insight that separates O&M from owning the asset

It is tempting to lump Inox Green in with the wind boom and the power producers riding it. But an O&M company has a fundamentally calmer, more defensive shape than a turbine-maker or an asset-owner. It carries no giant capex to build a plant, takes no merchant-power price risk, and does not live or die on winning a specific auction. It sells a service that the asset-owner cannot skip — you must maintain a ₹500-crore wind farm or watch it stop earning — for a fee set by contract, not by volatile power tariffs. That is why the market prizes O&M revenue: it is the part of the renewable value chain least exposed to the cycle that broke turbine-makers, and most like a subscription. The trade-off is that it is also lower-margin and slower-growing than the asset upside — steadier, but never explosive.

09

Rivals and the make-or-buy question

A fragmented service market where the biggest competitor is the customer itself.

Renewable O&M is not a tidy duopoly like turbine manufacturing. It is fragmented, and Inox Green's competition comes from three very different directions at once — rival turbine-makers' own service arms, independent O&M specialists, and, most importantly, the customer choosing to do it in-house.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, Inox Green's real edge is multi-brand scale: after Wind World it services turbines from several makers, backed by a spares network and a central monitoring room that a small regional maintainer or a single-owner in-house team cannot economically replicate. Scale in spares and diagnostics is the moat. Second, the perpetual competitor is the customer deciding to do it themselves — every contract is implicitly a make-or-buy pitch, and Inox Green wins only where it can credibly promise higher availability and lower total cost than an owner's own crew. That is why the ~96.5% availability figure is not a vanity metric: it is the proof-point that justifies outsourcing to Inox Green rather than staffing up.

At ~60× earnings on a profit that is partly non-operating, Inox Green is not priced as the small, thin-margin caretaker it is today; it is priced as the large, thick-margin annuity it hopes to become. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the share of the fleet on comprehensive contracts, read through its financial echo — EBITDA per MW under management. If comprehensive-mix climbs and EBITDA-per-MW rises quarter after quarter toward the guided 20–22% margin, the fleet is finally being monetised and the multiple is defensible. If the mix stays stuck and revenue keeps lagging the megawatt count, then the 13 GWp is an impressive number attached to a business that cannot charge for it — and a 60× stock re-rates on the shortfall. The fleet size is settled; whether Inox Green gets paid for it is the entire remaining question, and it prints in the margin line.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A sticky annuity with a real scale advantage — asked to prove it can charge full price.

Inox Green enters FY27 as the largest renewable-O&M platform in India: ~13 GWp under management, a captive pipeline from its parent, a transformational third-party fleet from Wind World, and blue-chip clients it never used to serve. What it has not yet proved is that this fleet can be charged full comprehensive rates and earn the fat, durable margin the valuation assumes. The debate is no longer about scale — it is about pricing power.

The bull case

  • Sticky annuity — 20+ year contracts on a 13 GWp fleet with high switching costs; revenue that compounds and rarely leaves.
  • Contract-mix runway — much of the book is thin legacy pricing; converting to comprehensive can roughly double the fee on the same machines.
  • Wind World scale & clients — ~4.5 GW of third-party fleet plus Greenko, Tata, ReNew and Hindustan Zinc relationships, bought at insolvency value.
  • Captive pipeline — parent Inox Wind's ~3.1 GW order book feeds new O&M contracts as turbines commission.
  • Structural, defensive market — a cumulative, cycle-resistant O&M pool that grows with every megawatt India ever installs.

The bear case

  • Thin core margin — FY26 operating margin ~8%; reported PAT flattered by non-operating income. The annuity isn't earning yet.
  • Mix-shift may be slow — legacy fixed-price contracts are sticky down too; re-pricing a captured fleet takes time and negotiating leverage.
  • Rich valuation — ~60× earnings on a partly non-operating profit prices in a near-flawless monetisation ramp.
  • Parent dependence & related parties — captive pipeline hostage to Inox Wind's commissioning; related-party pricing and receivables need scrutiny.
  • Integration & execution — absorbing a 4.5 GW distressed fleet, and staffing enough technicians for a growing multi-brand base, is real operational risk.
The bottom line

Inox Green has quietly assembled something scarce: the largest independent renewable-maintenance fleet in India, a business that gets paid whether the wind cycle is hot or cold, and that grew fastest by scavenging a bankrupt rival's turbines rather than waiting on its parent. That is a genuinely defensive asset in a chain full of cyclical ones. But it is not yet a well-paid one — its 13 GWp fleet still throws off pocket-money revenue because too much of it is serviced at yesterday's prices, and its headline profit leans on income that won't recur. The next stretch answers the whole question in one checkable place: whether EBITDA per megawatt climbs toward the guided 20–22% margin as the Wind World fleet integrates and legacy contracts roll into comprehensive ones. Watch the margin line each quarter as the comprehensive conversions land; it will tell you whether Inox Green is a great annuity finally being priced, or a giant caretaker that never learned to charge.
