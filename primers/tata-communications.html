00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Tata Communications is a global business-to-business (B2B) digital-infrastructure company. It does not sell mobile plans to consumers. Instead, it owns physical global plumbing — one of the world's largest networks of undersea (subsea) fibre-optic cables — and sells connectivity, cloud, security, collaboration, messaging and IoT services to large enterprises in 190+ countries. It is the former VSNL (Videsh Sanchar Nigam Limited), India's old state-owned international-telephone monopoly, privatised to the Tata Group in 2002.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It's an infrastructure + software company, not a telco you'd recognise. Its own claim is that its network carries ~30% of the world's internet routes at peak. Customers are ~300 of the Fortune 500 and thousands of global enterprises; it connects businesses to roughly 80% of the world's cloud providers. Think of it as the wholesale pipes and enterprise-IT layer behind the consumer internet, not the SIM in your phone.
  • A transformation is the whole story. The old, cash-cow international voice / wholesale business (routing phone calls between carriers) is in structural decline. Management is pivoting to higher-growth "Data & Digital Platforms." In FY26 (year ended Mar 2026), data revenue crossed ₹21,000 Cr and the digital portfolio (₹10,621 Cr) topped 50% of data revenue for the first time. The tension: this shift is real but slow, and the headline consolidated margin (~19–20%) is dragged down by the transition.
  • Growing revenue, but volatile profit. FY26 revenue rose 7.3% to ₹24,803 Cr, EBITDA rose to ₹4,822 Cr (19.4% margin) — but reported PAT fell ~36% to ₹1,044 Cr, largely because the prior year had one-off gains and this year carried higher costs and depreciation/finance charges. The stock still trades at a rich ~50× earnings, betting the digital pivot eventually re-rates margins and profit.
In plain terms

Picture the internet as a set of roads. Consumer telcos (Airtel, Jio) run the last mile to your house and phone. Tata Communications builds and owns the motorways and international bridges — the giant undersea cables between continents — and then also sells companies the toll-managed, secure, managed-service version of those roads: private global networks, cloud on-ramps, cyber-security, video and messaging platforms. Its old money came from carrying international phone calls in bulk; that business is shrinking as the world moved to WhatsApp and internet calling. So the company is racing to replace it with modern digital services — and the market is watching whether it can grow the new business faster than the old one shrinks.

01

What Tata Communications actually is (and isn't)

Kill the biggest misconception first — this is not a consumer mobile operator.

Because it carries the Tata name and the word "Communications," people assume Tata Communications is a mobile-phone company competing with Airtel and Jio. It is not. It has no consumer mobile business, no retail stores, no prepaid recharges. It is a wholesale-and-enterprise infrastructure provider — its customers are other businesses, carriers, banks, airlines, media companies and cloud giants, not individuals.

The four things it sells

  • Core connectivity (the network). Private global networks (MPLS, SD-WAN, Ethernet), internet backbone / IP transit, and international bandwidth — the ability for a multinational to link offices, factories and data centres worldwide over a secure, managed network. This rides on its own subsea and terrestrial fibre.
  • Digital platforms & collaboration. Cloud connectivity and hosting, cyber-security, unified collaboration, and — via the Kaleyra acquisition — CPaaS ("Communications-Platform-as-a-Service"): the plumbing behind the SMS/OTP/WhatsApp business messages you get from your bank or a delivery app. This is the fast-growing, higher-value layer.
  • Internet-of-Things (IoT) & mobility. Global connectivity for connected devices, fleets, vehicles and eSIMs — enterprises managing millions of SIM-connected sensors and machines across borders.
  • Legacy voice / wholesale. The old business: carrying international voice calls between telecom operators in bulk, plus some enterprise voice. Still generates cash, but structurally shrinking every year.
Why the B2B model matters

Selling to enterprises rather than consumers changes the whole shape of the business. There's no marketing war for retail subscribers, no spectrum auctions, no per-user churn battle. Instead, contracts are large, sticky and multi-year — a global bank that runs its worldwide network on Tata Communications does not switch vendors lightly, because the switching cost and risk are enormous. That stickiness is the moat. The flip side: growth depends on enterprise IT budgets and winning big deals, the sales cycle is long, and pricing on commodity bandwidth keeps falling as fibre gets cheaper — so the company must constantly move "up the stack" into higher-value managed services to defend margins.

02

The subsea backbone: owning the pipes under the ocean

The single most distinctive asset — a private, wholly-owned fibre ring around the planet.

The internet is not wireless. Around 99% of intercontinental data travels through fibre-optic cables lying on the sea floor. Tata Communications built the world's only wholly-owned subsea fibre ring encircling the globe — the Tata Global Network (TGN) — assembled largely from two landmark acquisitions in the mid-2000s: the Tyco Global Network and Teleglobe. This is the physical foundation of everything else it does.

Why owning cable matters

  • It's a scarce, capital-intensive asset. Laying a subsea cable across an ocean costs hundreds of millions of dollars and years of work — you can't just build one to compete next quarter. Owning the cable (rather than renting capacity) means Tata Communications controls its own cost base and can sell capacity to others.
  • The "~30% of internet routes" claim. The company states its network underpins roughly 30% of the world's internet routes at peak interconnectivity. Whether or not you take that figure literally, it captures the point: a very large share of global traffic touches its infrastructure — giving it reach and interconnection that few rivals match.
  • The AI tailwind. The explosion of AI, cloud and data-centre traffic means demand for long-haul bandwidth is surging. Tata Communications is investing in new AI-ready subsea routes (e.g. India–Singapore expansion), positioning the old "pipes" business as a beneficiary of the AI build-out — a rare growth angle for wholesale connectivity.
The catch with infrastructure

Owning oceans of cable is a double-edged sword. It gives Tata Communications a genuine, hard-to-replicate asset and a seat at the table of global internet plumbing. But raw bandwidth is a commodity: as fibre technology improves, the price per gigabit falls relentlessly, so simply "selling pipe" is a low-margin, price-deflating game. That's precisely why the company can't just be an infrastructure landlord — it has to bundle the pipe with managed services, security and software to earn a decent margin. The subsea network is the moat and the entry ticket; the profit increasingly has to come from the services layered on top.

03

Data vs Voice: two businesses moving in opposite directions

The core mechanic of the entire investment case, in one section.

Tata Communications reports its revenue in two big buckets, and they are heading in opposite directions. Data (the modern network + digital-platform business) is growing; Voice (the legacy international-call-carrying business) is declining. Understanding this "one up, one down" dynamic is the key to reading every quarterly result.

Why voice is dying — and why that's actually fine

The legacy voice business earns money by carrying international phone calls in bulk between telecom operators (wholesale "carrier voice"). That business is in terminal structural decline worldwide, because the world stopped making expensive international phone calls — it moved to WhatsApp, FaceTime, Zoom and internet-based calling, which travel as data, not billable voice minutes. So Tata Communications' voice revenue shrinks a few percent every year, and will keep doing so.

Crucially, management is relaxed about voice shrinking. Voice is low-margin and cash-generative but strategically dead; letting it run off while pouring investment into data is the deliberate plan. The problem is optical: because voice is shrinking, total reported revenue grows slower than the data business alone — the decline in one bucket masks the growth in the other.

The margin illusion to watch for

Here's the subtle trap in the numbers. Voice is a high-revenue, wafer-thin-margin business (you buy minutes and resell them for a sliver). Data — especially the digital portfolio — is lower-revenue-per-deal but higher-margin. As voice shrinks and data grows, you'd expect blended margins to rise. They have been slow to, because the company is simultaneously investing heavily (integrating acquisitions like Kaleyra, building new capabilities, higher depreciation and finance costs). So the reported consolidated EBITDA margin sits around 19–20% — below management's own 23–25% ambition. The bull case is that as the transition matures and investment normalises, margins mean-revert upward. The bear case is that they don't, because bandwidth deflation and integration costs keep eating the gains.

04

The digital portfolio: where the growth lives

The higher-margin, faster-growing engine management is betting the company on.

Inside the "Data" bucket sits the digital portfolio — the collection of modern, software-and-service offerings that are the strategic future. In FY26 it reached ₹10,621 Cr (up 16.7%) and, for the first time, made up more than half of all data revenue. Management wants it to reach $1 billion+ and dominate the mix. Here is what it actually contains.

The pieces, decoded

  • Next-gen connectivity — modern software-defined networking (SD-WAN, network-as-a-service) that lets enterprises run agile, cloud-first global networks. Higher value than raw pipe.
  • Cloud & security — helping enterprises connect to and secure their workloads across AWS, Azure and Google Cloud, plus managed cyber-security. A large, growing budget line for every big company.
  • Collaboration & CPaaS (the Kaleyra business) — the messaging plumbing behind bank OTPs, delivery notifications and marketing texts. Acquired via Kaleyra ($100m, Oct 2023); a high-growth, high-volume global business — though CPaaS is competitive and can be lower-margin than pure enterprise services.
  • Media services (the Switch business) — live video production and transmission for broadcasters and sport, added via The Switch Enterprises ($58.8m, May 2023). A niche, high-value vertical.
  • IoT & mobility — global connectivity for connected machines, vehicles and eSIM devices; a structural growth area as the world connects billions of devices.
Why the acquisitions matter: Kaleyra and Switch weren't random — they bought Tata Communications into the interaction layer (messaging, video) it couldn't build fast enough organically. The bet: bundle these platforms with the network underneath, cross-sell to the same global-enterprise customer base, and become a one-stop "CommTech" provider. The risk: integrating acquired companies is hard, CPaaS margins can be thin, and it added debt and goodwill to the balance sheet.
05

The company and who controls it

From state monopoly to Tata company — and how the government finally exited.

Tata Communications has an unusual pedigree: it began life as a government-owned monopoly and became a Tata Group company through one of India's landmark privatisations. That history — and the long overhang of the government's residual stake — shaped the stock for two decades until it was finally resolved in 2020–21.

Timeline

  • 1986
    VSNL (Videsh Sanchar Nigam Limited) incorporated as India's state-owned international telecom gateway — the monopoly on overseas calls and connectivity.
  • 1995
    Launches India's first public internet service (15 Aug) — the on-ramp for the country's internet age.
  • 2002
    Privatisation. The Tata Group, via Panatone Finvest, acquires management control from the Government of India — the seed of today's ownership.
  • 2004–06
    Acquires the Tyco Global Network and Teleglobe — assembling the wholly-owned global subsea fibre ring.
  • 2008
    Rebrands from VSNL to Tata Communications; repositions around enterprise and global network services.
  • 2016–17
    Divests majority (74%) of its data-centre business to ST Telemedia (STT GDC) — exiting a capital-heavy business it didn't want to fund alone (later sold its residual 26% too).
  • 2023
    Acquires The Switch (media/video, May) and Kaleyra (CPaaS messaging, Oct) — buying into the digital-interaction layer.
  • 2020–21
    The overhang ends. The Government of India sells down its residual ~26.12% stake — ~16% to the public via OFS, ~10% to the Tata promoters — fully exiting the company.
  • FY26
    Digital portfolio tops 50% of data revenue; long-time CEO A.S. Lakshminarayanan retires (Apr 2026); Ganesh Lakshminarayanan takes over as MD & CEO.

Ownership & management (as of early 2026)

  • Promoter (Tata Group): ~58.9%. Held mainly through Panatone Finvest (~44.8%) and Tata Sons (~14.1%). A firmly Tata-controlled company — the promoter parentage is a governance and credibility anchor.
  • Government of India: 0%. The GoI fully exited in FY21. This is important: for years, the market feared a government sell-down (a "SUUTI/BSNL-type overhang") pressuring the stock. That overhang is now gone — a structural positive, though the historical land-monetisation legacy (surplus VSNL land) is a separate, slow-burn value-unlock question.
  • Leadership transition: A.S. Lakshminarayanan, who led the digital pivot, retired in April 2026; Ganesh Lakshminarayanan (30+ years across ServiceNow, Airtel, Dell) became MD & CEO — signalling continuity of the enterprise/digital strategy.
The land legacy: VSNL historically sat on large parcels of surplus land. A separate entity (Panatone/HTIL structures historically, and a demerged "surplus land" arrangement) has governed monetisation of this real estate, with the government retaining an economic interest in some proceeds. It's a legacy asset that occasionally surfaces as an off-balance-sheet value question — worth knowing exists, but not central to the operating story.

The transformation bet: the ₹28,000 Cr & margin journey

The defining force for this specific company — a race against its own legacy.

Every part of the Tata Communications story reduces to one question: can it grow the new digital business fast enough, and profitably enough, to transform the whole company before the market loses patience? Management laid out an explicit medium-term ambition; the scorecard so far is "growing, but behind the original schedule."

Revenue is closing on the ambition; the margin gap is the real story — FY26's 19.4% sits well below the 23–25% target the company set for itself. The digital revenue crossing 50% of data is the leading indicator that the mix is finally shifting.

The strategic logic is sound. Wholesale bandwidth and legacy voice are deflating, low-margin commodities. The only durable way to lift returns is to sell higher-value managed services and software platforms — cloud, security, CPaaS, IoT — to the same trusted global-enterprise base. Each rupee that shifts from "voice minute" to "managed security contract" is a rupee at a structurally higher margin. That's the entire thesis.

Why the margin has lagged the ambition

Three forces have held the reported margin below target. (1) Investment drag: integrating Kaleyra and Switch, building new capabilities and sales, plus higher depreciation on capex, all hit the P&L before the revenue matures. (2) Mix within digital: some of the fastest-growing pieces (CPaaS messaging) are high-volume but relatively low-margin, so growth doesn't lift the blend as much as hoped. (3) Bandwidth deflation: the core connectivity business keeps seeing price erosion. The bull believes these are transitional and margins mean-revert to 23–25%; the bear believes structural deflation and CPaaS mix cap the margin nearer 20%. FY26's PAT falling ~36% (partly one-offs in the base) is exactly the kind of volatility this transition produces — and why the market fixates on margin trajectory, not just revenue growth.

The revenue is arriving; the profit is the argument. Watch the EBITDA margin and the digital-mix percentage each quarter — those two numbers decide whether the transformation is working.
07

The balance sheet, capex & cash

A capital-intensive business that already shed its heaviest asset — and its financial discipline.

Owning subsea cables and running global networks is capital-intensive, and acquisitions added debt. So the balance sheet — net debt, capex discipline, and free-cash-flow generation — is a central part of the story, not a footnote. The good news: the company has become notably more disciplined, and it already offloaded its most capital-hungry business (data centres) years ago.

  • Net debt ~₹9,600 Cr; net-debt/EBITDA ~1.99×. The company set a target of keeping leverage at or below 2× — and FY26 sits right at that line. Manageable, but this is a leveraged balance sheet, not a net-cash one; finance costs are a real drag on PAT.
  • Capex is deliberately controlled at ~9–10% of sales (₹2,433 Cr in FY26). Enough to keep investing in subsea/AI-ready routes and platforms, but disciplined enough to keep generating cash — a change from the more open-ended spending of the past.
  • It already sold the data-centre business. In 2016–17 it sold ~74% (and later the rest) of its data-centre operations to ST Telemedia (STT GDC). This was strategically important: data centres are enormously capital-hungry, and exiting let Tata Communications stay asset-lighter and focus capital on the network and digital platforms. (Ironically, data centres later became a red-hot AI asset class — a "one that got away," but the discipline was rational at the time.)
  • ROCE ~14.6% (FY26); ROE ~32.6%. The high ROE partly reflects the leveraged, relatively thin equity base (book value ~₹121/share). Management has flagged improving return on capital as a core objective; ROCE in the mid-teens is decent but below the 25%+ it once aspired to.
Why leverage + acquisitions is the balance-sheet tension

The growth strategy is partly acquisition-fuelled (Kaleyra, Switch) — and acquisitions consumed cash and added debt and goodwill. At the same time, the business must keep investing in physical infrastructure (new cables, network gear) that depreciates. So the financial balancing act is real: fund growth and M&A while keeping net-debt/EBITDA ≤2× and generating free cash flow. FY26 shows this working — record-ish free cash flow, leverage held at ~2×, disciplined capex. The risk is that a big future acquisition, a capex surge for AI infrastructure, or a margin stumble could stretch the balance sheet and pressure the dividend or the credit profile.

08

The industry and the rivals

A global B2B connectivity market — competing with carriers, cloud giants and Indian enterprise players.

Tata Communications competes in the global enterprise / wholesale connectivity and digital-services market — a very different arena from Indian consumer telecom. Its rivals are international carriers, hyperscale cloud providers moving into networking, and Indian enterprise-focused players. It's a big, fragmenting market where the winners move up the value stack faster than commoditisation drags them down.

The competitive tension to watch

Two forces define the battlefield. First, the hyperscaler paradox: AWS, Azure and Google are simultaneously Tata Communications' biggest partners (enterprises need to reach them, and Tata provides the on-ramp) and its biggest long-term threat (their own private global networks and direct interconnect can disintermediate traditional carriers). Second, the up-the-stack race: everyone in wholesale connectivity is fleeing the commoditising "sell pipe" business toward higher-margin managed services, security and platforms. Tata Communications' edge is its owned global infrastructure + trusted enterprise relationships + Tata brand, especially on India-and-emerging-market corridors. Its challenge is that well-capitalised US and European rivals — and the cloud giants — are chasing the exact same digital-services prize.

The structural backdrop: global enterprise IT is shifting to cloud, hybrid work and AI, which drives demand for secure global connectivity, cloud interconnect and cyber-security — a genuine tailwind. But it also compresses commodity bandwidth pricing and invites cloud-native competition. Tata Communications is positioned as a "CommTech" enabler of that shift; whether it captures the value or gets disintermediated is the industry-level version of its whole investment debate.

How to think about the valuation

At ~₹54,000 Cr market cap on ~₹1,044 Cr of FY26 profit, the stock trades near 50× trailing earnings — an expensive multiple for a business with mid-teens ROCE, ~2× leverage and a margin below its own target. That price makes sense only if you believe the transformation delivers: digital revenue compounding, the mix crossing decisively past 50%, and margins mean-reverting toward 23–25% so that profit grows much faster than revenue from here. Bulls point to a scarce global-infrastructure asset, a Tata-backed balance sheet, an ended government overhang, and a genuine AI-bandwidth tailwind. Bears point to the margin miss, the ~36% PAT drop, bandwidth deflation, CPaaS mix, and a multiple that leaves no room for a stumble. The dividend (₹17.50/share for FY26, ~0.9% yield) is a modest sweetener, not the reason to own it.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A unique global asset in mid-transformation — priced for the pivot to work.

Tata Communications is a genuinely differentiated business: a Tata-controlled owner of global internet infrastructure, mid-way through a credible pivot from dying voice to growing digital services, with the old government overhang finally gone. The investment debate is about margins, execution and valuation — not whether the underlying assets and demand are real.

The bull case

  • Scarce global asset — a wholly-owned subsea fibre ring carrying a huge share of world internet traffic; hard to replicate.
  • Digital mix inflecting — digital portfolio (₹10,621 Cr) now >50% of data revenue and growing ~17%; margin normalisation ahead.
  • AI tailwind — surging bandwidth demand turns legacy pipes into a growth asset; new AI-ready routes.
  • Overhang gone — the GoI fully exited in FY21; Tata promoters at ~59% provide a stable, credible anchor.
  • Sticky B2B base — ~300 Fortune-500 customers, long contracts, cross-sell runway; disciplined capex and FCF.

The bear case

  • Margin miss — 19.4% EBITDA vs a 23–25% ambition; the pivot hasn't yet delivered the promised profitability.
  • Volatile profit — FY26 PAT fell ~36%; earnings are lumpy and sensitive to one-offs, costs and finance charges.
  • Rich valuation — ~50× earnings on mid-teens ROCE and ~2× leverage leaves no room for error.
  • Commoditisation & disintermediation — bandwidth price deflation; hyperscalers can bypass traditional carriers; CPaaS margins thin.
  • Leverage & integration risk — acquisition-led growth added debt and goodwill; a further M&A or capex surge could stretch the balance sheet.
The bottom line

Tata Communications is a rare, real global-infrastructure asset attached to a company in mid-transformation. The strategic direction — run off dying voice, scale higher-margin digital platforms, ride the AI-bandwidth wave — is sound, and the leading indicators (digital past 50% of data, revenue closing on the ₹28,000 Cr ambition, leverage held at ~2×) are moving the right way. The unresolved question is profitability: FY26's 19.4% margin and ~36% PAT drop show the pivot has not yet translated into the profit the ~50× multiple is paying for. The two numbers that will settle the debate over the next few years are the EBITDA margin (is it climbing toward 23–25%?) and the digital-mix percentage (does digital keep taking share of data?). Get those right, and a unique asset re-rates on rising profit; stumble, and an expensive stock on a leveraged, transitioning business has a long way to fall.
