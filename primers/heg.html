00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

HEG Limited makes graphite electrodes — enormous, precisely machined rods of synthetic graphite, some over two metres long and up to about 800 mm across, that carry a colossal electric current into a furnace full of scrap steel and melt it. They are not machinery. They are a consumable: the electrode is progressively eaten away in the heat and has to be replaced, so a steel mill buys them again and again, forever, in proportion to how much steel it actually makes. HEG is one of only two significant Indian producers, and it manufactures essentially all of that output at a single integrated site at Mandideep, near Bhopal in Madhya Pradesh — a plant of roughly 100,000 tonnes a year that ranks among the largest single-location graphite electrode facilities anywhere outside China.

Four facts frame the whole company:

  • It is a pure-play, and it is concentrated. HEG is not a diversified engineering group with a graphite division. Graphite electrodes are close to the entirety of its operating revenue, made in one place, using one imported raw material (needle coke), sold largely into export markets — roughly the Middle East alone accounted for about 20% of FY26 sales. There is no second product line to cushion a bad electrode year, and no second factory to pick up the load if the first one stops.
  • FY26 was a solid, unspectacular recovery year — with a strange-looking Q4. Standalone net sales rose to ₹2,551.77 Cr from ₹2,137.34 Cr, EBITDA improved to ₹497 Cr (19% margin) from ₹388 Cr (17%), and profit before tax rose about 66%. Volumes grew roughly 20% on 90%-plus capacity utilisation. Yet the fourth quarter printed a reported loss of about ₹163 Cr — driven not by the furnace but by unrealised marks on foreign investments and rupee depreciation, alongside Middle East logistics disruption.
  • The balance sheet is a fortress by commodity standards. HEG closed FY26 with no long-term debt and a treasury balance of roughly ₹792 Cr. In a business whose earnings can swing several hundred percent between good and bad years, ending the cycle with cash rather than covenants is the difference between surviving a downturn and being restructured by one.
  • It is splitting in two. Under a composite scheme of arrangement, the graphite electrode business stays in HEG Limited, while the energy-transition assets — the graphite anode venture (through subsidiary TACC, The Advanced Carbons Company), hydropower interests and battery energy storage — move into a separately listed HEG Greentech. Buying the share today buys both a cash-generative cyclical and an unproven battery-materials start-up; the scheme is designed so that, in time, you can own them separately.
In plain terms

Every time a modern steel mill melts a pile of scrap, it does so by striking an electric arc — lightning, essentially — between three giant carbon rods and the metal below. Those rods burn away. HEG makes them. That is the whole of today's business: a consumable sold by the tonne, priced in dollars, made from an imported feedstock, in one factory, for steelmakers scattered across dozens of countries. What makes the company more interesting than a commodity converter is what it is trying to become. The same discipline that produces high-purity synthetic graphite for a furnace is, chemically, close cousin to the discipline that produces graphite anode powder for a lithium-ion battery — the black material that makes up the negative electrode of almost every EV cell on earth, and a market China supplies almost entirely. HEG has committed real capital to attempting that crossing. Whether it lands is the defining question about this share.

01

Graphite electrodes 101: the rod that eats itself

No metallurgy degree needed — five ideas and you can follow the whole business.

There are two broad ways to make steel. The old way starts from iron ore and coking coal in a blast furnace. The newer, lighter-footprint way melts scrap steel (and increasingly direct-reduced iron) using electricity in an electric arc furnace, or EAF. An EAF works by lowering three columns of graphite into a bath of scrap and passing a huge current through them; the arc that jumps between electrode tip and metal reaches thousands of degrees and melts everything in seconds. The graphite columns are the only material that can survive being both an extreme electrical conductor and a heat-resistant structure in that environment — and even graphite does not survive intact. It is consumed.

The five terms that explain everything
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for HEG)
EAFElectric arc furnace — a steel furnace that melts scrap with electricity rather than smelting ore with coke. Every EAF in the world consumes graphite electrodes. HEG's customer base is the global EAF fleet.
UHP grade"Ultra-high power" — the premium electrode grade able to carry the highest current densities, needed by large modern furnaces. It commands better pricing and is technically harder to make consistently. HEG's mix is weighted towards UHP, which is a meaningful part of why it can play in demanding export markets.
Needle cokeThe feedstock: a specialised, highly crystalline petroleum (or coal-tar) coke, made by only a handful of producers globally. India has none of consequence, so HEG imports it. Its price and availability are the single largest swing factor in HEG's cost sheet, and it is quoted in dollars.
GraphitisationThe step that turns baked carbon into true graphite by heating it to roughly 3,000°C — hotter than the surface of many stars' outer layers, and staggeringly power-hungry. This is why an electrode plant needs cheap, reliable electricity, and why HEG runs captive generation.
Specific consumptionKilograms of electrode consumed per tonne of steel produced — typically a small number (low single-digit kg/t on modern furnaces) and steadily falling as furnaces get more efficient. A quiet, permanent headwind on volume growth that steel-output growth has to outrun.
The one distinction that changes how you read the industry

Electrodes are tied to steel output, not steel capex. That is a much better place to sit than most industrial suppliers occupy. A firm that sells furnaces gets paid once, when a mill is built, and then waits years for the next order; a firm that sells electrodes gets paid every month that furnace runs, because the rods burn away and must be replaced. Demand therefore tracks tonnes of EAF steel actually melted — a running, repeating flow rather than a lumpy investment cycle. The catch is that this stable volume is sold at a violently unstable price. Steel mills will keep buying electrodes in a downturn because they must; what they will not do is pay a premium for them. So HEG's volumes are relatively defensive and its realisation is anything but.

Why the world is slowly moving HEG's way

Blast-furnace steelmaking is one of the most carbon-intensive industrial processes in existence. Melting scrap in an electric arc furnace, by contrast, emits a fraction of the carbon per tonne — and can be run on renewable power. Every serious decarbonisation pathway for global steel therefore involves a shift in the mix toward EAF and away from integrated blast-furnace routes, particularly in Europe and increasingly in India, where scrap availability and power supply are both improving. More EAF steel means more electrodes consumed. That is the structural, multi-decade argument beneath a business that otherwise looks like a pure commodity play, and it is the argument HEG's management leans on when explaining why it is expanding capacity into a soft market.

It is a real argument, but a slow one. Furnace conversions take years, scrap markets have to deepen, and in the meantime a wave of Chinese electrode capacity has pressed on global prices. Structural demand growth and cyclical price weakness can coexist for a long time, and for the past few years they have.

02

Inside Mandideep: how a graphite electrode is actually made

A six-month manufacturing cycle, 3,000°C, and why the working capital looks the way it does.

Most people assume a graphite rod is machined out of a block of something. It is closer to baking — a slow, staged, thermally brutal bake that takes months from raw coke to a finished, threaded electrode ready to ship. Understanding the sequence explains three things at once: why the plant is capital-heavy, why HEG carries so much inventory, and why cheap power is not a nice-to-have but the business model.

Why cheap power is the moat nobody photographs

Graphitisation is one of the most electricity-intensive steps in any manufacturing industry. Holding a furnace of carbon at around 3,000°C for days consumes power on a scale that would make a normal factory's tariff bill unrecognisable. HEG runs captive generation at and around Mandideep — thermal capacity supplemented by a small hydel unit, in the order of 75–80 MW in total (approx.) — precisely so that the most power-hungry step in the chain is not exposed to grid tariffs or grid reliability. In a business where the difference between a cost leader and a marginal producer is a few thousand rupees per tonne, owning your own electrons is a durable structural advantage, and it is a large part of why HEG describes itself as a low-cost producer globally.

The number that reframes the balance sheet

A six-month production cycle means HEG must buy dollar-priced needle coke roughly two quarters before it can invoice the resulting electrode. That single fact explains a great deal about the company's reported numbers. It means inventory is structurally enormous relative to sales. It means a sharp fall in electrode prices can strand expensive coke inside half-finished rods, compressing margins for two or three quarters after the market has already turned. It also means the reverse: when prices rise, cheap coke bought months earlier flows into product sold at new prices, and margins expand faster than anyone expects. HEG's reported margin in any given quarter is therefore partly a story about the electrode market today and partly a story about what coke cost last winter.

Capacity, today and planned

  • Today: about 100,000 tonnes per annum of graphite electrode capacity at Mandideep, expanded in stages over the past decade from a substantially smaller base.
  • Under way: a further 15,000 tonnes of brownfield expansion targeted for completion by early 2028, taking capacity to roughly 115,000 tonnes. Brownfield matters — adding a line inside an existing site is far cheaper per tonne than building a new plant, and it reuses the same power, utilities and labour base.
  • Utilisation: above 90% across FY26, and around 95% in the fourth quarter. For a commodity producer, running near-full while the industry complains about oversupply is a genuine competitive statement — it means HEG's product is being chosen even in a buyer's market.

03

One site, one company: the concentration math

The structural fact that most differentiates HEG from every other way to own graphite.

Industrial companies usually spread themselves. A cement maker has a dozen plants; an auto-component supplier has factories near each customer; even a mid-sized chemicals firm typically runs two or three sites so that a fire, a flood, a strike or a pollution-board order in one location is an inconvenience rather than an existential event. HEG does not work that way. Very nearly all of its electrode production sits at Mandideep, in one integrated complex, with its own power. That choice is simultaneously the source of its cost advantage and its single largest uninsurable exposure.

Why one site is an advantage

  • Scale economics compound in one place. A hundred thousand tonnes flowing through one set of bake furnaces, one graphitisation block and one machine shop spreads fixed overhead across every tonne. Two 50,000-tonne plants would need two of everything and two management layers.
  • Captive power only works at scale. Building 75–80 MW of your own generation is justifiable when one site consumes it. Replicate the plant and you replicate the power station, or you give up the tariff advantage.
  • Process consistency. UHP electrodes are unforgiving; consistency comes from a stable, tuned, long-lived process line and the accumulated know-how of people who have run it for decades. One site concentrates that institutional memory rather than diluting it.
  • Logistics simplicity. One inbound stream of imported needle coke, one set of export routings. Madhya Pradesh is landlocked, which adds inland freight, but a single flow is simpler to optimise than several.
The device that fits HEG and almost nothing else

Run the concentration arithmetic and it is stark. One location produces close to 100% of the electrode tonnage that generates close to 100% of HEG's operating revenue, which in FY26 was about ₹2,552 Cr. Divide that by the roughly 250 working days in a year and every single day that Mandideep does not run costs the company on the order of ₹10 Cr of sales — and, because the production cycle runs about six months, a stoppage does not merely defer that day's output; it punches a hole in the delivery schedule two quarters later, when customers have already been promised rods. Most diversified industrials can absorb the loss of a plant. HEG cannot lose Mandideep for any meaningful period without the year being lost with it. No amount of net cash on the balance sheet substitutes for the furnaces being lit.

04

The company, the group, and who controls it

An LNJ Bhilwara business, run by the Jhunjhunwala family, now restructuring itself.

HEG belongs to the LNJ Bhilwara Group, an old-line Indian industrial house with interests spanning textiles, power generation and graphite. The group is chaired by Ravi Jhunjhunwala (no relation to the late investor Rakesh Jhunjhunwala — a confusion worth clearing up early), with the next generation active in management. The graphite business dates back to the early 1970s, with the Mandideep plant on stream from the latter part of that decade (approx.), which makes HEG one of the older continuously operating specialist carbon producers outside the Western majors.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Jhunjhunwala family and LNJ Bhilwara group entities hold roughly 56% (approx.) of the equity — a comfortable control block, well above the level at which a promoter's interests and minority interests start to diverge on questions like capital allocation into a new venture.
  • What sits where: HEG Limited is the operating electrode company at Mandideep. TACC (The Advanced Carbons Company) is the anode-materials subsidiary building the battery business. The group also holds interests in hydropower generation through its Bhilwara energy lineage, and has flagged battery energy storage as an adjacency. Post-scheme, the second and third of these are intended to sit under HEG Greentech.
  • Balance sheet: no long-term debt at 31 March 2026 and a treasury balance of about ₹792 Cr. That treasury is not idle cash in a current account — a chunk sits in investments, which is precisely why the fourth quarter's mark-to-market movements were large enough to flip a profitable quarter into a reported loss.
Governance point worth checking yourself: when a debt-free cash generator funds a capital-hungry, technically unproven venture in an adjacent industry, minority shareholders are effectively being asked to underwrite a start-up out of the cash flows of the mature business. The demerger is the structural answer to that objection — it lets the market price the electrode cash flows and the battery ambition separately, and lets each raise its own capital. Read the scheme's share entitlement ratio and the asset allocation between the two entities carefully when they are notified; that document, not the press release, determines what you actually end up owning.
05

The customer: EAF steel, and why output beats capex

Who actually buys HEG's rods, where they are, and what makes them buy more.

HEG does not sell to India alone; it sells to the global fleet of electric arc furnaces, across a long list of countries. Roughly a fifth of FY26 sales went to the Middle East, a region that has built substantial EAF and direct-reduced-iron steel capacity on the back of cheap gas and power. The rest is spread across Europe, the Americas, Southeast Asia and the domestic Indian market. That geographic spread is a genuine strength — no single steel market's recession takes the company down — but it also means HEG's revenue is exposed to freight routes, sanctions, tariffs and shipping-lane disruption in a way a purely domestic supplier would not be.

The subtlety in "consumable"

Being a consumable supplier sounds unambiguously good — recurring revenue, no reliance on a customer's capital-spending mood. It is good, but it comes with a hidden erosion. The better HEG's electrodes get, the fewer tonnes of them a steel mill needs per tonne of steel. Specific consumption has fallen steadily for decades as furnace practice improved and electrode quality rose, which means the industry's own technical progress quietly shrinks its own market. For volumes to grow, the growth in EAF steel output has to comfortably outrun the decline in kilograms consumed per tonne. Over the long run it has, because the shift to electric steelmaking is powerful. But in any given flat year for steel, an electrode maker can find its tonnage going backwards for no reason other than that its customers got better at using the product.

Why the Middle East mattered in FY26

Concentration of about 20% of sales in one region is normally a footnote. In FY26 it was not: logistics disruption in the Middle East was explicitly cited as a drag on fourth-quarter operating profit. When shipping routes through the region are disrupted, an exporter of heavy, bulky, low-value-density goods from a landlocked Indian state feels it twice — in freight cost and in delivery timing. Deliveries slip out of a quarter; the cost of getting them there rises; and a customer waiting on rods has furnaces to run. It is a reminder that for HEG the distance between the factory gate and the customer's furnace is itself a business risk, not merely a line item.

06

The cycle, kept short

What you need to know about electrode prices — and why it is not the whole story here.

Graphite electrodes are a textbook boom-bust commodity, and the 2017–18 episode is the reference point everyone in the industry still measures against. Environmental shutdowns in China removed both electrode and needle-coke capacity at the same moment that EAF steel demand surged; prices multiplied several times over, and producers earned in a year what they had previously earned in a decade. Then supply came back, Chinese capacity expanded, and the price collapsed. Anyone extrapolating 2018 into perpetuity was destroyed.

The mechanism is simple and worth internalising once. HEG's profit per tonne is broadly the realisation minus the delivered needle-coke cost minus conversion cost, and because conversion cost is largely fixed, the operating leverage on that spread is extreme. A modest movement in realisation per tonne, applied to roughly 100,000 tonnes, is a very large number relative to a profit base of a few hundred crore. That is why the same plant, running at the same utilisation, with the same people, can produce wildly different annual profits.

Why this primer does not dwell on the spread

The spread-and-utilisation cycle is the standard lens for a graphite electrode producer, and it applies to HEG as much as to anyone. But treating it as the whole thesis would miss what is actually different here. HEG has already run its cyclical asset to 90%-plus utilisation in a weak market, already carries no long-term debt, and has already decided that the answer to a mature, China-pressured commodity is not to bid harder for the next tonne of electrode demand — it is to take the same material science somewhere else entirely. The cycle sets the floor and the funding. The anode venture sets the ceiling. An analysis that only models realisation per tonne is modelling the half of the company that is being deliberately de-emphasised by its own board.

The Chinese overhang, stated plainly: China has built very large graphite electrode capacity, and when its own steel demand softens, that capacity exports. The result over recent years has been persistent global price pressure, which is why HEG's 19% EBITDA margin — respectable in absolute terms — sits so far below what the same asset produced in 2018. Nothing about HEG's cost position or product quality changes this; a low-cost producer in an oversupplied market still faces an oversupplied market. Any forecast that assumes electrode realisations normalise upward is, at bottom, a forecast about Chinese capacity discipline.
07

The same molecule, two destinies

The crux of the investment case: from graphite that burns up melting steel to graphite that sits inside an EV battery.

Here is the fact that reframes HEG. The material HEG has spent fifty years learning to make — high-purity synthetic graphite — has two completely different industrial destinies. In destiny one, it is machined into a rod, lowered into a furnace, and consumed: burned away in minutes of arc, gone forever, sold by the tonne into a mature, Chinese-pressured commodity market. In destiny two, the same synthetic graphite is milled and processed into a fine black powder, coated onto copper foil, and becomes the anode — the negative electrode — of a lithium-ion battery cell, where it does not burn at all but shuttles lithium ions back and forth for a decade inside an electric car. Same molecule. Same feedstock family. Radically different customer, different growth rate, different price per tonne, and a market that today is supplied overwhelmingly by China.

HEG's core competence is not "electrodes". It is making synthetic graphite to specification at industrial scale. The question is whether that competence transfers from a furnace to a battery.

What the anode business actually is

Every lithium-ion cell has two electrodes. The cathode gets the attention — it is where the lithium, nickel, cobalt and manganese live, and where most of the cost sits. The anode is the quieter half, and in the overwhelming majority of cells produced today it is made of graphite, either natural graphite that has been purified and spheronised, or synthetic graphite made by graphitising petroleum coke at extreme temperature. Synthetic anode graphite is preferred for fast-charging and long-cycle-life applications because it is more consistent and more structurally stable. And it is made by a process that any graphite electrode producer would recognise: coke in, extreme heat, graphitisation, precise particle engineering out.

That is the strategic insight behind HEG's venture. The graphitisation furnaces, the coke handling, the high-temperature engineering, the quality systems for purity — these are the capabilities HEG already runs at 100,000-tonne scale. Building an anode plant is not diversifying into an unrelated industry; it is applying an existing core process to a new specification and a new customer. The distance is real, but it is far shorter than the distance a random industrial company would have to travel.

TACC: what has actually been committed

  • The vehicle: TACC — The Advanced Carbons Company — an HEG subsidiary formed specifically to build the graphite anode business, so that its capital, losses and eventual value are ring-fenced from the electrode P&L.
  • The money: the commitment has been announced in stages and reported at different figures as the plan scaled — an initial programme around ₹900–1,000 Cr over roughly three years, with later reported figures for the fuller build ranging up toward ₹1,850–2,000 Cr (approx.). Treat the exact number as fluid and verify it against the latest company disclosure; the direction of travel is unambiguous, which is that this is a capital commitment of the same order of magnitude as HEG's entire annual revenue.
  • The site: a greenfield anode facility in Madhya Pradesh, with ground formally broken. Building near the existing graphite ecosystem is deliberate — shared engineering talent, shared high-temperature know-how, shared industrial infrastructure.
  • The scaffolding: supporting arrangements have been signed as the project has progressed, including an industrial-gas supply tie-up (nitrogen, essential for inert-atmosphere processing) with INOX Air Products, and a research collaboration with the National University of Singapore's institute for functional intelligent materials aimed at next-generation materials. These are the unglamorous, necessary pieces of a real project rather than a press release.
The genuine surprise

China does not merely lead the battery anode market — it holds something close to a stranglehold on it, supplying the overwhelming majority of the world's graphite anode material and, at points, restricting the export of graphite technology and product outright. Every Western and Indian cell maker that wants to reduce that dependence needs a non-Chinese source of anode graphite, and there are startlingly few credible candidates outside China. Which means an old Bhopal-adjacent electrode maker, best known for a commodity that gets vaporised inside steel furnaces, is one of a very small number of companies on earth with the graphitisation scale, the process knowledge and the balance sheet to plausibly attempt it. That is an unlikely place to find a strategically significant asset — and it is the single most interesting thing about this company.

Why this is hard — the qualification wall

None of the above should be read as a prediction of success. Battery materials are not sold the way electrodes are sold. A steel mill will trial a new electrode supplier within a campaign or two. A cell maker will not: anode material determines a cell's energy density, fast-charge behaviour, cycle life and safety, and swapping it requires re-qualifying the entire cell design. The process runs through sample supply, characterisation, coin-cell and pouch-cell testing, pilot-line trials, automotive validation and then, only then, a commercial contract. It routinely takes two to four years per customer, and it can fail at any stage for reasons the supplier cannot fully control — particle morphology, tap density, first-cycle efficiency, impurity levels measured in parts per million.

Meanwhile the competitive field is not passive. Chinese incumbents such as BTR, Shanshan and Putailai operate at enormous scale with years of qualified history and a cost base built on cheap power. Korea's Posco Future M and a scatter of Western hopefuls are chasing the same non-Chinese demand pool. If Chinese anode prices stay low — and there is no reason to assume they will not — a new entrant must win on supply-chain security and qualification rather than on price. That is a real basis for competing, but it is a slower and less certain one.

Do not underwrite this as if it were done. Ground broken is not a plant commissioned. A plant commissioned is not a qualified sample. A qualified sample is not an offtake contract. An offtake contract is not a profitable tonne. The correct posture toward TACC is to treat it as a genuine, well-motivated, adequately funded attempt with a wide range of outcomes including failure, and to watch the specific milestones — commissioning date, first customer sampling, first qualification win, first commercial supply — rather than the capital-expenditure announcements, which prove only that money is being spent.
08

The split into two companies

A composite scheme that turns one share into a cyclical and a venture — deliberately.

In 2026 HEG announced a composite scheme of arrangement to separate its two halves into two separately listed entities. HEG Limited continues as the graphite electrode business — the Mandideep plant, the export franchise, the captive power, the cash flows. HEG Greentech takes the energy-transition portfolio: the anode-materials venture, hydropower interests and battery energy storage. The stated rationale is clearer visibility into each business and simpler capital raising for each.

That rationale is more substantive than the usual demerger boilerplate, because these two businesses are close to opposites in every dimension an investor cares about. One is mature, cash-generative, cyclical and valued on mid-cycle earnings. The other is pre-revenue, capital-consuming, growth-optional and valued on an assessment of probability. Housed together, each distorts the other: the venture's losses depress the cyclical's earnings, and the cyclical's earnings hide the venture's cash burn. Neither set of natural shareholders gets a clean instrument.

What the demerger does to your analysis

Before the scheme, valuing HEG required blending two incompatible frameworks into one multiple and hoping the average meant something. After it, the arithmetic becomes tractable: the electrode entity can be valued on mid-cycle earnings power from a defined 115,000-tonne asset base, and the greentech entity on the capital deployed and the probability-weighted outcome of a qualification process that has not yet happened. If you are researching this share today, the practical implication is that you should already be forming two separate views — because at some point you may find yourself holding two separate securities, with different risks, different volatility and, quite possibly, different reasons to sell.

The mechanics deserve care. The share entitlement ratio, the allocation of the treasury balance between the two entities, where the debt (if any) lands, and the treatment of TACC's committed but unspent capex all determine what each new share is actually worth. Those details come out in the scheme document and the regulatory and creditor approvals that follow, not in the announcement. Until then, the split is best understood as a statement of intent — that HEG's board wants the market to stop pricing a battery ambition as a rounding error inside a commodity multiple.

09

The industry and the rivals

A concentrated global electrode oligopoly, and an entirely different fight in anodes.

Graphite electrode manufacturing is a small club. The barriers are not obvious ones like patents — they are the six-month process cycle, the enormous power requirement, the scarce needle-coke supply relationships, and the fact that a steel mill will not risk a furnace campaign on an unproven rod. Outside China, a handful of names supply the world. HEG competes globally against all of them, and domestically against exactly one peer.

The anode field is a different sport

  • Chinese incumbents — BTR New Material, Shanshan, Putailai and peers dominate global anode supply at scale, with qualified positions inside the biggest cell makers and a cost base built on inexpensive power. They are not the competition HEG knows.
  • Korean and Japanese materials groups — Posco Future M and others supplying their domestic cell champions, with the advantage of being embedded in a national battery ecosystem.
  • Western aspirants — a set of synthetic and natural graphite hopefuls in North America, Europe and Australia chasing the same "non-Chinese anode" demand, many of them earlier-stage and less well capitalised than HEG, several of them dependent on government support.
  • The Indian angle — India's own cell manufacturing build-out, encouraged by production-linked incentives, creates a domestic anode customer base that did not exist five years ago. A local, qualified anode supplier would be strategically valuable to those cell plants. Whether that translates into orders depends entirely on qualification, not geography.
The competitive shape to hold in mind

In electrodes, HEG is a good operator in a difficult market — low on the cost curve, running near full, but a price-taker in a world where Chinese capacity sets the marginal price and its customers' technical progress slowly shrinks the tonnage they need. Nothing HEG does at Mandideep changes that structure; it can only be efficient within it. In anodes, the position is reversed: the market is growing rapidly, the customers are desperate for non-Chinese supply, and the scarce thing is not demand but a qualified supplier. HEG is attempting to move from a market where it is excellent but constrained into one where it would be a newcomer but unconstrained. That is a coherent strategy. It is also, unavoidably, a leap.

Financials & valuation

A real operating recovery, an optical fourth-quarter loss, and a multiple that is partly about a factory that does not yet exist.

FY26 was a better year than the headline suggests, and the reason is worth unpacking because it recurs. Operationally, the electrode business improved on every measure that matters: volumes up about 20%, utilisation above 90%, EBITDA up from ₹388 Cr to ₹497 Cr, margin up from 17% to 19%, profit before tax up around 66% to roughly ₹246 Cr. Then the fourth quarter reported a loss of about ₹163 Cr.

The reporting quirk you must adjust for

HEG holds a large treasury, and part of it sits in investments whose value moves with markets and with the rupee. That is a reasonable thing for a debt-free cyclical to do with surplus cash — but it means the reported profit line contains a component that has nothing whatsoever to do with how many tonnes of electrode were sold or at what price. In a quarter when those marks move against the company, a perfectly decent operating performance can print as a loss, and the headline will say so. When you assess HEG, separate the two explicitly: judge the business on EBITDA and realisation per tonne, and treat the investment marks as a separate, volatile, largely unrealised item. Investors who react to the reported profit line of a company like this will be whipsawed by their own scoreboard.

What it costs

Market capitalisation sat around ₹10,900 Cr in mid-2026 (approx.). Against consolidated FY26 profit of roughly ₹338 Cr, that is on the order of 32×; against standalone profit of ₹180.72 Cr it is roughly 60×. Neither number should be taken at face value, and the reason is instructive. Trailing earnings for a deep cyclical near the bottom of its price cycle overstate the multiple; trailing earnings for one near the top understate it dramatically. In 2018 this business earned a multiple of its current profit and traded on a single-digit P/E — the classic trap of a cyclical looking cheapest exactly when it is most dangerous. Today's inverse applies: HEG looks expensive on depressed earnings, which is not the same as being expensive.

What the price also embeds is the anode venture. A pre-revenue business consuming capital contributes nothing to the E in the P/E while absorbing cash that would otherwise show up as profit or dividends. Some portion of HEG's market value is therefore an assessment of TACC's eventual worth rather than of electrode earnings — which is precisely the ambiguity the demerger is designed to resolve.

The metric that settles the thesis

Not the P/E, and not the order book — an electrode maker does not have one in any meaningful sense. Two numbers decide this case, and they belong to the two halves of the company.

  • For the electrode business: realisation per tonne, read alongside utilisation. Utilisation is already in the nineties, so it cannot do much more work; the entire earnings swing from here is price. A recovery in electrode realisation against a broadly stable needle-coke cost, applied across roughly 100,000 tonnes, is what takes EBITDA margin from 19% back toward the mid-twenties or higher. Every rupee of that flows almost undiluted to profit, because the plant is already full and the conversion cost is already fixed.
  • For the battery business: the anode plant's commissioning date, followed by qualification wins. One number matters more than all the capital-expenditure announcements combined — the count of cell manufacturers that have taken TACC's material through full qualification and placed commercial orders. Until that count is above zero, the anode venture is a well-funded intention. Once it is above zero with a credible customer, it is a business, and the market will value it as one.
The uncomfortable pairing: those two metrics are not independent in the way an investor would like. Both the electrode plant and the anode plant want needle coke, and the global battery-anode build-out is a growing bidder for the same specialised feedstock HEG must import for its rods. Success in anodes therefore tightens the input market for electrodes. It is an unusual internal tension and worth watching in the feedstock cost line as TACC scales.
11

Future, risks, bull and bear

What has to go right, what could go wrong, and the specific dates that will tell you which.

HEG in mid-2026 is a business with a strong balance sheet, a full factory, a mediocre price environment and an expensive ambition. The next three years contain a small number of checkable events that will resolve most of the uncertainty — the electrode expansion, the anode commissioning, the qualification outcome, and the completion of the demerger.

The risks, set out plainly

  • Cycle volatility. Electrode realisations swing violently and earnings swing more. Chinese overcapacity has kept the market weak for years and there is no mechanism that forces it to tighten. A company earning 19% margins today has earned far more and far less within living memory of its current management.
  • Needle coke. Imported, dollar-priced, sourced from a handful of global producers, with no Indian alternative and a new set of battery-industry bidders competing for it. A price spike or an availability squeeze hits HEG directly, and the six-month cycle means the damage shows up in margins two quarters after the event.
  • Single-site concentration. Fire, flood, extended power failure, industrial action, or a regulatory stoppage at Mandideep would be a company-level event, not a plant-level one. The 15,000-tonne expansion deepens the same site rather than diversifying away from it.
  • Anode execution. A capital-heavy entry into a technically demanding, Chinese-dominated market where the gate is multi-year customer qualification. It may be delayed; it may cost more; it may simply fail to qualify. The honest position is that the timing is not fully within HEG's control, because the testing calendar belongs to the customer.
  • Demand timing. The EAF transition and the EV transition are both real and both slower than their advocates project. HEG is exposed to the pace of two energy transitions simultaneously, and neither pays it for being early.
  • Currency and treasury marks. As FY26 demonstrated, reported profit can diverge sharply from operating performance because of unrealised movements on investments and the rupee. This is a permanent feature of how this company reports, not a one-off.
  • Competition. Graphite India domestically; GrafTech, Resonac, Tokai and Chinese producers globally; BTR, Shanshan, Putailai and Posco Future M in anodes. In neither market is HEG the largest player.
  • Scheme risk. The demerger requires regulatory, creditor and shareholder approvals and its final terms — entitlement ratio, allocation of treasury and liabilities — are not yet fixed in public. What you own after the split may differ from what you assumed.

The constructive case

  • A genuinely low-cost, full plant. 90%-plus utilisation in a soft market, with captive power covering the most expensive process step, is evidence of real cost position rather than assertion.
  • Operating leverage waiting on price. With the plant full and conversion cost fixed, any recovery in electrode realisation lands almost entirely in EBITDA across ~100,000 tonnes.
  • A fortress balance sheet. No long-term debt and ~₹792 Cr of treasury means HEG can fund the electrode expansion and the anode build without dilution or distress — an option most cyclical peers do not have.
  • The EAF structural shift. Global steel decarbonisation points in exactly one direction for electric arc furnace share, and every incremental EAF tonne consumes electrodes.
  • A scarce anode position. Very few companies outside China combine graphitisation at scale, process know-how and the capital to attempt battery anode material. If qualification lands, HEG owns something structurally scarce.
  • Structural clarity coming. The demerger lets each business be financed and valued on its own terms rather than averaged into a single confused multiple.

The sceptical case

  • The price is set in China. HEG's efficiency cannot offset an oversupplied global electrode market; realisation is out of its hands.
  • The market shrinks itself. Falling specific consumption means better products sell fewer tonnes — a permanent drag that steel-output growth must outrun every year.
  • Everything depends on one location. A serious disruption at Mandideep is not recoverable within a financial year, given a six-month production cycle.
  • The anode plant is years from proof. Ground broken, capital committed, and not one qualified commercial tonne shipped. Qualification failures in battery materials are common and rarely well-telegraphed.
  • Reported earnings are noisy. A treasury-driven ₹189 Cr swing turning a decent quarter into a loss is not a one-off quirk; it is how this balance sheet behaves.
  • The multiple already assumes something. Roughly 32× consolidated FY26 earnings for a commodity producer implies either a strong cyclical recovery or a successful battery venture — and the second of those has no evidence yet.

The bottom line

HEG has spent half a century perfecting a material whose purpose is to be destroyed — a rod that carries lightning into molten scrap and is gone. It is very good at it: one enormous plant, its own power station, a full order sheet in a market its customers describe as oversupplied, and no debt to service while it waits for prices to turn. But the destination of that material has always been a furnace, and the company has now decided that the same graphite deserves a longer life, inside a battery that runs for a decade. Two dates will tell you whether that decision was visionary or expensive: the day TACC's anode line at its Madhya Pradesh site actually starts producing, and the day a cell manufacturer signs off on the material it makes. Until the first, this is a debt-free cyclical with a construction site attached. After the second, it is something India does not currently have.
