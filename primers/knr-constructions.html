KNR Constructions, the low-cost road-builder waiting to be paid

A complete walk through one of India's most cost-efficient highway contractors — how a company earns money by building roads, dams and water pipelines for the government, why its famously high profit margins have suddenly collapsed, and why almost the entire investment debate now sits in a single stalled cheque of roughly ₹1,300 Cr owed by the Telangana irrigation department.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

KNR Constructions is a Hyderabad-based infrastructure contractor — it builds things for the government and gets paid to build them. Its bread and butter is roads and highways: it has laid over 6,000 lane-kilometres across a dozen states. Around that core it also builds irrigation works (canals, dams, lift-irrigation schemes) and urban water pipelines, and it has lately pushed hard into mining contracts. It is not a household name, but within the sector it has a specific reputation: for decades it was the contractor that earned the fattest profit margins in the business while carrying almost no debt of its own. That reputation is exactly what is now in question.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Its best-in-class margins have collapsed — for a knowable reason. For years KNR ran EBITDA margins of ~18–20%, well above the ~11–13% typical of road contractors. In FY26 that fell to a standalone ~8.5%, and in one quarter (Q3 FY26) as low as ~5%. This is not a broken company; it is a company whose highest-margin work — Telangana irrigation — stopped paying, so it kept building lower-margin roads and mining work to fill the gap.
  • Roughly ₹1,300 Cr is stuck in a government's pocket. KNR did the work on two big Telangana irrigation packages (part of the giant Kaleshwaram scheme) but collections have been stalled since 2023–24. It has continued spending its own cash to honour the contracts. Freeing that money is the single largest swing factor in the story — and it depends on a state government's finances, not on anything KNR controls.
  • The order book is at a record — but it changed shape. KNR exited June 2026 with a record ~₹15,234 Cr backlog, roughly 5–6× annual revenue. Yet the mix has swung sharply: ~45% is now mining, ~38% roads, and only ~11% irrigation. A famously roads-and-irrigation firm has quietly become nearly half a mining contractor — which changes both its risk and its margins.
In plain terms

KNR is a highly efficient builder that took on very profitable government dam work, finished a lot of it, and then couldn't get paid. The business itself still works — it wins plenty of new orders and executes them cheaply — but its cash and its margins are being held hostage by one slow-paying customer. The bet a buyer of the stock is making is that the Telangana money eventually arrives, margins climb back toward the high teens, and the record order book converts into revenue. The risk is that the receivable drags on, the new mining-heavy book earns thinner margins, and a company that used to be the sector's quality name spends years just running to stand still.

01

Road-building 101: EPC, BOT and HAM

No jargon left standing — the three ways a contractor gets paid to build a road.

To understand KNR you need to understand how the government hands out road work, because the contract type decides who puts up the money, who carries the risk, and how the contractor gets paid. There are three models, and KNR does all three. Get these straight and the whole company becomes readable.

The three road-contract models
ModelWho funds it & how the builder is paid
EPC"Engineering, Procurement & Construction." The government pays the contractor to build the road, in stages, as work progresses. The builder takes construction risk but not funding or traffic risk. Clean, cash-generative, and KNR's preferred bread-and-butter.
HAM"Hybrid Annuity Model." A middle path: the government funds ~40% during construction; the contractor arranges the other ~60% (equity + project loans) and is then repaid over ~15 years in fixed instalments (annuities) plus interest. The builder must invest its own equity into a separate company (an "SPV") for each road.
BOT"Build-Operate-Transfer." The contractor funds the whole road itself and earns back its money over 15–25 years from tolls. It carries traffic risk — if fewer cars come than expected, it loses. The most capital-hungry and riskiest model; KNR has largely exited it by selling its BOT assets.

The gradient of risk runs cleanly across the three. EPC puts the least of the builder's own capital at stake and carries only construction risk. HAM sits in the middle — the contractor arranges roughly 60% of the funding and parks equity in the road, but is repaid on a fixed government schedule. BOT is the most capital-hungry and the only one carrying traffic risk, which is precisely why KNR keeps its EPC parent debt-free by favouring EPC work, using HAM selectively, and having walked away from toll-based BOT roads almost entirely.

Why the model choice is the whole risk profile

An EPC road is a job: build it, invoice it, get paid, move on — your balance sheet stays light. A HAM road is half job, half investment: you build it and you park equity in it, so you become a small owner of the road who collects instalments for 15 years. A BOT road is almost pure investment: you own the road and live or die by how many cars use it. The genius — and the discipline — of KNR's model is that it earns most of its money doing the low-risk EPC job, uses HAM only where the maths works, and then sells the HAM roads once they are built and de-risked, recycling the cash back into more building. That is how a road-builder stays debt-free at the parent while others drown in project loans.

One more term worth knowing: the SPV, or "special purpose vehicle." Every HAM or BOT road is built inside its own separate company, ring-fenced from the parent. The project loan sits in that SPV, not on KNR's own books. This is why you will see KNR described as "debt-free standalone" while its consolidated accounts still show a couple of thousand crore of debt — that debt lives in the road SPVs and is repaid by the government's annuity instalments, not by KNR's construction cash. Keeping those two pictures separate in your head is essential to reading this company.

02

What KNR actually builds: four kinds of work

One contractor, four end-markets — each with its own economics and its own customer.

KNR started as a pure roads company and has widened into four related lines of civil-construction work. They share the same core skills — earthmoving, concrete, project management — but the customers, margins and payment behaviour differ enormously. Read this once and the swings in the financials stop looking random.

All four ultimately depend on a government or public-sector customer paying its bills on time — which is precisely where the risk in this business lives.

The four lines, decoded

  • Roads & highways — the original and still the identity. KNR builds national highways for the NHAI and state roads, mostly on EPC terms, and has built a reputation for finishing ahead of schedule. Margins here are decent but not spectacular; the appeal is that EPC roads pay reliably and don't tie up capital.
  • Irrigation — the double-edged sword. Big state irrigation projects (canals, barrages, lift-irrigation) historically carried KNR's highest margins, which is why its blended profitability towered over rivals'. The catch is the customer: a single state government, whose willingness and ability to pay can change with its politics and its budget. Telangana irrigation made KNR's margins — and has now broken them.
  • Urban water & pipelines — laying drinking-water pipelines and building water-treatment and supply infrastructure, often under nationally-funded schemes. Steadier and more geographically spread than a single state's irrigation programme, this diversifies the customer base away from any one government.
  • Mining — the newest and now the largest single slice of the order book. In contract mining, KNR is paid to remove earth and rock (overburden) and extract material for a mine owner. It is a different rhythm from road-building — longer, more repetitive, volume-driven — and it has become the swing factor in the order book almost overnight.
Why the mix matters more than the size

Two contractors can report the same revenue and be completely different businesses depending on what they are building and who is paying. KNR's blended margin has always been a weighted average of these four lines: lots of high-margin irrigation pulled the whole company up; a book that is now nearly half mining and only a tenth irrigation pulls it toward a more ordinary contractor's profitability. So when you read "order book at a record ₹15,234 Cr," the more important question is not the headline number — it is how much of that book is the profitable irrigation work that made KNR special, versus the higher-volume, thinner-margin mining and roads that now dominate it.

03

The company, the family, and who controls it

A founder-run South Indian contractor, promoter stake just under half.

KNR Constructions was founded in 1995 in Telangana by K. (Kamidi) Narsimha Reddy, who remains its Managing Director three decades later. It is a classic promoter-driven South Indian infrastructure house — engineering-led, cost-obsessed, and still controlled by the founding family, though the family's stake has drifted to just under half.

The flip side is key-man dependence on one ageing founder, a stake that has slipped below the psychologically important 50% line, and the usual governance questions that come with any family-run contractor. None of these are red flags on their own; they are simply features to weigh.
04

The margin machine — and why it seized up

The single most important thing about KNR, and the single biggest thing that changed.

For most of its listed life, one number set KNR apart from every other road contractor: its operating margin. Where peers earned ~11–13% EBITDA margins, KNR routinely earned ~18–20%. That premium is the entire reason the stock ever commanded attention — and understanding where it came from, and why it vanished, is the core of the business case.

Margins sat in the high teens for years, cratered in FY26, and have partially recovered at the consolidated level (which includes higher-margin HAM annuity income). The FY26 standalone figure of ~8.5% is the true low point of the construction business itself.

Where did the historic premium come from? Three things. First, high-margin irrigation work — big state canal and lift-irrigation jobs simply earned more than roads, and KNR did a lot of them. Second, in-house execution — KNR owns much of its own equipment and self-performs rather than sub-contracting, capturing margin that others hand to sub-contractors. Third, a tight, disciplined working-capital cycle and geographic concentration in home turf (Telangana/Andhra/Karnataka), which kept costs and mobilisation efficient.

Why the margin fell — the mechanism, spelled out

The collapse was not caused by cost overruns or bad bidding. It was caused by the disappearance of the high-margin mix. When Telangana stopped paying for irrigation, KNR wound that work down — and irrigation was its most profitable line. To keep its crews and revenue busy, it leaned on lower-margin roads and, increasingly, on mining. So the blended margin fell not because each job got worse, but because the rich jobs shrank as a share of the whole. Layer on negative operating leverage — when revenue drops ~38% but fixed costs (equipment, staff, overheads) don't, margins compress further — and you get a fall from ~19% to ~8.5%. The margin will only fully recover when either irrigation collections resume (bringing back the rich work) or the new mining/roads book scales enough to spread the fixed costs again.

A distinction worth holding onto: the recovering ~16% margin you see at the consolidated level flatters the picture, because consolidated numbers fold in the steadier, higher-margin annuity income from HAM road SPVs. The standalone construction margin — the number that tells you how the actual building business is doing — is the one that fell to ~8.5% and is the truer read on operating health. Always check which margin a headline is quoting.
05

The Telangana irrigation receivable

The single most important idea in the entire case — and it is specific to this company.

Every contractor faces the risk that a customer pays late. But for KNR, one particular late payment has grown large enough to dominate the whole investment case. The company did the work on two big Telangana irrigation packages — part of the vast Kaleshwaram lift-irrigation scheme — and roughly ₹1,200–1,300 Cr of billed money has been sitting uncollected, with collections effectively stalled since around 2023–24. To grasp KNR today, you have to sit with how big that number is relative to the company.

The stuck money is more than three times a full quarter's revenue and close to a third of the entire market capitalisation. A receivable this size relative to the company is why one government's cheque, not the order book, is the real swing factor.

Here is the mechanism, without spin. Under the contract, KNR was obliged to keep executing the irrigation work to avoid default — so it kept spending its own money on labour, materials and equipment while the customer stopped paying. That does three damaging things at once. It drains cash (money goes out; nothing comes in). It ties up the balance sheet (a big receivable that isn't converting to cash). And it removes the highest-margin work from the revenue mix, which is what crushed the reported margin. One stalled payment therefore shows up in every part of the financials — cash flow, working capital, and profitability — simultaneously.

The device that only fits KNR: watch the collection, not the order book

Most contractors you track through their order book and execution pace. For KNR, the number that actually moves the needle is how much of the Telangana irrigation money gets collected, and when. If a meaningful slice is recovered, it does three good things together: it releases a large lump of cash, it shrinks the bloated receivable back toward normal, and it lets management re-engage high-margin irrigation work — lifting margins back toward the high teens. If it drags on for more years, KNR remains a cash-constrained builder earning ordinary margins on a mining-and-roads book, with a big asset on its books that the market keeps discounting. The order book tells you the company can win work; only the collection tells you whether the old KNR comes back.

It is worth being blunt about what this risk is. It is not a doubt about whether the work was done or whether the money is contractually owed — it is a timing and political-will risk on a single state government whose finances are stretched. The market is pricing that uncertainty, not writing the money off — which is exactly why the stock trades at a low multiple despite a record order book.

06

HAM assets and the monetisation engine

The quieter half of the story — how KNR turns built roads back into cash.

Alongside its construction work, KNR builds and owns a portfolio of HAM road assets — the roads where it invested its own equity in exchange for 15 years of government annuities. These are real, cash-generating assets, and KNR runs a deliberate cycle: build the road, let it stabilise, then sell it to a long-term infrastructure investor and recycle the money into new building. That recycling engine is a core part of how a construction company stays capital-light.

KNR agreed to sell 100% of four HAM road SPVs (including sub-debt) to Indus Infra Trust for ~₹1,543 Cr against an original investment of ~₹567 Cr — roughly 2.7× the money, freeing capital and crystallising a large book gain that flatters FY26 consolidated profit.

Here is why this matters for how you read the numbers. When KNR sells a batch of HAM roads at a big premium to what it invested, the gain flows into consolidated profit. That is a genuine, cash-backed gain — but it is also lumpy and non-recurring: it happens when a deal closes, not every year. So a year like FY26, where standalone construction profit fell hard but consolidated profit held up at ~₹437 Cr, owes part of that resilience to asset-sale gains rather than to the building business humming along. Separating the recurring construction earnings from the one-off monetisation gains is essential to valuing the company sensibly.

Why the recycling model is genuinely valuable

A road-builder has a permanent tension: HAM and BOT roads earn attractive long-term returns, but building them ties up equity that could instead fund new construction. KNR's answer is to treat its HAM roads as inventory, not heirlooms — build them well, prove out the annuity, then sell to a pension-style buyer (an InvIT or an infrastructure trust) that wants steady 15-year cash flows and will pay a premium for a de-risked asset. KNR pockets a gain, gets its equity back, and redeploys it into the next set of projects. Done repeatedly, this lets a mid-sized contractor punch above its balance sheet — and it is why the parent can stay debt-free while still participating in higher-return HAM roads. The 2020 sale of the Walayar toll road to Cube Highways and the 2025 Indus Infra Trust deal are two turns of the same flywheel.

The nuance: monetisation is a strength, but it is also a reason to read consolidated profit carefully. In years with a big asset sale, headline profit looks strong; in years without one, it can look weak even if the underlying building business is unchanged. When judging KNR's operating momentum, strip out the asset-sale gains and look at the standalone construction line — that is the engine that has to keep running whether or not a HAM deal closes that year.

07

The order book and the mining pivot

A record backlog that quietly stopped being a roads-and-irrigation story.

On the surface, KNR's order book has never looked better: a record ~₹15,234 Cr at June 2026, up from ~₹11,903 Cr in March, worth roughly 5–6× annual revenue — years of work already booked. That is real execution visibility and a genuine positive. But the more revealing story is in the composition, which has shifted so far that KNR is arguably a different company than the one investors bought a few years ago.

A company built on roads and irrigation now carries a book that is nearly half mining and only about a tenth irrigation. The backlog is bigger than ever, but its margin character has changed — mining and roads are steadier but thinner than the old high-margin irrigation work.

The book jumped from ~₹11,903 Cr in March 2026 to the record ~₹15,234 Cr by June, driven heavily by fresh HAM road and mining wins in Q1 FY27; the older "core" backlog excluding those recent additions is a more modest ~₹8,667 Cr. Both the size and the freshness of the book support a revenue recovery from FY27 into FY28. The roads slowdown behind this deserves a straight telling. National-highway awarding by the NHAI has been patchy, and KNR itself let its roads execution wind down as older projects finished and irrigation cash tightened — which is exactly why FY26 revenue fell so hard. The pivot into mining is the management response: a large, repetitive, volume-based revenue stream to keep equipment and crews utilised while roads awarding recovers and irrigation is resolved. It is a sensible diversification, but it is also an admission that the traditional roads-and-irrigation pipeline was not enough on its own.

Reading the book honestly

A record order book is genuinely good news — it underwrites the revenue guidance of roughly ₹2,200–2,300 Cr for FY27 and ₹3,000-plus Cr for FY28, and it means the company is winning, not shrinking. But two cautions belong alongside it. First, an order book is a promise of revenue, not of margin — and a mining-heavy book will convert at lower margins than the old irrigation-heavy one, so bigger revenue need not mean proportionally bigger profit. Second, mining is a newer competency for KNR; executing it at scale, on the margins management projects, is something the company still has to prove quarter by quarter. The backlog removes the fear of running out of work; it does not by itself restore the premium margins.

08

The balance sheet — light where it counts

Debt-free at the parent, project debt in the SPVs — and why the distinction protects you.

If the receivable is the wound and the margin is the symptom, the balance sheet is the shield — the thing that lets KNR survive a multi-year cash squeeze that would sink a more leveraged builder. KNR's defining financial trait, beyond its margins, is that its operating parent carries essentially no debt of its own, even while it funds a ₹1,300 Cr government receivable out of pocket.

The ~₹2,444 Cr of consolidated debt (Mar 2026) lives in the ring-fenced road SPVs and is serviced by the government's fixed annuity instalments — not by KNR's construction cash flow. The construction parent that actually builds and bids is debt-free.

Why does this distinction protect an investor? Because the two kinds of debt behave completely differently. Project (SPV) debt is non-recourse-like and self-liquidating: the road's own government annuity pays it off over 15 years, and if KNR sells the SPV, the debt leaves with it (as it did in the Indus Infra Trust deal). Parent debt would be a different animal — a claim on the construction business itself, requiring interest payments out of the same cash flow that funds new projects. KNR having none of the latter means that even with ₹1,300 Cr stuck in Telangana, no lender is forcing its hand. It can wait out the receivable rather than firesale assets or dilute shareholders to service borrowings.

Why a light balance sheet is the reason KNR is still standing

Picture the counterfactual. A road contractor with the same ₹1,300 Cr receivable but a heavily indebted parent would, by now, be in serious trouble: cash going out to the government and to lenders, with a covenant breach one bad quarter away. That is roughly the trap that has broken several Indian infra names over the years. KNR avoided it by never levering the parent in the first place — favouring cash-generative EPC work, using HAM selectively, exiting toll BOT, and recycling HAM equity through asset sales. The debt-free parent does not make the receivable problem disappear; it makes the problem survivable, buying the company the years it may need to collect. In a sector where the graveyard is full of over-borrowed builders, that discipline is the quiet reason KNR still gets the benefit of the doubt.

The book-value angle: because so much value is tied up in receivables and HAM equity rather than reported earnings, KNR often trades near or below its book value per share (~₹177). A low price-to-book on an asset-backed, debt-free contractor is part of what draws value investors — but it only pays off if those book assets (chiefly the Telangana receivable) actually convert to cash. Book value is worth what you can collect.
09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded field of mid-cap road builders — where KNR's edge is quality, not size.

Indian road construction is a competitive, mid-cap-heavy industry: a dozen listed EPC and HAM contractors bidding for the same NHAI and state tenders, where the government's awarding pace sets the tide for everyone. KNR is not the biggest, but it has historically been regarded as one of the best-run — the quality name rather than the scale name.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, this is not a business of proprietary technology or moats — every contractor can pour concrete and lay asphalt, so the edge comes from execution discipline, cost control and balance-sheet health. That is precisely where KNR has historically won: better margins and less debt than most. Second, the whole field rises and falls together on government awarding — when the NHAI and states tender heavily, everyone's order book swells; when awarding slows, everyone competes harder for less work, and bids get keener (worse for margins). KNR's diversification into mining, water and asset monetisation is partly an attempt to be less hostage to the road-awarding cycle than a pure-play roads peer. Its challenge is to prove that its famous margin premium can be rebuilt in a book where the highest-margin irrigation work has shrunk and lower-margin mining now dominates.

The debate is whether this is a temporary trough or a permanently lower-margin company.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A disciplined builder in a self-inflicted-looking trough — asked to prove the quality is intact.

KNR enters FY27 in a strange position: a record order book and a spotless parent balance sheet on one hand, collapsed margins and ₹1,300 Cr of stuck cash on the other. The debate is not whether the company survives — its light balance sheet makes that near-certain — but whether it re-earns the premium reputation that made it worth owning, or settles into being an ordinary, cheaper contractor.

The bull case

  • Receivable is a timing problem, not a write-off — the work was done and the money is owed; collection releases a large cash lump and revives high-margin irrigation work.
  • Debt-free parent — can wait out the squeeze with no lender pressure; a rare survivor's balance sheet in a leveraged sector.
  • Record order book — ~₹15,234 Cr (~5–6× revenue) underwrites a multi-year revenue recovery into FY28.
  • Proven monetisation engine — repeatable HAM asset sales (₹1,543 Cr Indus deal at ~2.7× cost) recycle capital and prove asset value.
  • Cheap on assets — near book value, single-digit P/E, with genuine hidden value in receivables and HAM equity.

The bear case

  • Receivable could drag for years — recovery hinges on Telangana's stretched finances and politics; every stuck quarter costs cash and margin.
  • Margins may not fully recover — a mining-and-roads-heavy book earns thinner margins than the old irrigation mix; the ~18–20% premium may be structurally gone.
  • Mining is unproven at scale — nearly half the book is a newer competency KNR must still execute on projected margins.
  • Government-customer concentration — the same dependence that caused the receivable applies across roads, irrigation and water; awarding cycles remain lumpy.
  • Key-man & promoter stake — founder-dependent, with promoter holding slipping below 50% and some selling.
The bottom line

KNR Constructions spent years being the road-builder investors trusted most — best margins, least debt, cleanest execution. FY26 tested every part of that reputation at once, and the culprit was almost entirely one thing: a state government that took the work and stopped paying, dragging margins from the high teens to single digits and draining the cash the business runs on. What remains genuinely intact is the discipline — a debt-free parent, a record order book, and a monetisation engine that keeps turning built roads back into cash. What is unproven is whether the premium margins come back or whether KNR is now simply a cheaper, more ordinary contractor. The next several quarters answer it in two checkable figures — cash collected against the Telangana receivable, and the standalone margin climbing back toward the mid-teens. Watch those two prints; together they tell you whether the quality name is returning or quietly fading.
