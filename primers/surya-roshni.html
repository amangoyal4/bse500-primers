Surya Roshni, two engines: steel pipes and lightbulbs

A complete walk through one of India's oddest listed pairings — the country's largest producer of galvanised steel pipes bolted to the "Surya" lighting-and-fans brand on millions of ceilings. Why one business is a low-margin, steel-price-driven volume machine and the other a branded-consumer story, why the mix of value-added pipe and the health of the lighting brand are the two margin levers that matter, and why the loudest question hanging over the stock is whether these two very different businesses should stay married at all.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're actually looking at, before we go deep.

Surya Roshni is a diversified manufacturer with two completely different businesses stapled inside one share. The larger one, by a wide margin, makes steel pipes — it is India's biggest producer of galvanised (GI) steel pipes and one of the country's largest makers of ERW, API line pipe, spiral and structural pipes, plus cold-rolled steel strips. The smaller one is the "Surya" lighting and consumer-durables brand — LED lamps, luminaires, streetlights, fans, pumps and home appliances that compete on retail shelves against Havells, Bajaj and Crompton. One is a commodity-cyclical volume game; the other is a branded-consumer game. They share a name, a promoter and a balance sheet, and almost nothing else.

Three facts frame the whole company:

  • Steel pipes are the volume, lighting carries the brand. In FY26 the Steel Pipes & Strips segment was roughly 76% of the ~₹7,540 Cr revenue; Lighting & Consumer Durables was the other ~24% (about ₹1,809 Cr). Yet most Indians have never heard of Surya's pipes and know it only as a lightbulb. That mismatch — the business people recognise is the smaller one — sits at the centre of everything, including the valuation.
  • The margins are thin, and steel prices drive them. Pipe-making is a spread business: buy hot-rolled steel coil, form it into tube, sell it on for a conversion margin. When steel prices swing, so does profitability, and FY26 was a squeeze year — group EBITDA margin fell to ~8% and reported PAT actually dropped to ~₹286 Cr (from ~₹347 Cr) even as revenue edged up. The lever against that cyclicality is selling more value-added pipe (galvanised, API-grade, coated) and growing the higher-margin lighting brand.
  • A demerger rumour did what years of results couldn't. In May 2026 speculation that Surya would split its pipes and lighting into two separate companies sent the stock up roughly 25% in days. Nothing was board-approved — but the market's reaction revealed the thesis: investors suspect the branded lighting jewel is worth far more on its own than the blended, pipe-weighted multiple the combined company earns. The stock trades near 15× earnings and around one times sales.
In plain terms

Surya Roshni is really two companies wearing one nameplate. Picture a heavy, low-margin steel-pipe factory — enormous tonnage, wafer-thin spreads, fortunes tied to the price of steel coil — sitting next to a branded electricals business selling bulbs and fans through thousands of dealers at consumer-goods margins. For decades they've been run together and valued together, which means the whole thing gets priced like a pipe maker. The live question is whether keeping them married still makes sense, because a stand-alone "Surya" consumer brand and a stand-alone pipe champion might each be worth more than the single company that owns both. That is why a demerger whisper moved the stock more than a year of earnings did.

01

A two-engine company: how to read it at all

The single mental model that makes the rest of the primer click into place.

Most companies have one engine. Surya Roshni has two, and they run on entirely different fuel. To follow the business you have to hold both in your head at once — because a headline number for "Surya Roshni" is always an average of a commodity business and a consumer business that are pulling in different directions.

Why the split, not the total, is the story

If you only track group revenue, you'll conclude Surya Roshni is a steel-pipe company — three-quarters of the sales say so. But if you track where value is created and where investors would pay the higher multiple, the lighting quarter punches well above its weight. That is the tension that runs through every section that follows: the company's size lives in pipes, but a meaningful chunk of its potential re-rating lives in lighting. Almost every debate about Surya — margins, the demerger, the valuation — is really a debate about which engine the market should price it on.

One more framing point. Because pipes are so steel-price sensitive, Surya's reported profits are noisier than a pure consumer company's. A quarter can look weak not because it sold less pipe, but because the steel it bought fell in price and left an inventory loss on already-made stock; a quarter can look strong for the mirror-image reason. Reading the company means separating volume growth (real demand) from price effects (steel's swings) — a distinction we return to in the financials.

02

Engine one: the steel-pipe machine

The bigger business — what it forms, coats and sells, and why the spread is everything.

Strip away the brand and the bulbs, and Surya Roshni's larger half is a steel-tube conversion business at national scale. It buys flat steel — hot-rolled and cold-rolled coil — and forms it into pipe of every kind an economy that is laying water lines, gas grids, structures and irrigation actually needs. It is India's largest producer of GI (galvanised) pipes and its largest exporter of ERW pipes, with a full ladder of products from cheap commodity tube to specialised line pipe.

The product ladder — from commodity to value-added

  • ERW & black pipe — Electric Resistance Welded pipe, the workhorse tube. Made by rolling coil into a cylinder and welding the seam. High volume, lowest margin — the base of the ladder.
  • GI (galvanised) pipe — ERW pipe dipped in zinc so it resists rust; used for water, plumbing and fencing. Surya is India's largest GI producer, and galvanising adds a step of value (and margin) over plain black pipe.
  • API line pipe & 3LPE-coated pipe — pipe built to American Petroleum Institute standards to carry oil, gas and water under pressure over long distances, often with a three-layer polyethylene (3LPE) anti-corrosion coating. This is the high end — bigger orders, tighter specs, better margins, and where export demand (including the US market) sits.
  • Spiral & hollow-section / structural pipe — large-diameter spiral-welded pipe for water and gas trunk lines, plus square/rectangular hollow sections used as structural steel in buildings, warehouses and infrastructure. The hollow-section category is a growth push, made on modern Direct Forming Technology (DFT) mills.
  • Cold-rolled (CR) strips — flat steel processed in-house, part-feeding the pipe mills and part-sold, giving Surya some backward integration on its own raw material.

The economics that decide a pipe maker: the conversion spread

A pipe maker does not really sell "pipe" — it sells the conversion of flat steel into tube. Its profit is the gap between what it pays for coil and what it gets for the finished pipe, minus the cost of forming and coating. That gap, per tonne, is the number that matters; Surya reports it as EBITDA per tonne (recently in the region of ₹4,000–5,000 a tonne). Two things move it: utilisation (a mill running full spreads its fixed costs over more tonnes) and mix (a tonne of coated API pipe earns far more than a tonne of plain black pipe). When steel prices fall sharply, a third thing bites — inventory loss, because pipe made from expensive coil has to be sold into a cheaper market. That is why a pipe maker's quarter can disappoint on price even when volumes are fine.

The demand behind the tonnage is some of the most durable in the Indian economy: piped water schemes (the Jal Jeevan Mission and its successors), city gas distribution networks, oil-and-gas pipelines, the steel-intensive shift to pre-engineered and structural construction, and irrigation. Surya sells into all of them, which spreads its risk across several capex cycles rather than betting on one. The catch is that all of it is project demand — it moves with government and corporate capex, and it competes fiercely on price.

03

Engine two: the Surya lighting & durables brand

The smaller half by revenue — but the name on the box, and the higher margin.

The other engine is the one ordinary consumers actually know. Under the "Surya" brand, the company makes and sells LED lamps and luminaires, streetlights, ceiling and other fans, water pumps, and a spread of home and kitchen appliances. It is one of the larger domestic lighting names — long positioned around the number-two spot in Indian lighting — and it competes not on tonnage but on brand, distribution and product.

What sits inside the consumer engine

  • LED lighting — the core: bulbs, battens, panels, downlighters and luminaires for homes, offices and industry, plus streetlights sold heavily into government and municipal tenders. Lighting is the heart of the "Surya" brand and the anchor of the segment.
  • Fans — ceiling, table and specialty fans, an adjacent category where the same dealer network and brand carry over, and where premium (BLDC, energy-efficient) models lift margins.
  • Consumer durables & appliances — home and kitchen appliances and small durables, an extension of the brand into the wider electricals basket that Havells and Bajaj also chase.
  • Pumps & allied — water pumps and related products, sold through overlapping rural and semi-urban channels.
Why lighting is the higher-quality engine

Pipes sell on price; lighting sells on a name. A homeowner or an electrician reaching for a Surya bulb or fan is buying trust in the brand, not a spec sheet — which is exactly why branded electricals earn steadier, structurally higher margins than commodity tube. Surya's recent quarters show it: even in a tough period, the lighting-and-durables segment ran an EBITDA margin around 8% versus roughly 5% in steel, on a fraction of the revenue. The consumer engine is smaller, but per rupee of sales it is the better business — and it is the one a would-be acquirer or a re-rating would pay up for.

The consumer engine has its own growth playbook, and it looks nothing like the pipe strategy. It runs on distribution reach (more dealers, more towns, more shelf space), premiumisation (nudging the mix toward higher-value LED luminaires, BLDC fans and appliances rather than commodity bulbs), the LED product cycle (staying current as lighting technology and smart features evolve), and a growing export book (lighting exports being pushed toward the ₹60–70 Cr range and up sharply year on year). Management has guided the segment to grow revenue in the low-20s percent with even faster volume growth — a genuine consumer-goods growth rate that the steel engine cannot match.

The recognition gap, made concrete: Surya Roshni is India's largest galvanised-pipe producer and largest ERW-pipe exporter — a genuine national champion in a business almost no consumer thinks about. Yet if you stopped a hundred people and said "Surya," most would picture a lightbulb. The company's fame and its bulk live in different segments. That single fact — the small business is the famous one, and the famous one is the higher-margin one — is the seed of the entire demerger debate.
04

The company, the family, and who controls it

A half-century-old Agarwal-family group, built pipe-first, brand-second.

Surya Roshni was incorporated in 1973 and built by the Agarwal family, led for decades by chairman Jai Prakash Agarwal, with the next generation now in operating roles across the two businesses. It grew up as a lighting-and-pipes house and consolidated its steel-pipe scale through acquisition, arriving at today's twin structure inside a single listed company.

Ownership & balance sheet

  • Promoters: the Agarwal family and associated entities hold roughly 63% of the company (about 63% as of mid-2026) — a firm controlling stake, with the balance held by institutions and the public. Investors should track promoter-pledge levels directly in the shareholding filings.
  • Balance sheet: Surya has deleveraged to a near net-cash position — borrowings of only about ₹80 Cr at end-FY26 and a net-cash surplus (~₹155 Cr) reported at Q1 FY27, on a net worth that puts book value around ₹120 a share. For a working-capital-heavy steel business, funding its own inventory and receivables without leaning on debt is a real structural strength.
  • Returns & payout: return on capital in the mid-teens and return on equity around 11%, with a dividend the company has kept paying (a payout in the high-30s percent of earnings, a dividend yield above 2%). This is a cash-generative, shareholder-returning business, not a capital sink.
Why the structure is the whole debate: a single company, one promoter, one balance sheet — but two businesses with nothing operational in common. That structure has served the family well for fifty years, sharing overheads and a cash pool across cycles. The question now openly on the table is whether that same structure is costing shareholders a re-rating, because the market can't cleanly value a consumer brand and a pipe mill welded together. Section 08 takes that head-on.

The value-added lever: how a thin-margin pipe maker earns more

The single most important operating idea in the steel engine — mix, not just tonnage.

Here is the trap in a commodity pipe business: you can grow tonnage forever and still not grow profit, because plain black pipe barely earns a spread. Surya's answer — and the number to watch in the whole steel engine — is the share of volume that is value-added: galvanised, API-grade, 3LPE-coated, spiral and hollow-section pipe rather than undifferentiated tube. Every point of mix that shifts up the ladder lifts the average margin per tonne without needing a single extra tonne of capacity.

The value-added share has climbed from roughly a third toward half of volumes. The goal is to keep pushing it up — because a coated API tonne earns multiples of a plain black tonne, mix is the cleanest way to raise pipe margins.

The logic is simple arithmetic on the conversion spread. A tonne of plain ERW pipe might earn a modest few thousand rupees of EBITDA; a tonne of galvanised, coated or API-grade pipe earns considerably more, because it has passed through extra processing steps that customers pay for and rivals can't all match. So the same mill, the same tonnage and broadly the same steel input can throw off very different profit depending on what comes out the far end. Raising the value-added share is therefore worth more to margins than simply running the plant harder.

The metric that runs the steel engine: EBITDA per tonne

Watch two numbers together and you understand Surya's pipes: tonnage (is the plant selling more?) and EBITDA per tonne (is each tonne worth more?). Recently EBITDA per tonne has sat in the ₹4,000–5,000 range and has been squeezed — a fall in the high-margin API and coated categories, plus steel-price inventory losses, pulled it down in FY26 even as volumes grew. That is the whole tension in one line: volume growth is real and steady, but the quality of that volume — the value-added mix and the per-tonne spread — is what decides whether the steel engine's profit actually rises. A tonnage-up, margin-down quarter is a warning; a tonnage-up, per-tonne-up quarter is the thesis working.

This is also why capacity expansion and mix improvement have to move together. Adding tonnage that all lands at the bottom of the ladder would grow revenue and barely touch profit. Surya's plan — the DFT mills and hollow-section push in Section 06 — is explicitly about adding capacity in the higher-value categories, so that more tonnage and richer mix arrive at the same time. Get that combination right and the thin-margin engine can grow profit faster than tonnage; get only the tonnage and the spread stays flat.

06

Capacity, DFT and the export push

The steel engine's growth plan — more tonnes, newer mills, and a bigger export book.

The steel business is in a deliberate capacity-build phase, and it is doing it with modern technology aimed at the higher-value end. The headline plan: lift pipe capacity from about 14 lakh tonnes toward 16 lakh in FY27, and on toward 2 million tonnes (20 lakh) by FY28–29 — a roughly 40% expansion over a few years, weighted to structural and value-added product.

Three new Direct Forming Technology (DFT) mills are commissioning through late 2026, plus a brownfield expansion at Hindupur (first mill due early 2027, ~3 lakh tonnes). The build is tilted toward hollow-section and value-added pipe, not plain tube.

Direct Forming Technology (DFT) is the tell in this plan. DFT mills can produce a wide range of pipe sizes — especially the square and rectangular hollow sections used as structural steel — with less waste and faster changeovers than older mills. That matters because structural/hollow-section pipe is a fast-growing, better-margin category (the same category APL Apollo built an empire on). By adding DFT capacity, Surya is buying its way further up the value ladder rather than just making more of the cheap stuff.

Exports are the second growth leg, targeted to nearly double from ~1.36 lakh tonnes in FY26 toward ~2.5 lakh tonnes, led by high-spec API line pipe. Surya has reported a sizeable book of US API export orders (~78,000 tonnes) and is opening newer geographies including the UK — exports being both a growth and a margin lever, since API is value-added.

Why exports do double duty

Growing exports isn't just about finding more buyers — it is a mix upgrade in disguise. The pipe that clears export specifications, and especially the API line pipe headed to the US and other markets, sits at the high-margin end of Surya's range. So doubling the export book from ~1.36 to ~2.5 lakh tonnes does two things at once: it adds volume the domestic market alone might not absorb, and it tilts the overall mix toward value-added product. The risk is the flip side of any export story — ocean freight (a real EBITDA headwind in recent quarters), trade policy, and currency can all move against you between order and delivery.

Put the pieces together and the steel engine's growth plan is coherent: more capacity, built with technology aimed at structural and value-added pipe, feeding a domestic infrastructure/water/gas cycle and a deliberately expanded export book. If it executes, tonnage and mix rise together and the thin-margin engine earns more per tonne even as it makes more of them. If steel prices stay volatile and the value-added categories soften, the same expansion just adds low-spread tonnage — growth without profit.

07

The lighting turn: from bulbs to a branded electricals business

How the consumer engine grows — and why its margin, not its size, is what counts.

The lighting engine has already lived through one wrenching transition — the industry-wide shift from conventional lamps to LED — and it is now in the slower, more valuable phase of that story: turning a lightbulb brand into a broader branded-electricals business. The growth here doesn't come from tonnage; it comes from distribution, premiumisation and product.

On roughly a quarter of the revenue, lighting ran the higher EBITDA margin in Q1 FY27. Steel's margin was compressed by high ocean freight and steel-price effects; even in better steel quarters, branded lighting tends to be the steadier, richer engine per rupee.

The LED transition was existential when it happened. When the market flipped from filament and CFL to LED, every lighting company had to reinvent its product line or die — LED lamps last longer, use far less power, and collapsed the old replacement-driven volume model. Surya made the jump and kept its position among the leading domestic lighting names. That transition is now largely behind the industry, which changes the game: growth no longer comes from converting the country to LED (mostly done), but from premium mix (higher-value luminaires, smart and decorative lighting, BLDC fans, appliances) and from widening distribution into more towns, more dealers and more of the modern-retail and e-commerce shelf.

  • Premiumisation — nudging the basket from commodity bulbs toward luminaires, professional and architectural lighting, energy-efficient fans and appliances that carry better margins and less price competition.
  • Distribution — the classic consumer-electricals lever: more dealers, deeper rural and semi-urban reach, and stronger positions in modern trade and online, so the brand shows up where buyers are.
  • Institutional & streetlight tenders — government and municipal LED-streetlight and lighting programmes, a lumpy but large channel Surya has long served.
  • Exports — a small but fast-growing lighting export book (pushed toward the ₹60–70 Cr range, up ~30% or more), extending the brand beyond India.
The comparison that sets the ceiling — and the opportunity

The right yardstick for Surya's lighting arm is not its own steel engine; it is Havells, Bajaj Electricals and Crompton — branded electricals companies that trade at rich, consumer-style multiples because investors value their brands and distribution. Surya's lighting business competes in the same aisles but has never been valued on that basis, because it is buried inside a pipe company and priced with it. That is the opportunity the market keeps circling: if Surya's consumer engine could be seen — and valued — as a stand-alone branded-electricals business, its worth would be judged against Havells and Crompton, not against APL Apollo. The gap between those two valuation worlds is precisely what a demerger would try to unlock.

08

The demerger question: should the two engines split?

The defining live issue — and the one that moves the stock more than earnings do.

In May 2026, on nothing more than speculation that Surya Roshni might separate its steel-pipe and lighting businesses into two independent companies, the stock jumped roughly 25% in a matter of days. No board resolution, no scheme, no swap ratio — just the rumour. That reaction is the single most revealing event in the company's recent life, because of what it says about how the market values the two engines together versus apart.

Blended together, Surya is priced closer to a plain pipe maker (~15× earnings, ~1× sales) than to a branded-consumer company. Freed and valued separately, a stand-alone lighting business could in principle command a far higher multiple than the combined entity gets today. That gap is the whole demerger thesis.

The argument for splitting is a sum-of-the-parts one. When a branded-consumer business and a commodity business live in one share, the market rarely pays a full consumer multiple for the good half — it discounts the whole thing toward the cyclical half, because the cash flows are pooled and the reporting is blended. Free the lighting business as its own listed company and it can be valued against Havells and Crompton; free the pipe business and it can be valued against APL Apollo as a focused structural-pipe play. The parts, separately, may add up to more than the combined whole is worth. That is why private-equity names (Warburg Pincus, Bain Capital have been reported as interested) and strategic buyers like Crompton have reportedly circled the lighting asset.

The device unique to Surya: the small engine may be worth as much as the big one

Run the logic to its conclusion and you get the fact that makes this company genuinely unusual. Steel pipes are ~76% of revenue but earn a thin, cyclical spread and a pipe-maker's multiple. Lighting is ~24% of revenue but earns a fatter, steadier margin and — valued as a consumer brand would be — a much richer multiple. Put those together and the quarter of the business people actually recognise as "Surya" could be worth close to as much, to a valuer, as the three-quarters they've never heard of. A demerger is simply the act of letting the market price each on its own terms. The 25% pop on a mere rumour was the market saying it believes that gap is real — and that the combined structure has been hiding it.

Honest status check: as of the latest results, this is not a done deal. The board has flagged a reorganisation review and asked a committee to weigh options, but there is no approved scheme, no swap ratio and no timeline — management has called it a matter still awaiting "board consensus." A demerger is a multi-year legal and tax exercise even once approved, and it can be watered down, delayed or dropped. So the split is a real, value-relevant possibility to track — not a catalyst to bank. Read every "demerger" headline against that gap between an option under study and a resolution passed.

There is a counter-argument worth holding too. Keeping the two together shares overheads, pools cash across cycles, and lets a strong balance sheet in one business support investment in the other — a structure that has funded fifty years of growth. A split is not free: it incurs cost, splits the treasury, and exposes each business to the market's full glare on its own numbers. Whether the re-rating outweighs the cost of separation is exactly the calculation the board is now making — in full public view, with the share price hanging on the answer.

09

Two arenas, two very different fights

Surya competes against pure pipe makers on one flank and pure electricals brands on the other.

Because it runs two engines, Surya Roshni fights on two fronts at once — and its rivals in one arena have nothing to do with its rivals in the other. Understanding the company means seeing both battle lines, because a pure pipe maker and a pure lighting company are each a cleaner, more focused competitor than Surya is in either ring.

The strategic shape to hold in mind

Surya's position in each arena is "strong but not the pure-play leader." In pipes, APL Apollo is the focused champion the market prices as the structural-tube play; Surya is broader (GI, API, exports) but carries the drag of a second business. In lighting, Havells and Crompton are the branded-electricals reference points; Surya has a real brand and distribution but less scale and capital behind it than a dedicated electricals company. That "good in both, purest in neither" position is a strength operationally — diversification smooths the cycle — and a weakness for the multiple, because focused rivals are easier for the market to value and reward. It is, again, the case for separation stated from the competitive angle.

The numbers that settle the thesis: value-added mix, lighting margin — and the demerger decision

At ~15× earnings and roughly one times sales, Surya is not priced as a growth compounder — it is priced as a steady, cyclical pipe maker with a lighting side-business. So the case turns on three specific, checkable numbers, not on a vague re-rating hope. First, the value-added share of steel volume and EBITDA per tonne — is the mix climbing and the per-tonne spread holding, so tonnage growth actually becomes profit growth? Second, the lighting segment's margin and revenue growth — is the consumer engine compounding at branded-electricals rates and holding its ~8% margin? Third, and uniquely for this company, the demerger decision — does the board actually move to separate the two, letting the market price the lighting brand against Havells rather than against pipe? Track those three and you are tracking the whole investment case; miss them and you are just watching steel prices bounce the quarterly print around.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cash-generative two-engine manufacturer, cheaply priced, waiting on mix and a structural decision.

Surya Roshni enters FY27 in solid operating shape: near net-cash, a capacity ramp aimed at value-added pipe, a resilient lighting brand, and a modest valuation. What it is waiting on is proof that the operating levers — richer pipe mix, higher lighting margins — can lift blended profitability through the steel cycle, and clarity on whether the two businesses will ultimately go their separate ways.

The bull case

  • Cheap on the parts — a ~15× blended multiple undervalues a branded lighting business that, freed, could be priced against Havells/Crompton; a demerger is a genuine re-rating lever.
  • Mix & capacity together — DFT mills and hollow-section/API push lift both tonnage and value-added share, so profit can grow faster than revenue.
  • Durable pipe demand — water, gas, infrastructure and structural steel underpin years of pipe tonnage; exports and US API add a growth leg.
  • Higher-quality lighting engine — a branded consumer business growing in the low-20s percent at a steadier, richer margin than steel.
  • Strong balance sheet — near net-cash, mid-teens ROCE, a dividend — funds its own expansion without leverage risk.

The bear case

  • Steel-price cyclicality — thin pipe spreads swing with coil prices; inventory losses and freight can sink a quarter regardless of volume, as FY26 showed.
  • Demerger may not happen — the re-rating case leans on a split that is only under study, with no scheme, ratio or timeline; it can be delayed or dropped.
  • Purest in neither arena — APL Apollo out-focuses it in pipe, Havells/Crompton out-scale it in electricals; the split-focus discount is real.
  • Margin structurally low — even at its best the pipe engine earns single-digit margins; blended profitability is modest and mix gains can be slow.
  • Execution & competition — capacity adds must find value-added demand, not just cheap tonnage; lighting faces well-capitalised branded rivals.
The bottom line

Surya Roshni is two decent businesses sharing one modestly priced share: a national pipe champion that lives and dies by the steel spread, and a branded lighting-and-fans business that quietly earns the better margin. For fifty years they've been run and valued as one — which is why the whole company gets a pipe-maker's price even though a quarter of it behaves like a consumer brand. The next chapters are already visible in three numbers to watch: the value-added pipe mix climbing past its current ~47%, the lighting segment's margin holding near 8% while it grows in the low-20s percent, and — the one that could reprice the whole thing overnight — whether the board turns a demerger review into an actual resolution. Two of those are operating grind; the third is a decision. Watch which of them prints first.
