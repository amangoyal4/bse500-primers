Vedanta, a leveraged basket of commodities in the middle of splitting itself apart

A complete walk through India's largest diversified natural-resources group — a single company that mines and smelts aluminium, zinc, silver, copper, iron ore and ferro-alloys, pumps oil and gas, and runs power plants, so its earnings rise and fall with world metal and oil prices it cannot control. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) the group posted record revenue of ₹1,74,075 crore (+15%), record EBITDA of ₹55,976 crore (+29%) and record profit of ₹25,096 crore (+22%). But the story that now defines it is the five-way demerger — completed in mid-2026 — that split Vedanta into separately-listed aluminium, oil-&-gas, power and iron-&-steel companies, leaving a residual Vedanta wrapped around its crown jewel, Hindustan Zinc. Hanging over all of it: a mountain of debt, at both Vedanta and its even more leveraged parent Vedanta Resources, and one of the market's highest dividend yields — cash that flows up to the promoter to service that debt.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Vedanta Limited is India's largest diversified natural-resources company — a sprawling conglomerate that digs, smelts and pumps a whole basket of commodities: it is the country's biggest aluminium producer, controls the near-monopoly zinc-silver miner Hindustan Zinc, owns India's largest private oil & gas business (Cairn), and runs iron ore, steel, ferro-alloys, copper and power businesses on top. It is controlled by Anil Agarwal's London-based holding company, Vedanta Resources. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) it earned record revenue of ₹1,74,075 crore, record EBITDA of ₹55,976 crore and record profit of ₹25,096 crore. And in 2026 it did something dramatic: it split itself into five separately-listed companies.

Five facts frame everything:

  • It is a leveraged bet on commodity prices. Aluminium, zinc, copper, iron ore and oil all sell at single world prices set on global exchanges (the LME, Brent). Vedanta is a price-taker on every one of them. So its profit is the gap between those world prices and its own cost of production — and because the group carries debt, small moves in those prices swing the equity value a lot. Owning Vedanta is, in effect, a geared basket-bet on metals and oil (§01).
  • The demerger is the defining event. To unlock value and simplify the structure, Vedanta split into five pure-play companies — Vedanta Aluminium, Vedanta Oil & Gas, Vedanta Power, Vedanta Iron & Steel, and a residual Vedanta (which keeps Hindustan Zinc, copper and new ventures). Four of them listed on 15 June 2026; you now buy the pieces, not one giant (§02).
  • Aluminium and zinc are the two big engines. Between them they generate the large majority of profit: aluminium ~half of group EBITDA (a huge, low-cost, vertically integrated business), and Zinc India ~39% (Hindustan Zinc, the crown jewel — §03, §04). Oil, iron/steel and power are the smaller cylinders.
  • Debt is the shadow over everything. Vedanta itself is moderately geared (net debt/EBITDA ~0.95x). But one level up, parent Vedanta Resources carries heavy debt, and it relies on dividends and fees pulled up from Vedanta to service it. That promoter-cash-upstreaming — funding a huge dividend partly to feed the parent — is the core controversy and the heart of the bear case (§06–§08).
  • It is a very high-yield, very cheap, very cyclical stock. The residual Vedanta trades at a low single-digit P/E and a double-digit dividend yield — the market's way of pricing in the debt, the governance discount and the commodity risk. The bull case is a sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) value-unlock; the bear case is that the discount is deserved (§10).
In plain terms

Think of Vedanta as a whole mining-and-drilling empire strapped to a large debt engine, now being cut into pieces. The empire is real and valuable: it makes aluminium more cheaply than most of the world, it owns one of the planet's best zinc-and-silver mines, it pumps a quarter of India's private oil, and it earns a torrent of cash when metal prices are firm. But two things complicate the picture. First, everything it sells is a global commodity whose price it can't set — so its profits heave up and down with world markets, and because there's borrowed money on top, the share price heaves even more. Second, the whole group sits under a debt-laden promoter (Vedanta Resources) that needs cash pulled up from below to stay current — which is why Vedanta pays such enormous dividends, and why minority shareholders worry about whose interests the cash really serves. The 2026 demerger is management's answer: break the conglomerate into five clean, single-commodity companies so investors can value (and the group can borrow against) each one on its own merits — and, ideally, close the "conglomerate discount". Whether that unlocks lasting value or simply re-labels the same debt and governance questions across five tickers is the debate around Vedanta today.

01

A leveraged basket of commodities — the one idea that drives everything

Why Vedanta's profit is really a bet on prices it does not control — amplified by debt.

Before any of the segments make sense, grasp the single mechanism beneath all of them. Every product Vedanta sells — aluminium, zinc, silver, copper, iron ore, crude oil, natural gas — is a global commodity with a single world price, set on exchanges like the London Metal Exchange (LME) or the Brent oil market. Vedanta doesn't set those prices; it takes them. An identical tonne of aluminium sells for the same price whether it comes from Odisha, China or the Middle East. So Vedanta, like any commodity producer, cannot compete on price — it can only compete on cost. Its profit on each unit is simply the gap between the world price (handed to it by the market) and its own cost of digging, smelting or pumping (which it controls).

The left side is given to Vedanta by the world market — it can't change it. The right side is the only thing it controls. That is why "low-cost producer" is the entire competitive strategy for each segment, and why cost-cutting (cheaper captive power, higher-grade ore, backward integration) matters so much.

Why "diversified" cuts both ways

Vedanta is unusual because it doesn't bet on one commodity — it holds a whole basket. That has an upside and a downside. The upside is diversification: when zinc is weak, aluminium or oil may be strong, smoothing the group's earnings versus a pure single-metal miner. The downside is that a broad commodity downturn — a global slowdown that drags all metals and oil down together, as often happens — hits every cylinder at once, with nowhere to hide. And because commodities are correlated (they all ride the same world growth cycle, the same China demand, the same US dollar), the diversification is real but partial.

Why debt turns a commodity swing into an equity earthquake — operating + financial leverage

Here is the part that makes Vedanta so much more volatile than the metal prices themselves. Two kinds of "leverage" stack on top of each other. First, operating leverage: a miner has huge fixed costs (mines, smelters, power plants) that don't shrink when prices fall. So if the aluminium price drops 15%, the cost per tonne barely moves, and almost the entire price fall comes straight out of profit — profit falls far more than 15%. Second, financial leverage (debt): Vedanta and especially its parent carry a lot of borrowed money, and the interest on that debt is fixed regardless of prices. Debt-holders get paid first; shareholders get what's left. So when EBITDA drops, the slice left for equity drops even harder. Layer the two together and you get the defining feature of Vedanta as an investment: a modest move in world commodity prices produces an outsized move in profit, and an even larger move in the share price. This is why the stock can double in a good metals year and halve in a bad one. It is not a defect — it is what a leveraged, diversified commodity play is. The reward for bearing that volatility is a cheap valuation and a fat dividend; the risk is that in a bad cycle the debt bites hardest exactly when earnings are weakest.

The reading note for a non-specialist: don't try to judge Vedanta on any one quarter's profit — that's mostly the commodity weather, out of the company's hands. Judge it on three things it can influence: its cost position in each metal (is it low-cost enough to earn through the cycle?), its debt load (can it survive a downturn?), and its capital allocation (does cash fund growth, or get pulled out as dividends?). The price cycle is the backdrop; those three are the company.

02

The demerger — the event that now defines Vedanta

One giant conglomerate became five focused, separately-listed companies in 2026.

For decades Vedanta was a single, sprawling company: aluminium, zinc, oil, iron, steel, copper and power all inside one balance sheet. In 2026 that changed. To unlock value and simplify the structure, Vedanta demerged into five separately-listed companies, each a pure play on one part of the empire. The plan, board-approved in April 2026 with a record date of 1 May 2026, cleared the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT) and completed with the listing of four new entities on the NSE and BSE on 15 June 2026. If you held one Vedanta share on the record date, you now hold that same (residual) Vedanta share plus one share each in the four new companies — a 1:1 entitlement.

Why break up a working conglomerate? — the "conglomerate discount" and value unlock

The logic is a classic one in finance, and it's worth understanding. When six different businesses sit inside one company, the stock market tends to value the bundle at less than the sum of its parts — a phenomenon called the conglomerate discount. Why? Because a specialist aluminium investor doesn't want to also own oil and zinc; a zinc fund can't buy the pure asset without the messy rest; analysts struggle to model six cyclical businesses at once; and the group's overall debt and governance concerns tar every good asset with the same brush. So the market applies a haircut to the whole. The demerger's promise is to remove that haircut. By splitting into five clean, single-commodity companies, each can attract its natural investor base and be valued on its own metrics — a low-cost aluminium smelter can trade like a low-cost aluminium smelter, not like "a bit of a debt-laden conglomerate". Analysts' sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) valuations put the five pieces together at well above where the combined Vedanta traded before — the value-unlock thesis. There's a second, quieter motive too: debt. Splitting the group lets each entity carry its own borrowings matched to its own cash flows (aluminium takes the most, oil & gas almost none), and — crucially — lets the promoter potentially raise money against, or sell down, individual pieces to pay down the heavy debt above. The bear counter: a demerger doesn't create a single rupee of new cash flow or erase a single rupee of debt — it just re-labels the same assets and liabilities across five tickers. If the governance and leverage issues follow the assets (and they do), the discount may simply reappear on the parts.

Where things stand (mid-2026): the demerger is done — Vedanta Aluminium, Oil & Gas, Power and Iron & Steel listed on 15 June 2026, and residual Vedanta Ltd (VEDL, the ticker in this primer) now is essentially a holding company around Hindustan Zinc plus copper, base metals and ventures. Note one earlier wrinkle: a government ministry had objected to aspects of the scheme at the NCLT (given the state's residual stakes in Hindustan Zinc/BALCO), but the tribunal ultimately approved it. From here, each piece lives or dies on its own commodity and balance sheet.
03

Aluminium — the biggest engine

India's largest aluminium business, and the single largest contributor to Vedanta's profit.

If you want to understand where most of Vedanta's cash comes from, start here. Aluminium is the group's biggest EBITDA driver — roughly half of total group EBITDA in FY26 — and Vedanta is India's largest primary aluminium producer, with capacity of around 2.8 million tonnes a year. In FY26 it produced a record 2.46 million tonnes of aluminium at its smelters, and it did so at the lowest cost of production in five years — about $1,752 per tonne. The whole aluminium business now trades as a separate company, Vedanta Aluminium (§02), but the economics are worth understanding whichever ticker you own.

How aluminium is made — and why integration is the moat

Making aluminium is a three-step chain, and Vedanta owns all of it — the source of its cost advantage:

  • Bauxite → alumina. Aluminium starts as bauxite ore, which is refined into a white powder called alumina at Vedanta's Lanjigarh refinery (Odisha). In FY26 Lanjigarh produced a record 2.9 million tonnes of alumina (up 48%) and was exiting the year at a 4-MTPA run-rate — a big step toward feeding its own smelters instead of buying alumina on the market.
  • Alumina → aluminium. Alumina is smelted into metal in vast electrolytic pot-lines — an incredibly power-hungry process. Vedanta's smelters are at Jharsuguda (Odisha) and BALCO (Bharat Aluminium, Chhattisgarh). Smelting aluminium is essentially "congealed electricity": power is the single biggest cost, often a third or more of the total.
  • Captive power. Because electricity dominates the cost, Vedanta runs large captive power plants (mostly coal, increasingly with renewables and its own coal linkages/mines) next to the smelters. Controlling its own cheap power is the core of its cost advantage.
Why owning the whole chain makes Vedanta a low-cost smelter — vertical integration

Here's the mechanism that puts Vedanta's aluminium among the more competitive in the world. A smelter that has to buy its alumina and buy its power on the open market is at the mercy of two volatile input prices — and earns a thin, exposed margin. Vedanta instead owns the bauxite links, the alumina refinery (Lanjigarh), the smelters (Jharsuguda, BALCO) and the power plants that feed them. Every step it internalises is a step where it captures margin instead of paying it to a supplier, and a step it insulates from market swings. As Lanjigarh ramps up to fully feed the smelters, Vedanta stops paying the global alumina price and starts making that alumina itself — a direct cost saving that showed up in FY26's five-year-low cost of $1,752/tonne. The pay-off is exactly the price-taker logic from §01: since Vedanta can't control the LME aluminium selling price, the only way to widen its margin is to grind its cost down — and vertical integration plus cheap captive power is how it does that. This is why aluminium, despite being a brutally competitive global commodity, is Vedanta's biggest and most durable profit engine. The remaining vulnerability: it's still coal-heavy power, so carbon costs and coal prices are the long-run risk to that cost edge.

Where aluminium demand comes from: aluminium is light, conductive and corrosion-resistant, so it's everywhere modern economies grow — power transmission cables, construction, autos and EVs (light-weighting), packaging (cans, foil), and electronics. The flip side: it's deeply cyclical and China makes over half the world's supply, so the LME price swings on Chinese output and global growth.

04

Zinc & the Hindustan Zinc crown jewel

The other big engine — and the asset that now anchors the residual Vedanta.

Vedanta's second great profit engine, and arguably its finest single asset, is Hindustan Zinc (HZL) — the ~63%-owned, separately-listed subsidiary that is India's near-monopoly zinc producer and one of the world's largest, lowest-cost integrated zinc-lead-silver miners. In FY26 the Zinc India business earned EBITDA of ₹22,056 crore — around 39% of Vedanta's consolidated EBITDA. After the demerger, HZL is the single most valuable thing inside the residual Vedanta Ltd (the VEDL you buy today): most of that residual company's worth is simply its stake in Hindustan Zinc.

Why Hindustan Zinc matters so much to a Vedanta shareholder — the "holdco" you're really buying

This is the single most important thing to grasp about the residual Vedanta after the 2026 demerger. Once aluminium, oil, power and steel were carved out into their own listed companies, what's left in Vedanta Ltd is dominated by its ~63% stake in the separately-listed Hindustan Zinc (plus smaller copper, base-metals and new-venture pieces). That makes residual Vedanta effectively a holding company whose value is mostly a claim on HZL's cash flows and dividends. Two consequences follow. First, HZL is a superb asset — a near-monopoly, first-decile-cost zinc miner with a huge free silver kicker and a fortress balance sheet — so residual Vedanta owns something genuinely world-class. Second, holding companies almost always trade at a "holdco discount" to the value of what they own, because you get the asset only indirectly, filtered through Vedanta's own debt, taxes and governance. So the residual Vedanta is, in plain terms, a leveraged, discounted, indirect way to own a wonderful zinc mine — with the upside that the discount could narrow, and the downside that Vedanta's debt and the promoter's cash needs sit between you and HZL's cash. (Hindustan Zinc has its own full primer in this library — worth reading alongside this one.)

The silver bonus: HZL's Rajasthan ore carries silver as a byproduct, so Vedanta is quietly one of the world's largest silver producers. With silver at multi-decade highs, this near-pure-margin metal was a meaningful driver of FY26's record profits — and gives Vedanta a free, leveraged bet on precious-metal prices on top of its base-metals basket.

05

Oil & gas, iron & steel, and power — the other cylinders

Smaller than aluminium and zinc, but each a real business — and now each its own listed company.

Beyond the two big engines, Vedanta ran three further businesses — oil & gas, iron/steel/ferro-alloys, and power — that together rounded out the diversified basket and now trade as their own demerged entities. Each has a distinct commodity exposure, which is precisely why splitting them out lets investors pick the exposure they want.

Oil & gas — Cairn, India's largest private crude producer

Cairn Oil & Gas is Vedanta's energy arm and India's largest private-sector oil producer, pumping roughly a quarter of the country's domestic crude. Its heartland is the Rajasthan / Barmer block, with additional offshore and onshore assets. In FY26 it produced average gross operated volumes of about 87.2 thousand barrels of oil equivalent per day (kboepd), and it announced new discoveries (e.g. Ambe on the west coast; Hazarigaon gas in Assam) as it fights the natural decline of ageing fields. Its profit rides the Brent oil price minus its lifting cost — the same price-taker logic as the metals. Notably, the demerged oil & gas company is essentially net-debt-neutral, making it the least-leveraged of the five entities.

Iron ore, steel & ferro-alloys

Vedanta's ferrous business spans iron ore mining in India (Goa, Karnataka) and Liberia, the ESL steel plant, and pig iron and ferro-alloys (ferrochrome, used in stainless steel). In FY26 it produced roughly 895 kt of pig iron and ~101 kt of ferrochrome. It's the smallest of the segments and the most fragmented, but it adds another commodity leg (iron ore / steel prices) to the basket. It carries relatively little of the group's debt.

Power

Vedanta operates around 4,780 MW of thermal power across four plants (including Talwandi Sabo in Punjab). Power plays a dual role: it feeds the group's own smelters (captive power is the backbone of the aluminium cost advantage, §03) and sells surplus electricity to the grid (merchant/PPA power). In FY26 the group commissioned around 1.3 GW of new capacity. As a standalone entity, Vedanta Power is a regulated-ish cash generator, valued more like a utility than a cyclical miner.

Why five separate companies makes sense — different commodities, different investors

Look at what the four demerged businesses actually are: an aluminium smelter (bet on LME aluminium + power costs), an oil producer (bet on Brent, net-debt-free), a power utility (steady, regulated-style cash flows), an iron & steel operation (bet on steel/iron-ore), and the zinc-anchored residual (bet on zinc + silver). These are wildly different risk profiles that used to be blended into one confusing stock. A pension fund that wants a stable, dividend-paying utility can now buy Vedanta Power without also taking oil-price risk. A commodity fund bullish on aluminium can buy the pure smelter. An income investor can target the high-yield zinc holdco. By letting each business find its natural owner and its natural valuation multiple — utilities trade rich, cyclical miners trade cheap — the theory is that the sum of the five clean pieces is worth more than the one muddled whole. Whether that theory survives contact with the group's shared debt and governance overhang is §06–§08 and §10.

06

The high-debt + high-dividend tension

The core controversy — and the heart of the bear case.

Now to the issue that hangs over everything and defines the Vedanta debate. Vedanta is famous for two things at once that sit in tension: a lot of debt and an enormous dividend. Normally a heavily indebted company conserves cash to pay down borrowings; Vedanta instead pays out one of the market's largest dividends. Understanding why is the key to understanding the stock — and it leads directly up to the parent (§07).

The debt, at two levels

Debt at Vedanta exists on two separate storeys, and conflating them is the most common mistake:

  • Vedanta Limited (the listed company). At the operating-company level, leverage is actually moderate and improving: FY26 net-debt/EBITDA fell to 0.95x from 1.22x a year earlier, helped by record EBITDA and strong cash generation (cash rose to ₹28,485 crore). By itself, that's a manageable balance sheet for a cash-rich miner.
  • Vedanta Resources (the parent, "VRL"). One level up sits the promoter's London-based holding company, which historically carried far heavier, more expensive debt — the borrowings taken on to buy control of these assets in the first place. This is where the real leverage pressure has always lived. Encouragingly, VRL cut its net debt to about $4.9 billion (Mar 2026), down from ~$8.9 billion five years earlier, and in 2026 lined up a large (~$5.2 billion) refinancing to lower its interest costs after credit-rating upgrades.
Why a debt-laden group pays a huge dividend — the mechanism, plainly

This looks paradoxical until you see the structure. The heavy, urgent debt sits at the parent (Vedanta Resources), which is above the listed Vedanta and owns most of it. But the parent is just a holding company — it has no operations of its own and therefore no cash of its own. Its only real source of cash to service its debt is money pulled up from the operating companies below it — chiefly Vedanta Ltd, and through it, Hindustan Zinc. There are two main pipes for pulling that cash upward: dividends (Vedanta pays a big dividend; the parent, owning ~56%, receives its share) and brand / management fees (the parent charges the subsidiaries a fee for the "Vedanta" brand and services). So the enormous dividend isn't mainly a gift to ordinary shareholders — it is, in large part, the plumbing that carries cash up to the indebted parent so it can pay its lenders. Ordinary minority shareholders receive the same per-share dividend (that's the high yield you see), but the reason the payout is set so high is the parent's need, not a considered return-of-capital policy. That is the crux of the governance concern: the dividend is being sized to serve the promoter's balance sheet, and the money to fund it sometimes has to be borrowed or extracted from cash-rich subsidiaries like HZL — potentially at the expense of reinvestment or minority interests.

That refinancing-and-upstreaming risk, not the operating business, is what has periodically frightened Vedanta's lenders and shareholders.

07

The parent, Vedanta Resources, and the cash-upstreaming machine

To understand Vedanta the stock, you must understand the company above it.

Vedanta Limited does not sit at the top of the tree. Above it is Vedanta Resources Limited (VRL), the UK-based holding company through which billionaire Anil Agarwal and his family control the group. VRL owns roughly 56% of Vedanta Ltd (via entities like Twin Star Holdings). Because VRL is a pure holding company with the group's heaviest debt and no operations of its own, its financial health depends entirely on cash flowing up from Vedanta Ltd and Hindustan Zinc. This upstreaming — and the governance questions around it — is the single most important non-operating factor in the Vedanta story.

Cash travels up the chain via dividends and brand/management fees. The dividend that reaches an ordinary VEDL shareholder is the same per-share cash the parent receives — but the size of the payout is driven substantially by the parent's debt-servicing need.
The governance concern — related-party fees and forced payouts, explained fairly

Why do critics and short-sellers keep circling Vedanta? Because when a controlling shareholder both sets the dividend policy and needs the dividend for its own survival, the interests of minority shareholders and the promoter can diverge. Three specific mechanisms draw scrutiny. (1) Aggressive, sometimes debt-funded dividends: if a subsidiary is pushed to pay out more than it comfortably generates — even borrowing to do so — that serves the cash-hungry parent but can starve the business of reinvestment. (2) Brand and "strategic services" fees: the parent charges the operating companies a fee for the group brand and services; critics argue these related-party payments are a way to extract cash over and above dividends, bypassing the pro-rata fairness that dividends at least preserve. Hindustan Zinc's brand-fee payments to Vedanta, for instance, have been challenged by minority-shareholder groups and a US short-seller. (3) Complex, opaque structure: the multi-layer holdco chain, pledged shares and cross-flows make it hard for outsiders to be sure value isn't leaking upward. In fairness, none of this is illegal, related-party transactions do get board and (sometimes) shareholder approval, and Vedanta disputes the short-sellers' characterisations; the group has also genuinely deleveraged the parent. But the structural tension is real and permanent: as long as a heavily-indebted promoter controls the company and depends on its cash, the market will apply a governance discount — and that discount is a big reason the stock is so cheap (§10). This is the honest centre of the bear case.

Why the demerger connects here: splitting the group into five listed pieces also gives the promoter more levers to raise money against, or sell down, individual businesses to pay parent debt — which is partly why it was pursued. That can be value-accretive (cleaner entities, lower cost of capital) or, viewed cynically, simply five new taps from which cash can be drawn upward. Watch how each entity's dividend and fee policy is set post-listing.

08

Ownership, pledges & the governance discount

Who owns Vedanta, and why the share pledging matters.

Vedanta's shareholding tells the governance story in numbers. The promoter (Vedanta Resources / the Agarwal family) owns roughly 56%, with Twin Star Holdings alone holding ~40%. The rest is split between foreign institutions (~12%), domestic institutions (~7%) and a large retail base (~16%), the last drawn in substantially by the dividend yield. Two features make this ownership structure a live governance issue: share pledging and the Government of India's residual stake in Hindustan Zinc.

  • Pledged / encumbered promoter shares. A significant chunk of the promoter's Vedanta shares has historically been pledged or encumbered as collateral for Vedanta Resources' bond borrowings. Pledging is a red flag for governance-minded investors because it links the share price to the parent's debt: if the stock falls sharply, lenders can demand more collateral or, in extremis, sell pledged shares — a forced-selling risk that can feed on itself. Combined promoter entities (Twin Star + Welter Trading) have had large encumbered positions.
  • Promoter stake sales. The promoter periodically sells small slices of Vedanta (e.g. block deals) to raise cash for the parent — as happened again in June 2026, when a promoter block-sale knocked the stock. Each such sale is cash for the parent but a supply overhang for the share price.
  • The Government of India owns ~28% of Hindustan Zinc. The state never fully exited HZL after privatisation, so it sits as a partial counterweight to Vedanta's control of the crown jewel — and a potential future seller. It also objected to aspects of the demerger at the NCLT, given its stakes in HZL and BALCO, before the scheme was approved.
In plain terms — why all this shows up as a cheap valuation

Put the ownership picture together and you can see why the market values Vedanta so conservatively despite genuinely excellent assets. You have a controlling promoter that needs cash, funds itself partly by pledging the very shares you own and by selling slices into the market, and sits atop a complex multi-layer structure where value can travel upward through fees as well as dividends. None of these individually is fatal, and the promoter has been reducing parent debt — but collectively they mean an ordinary shareholder doesn't fully control the cash the assets generate, and faces recurring supply and forced-selling risks. The market's rational response is a governance discount: it pays a lower multiple for a rupee of Vedanta's earnings than it would for the same rupee at a cleanly-owned, low-debt miner. That discount is the price of the structure. The bull bet is that the demerger, deleveraging and rising transparency shrink the discount over time; the bear bet is that as long as the Agarwal group controls the company and needs its cash, the discount is permanent and deserved.

09

The company & how it got here

From a Mumbai scrap-metal trader to a diversified resources empire.

Vedanta's history is the story of Anil Agarwal's serial, debt-funded acquisitions — a pattern that built the empire and also seeded the leverage that defines it today. Understanding the timeline explains both the sprawling asset base and the persistent debt-and-governance questions.

  • 1970s–80s
    Anil Agarwal starts as a scrap-metal and cable trader in Mumbai, building Sterlite Industries into a copper and metals business.
  • 2002–03
    Sterlite/Vedanta acquires a controlling stake in Hindustan Zinc from the Government of India in a landmark privatisation — the crown jewel enters the fold (state retains ~28%).
  • 2003
    Vedanta Resources lists in London — the first Indian-founded company on the London Stock Exchange — raising capital to fund an acquisition spree.
  • 2007
    Acquires control of Sesa Goa (iron ore), expanding into the ferrous business.
  • 2011
    Buys Cairn India's oil & gas business (Rajasthan/Barmer) in a large, debt-funded deal — adding the energy leg and a big chunk of the group debt.
  • 2012–13
    Merges Sesa Goa and Sterlite to create Sesa Sterlite, later renamed Vedanta Limited — consolidating the Indian assets into one listed vehicle.
  • 2018–20
    Agarwal delists Vedanta Resources from London (2018) and repeatedly tries to take Vedanta Ltd private (2020 attempt fails) — moves that highlight the promoter's desire for control and cash flexibility.
  • 2022–23
    A push into semiconductors & display (a JV with Foxconn, later dissolved) signals diversification ambitions; heavy parent-debt maturities spark refinancing stress and rating scrutiny.
  • 2023–25
    Board approves the plan to demerge the group into pure-play companies; parent Vedanta Resources undertakes a multi-year deleveraging (net debt ~$8.9bn → ~$4.9bn).
  • 2026
    Record FY26 results (revenue ₹1.74 L Cr, EBITDA ₹55,976 Cr, PAT ₹25,096 Cr). The five-way demerger completes: record date 1 May, four new entities list on 15 June. Residual Vedanta becomes essentially a Hindustan Zinc holding company. Parent lines up a ~$5.2bn refinancing.
The through-line: Vedanta was built by buying world-class assets (Hindustan Zinc, Cairn, Sesa) with borrowed money, layered into a multi-tier holding structure. That strategy assembled a genuinely enviable resource portfolio — and simultaneously created the leverage and complexity that the market discounts and the 2026 demerger is trying to unwind.

Financials & valuation

Record earnings, a rock-bottom multiple, and a double-digit dividend yield — all at once.

FY26 was a genuine record year for Vedanta on every headline line, driven by firm aluminium volumes at a five-year-low cost, a strong zinc/silver contribution, and operating leverage. Yet the stock trades at one of the lowest multiples and highest dividend yields in the large-cap market — the numerical fingerprint of the debt, governance and cyclicality discussed above.

How to think about valuing Vedanta — three traps to avoid

Valuing Vedanta is genuinely tricky, and three traps catch newcomers. Trap 1 — the peak-earnings illusion. FY26 was a record, powered by firm aluminium, a five-year-low cost and a silver windfall. A P/E of ~6x on peak earnings can look cheap and yet be dear if earnings then mean-revert with the commodity cycle. Professionals normalise to mid-cycle prices and lean on EV/EBITDA across the cycle plus each segment's cost-curve position (a low-cost aluminium and first-decile zinc producer deserves a premium because it earns through downturns). Trap 2 — the yield trap. A ~11–16% dividend yield is seductive, but the payout ratio exceeds 100% and the dividend is set to serve the parent's debt (§06). A yield that high is the market warning you it may not be sustainable if the cycle turns — treat it as a risk signal, not a free lunch. Trap 3 — ignoring the demerger. After May 2026, the "Vedanta" you buy (VEDL) is a different, smaller company than the pre-demerger group — essentially a Hindustan Zinc holdco. Old ratios computed on the full conglomerate don't map onto the residual entity; you must value the pieces (SOTP) or the specific ticker you're buying. The honest bottom line: Vedanta is statistically very cheap for good reasons. The bull case is that the demerger and deleveraging finally close the gap between price and asset value; the bear case is that the cheapness is a permanent, deserved discount for debt, cyclicality and who controls the cash.

The one-off to flag: FY26's record was flattered by an unusually strong silver price and firm aluminium, and profits benefited from operating leverage on a low cost base — genuinely good, but partly cyclical. Separate the durable value (low-cost assets, integration) from the cyclical cherry (peak metal/silver prices) when reading the numbers.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

World-class assets, a value-unlock in motion — guarded by debt and a governance asterisk.

Vedanta is the sharpest expression of a familiar Indian-market dilemma: superb assets, wrapped in leverage and governance concerns, trading at a discount that could be an opportunity or a trap. Post-demerger, the debate splits five ways, but the core tension is unchanged — whether firm commodity prices and a cleaner structure let the value shine, or whether debt and promoter cash-needs keep the discount permanent.

The bull case

  • Genuinely world-class assets — India's largest, low-cost aluminium business ($1,752/t, 5-yr low); the near-monopoly, first-decile Hindustan Zinc with a free silver kicker; India's largest private oil producer. Few resource portfolios match it.
  • Value-unlock demerger — five clean, single-commodity companies can attract natural investors and shed the conglomerate discount; SOTP (~₹820) sits well above the old combined price.
  • Deleveraging underway — parent net debt down ~$8.9bn → ~$4.9bn; opco net-debt/EBITDA just 0.95x; a large refinancing lowering interest costs after rating upgrades.
  • Record cash generation & a huge yield — FY26 records across revenue, EBITDA and PAT; a double-digit dividend yield for income seekers.
  • Structural demand — aluminium and copper are energy-transition metals; zinc/silver ride infrastructure and solar; India-growth exposure across the basket.

The bear case

  • Debt & parent dependence — the heavily-indebted parent (VRL) relies on upstreamed dividends and brand fees; a bad cycle squeezes cash exactly when the debt still needs servicing.
  • Governance discount — related-party fees, aggressive/at-times debt-funded payouts, pledged promoter shares and periodic block-sales; short-seller scrutiny. Minorities don't fully control the cash.
  • Deep cyclicality + leverage — a pure price-taker across aluminium, zinc, copper, iron ore and oil; operating + financial leverage means a modest price fall can crush profits and the equity.
  • Demerger may just relabel the problem — splitting assets creates no new cash flow and erases no debt; the discount could reappear on the parts if governance follows the assets.
  • Payout sustainability — a >100% payout ratio and yield this high signal the dividend may be cut if the cycle turns; the yield is a warning as much as a reward.
The bottom line

Vedanta is a portfolio of genuinely excellent commodity assets strapped to a large debt engine and a demanding promoter — now cut into five pieces to try to close the gap between what those assets are worth and what the market will pay. The assets are not in doubt: India's largest and low-cost aluminium business, a world-class zinc-and-silver mine, the country's biggest private oil producer, real power and iron/steel operations. On firm metal and oil prices, this basket gushes cash — FY26 proved it, with records across the board. But the honest framing is the same one that has always applied to Vedanta: you are buying wonderful assets, but you do not fully control the cash they generate. A heavily-indebted parent sits above the listed company and depends on the dividends and fees pulled up from it; the promoter pledges and periodically sells the very shares you hold; and everything the group sells is a global commodity whose price swings the leveraged equity violently. The 2026 demerger is the serious attempt to fix the structure — cleaner entities, matched debt, natural investors, a shot at the value-unlock. Whether it fixes the governance is the open question, because splitting the company doesn't change who controls it or why they need its cash. Get firm commodities, disciplined deleveraging and fair capital allocation across the new entities, and Vedanta's pieces could re-rate hard from today's deep discount. Get a commodity downturn, a stretched parent and continued cash-extraction at the same time, and even a superb cost position can only cushion so much. It is one of the market's highest-risk, highest-yield, deepest-value bets on Indian and global commodities — and the governance asterisk never fully goes away.

The next chapter for Vedanta isn't about whether the mines and wells keep minting cash when prices are firm — they will. It's about whether five clean companies, a lighter parent and firmer commodity prices can finally out-argue the market's long-held suspicion of who the cash is really for. The assets were never the question; the structure was — and now the structure is on trial in public.
