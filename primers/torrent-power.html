00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Torrent Power is one of India's largest and best-run private integrated power utilities — the flagship power arm of the Ahmedabad-based Torrent group (the Mehta family, the same promoters behind Torrent Pharma, though this is a completely separate company). It does the whole electricity job end to end: it generates power (gas, coal and a fast-growing renewables fleet), it transmits it, and — this is the crown jewel — it distributes and sells electricity to ~4.2 million customers across Ahmedabad, Surat and other parts of Gujarat, plus franchise territories in Bhiwandi, Agra and Shil-Mumbra-Kalwa (Maharashtra/UP). In FY26 it earned a net profit of ₹2,469 crore on revenue of ₹28,966 crore.

Three facts frame the whole story:

  • The heart of the company is a regulated annuity, not a power plant. Around 70% of Torrent's EBITDA comes from its regulated + franchise distribution business — supplying power to homes and factories under tariffs that let it earn a government-assured ~14–15.5% return on equity. That is a steady, contracted, bond-like cash stream that most generators simply don't have. It is what makes Torrent Power a low-risk utility rather than a volatile merchant.
  • It runs India's most efficient discom operation. Torrent's distribution losses of 2.33% in its licensed areas are among the lowest in the country (many state discoms lose 15–25%), and it has topped national DISCOM rankings. This operational excellence is the moat — and it lets Torrent take over broken utilities and fix them for a franchise fee (Agra went from ~59% losses at takeover to ~7%).
  • That stable cash is now funding a big growth bet. Torrent is deploying ₹60,000 crore-plus of capex over FY26–32 to nearly treble its clean footprint: a renewables pipeline toward ~10 GW (solar + wind + hybrid), a 3 GW pumped-storage project, green hydrogen, plus more thermal (a ₹6,889 Cr acquisition of the 1,400 MW Nabha coal plant). The bull case is a re-rating on that growth; the bear case is the leverage and execution it demands.
In plain terms

Picture Torrent Power as a toll road that owns the power grid of several cities. Every month, millions of customers in Ahmedabad, Surat and beyond pay their electricity bill — and the regulator guarantees Torrent a fixed return on the money it sinks into wires, meters and substations. That's the toll: predictable, legally protected, and it grows as the network grows. Torrent runs that grid so well that it loses almost no power to theft or leakage, which is where the real profit hides. Now it's taking that dependable toll income and pouring it into a very different business — building giant solar and wind farms, a huge "water battery" (pumped storage), and even green hydrogen — to become a clean-energy growth company. The safe half pays for the ambitious half.

01

Power economics 101: how a utility actually earns

No engineering degree required — just the three different ways electricity makes money.

"Making money from electricity" is not one business — it is three very different businesses bolted together, each with its own risk profile. This is the single most useful idea for understanding Torrent Power, because it does all three, but the market treats them very differently — and Torrent deliberately leans on the safest one.

The three ways a power company earns — and how risky each is
ActivityHow it earnsRisk / who bears it
Generation (regulated / PPA)Builds a plant and signs a long-term Power Purchase Agreement (PPA) at a fixed tariff for 20–25 years, or earns a regulated return.Low — price and volume largely locked in. Main risk is fuel cost and the buyer paying on time.
Generation (merchant)Sells power on the open market/exchange at whatever price the market pays that day. Torrent's gas plants often run this way when demand spikes.High — profit swings with market prices, demand and fuel costs. This is the volatile "swing" part of Torrent.
Transmission & distributionRuns the wires that carry and deliver power. A regulator sets a tariff that lets the company earn a fixed return on equity (RoE) on the assets it builds.Very low — a regulated annuity. Invest capital, run efficiently, and the return is effectively legislated.
The single idea that unlocks Torrent: "regulated return"

In the wires-and-supply business, Torrent can't charge whatever it likes — a state regulator (the Gujarat Electricity Regulatory Commission, and its peers) decides the tariff using a formula. The regulator lets the utility recover its costs plus earn a fixed percentage return — roughly 14–15.5% post-tax — on the equity it has invested in poles, wires, meters and substations. So the more Torrent invests in approved grid assets, the more absolute profit it is allowed to earn — like a landlord whose rent is set by law at a guaranteed yield on every building they put up. That is why distribution is a "regulated annuity": boring, predictable, and it compounds as the asset base grows. It's the opposite of a merchant gas plant, whose profit can vanish overnight if power prices or gas costs move.

Torrent's whole strategy sits on this table. Its de-risked base is the regulated distribution annuity (bottom row) — ~70% of group EBITDA. On top of that stable platform it runs the volatile bit (merchant gas, middle row) as an upside swing, and is now building out the contracted bit (long-PPA renewables, top row) as the growth engine. Keep that structure in mind — it is the thread through every section that follows.

02

The integrated value chain — but weighted to the safe end

Why Torrent, unlike most, makes most of its money at the customer-facing end of the chain.

Most power companies do one thing: NTPC and Adani Power are mostly generators; Power Grid is a transmission company; a state discom is a distributor. Torrent Power, like Tata Power, is vertically integrated — it owns every link from the power plant to the plug. But its centre of gravity is unusual: unlike a typical integrated player, Torrent earns the bulk of its profit at the distribution (delivery) end, the safest link in the chain.

A pure generator lives and dies at Stage 1's volatile economics. Torrent makes most of its money — and nearly all its predictable money — at Stage 3, where a regulated/franchise return de-risks the whole group.

Why this structure matters — three concrete benefits

  • Earnings stability. When merchant gas profits wobble (a bad quarter for demand or gas prices — exactly what happened in Q4 FY26), the regulated distribution annuity keeps paying. Regulated businesses have delivered ~70% of EBITDA over the last three years — a large, steady base that absorbs the generation swings.
  • Fuel-cost pass-through. In its licensed distribution areas, most of the cost of buying power is passed through to customers via the tariff. So Torrent isn't betting its supply margin on power-price movements the way a merchant generator is — its distribution margin is a regulated return, not a trading spread.
  • A platform to grow. Because it runs a rock-solid, cash-generating distribution utility, Torrent has the balance-sheet strength and credibility (AA+/AAA-band ratings) to raise cheap debt and fund a large renewables + storage build-out — something a pure merchant player can't do as safely.
The plain-English payoff

A pure gas generator is a speed-boat: fast when conditions are good, but tossed around by every wave (gas prices, demand, merchant tariffs). Torrent is a large ship with a speed-boat lashed to the deck. The ship — the regulated distribution business — sails a steady course whatever the weather, which is why even in a soft quarter for gas the group stays solidly profitable and keeps paying dividends. The speed-boat (merchant gas) adds bursts of speed in good times; the new sails being hoisted (renewables + storage) are meant to make the whole vessel go faster over the next decade. That ballast is exactly why Torrent trades as a quality utility, not a punt on power prices.

From an Ahmedabad electricity licensee to the Torrent group's energy powerhouse.

04

The distribution annuity — the core of everything

The low-risk, cash-gushing engine that ~70% of the company's profit rests on.

If you understand one thing about Torrent Power, understand this: its licensed power-distribution business is the crown jewel. Torrent is the sole distribution licensee — a legal monopoly to supply power — across some of India's most industrialised, high-demand urban areas, and it earns a government-assured return on the capital it puts into that network. This is the annuity that de-risks the whole group.

The licensed footprint — a monopoly on prosperous cities

Across these licensed areas Torrent serves more than 4.2 million consumers and distributes roughly 30 billion units of electricity a year. Because these are licensed monopolies in growing, industrial regions, demand grows structurally with the local economy — and every new factory, mall or housing society is a new, captive customer.

Why the regulated-RoE model is such a good business

The regulator's job is to protect consumers from a monopoly — so it caps the tariff. But it does so with a formula that guarantees the utility a fair profit: Torrent is allowed to recover its efficient costs and earn a fixed post-tax return (roughly 14–15.5%) on the equity it has invested in the network, and it passes through the cost of the power it buys. So Torrent's distribution profit grows in two low-risk ways: (1) invest more capital into the network (bigger regulated asset base → bigger allowed profit — hence ~₹2,000 Cr a year of distribution capex planned), and (2) run more efficiently than the regulator assumed. That second lever is where Torrent shines.

The efficiency moat — losing almost nothing

Every utility "loses" some power between the plant and the meter — through theft, faulty meters, un-billed supply and technical leakage. This is measured as distribution / AT&C losses ("Aggregate Technical & Commercial"). For most Indian state discoms it is a chronic wound: 15–25% of all power generated is never paid for. That's the difference between a distributor that makes money and one that bankrupts a state government.

05

The franchise model — renting out the turnaround skill

A clever, capital-light way to earn from distribution networks Torrent doesn't even own.

Beyond its owned licensed cities, Torrent runs distribution franchises — a distinctive model where it takes over the running of a state-owned discom's network for a period, invests to fix it, and keeps the margin between the (low) rate it pays the state for power and what it collects from customers. It doesn't own the wires; it rents out its turnaround expertise. This is Torrent's operational excellence, monetised.

Torrent's distribution franchises — turnaround stories
FranchiseLocationThe turnaround
AgraUttar PradeshTaken over in 2010 with ~59% AT&C losses; driven down to a historic-low ~6.9% — one of India's great discom turnarounds.
BhiwandiMaharashtraIndia's first-ever distribution franchise (from 2007); the model that proved private operators could rescue a discom.
Shil-Mumbra-Kalwa (SMK)Maharashtra (near Mumbai)Taken over in 2021 with ~45% losses; already cut to ~28% and falling — the newest turnaround in progress.
How the franchise model actually makes money

Imagine a leaky, badly-run water utility. The city government keeps it but says: "whoever can plug the leaks and collect the bills can keep the difference for 10–20 years." That's a franchise. Torrent takes over a discom losing, say, half its power to theft and non-payment, and buys power from the state at an agreed rate ("input-based" franchise). Then it invests in new meters, replaces stolen connections, digitises billing and cracks down on theft. As losses fall — Agra went from 59% to under 7% — the same amount of power bought now generates far more collected revenue. Torrent keeps that widening gap. The beauty: it earns strong returns on relatively little asset ownership (it's the state's network), and every franchise is a repeatable use of a skill Torrent already has. The risk: franchises are time-limited contracts, and negotiating fair terms with cash-strapped state governments can be fraught.

Owned licences give Torrent a guaranteed return on capital; franchises give it a return on competence. Both rest on the same thing — being the best in India at running a discom.

Together, the licensed + franchise distribution business is why Torrent is often called a "distribution company that also generates power," rather than the other way round. It is the stable, annuity-like, low-risk cash engine that funds — and de-risks — everything the company is now building.

06

Gas generation & LNG — the swing factor

The most volatile piece of Torrent — a big gas fleet whose fortunes rise and fall with demand and fuel.

Torrent is one of India's largest gas-based power generators. Its ~5.1 GW of operating capacity is dominated by two big gas-fired combined-cycle plants — SUGEN (~1,148 MW) at Surat and DGEN (~1,200 MW) at Dahej — plus the smaller Sabarmati (AMGEN) coal station (362 MW) and its growing renewables. The gas fleet is efficient and modern; the problem has never been the plants — it's the fuel.

The gas problem — great plants, unreliable fuel

India lacks enough affordable domestic natural gas, so plants like DGEN must run on imported LNG, which is expensive and price-volatile. For years this left DGEN partly stranded — it had no long-term PPA and couldn't profitably run on costly LNG, so it operated at low utilisation (a plant load factor, or PLF, of only ~9–15% in recent years). A plant sitting idle earns little.

Why gas is the "swing factor" — the good years and the bad

Here's the mechanism. When India's power demand surges (a hot summer, a supply crunch), electricity prices on the merchant exchange spike — and the government can invoke Section 11 of the Electricity Act to compel gas plants to run. In those windows, Torrent fires up SUGEN and DGEN and sells power at high merchant prices, earning a fat, one-off profit. That's what happened in FY25, lifting earnings. But when demand is soft or gas is dear (as in Q4 FY26, when the generation segment actually swung to a small EBIT loss), that upside evaporates. So gas is Torrent's swing factor: it can add a lot of profit in a tight market and drag in a loose one. This is exactly the volatility the regulated distribution annuity is there to absorb — and why a weak gas quarter (like Q4 FY26) doesn't threaten the group, it just trims the upside.

Reading the FY26 numbers correctly: Torrent's headline profit fell in FY26 (PAT ₹2,469 Cr vs ₹3,059 Cr), and Q4 PAT dropped ~70% to ₹318 Cr — which looks alarming until you unpack it. Most of the fall is a high base, not a broken business: FY25 carried a ₹637 Cr one-off deferred-tax reversal gain and a strong merchant-gas contribution that didn't repeat. Adjusted for the one-off tax item, FY26's underlying comprehensive income actually edged up. The soft spot was purely the swing-factor gas business in a looser market — the regulated core kept compounding.

07

The renewables mega-build — the growth engine

The transformation from a gas-heavy utility into a clean-energy growth company.

If distribution is the stable heart and gas is the swing, renewables are Torrent's future. The company is executing one of the more ambitious clean-energy build-outs among India's private utilities — aiming to take its renewable footprint toward ~10 GW, and its total generation-plus-storage portfolio toward ~12 GW of generation and 3 GW of pumped storage. This is the growth thesis, and it is a genuine strategic pivot, not a slogan.

Why renewables fit Torrent so well

  • They swap volatile earnings for contracted ones. A solar or wind farm sells power under a 25-year PPA at a fixed tariff, with sunlight/wind as free fuel. That is the opposite of merchant gas — it's stable, bankable cash flow. As renewables grow, they steadily dilute the gas-driven volatility and add to the "predictable" bucket alongside distribution.
  • They ride India's structural tailwind. India is adding tens of gigawatts of solar and wind a year to hit its 2030 clean-energy targets. Torrent, with its balance-sheet strength, execution record and in-house transmission arm, is well-placed to win and build this capacity — including large projects in Gujarat's giant Khavda renewable zone.
  • They leverage the whole group. Torrent can build the generation, string the transmission line to evacuate it (its grid arm won lines for 4.5 GW of Khavda RE and 1,500 MW at Solapur), and — via storage — firm it up. Owning the chain is a real edge as the grid absorbs intermittent power.
The transformation in one sentence

For a decade, Torrent was a gas-and-distribution company whose growth was capped by the availability of affordable gas. The renewables build changes the plot: it gives Torrent a large, contracted, low-risk growth runway that doesn't depend on LNG prices at all. If it executes — commissioning the ~3.8 GW under construction on time and on budget, then scaling toward 10 GW — Torrent stops being "a gas utility with a great discom" and becomes "a clean-energy growth utility with a great discom." That re-rating potential is the whole bull case. The catch is execution and funding at scale (see §09).

08

Storage, hydrogen & the new coal — firming the portfolio

The pieces that make the renewables build actually work — plus a pragmatic return to coal.

Solar and wind have one flaw: they only produce when the sun shines or the wind blows. To build a reliable clean utility, you need ways to store and firm that power — and Torrent is investing in exactly that, alongside a pragmatic addition of dependable thermal capacity.

Pumped storage — the "water battery"

Torrent's flagship storage bet is a 3 GW pumped-storage hydro project in Raigad district, Maharashtra, at an estimated cost of ~₹14,000 crore, targeted for commissioning around October 2028. Pumped storage is the world's most proven grid-scale battery: when power is cheap/surplus (midday solar), it pumps water uphill to a reservoir; when power is scarce/expensive (evening peak), it lets the water fall back down through turbines to generate. It's a giant rechargeable battery made of water and gravity — and it earns by buying low and selling high, or under long-term storage contracts.

Green hydrogen — the early option

Torrent has secured 18 KTPA (kilo-tonnes per annum) of green-hydrogen capacity under the government's PLI (production-linked incentive) scheme and inaugurated an early green-hydrogen facility. This is a small, optional bet today — green hydrogen (made by splitting water with renewable electricity) is a nascent, next-generation clean fuel — but it positions Torrent early in a potentially large future market. Treat it as cheap optionality, not a core earnings driver.

Nabha Power — the pragmatic coal addition

In a move that surprised some, Torrent is acquiring the 1,400 MW Nabha Power coal plant (in Punjab) from L&T for an enterprise value of ₹6,889 crore, and is also planning a new ~1,600 MW coal project in Madhya Pradesh. Why add coal in a green-pivot story? Because India still needs firm, always-available "baseload" power to keep the grid stable as it adds intermittent renewables — and Nabha is a high-quality, well-run plant (PLF ~94%) with a long-term PPA, i.e. contracted, low-risk cash flow. It's a return-focused, de-risked addition, not a bet on merchant coal.

How the pieces fit together

Think of Torrent building a balanced clean-power system, not just solar farms. Renewables provide cheap, green energy but only intermittently. Pumped storage shifts that energy from when it's abundant to when it's needed — turning "sometimes" power into "on-demand" power. Firm thermal (Nabha) and gas provide the always-on backbone. And green hydrogen is a long-dated call option on a future fuel. Stitched together, this is a portfolio designed to sell reliable power around the clock — which is worth far more than raw intermittent solar. It's a more sophisticated growth plan than "just build solar," and it plays directly to Torrent's engineering and grid strengths.

09

The ₹60,000-crore-plus capex & the balance-sheet question

The mega-build that defines the next few years — and the leverage it will add.

Torrent's transformation carries a price tag: a capital-spending programme exceeding ₹60,000 crore over FY26–32, spanning renewables, pumped storage, transmission, distribution and thermal. How that money is spent — and funded — is the crux of the investment case, because it will move Torrent from an under-geared utility toward a normally-geared one.

How it's funded — and why leverage is the number to watch

  • A strong starting point. Unusually for a utility about to spend big, Torrent begins lightly geared: net-debt/equity of just 0.67× and net-debt/EBITDA of ~2.06× at FY26, with borrowings of ~₹14,000 Cr. That headroom is precisely why it can afford an ambitious plan — it's growing into a normal balance sheet, not stretching a stretched one.
  • The maiden equity raise. In 2025 Torrent completed a ₹3,500 Cr QIP at ₹1,503/share — the first equity raise by the Torrent group in ~three decades — using a large chunk to cut debt and prime the balance sheet for capex. A future listing/monetisation of the renewables arm is a widely-discussed potential funding lever.
  • Debt-funded, but matched to cash flows. Management guides the build will be financed at roughly a 70:30 debt-to-equity ratio. Debt will rise — that's the point of the plan — but most of it funds assets that produce contracted or regulated cash (a solar PPA, a regulated wire, a PPA-backed coal plant), so the borrowing is matched to predictable income rather than a merchant gamble.
The honest tension: the build is genuinely value-creating if every gigawatt earns its expected return and gets funded without over-stretching the balance sheet. But leverage will climb from today's comfortable 0.67× as the ₹60,000 Cr+ deploys, interest costs will rise, and big projects (pumped storage, giant RE) carry execution and timing risk. The bull watches the growing regulated-plus-contracted asset base; the bear watches net-debt/EBITDA and whether returns hold up as Torrent builds outside its home turf. Both are staring at the same capex plan.

Why the market pays ~28× for a "utility"

On the surface, ~28× earnings looks steep for a power company. But investors are paying for two things a plain generator lacks. First, earnings quality: ~70% of EBITDA is a regulated/franchise distribution annuity — bond-like, predictable cash that deserves a higher multiple than volatile merchant profit. Second, growth: the ₹60,000 Cr+ pivot into renewables and storage promises to grow the earnings base substantially over the next 5–7 years, and to add more contracted (high-quality) profit as it does. So the bull pays up for "a safe base plus a credible growth runway." The bear counters that ~28× and ~3.6× book leave little room if the gas swing keeps dragging, if the capex crimps near-term returns, or if leverage climbs faster than the new assets earn. That tension — quality-and-growth versus the price and the build risk — is the Torrent Power debate.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A best-in-class utility using its stable base to buy a growth story — priced for it to work.

Torrent Power is a rare thing: a genuinely well-run, cash-generative regulated utility that is also attempting a real growth transformation, from its position of balance-sheet strength. The debate isn't about whether the distribution annuity is good — it plainly is. It's about execution, funding and the price you pay for the renewables-and-storage build layered on top.

The bull case

  • Best-in-class distribution annuity — ~70% of EBITDA from regulated/franchise supply at a ~14–15.5% assured RoE, with India's lowest losses (2.33%). A rare, low-risk cash engine.
  • The turnaround skill compounds — the franchise model (Agra 59%→7%) is a repeatable way to grow distribution with little capital.
  • Credible growth pivot — a ~10 GW renewables ambition + 3 GW pumped storage + green hydrogen, all long-contracted, adding high-quality earnings.
  • Conservatively geared to start — 0.67× net-debt/equity gives real room to fund the ₹60,000 Cr+ plan; maiden ₹3,500 Cr QIP done.
  • Blue-chip owner & ratings — a disciplined Mehta-family group, strong credit, and a re-rating call-option if the pivot lands.

The bear case

  • Gas volatility — merchant-gas earnings swing hard (Q4 FY26 generation slipped to an EBIT loss); the "good years" don't repeat on demand.
  • Rising leverage — the ₹60,000 Cr+ build will lift debt from today's comfortable base; interest and execution risk grow with it.
  • Valuation — ~28× P/E and ~3.6× book price in success; a stumble in the ramp or a soft gas patch bites.
  • Building outside its comfort zone — large RE, pumped storage and out-of-state coal are further from the home-turf discom expertise.
  • Regulatory dependence — the annuity relies on regulators granting fair returns and timely true-ups; adverse orders or franchise-term disputes hurt.
The bottom line

Torrent Power is a high-quality regulated utility using a rock-solid distribution annuity to fund a credible clean-energy growth pivot — and it's doing it from a position of unusual financial strength for the sector. The business risk in the core is genuinely low; the valuation, funding and execution risks in the growth layer are what matter. The story over the next few years will be told in a handful of numbers: the pace of renewable + storage commissioning (is the ~10 GW / 3 GW pipeline on track?), the net-debt/EBITDA trajectory (is the build affordable?), and the gas/merchant contribution (does it swing back up or keep dragging?). Get those right and a premium multiple on a rare quality utility is defensible; stumble on the build or the leverage, and a ~28× stock has room to cool. The safe half of Torrent is as good as it gets in Indian power; the whole thesis rides on the ambitious half.
