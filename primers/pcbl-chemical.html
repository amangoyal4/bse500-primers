PCBL Chemical, the powder inside every tyre

A complete walk through India's largest maker of carbon black — the fine black powder that makes a tyre strong, formerly named Phillips Carbon Black and just renamed to shed the commodity label. How a business that sells a crude-oil-derived industrial filler actually earns its money on a per-tonne conversion spread, harvests clean electricity from the waste gas of making it, and is trying to diversify — via specialty grades and a water-treatment-chemicals acquisition — out of the tyre cycle it has ridden for sixty years.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

PCBL Chemical makes carbon black — a fine, sooty black powder produced by partially burning heavy oil. It sounds obscure, but you touch it every day: carbon black is the essential reinforcing filler in rubber, and roughly a quarter of every car tyre by weight is this one powder. It is what makes a tyre black, and, far more importantly, what makes rubber strong and durable enough to last 50,000 kilometres. PCBL is India's largest and the world's 7th-largest carbon-black producer, part of the Kolkata-based RP-Sanjiv Goenka (RPSG) group, and for sixty years it was called Phillips Carbon Black. It has just renamed itself PCBL Chemical — a deliberate signal that it wants to be seen as more than a commodity carbon-black maker.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a spread business, not a price business. PCBL buys carbon-black feedstock oil (a crude-oil derivative) and converts it into carbon black at a per-tonne conversion margin. The number that matters is not revenue and not the carbon-black price — it is EBITDA per tonne, roughly ₹14,939 in FY26. Feedstock cost passes through; the spread is the profit.
  • FY26 sold record volume yet earned less. Carbon-black volume hit an all-time high of ~619,000 tonnes (+4%) — and profit still fell hard. EBITDA dropped from ₹1,384 Cr to ₹1,081 Cr and net profit halved from ₹435 Cr to ₹198 Cr. More tonnes, less money: the whole cyclicality of this business lives in that sentence.
  • It is trying to escape the tyre cycle. Two moves define the strategy: growing higher-margin specialty carbon blacks (for plastics, inks, batteries — not tyres), and the 2024 acquisition of Aquapharm, a water-treatment / phosphonates chemicals maker that is now a second specialty-chemicals leg and the reason for the "Chemical" in the new name. The stock trades near 53–58× earnings — a re-rating that already prices in the diversification working.
In plain terms

PCBL runs what is really a refinery-style conversion machine: oil goes in, black powder comes out, and the profit is the gap between the two, measured per tonne. That gap widens and narrows with feedstock prices, tyre demand and Chinese competition — which is why a record-volume year can still be a poor-profit year. The company is now spending its scale and cash to add two things the core lacks: higher-margin specialty grades and a separate chemicals business (Aquapharm) that doesn't ride tyres at all. The market has already paid up for that pivot; the job now is to prove the per-tonne spread and the specialty mix can carry a 55× valuation.

01

Carbon black 101: the powder that makes rubber work

No chemistry degree needed — just what the stuff is, and why a tyre can't do without it.

Carbon black is almost pure carbon in the form of extremely fine particles — soot, engineered and controlled. It is made by partially burning a heavy oil (called carbon-black feedstock, or CBFS) in a furnace: the oil doesn't fully combust, and the leftover carbon condenses into microscopic black spheres that clump into a powder. That powder is then sold by the tonne to anyone who needs to make rubber stronger or make something deeply, permanently black.

Why does rubber need it? Raw rubber on its own is weak and wears out fast. Blend carbon black into it and the tiny carbon particles grip the rubber's molecular chains, dramatically increasing strength, abrasion resistance and durability. This is called reinforcement, and it is not optional — a tyre without carbon black would shred in days. That is why carbon black is packed into tyres at roughly a quarter of their weight, and why the fortunes of a carbon-black maker are chained to the tyre and rubber industry.

Where carbon black goes
UseWhat it does there (and why it matters here)

The one distinction that runs through the whole company

Not all carbon black is equal. Rubber black — the tyre grade — is a bulk commodity: huge volumes, thin margins, sold to price-sensitive tyre makers, competing on cost against Chinese and global producers. Specialty black — the grades for plastics, inks, conductives and coatings — is made in smaller batches to tighter specifications, sells for much more per tonne, and earns a far fatter margin. Every strategic move PCBL makes is, at bottom, an attempt to shift its mix up this ladder — from cheap rubber black toward premium specialty black. Hold that distinction and the entire investment case becomes legible.

Who buys from PCBL

  • Tyre manufacturers — the bulk buyers: the big Indian and global tyre companies (MRF, Apollo, CEAT, JK, Bridgestone-type customers). Their production volumes set PCBL's rubber-black demand.
  • Rubber-goods makers — belts, hoses, footwear, seals and industrial rubber, all of which need reinforcement.
  • Plastics, ink, paint & battery makers — the specialty-black customers, where PCBL sells premium grades at premium prices. This is the class it most wants to grow.
02

The per-tonne spread: why revenue lies and EBITDA-per-tonne tells the truth

The single most important idea in the company — read this once and PCBL's cyclicality clicks into place.

If you watch PCBL's revenue, you will misread the business constantly. Here is why. PCBL's biggest cost by far is feedstock oil, a crude-oil derivative whose price swings with global oil markets. When oil is expensive, PCBL charges more for carbon black and revenue rises; when oil is cheap, prices fall and revenue drops — but in both cases the profit per tonne can be almost unchanged, because carbon black is largely sold on a cost-plus, pass-through basis. The feedstock cost flows through to the customer. What PCBL actually keeps is the conversion spread: the fixed-ish margin it earns for turning a tonne of oil into a tonne of carbon black. That is why the industry, and this primer, measures everything in EBITDA per tonne.

Now watch what this does to the numbers. In FY26, PCBL sold a record ~619,000 tonnes of carbon black — more powder out the door than ever in its history. And yet EBITDA fell from ₹1,384 Cr to ₹1,081 Cr, and net profit was cut in half. The reason is entirely in the spread: EBITDA per tonne compressed to ~₹14,939, well below the ₹17,000-plus levels the company had enjoyed and had targeted. The tonnes grew; the margin per tonne shrank — squeezed by soft global demand, cheap Chinese carbon-black exports pressing on prices, and feedstock-vs-realisation timing.

The device that fits only PCBL: same tonnes, different spread

PCBL ran its furnaces harder than ever in FY26 and shipped its highest-ever volume — and shareholders earned less than the year before. That is the signature of a conversion-spread business: profit is not tonnes × price, it is tonnes × per-tonne spread, and the spread is the volatile part. A swing of just ₹2,000–3,000 in EBITDA-per-tonne, applied across ~619,000 tonnes, moves group profit by ₹120–190 Cr — enough to halve net income without a single tonne of volume being lost. Anyone valuing PCBL on revenue growth is watching the wrong needle; the needle that moves the P&L is the spread per tonne, and in FY26 it moved the wrong way.

Each is a way to defend or expand EBITDA-per-tonne when the commodity spread is under attack.

03

The company, the group, and the rename

An RPSG / Goenka company shedding a sixty-year commodity name.

PCBL Chemical was born in 1960 as Phillips Carbon Black, in Kolkata, and has long been the carbon-black flagship of the RP-Sanjiv Goenka Group (RPSG) — the industrial house whose interests span power (CESC), retail (Spencer's), FMCG, IT services and even an IPL cricket franchise. For most of its life the company was a straightforward, if cyclical, commodity leader: make rubber black, sell it to tyre makers, ride the auto cycle. The last few years have been a deliberate campaign to become something more, and the name changes are the flag planted on that campaign.

  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter: the RP-Sanjiv Goenka group (Sanjiv Goenka family, through group holding entities) controls the company with a promoter stake of roughly 50% (approx.); the rest is with institutions and public shareholders. The promoter's other listed vehicles (CESC, RPSG-branded businesses) sit alongside but are separate — don't conflate PCBL's balance sheet with the group's.
  • Reporting segments: the company now reports across Carbon Black (the core — rubber + specialty black), Chemicals (the Aquapharm phosphonates business), Power (captive green power from tail gas, with surplus sold to the grid), and an emerging Battery Chemical effort. That four-segment shape is itself the diversification story in one line.
  • Balance sheet: the Aquapharm acquisition was debt-funded, so PCBL carries meaningful net debt — roughly ₹3,000 Cr (approx.) — but FY26 saw it cut net debt by ~₹454 Cr while generating ~₹1,576 Cr of operating cash flow (more than double FY25's ₹760 Cr). Deleveraging the acquisition is now an explicit priority.
Why the rename is more than cosmetics: a company called "Phillips Carbon Black" is priced by the market as a commodity carbon-black cyclical. A company called "PCBL Chemical" is inviting the market to value it as a diversified specialty-chemicals platform — a higher multiple. The name change is a genuine strategic bet: it only earns that re-rating if the specialty black, Aquapharm and battery efforts actually grow into a materially larger, less-cyclical share of profit. Until then, the label is ahead of the mix.
04

The specialty ladder: climbing out of the commodity

The mix-shift that decides whether PCBL is a cyclical or a compounder.

The clearest lever PCBL controls is its product mix. Rubber black is a low-margin commodity; specialty black earns multiples of the margin per tonne. Every incremental tonne PCBL can sell as specialty rather than rubber grade lifts blended EBITDA-per-tonne and dilutes the tyre-cycle dependence. This is the "up the ladder" story, and FY26 gave it real, if early, momentum.

Specialty black was ~70,000 tonnes of ~619,000 total (~11%), but it grew ~12% (and ~26% in Q4) — far faster than the rubber-black core. It punches above its volume weight on profit, because each specialty tonne earns a far higher margin than a rubber tonne.

The point of specialty black is not its tonnage — at ~11% of volume it looks minor. The point is its margin leverage and its independence from tyres. Specialty grades sell into plastics, coatings, inks and conductive applications, whose demand cycles are different from (and often steadier than) the tyre cycle. So as specialty grows from a tenth toward a larger slice, two good things happen at once: the blended margin rises, and the whole company becomes less hostage to auto production. PCBL has been expanding specialty-black capacity (its stated capacity mix carved out ~112 KT for specialty) precisely to accelerate this.

Why an 11% segment can matter more than its size

A useful way to see it: if specialty black earns, say, two-to-three times the per-tonne margin of rubber black, then a segment that is ~11% of volume can contribute a much larger share of segment profit — and it grows faster. Doubling specialty from ~70 KT toward ~140 KT would barely move total tonnage but could add a disproportionate slice of EBITDA, all of it less tyre-dependent. This is the mechanism behind the "PCBL Chemical" ambition: the company doesn't need to stop being a carbon-black giant, it needs the premium sliver of its output to keep outgrowing the commodity bulk until the mix — and the margin — is transformed.

05

Aquapharm: the second leg, and the reason for the name

A carbon-black company buying a water-treatment-chemicals firm — the surprise that explains the "Chemical".

Here is the move that would puzzle a smart generalist: a carbon-black maker bought a water-treatment chemicals company. In 2024 PCBL acquired Aquapharm Chemicals, a specialty-chemicals business best known for phosphonates — molecules used to treat industrial water (preventing scale and corrosion in cooling towers, boilers and pipelines) and in detergents, oilfield and other applications. Aquapharm is a top-3 global producer of phosphonates with a catalogue of 275-plus products. It has nothing to do with tyres — and that is exactly the point.

Aquapharm now contributes about 13% of group revenue and, crucially, at a higher EBITDA margin than the carbon-black core — roughly ₹162 Cr of EBITDA on ₹1,443 Cr of revenue in FY26, a margin well above the commodity black business. It gives PCBL a genuine second specialty-chemicals platform, with its own customers, end-markets and growth vectors (water treatment demand rises with industrialisation, data-centre cooling, and tighter environmental norms). This is the acquisition that earned the company its new surname.

The logic behind an odd-looking deal

Carbon black and water-treatment chemicals share almost no chemistry — but they share a strategic problem PCBL was trying to solve: too much of the company depended on one cyclical end-market (tyres). Aquapharm bolts on a business whose demand is driven by industrial water use, not automobile production, at a structurally higher margin. It converts "PCBL, the carbon-black cyclical" into "PCBL, the specialty-chemicals group that also happens to lead carbon black." The trade-off is honest: the deal was debt-funded, so it added leverage and integration risk to a company that had run a cleaner balance sheet — and in a soft FY26, Aquapharm's own EBITDA slipped, so the diversification is real but not yet a rescue.

Watch the integration, not just the logic: the strategic case for Aquapharm is sound, but bolting a specialty-chemicals company onto a carbon-black manufacturer is an execution project — different plants, different customers, different sales motion — funded with borrowed money. The bull case needs Aquapharm's revenue and margin to grow under PCBL's ownership and the acquisition debt to come down. FY26's flat-to-soft Aquapharm result is a reminder that a second leg only helps if it actually strides.
06

Clean power from dirty gas: the byproduct that pays

The genuine surprise — a green power plant hiding inside a carbon-black furnace.

Making carbon black is, on its face, a "dirty" business: you burn heavy oil to make an industrial black powder. But the process throws off a large volume of hot, combustible waste gas — called tail gas — that would otherwise be flared into the atmosphere. PCBL captures that tail gas and burns it to run captive power plants, generating electricity it uses in its own factories and sells the surplus to the grid. The waste of a carbon process becomes clean, near-free power. This is one of the few places where PCBL's economics are genuinely distinctive.

PCBL generated ~839 million units (MU) of green power in FY26 (a record, +14%), of which ~511 MU was sold externally, from ~122 MW of capacity across five sites. Because the fuel is waste tail gas, this power is unusually cheap to produce — and it counts as green/renewable generation.

Why does this matter to an investor rather than just an engineer? Because it directly lifts the metric that runs the company — EBITDA per tonne. Every tonne of carbon black PCBL makes also produces tail gas, and that tail gas produces saleable power at almost no marginal fuel cost. So the "true" economics of a tonne of carbon black are the conversion spread plus a slug of near-free power revenue. When you hear management defend per-tonne margins in a soft carbon-black market, part of the defence is this byproduct: it cushions the spread when the commodity price is under pressure, and it carries green-energy credentials that a pure carbon business would lack.

The counter-intuitive bit worth sitting with

A quarter of every tyre is carbon black, and carbon black is made by burning oil — about as un-green as an industrial input sounds. Yet PCBL harvests hundreds of millions of units of renewable-classified electricity from the waste gas of making it, selling roughly 60% of that power to the grid. The company that puts the black in your tyres also runs a small green-power utility off the exhaust. It is a real, tonne-linked profit stream, not an ESG press release — and it is exactly the kind of structural quirk that a spreadsheet built only on "carbon-black price × volume" will miss entirely.

07

Volume, capacity and the import-substitution tailwind

The growth side of the story — more tonnes, from India's own tyre boom.

If the spread is the margin story, volume is the growth story. PCBL's tonnage rises with three things: Indian tyre and auto production, the country's rising tyre exports, and import substitution as domestic capacity replaces carbon black India used to buy from abroad. FY26 showed all three, even as pricing was weak.

What drives PCBL's tonnage
DriverWhat it isWhy it helps PCBL
Domestic tyre demandIndia's car, truck and two-wheeler production plus replacement tyres.Domestic carbon-black sales grew ~9% in FY26 — the steadiest demand pillar, tied to India's auto growth.
Tyre-export boomIndia is becoming a tyre-export hub as global makers source here.Every tyre exported carries PCBL's carbon black; rising Indian tyre exports pull carbon-black volume even when local car sales are flat.
Import substitutionIndia historically imported some carbon black; new domestic capacity displaces it.PCBL's capacity additions let Indian tyre makers buy local instead of importing — a captive-share tailwind.

The counter-current in FY26 was exports. PCBL's own export volumes fell (down ~13% in Q3) as US tariff uncertainty — a threatened 50% rate on some Indian goods — and global destocking hit overseas shipments. So the year's volume growth leaned on the domestic market (+9%) while direct exports softened. The nuance is that India's tyre exports still indirectly pull PCBL's domestic carbon-black sales, so the export theme cuts both ways: PCBL's own exports weakened, but its customers' exports helped.

The capacity that has to earn its keep

PCBL now sits at ~880,000 tonnes of carbon-black capacity after the Tamil Nadu line. Capacity is only valuable if it runs full and at a healthy spread. In a soft-spread year, adding rubber-black capacity is a double-edged move: it grows volume and defends market share against imports and rivals, but it also adds fixed cost and depreciation that hurt if the spread stays thin. The volume growth is real and the import-substitution logic is sound — but tonnage alone, as FY26 proved, does not make the profit. The new capacity pays off only when the per-tonne spread recovers.

08

The industry and the rivals

A concentrated global business — with China as the perennial swing factor.

Carbon black is a scale-and-cost business dominated by a handful of large producers, because efficient furnaces, feedstock access and tyre-maker relationships take decades and capital to build. PCBL leads India, but it competes in a global market where the biggest structural force is not any single rival — it is Chinese overcapacity and the exports it periodically dumps into world markets.

The competitive dynamic to hold in mind

Two forces shape PCBL's world. First, at home it is the clear leader with scale, captive power and tyre-maker relationships that make it hard to displace — a genuine moat in the Indian rubber-black market, reinforced by import substitution. Second, globally it is a mid-sized player in a market where the marginal price is often set by Chinese overcapacity: when China's producers export aggressively, carbon-black prices and spreads soften everywhere, which is a large part of what pinched FY26. PCBL cannot control that swing factor — so its strategic response is to reduce its exposure to it, by climbing into specialty grades and Aquapharm chemicals where Chinese commodity exports don't reach. The competitive game and the diversification strategy are the same game.

09

The battery-carbon option

The frontier bet — small today, large if it works.

There is one more string to the diversification bow, still early and speculative: battery materials. Certain engineered carbons are used as conductive additives in battery electrodes, and PCBL is pushing into this frontier through its Nanovace initiative — a pilot aimed at advanced battery-grade carbon materials. The pitch is ambitious: materials that could extend battery range, lengthen life, enable faster charging and cut production emissions. It is unproven and tiny relative to the core, but it is a real option on a large, fast-growing end-market.

Why does an option like this matter for the valuation? Because carbon black's expertise — engineering carbon particles to precise specifications for conductivity and structure — is genuinely adjacent to what battery-conductive carbons require. If electric-vehicle and energy-storage demand scales, a carbon-black leader with the right specialty grades could plug into a market with far better growth and margins than tyres. That is the "optionality" the market partly pays for in PCBL's multiple.

How to weigh an option you can't yet size

Nanovace and battery carbon should be treated as exactly that — an option, not a forecast. It contributes negligibly to profit today and may take years to matter, if it ever does; battery-materials competition is fierce and technology-dependent. The disciplined way to hold it: give PCBL credit for the strategic adjacency and the fact that it is investing ahead of the curve, but underwrite the stock on the carbon-black spread, specialty mix and Aquapharm — the things that are already in the P&L. If battery carbon works, it is upside on top; if it stalls, the core case must still stand on its own.

10

Financials & valuation

A soft-spread year priced on a diversification premium.

FY26 was the tension in this stock made numerical: record volume, falling profit, and a valuation that ignores the near-term dip in favour of the long-term pivot. Revenue slipped to ~₹8,189 Cr, EBITDA fell ~22% to ~₹1,081 Cr, and net profit halved to ~₹198 Cr — all driven by the compressed carbon-black spread, not by lost volume. Meanwhile the stock trades near 53–58× earnings. The gap between a down-earnings year and a premium multiple is the whole valuation debate.

At ~55× depressed earnings, PCBL is priced as a company whose spread recovers and whose specialty/chemicals mix keeps climbing. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is EBITDA per tonne — the ~₹14,939 of FY26 climbing back toward, and past, the ₹16,000–17,000 the company targets — read together with the specialty-black share of volume and Aquapharm's revenue and margin, and with net debt continuing to fall. If per-tonne spread recovers and specialty plus Aquapharm keep gaining share, a down FY26 was a trough and the multiple is defensible. If the spread stays pinned by Chinese exports and soft tyre demand while the diversification only inches forward, then a 55× multiple is sitting on top of a commodity earnings base — and re-rates down to match it. Everything else is texture; the per-tonne spread and the mix are the scoreboard.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A carbon-black leader mid-pivot — asked to prove the spread and the mix.

PCBL Chemical enters FY27 as India's carbon-black leader with three growth levers — specialty black, Aquapharm chemicals and battery-carbon optionality — and one stubborn dependency: a commodity core whose per-tonne spread is set partly in China. The debate is whether the diversification lifts margin and dampens the cycle fast enough to justify a re-rated price, or whether FY26's spread squeeze is a preview of a commodity business that keeps getting revalued down to what it is.

The bull case

  • Domestic leadership + import substitution — India's largest carbon-black maker, growing domestic volume ~9% and displacing imports, with new Tamil Nadu capacity.
  • Spread recovery — EBITDA-per-tonne of ~₹14,939 is a cyclical trough; a recovery toward ₹16,000–17,000 flows straight to profit across ~619 KT of volume.
  • Specialty mix climbing — specialty black growing ~12% (26% in Q4), a higher-margin, less-tyre-dependent slice compounding faster than the core.
  • Aquapharm second leg — ~13% of revenue at richer margins, plus the tail-gas green-power byproduct cushioning per-tonne economics.
  • Battery-carbon option — Nanovace as a real, if early, call on conductive/battery materials adjacent to PCBL's core expertise.

The bear case

  • Commodity & China risk — the core is a tyre-cyclical commodity; Chinese overcapacity and dumping can pin the spread low, as in FY26.
  • Feedstock & FX volatility — crude-derived CBFS cost and inventory timing swing the per-tonne spread; a bad quarter can halve profit without any volume loss.
  • Debt-funded diversification — Aquapharm added leverage and integration risk, and its own EBITDA slipped in FY26; the pivot is unproven and borrowed-money-financed.
  • Rich valuation on trough earnings — ~55× a depressed PAT prices in a spread recovery and mix-shift that must both materialise.
  • ESG & regulation — carbon black is emissions-heavy; tightening environmental rules and the "dirty carbon" optics are a persistent overhang despite the green-power offset.
The bottom line

PCBL has done the hard strategic thinking a commodity leader is supposed to do: it is climbing the specialty ladder, it bought a genuine second chemicals leg, it harvests clean power from its own waste gas, and it changed its sixty-year name to force the market to look again. FY26 was the reminder that none of that has yet outrun the core — a record-volume year still delivered halved profit, because the carbon-black spread is the master variable and it was squeezed. The next few quarters resolve it in one checkable print: whether EBITDA-per-tonne climbs back off its ~₹14,939 trough while specialty black and Aquapharm keep taking share. If the spread turns and the mix keeps shifting, the "Chemical" in the name starts to earn its multiple. If the spread stays hostage to China and the pivot only inches, PCBL is a carbon-black cyclical wearing a specialty-chemicals price tag — and the two eventually have to meet.
