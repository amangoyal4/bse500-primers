00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Tejas Networks designs and sells telecom equipment — the boxes, cards, radios and software that a phone company installs so that calls, video and data can actually travel. It does not run a network and it does not sell you a SIM card; it sells the machinery to the people who do: Bharti Airtel, Reliance Jio, BSNL, Vodafone Idea, plus utilities, railways, defence users, internet providers and governments in about 75 countries. Headquartered in Bengaluru, founded in 2000 by a group of Indian engineers, and since 2021 controlled by the Tata group through Panatone Finvest (a Tata Sons subsidiary), it is the Indian answer to Ericsson, Nokia and Huawei — a country's attempt to own the hardware its own network runs on.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • It became a big company because of one order, and shrank because of the same one. Under India's indigenous-4G programme, a consortium of C-DOT (the state telecom R&D lab) + TCS + Tejas was chosen to build BSNL's home-grown 4G network across roughly 100,000 sites, with Tejas supplying the radio hardware. Revenue went from ~₹1,232 Cr in FY24 (approx.) to ₹8,923 Cr in FY25 — and then, when that shipment wave finished, back down to ₹1,103 Cr in FY26. Profit followed: +₹447 Cr in FY25 to a −₹909 Cr loss in FY26.
  • It owns its technology, which is rarer than it sounds. Tejas designs its own chips-to-software stack in-house: optical transport, fibre broadband access and — the hard one — a full 4G/5G radio access network (RAN). It has filed 676 patents cumulatively, of which 371 are granted. Outside China, the US, Europe, Japan and Korea, the list of companies with a nationally-deployed 4G/5G RAN of their own design is very, very short. Tejas is on it.
  • The entire question now is order replacement. The closing order book was ₹1,514 Cr at March 2026 — up 49% year on year, but only about a sixth of what the company billed in FY25. Against that sits ₹3,531 Cr of net debt and a fixed R&D cost base that does not shrink when orders pause. The stock, at roughly ₹9,800 Cr of market value (approx.), has no earnings to be measured against — it is priced on what the order book becomes, not on what the P&L printed.
In plain terms

Two businesses live inside Tejas Networks and they behave nothing alike. One is a technology company: a few thousand engineers in Bengaluru writing the software and designing the boards that make a telecom network run, funded year after year whether or not anyone is buying. The other is a project-shipment company: crates of hardware that go out of the door in enormous bursts when a customer's rollout is live, and not at all when it isn't. The first has a steady, stubborn cost. The second has revenue that can multiply by seven and then divide by eight inside twenty-four months. Every number in this primer is some version of those two things colliding.

01

Telecom equipment 101: who actually buys this stuff

Where a company like Tejas sits between you, your phone, and the internet.

Start from the phone in your hand. When you open a video, the request leaves your handset over the air, reaches a tower a few hundred metres away, travels down a fibre-optic cable to a local exchange, hops across the country on a long-distance fibre backbone, and eventually reaches a data centre. Every one of those steps needs physical equipment — radios on the tower, cards that pump light down the fibre, routers that decide where the traffic goes. Airtel, Jio and BSNL operate that chain and bill you for it. They do not build the boxes. Companies like Ericsson, Nokia, Huawei, ZTE, Cisco, Ciena — and Tejas Networks — build the boxes.

Broadband accessThe "last mile" — gear that brings fibre into a home, office or village. The main technology is GPON (fibre-to-the-home). Tejas supplies this to operators and to India's rural BharatNet programme.

Why a country cares who makes its network gear

Telecom equipment is not an ordinary import. Every call, message and payment in a country flows through it, which makes it a national-security question as much as a procurement one. Several governments have restricted or banned Chinese vendors from their networks; India has effectively done the same through a "trusted sources" regime. That leaves most countries choosing between two Europeans (Ericsson, Nokia) and a Korean (Samsung), all of whom price like the small club they are. India's response was to fund its own 4G stack — state lab C-DOT for the core network software, Tejas for the radios, TCS as system integrator. That is not industrial nostalgia; it is a deliberate decision that the country wanted a domestic supplier to exist. Tejas is the listed way to own the outcome of that decision.

Who buys network equipment in India

  • Private operators — Airtel, Jio, Vodafone Idea. They buy continuously but negotiate ruthlessly, run multi-vendor policies, and have deep incumbent relationships with the global majors.
  • State-owned operators — BSNL and MTNL. Slower, tender-driven, policy-directed, and capable of placing an order so large it re-scales a supplier overnight. Also capable of pausing one.
  • Government programmes — BharatNet, the enormous project to run fibre to every village panchayat, buys routers and access gear directly through packages awarded to system integrators.
  • Utilities, railways, defence and enterprises — power grids, metro rail, oil and gas, defence networks and large campuses all run private networks that need the same transport and access gear, often with tighter reliability requirements than a consumer network.
  • International customers — operators and internet providers across roughly 75 countries, historically Tejas's steady, unglamorous, higher-margin base.
02

What Tejas actually sells: three product legs

Wireline (optical), broadband access, and the leg that changed the company — wireless.

Tejas organises itself around two reported halves — "wireline" and "wireless" — but it is easier to understand as three product families, because they were built in three different eras and they have three different economics. Optical is the founding business. Broadband access is the government-and-rural business. Wireless is the one that turned a mid-cap engineering firm into a multi-thousand-crore shipper of hardware, and then handed it a loss.

The three legs, decoded

  • Optical / wireline transport — the founding business and the technical calling card. Tejas's kit carries traffic across national fibre backbones, and its newest generation supports 400G and 800G coherent DWDM — meaning a single wavelength on a fibre carries 400 or 800 gigabits per second. Demand here is being pulled by two things at once: the traffic explosion from 5G, and the arrival of AI data centres that need enormous, low-latency fibre links between sites. This leg sells to Indian and international operators, utilities and internet providers, and it does not depend on any single government programme.
  • Broadband access — fibre-to-the-home (GPON) gear plus IP/MPLS routers. This is the leg most exposed to Indian public programmes, above all BharatNet Phase III, the current stage of the national village-fibre project. In FY26 Tejas was selected as the router supplier for 7 of the 12 BharatNet Phase-III packages awarded and shipped over 17,000 routers across nine states and five union territories. Not glamorous, genuinely large, and it recurs as each phase of the programme is funded.
  • Wireless / RAN — the transformational leg. Tejas designs and manufactures the radio units and baseband equipment that sit on a mobile tower, supporting 4G and 5G, and increasingly built to Open RAN standards (open interfaces so an operator can mix vendors rather than being locked to one). This is the product that BSNL's national network runs on, the product behind FY25's revenue explosion, and the product that must now find buyers outside BSNL.
Why the wireless leg is a different kind of business

Optical and access gear sells the way most industrial equipment sells: an operator upgrades a route, adds capacity in a city, wins a BharatNet package — steady, repeatable, spread across dozens of customers and geographies. RAN does not work like that. A national radio rollout is a campaign: tens of thousands of sites, contracted in one go, shipped over a handful of quarters, then finished. While it runs, the supplier looks like it has been re-founded at ten times the size. When it stops, nothing has gone wrong — no customer lost, no product failed, no competitor won — and yet the revenue is simply not there next quarter. Tejas has now lived through both halves of that sentence inside two financial years.

A distinction worth holding on to: Tejas does not build fibre-optic cable — that is Sterlite Technologies' and HFCL's world, a commodity-ish manufacturing business. Tejas builds the intelligent equipment that lights the cable up. Cable is glass drawn in a factory; Tejas's product is design — silicon, firmware, signal processing and network software, manufactured largely through contract partners. The distinction matters because it explains where the money goes: not into furnaces, but into R&D salaries.
03

Founders, the IPO, and the Tata takeover

A 2000-vintage Bengaluru engineering startup that ended up inside India's largest business house.

Tejas Networks was founded in 2000 in Bengaluru by a small group of Indian engineers — including Sanjay Nayak, Kumar Sivarajan (an optical-networking academic from IISc, and a genuinely serious name in the field) and Arnob Roy — with the then-unfashionable idea that an Indian company could design carrier-grade optical equipment rather than merely resell someone else's. It survived the dot-com bust, the long famine of Indian telecom capex, a decade of competing against firms fifty times its size, and listed on the exchanges in 2017. For twenty years it was an admired, sub-scale, frequently loss-making technology house.

Ownership & control

  • Promoter: Panatone Finvest Ltd, a subsidiary of Tata Sons, is the controlling shareholder — reported at about 55.6% as of March 2025, with more recent screens showing promoter holding around 53% (approx.) after equity issuance and employee-option dilution. Treat the exact figure as approximate and check the latest shareholding filing; the fact that matters is that Tejas is unambiguously a Tata-controlled company.
  • What Tata brings: three things a sub-scale equipment maker cannot buy — balance-sheet credibility (an operator awarding a national contract is asking "will you exist in ten years?"), procurement access through TCS as the BSNL system integrator, and the patience to fund R&D through loss years. Tejas's FY26 loss is survivable in a way it would not have been for the independent company.
  • What changed at the top: the founder-CEO era ended under Tata ownership and the company is now run by professional management appointed since the takeover (approx. — verify current designations in the latest annual report). Founder-level technical depth remains a core part of the R&D organisation.
  • The listed peer inside the family: Tejas is not to be confused with Tata Communications or Tata Teleservices, which are network operators/service providers. Tejas is the group's equipment-maker — it sells to carriers rather than competing with them.
The takeover is more interesting than it looks: India's largest business house bought a founder-led networking company at a moment when the country had decided, as policy, that it needed a domestic telecom-equipment champion. Read the 2021 investment and the 2023 BSNL award together and the sequence stops looking like two separate events. Tata did not buy a small optical vendor; it bought the only credible Indian candidate for a national 4G stack — and then that stack got built.
04

The BSNL 4G windfall: how one order re-founded a company

What actually got built, why India insisted on building it, and what it did to the P&L.

BSNL, India's state-owned operator, spent years without a 4G network while private rivals moved to 5G. Rather than simply buying European equipment, the government made a bet: it would fund an indigenous 4G stack — core network software from C-DOT (the state telecom R&D lab), radio access equipment from Tejas Networks, and TCS as the system integrator that stitched it together and carried the contract. The consortium was awarded BSNL's rollout across roughly 100,000 sites, a programme reported at around ₹19,000 Cr (approx.) in total value, of which Tejas's share for the RAN hardware was an advance purchase order of roughly ₹7,500 Cr (approx.).

For a company that had billed ₹1,232 Cr in FY24 (approx.), a single order worth six years of revenue is not a growth event — it is a change of species. Tejas had to scale manufacturing (largely through contract manufacturing partners), fund an enormous inventory build, ship at a pace it had never attempted, and simultaneously keep a brand-new radio product working across tens of thousands of live sites in every kind of Indian terrain. In FY26 that job reached its milestone: BSNL's pan-India 4G network launched commercially, running on Tejas's indigenously designed 4G/5G RAN products.

The thing that surprises people about FY25

FY25 was the best year in Tejas's history — ₹8,923 Cr of revenue, ₹447 Cr of profit. But look inside it at the fourth quarter: revenue of ₹1,907 Cr and a net loss of ₹72 Cr. A quarter that billed nearly twice the entire following year's revenue still lost money. That tells you something important about the economics of this contract: supplying RAN hardware into a price-negotiated national programme is a thin-margin, volume business, and the profit in the good year came from the quarters where mix and execution happened to line up, not from a structurally fat margin. The windfall was real, but it was a volume windfall, not a pricing one.

05

The cliff: the ₹7,820 Cr that walked out of the door

The central mechanic of this company, and it exists in almost no other listed name.

Between FY25 and FY26, Tejas Networks lost ₹7,820 Cr of annual revenue — the difference between ₹8,923 Cr and ₹1,103 Cr. Here is what did not cause it: no customer defected, no product was recalled, no competitor undercut it, no market share was surrendered, no factory burned down, no regulation changed. The rollout it had been contracted to supply reached the end of its ordered quantity, and the shipments stopped. That is the whole explanation.

This is a genuinely unusual risk shape. Most cyclical businesses decline gradually — a commodity price drifts down, volumes soften, margins compress over several quarters. Tejas's revenue did not drift; it fell off a shelf, because a purchase order is a step function, not a curve. And underneath the step sits a cost base that behaves in exactly the opposite way: an R&D organisation of thousands of engineers, developing 5G radios and 800G optical products, whose cost is essentially flat from quarter to quarter regardless of whether anything ships.

Q4 FY26 revenue of ₹333 Cr came with a ₹211 Cr net loss; Q3's ₹307 Cr came with a ₹197 Cr loss. Two consecutive quarters where the loss was roughly two-thirds of revenue — the signature of a fixed cost base running against an empty shipment schedule.
The device that only fits Tejas: read the order book, not the P&L

For most companies, this year's revenue is a decent estimate of next year's. For Tejas it is close to worthless: FY25's ₹8,923 Cr told you nothing useful about FY26's ₹1,103 Cr, and FY26's ₹1,103 Cr tells you nothing useful about FY27. The reported P&L is a rear-view record of a shipment campaign that has already ended. The only forward-looking instrument on this company is the order book — ₹1,514 Cr at March 2026 — and the pace of new inflow into it. That is why a 49% increase in the order book, in a year the loss widened to ₹909 Cr, was the more informative of the two numbers. Investors who track this stock through its earnings line will always be looking one full cycle behind.

Two secondary effects of the cliff deserve naming, because they are what turned a revenue decline into a nine-figure loss. First, operating deleverage: when ₹333 Cr of quarterly revenue has to absorb an R&D-plus-overhead base built for ₹2,000 Cr quarters, the gross profit does not come close to covering it. Second, the inventory hangover: a company that geared up to ship a national rollout carries components and finished goods bought for a run-rate that has since vanished, and carrying (or writing down) that stock is expensive. The FY26 pre-tax loss of ₹1,354 Cr is wider than the fall in gross profit alone would explain, which points to exactly these one-off and provisioning effects layered on top.

Do not model this company as a smooth grower. A discounted-cash-flow built on "FY26 revenue growing 20% a year" is meaningless here, in either direction. Revenue in FY27 and FY28 will be whatever the order book plus new inflow allows it to be — potentially several multiples of FY26, potentially not. The correct mental model is a sequence of campaigns of uncertain size and timing, against a known and largely fixed annual cost of being in the business at all.

07

The R&D engine: 676 patents and a bill that never pauses

Why owning the technology is the asset, the moat, and the recurring liability — all three.

Strip away the order-book drama and what remains is a research organisation. Tejas's product is not metal; it is design — signal-processing algorithms, silicon and board architecture, embedded firmware, radio software and network-management systems, developed in-house in Bengaluru. The tangible score sheet: 676 patents filed cumulatively, 371 of them granted, with 63 filed in the fourth quarter of FY26 alone — a year of heavy losses in which the company kept filing at pace. That last detail is the clearest statement of intent management made all year.

The economics of owning your own stack cut in two directions, and both are large. On the upside, Tejas keeps the intellectual property, controls its own product roadmap, does not pay licence fees to a foreign parent, and can customise deeply for a customer — which is exactly what a national indigenous-4G programme required, and what no reseller could have delivered. On the downside, the R&D bill is the most senior claim on the company's cash. It must be paid in the quarters when nothing ships, and it must keep pace with rivals who spend more on research in a single year than Tejas has earned in revenue across its entire existence.

The uncomfortable maths of competing on R&D

Ericsson, Nokia, Huawei, ZTE and Samsung each spend research budgets measured in billions of dollars a year. Tejas's total revenue in a peak year was a little over one billion dollars, and in FY26 roughly an eighth of that. It cannot win by out-spending anyone. It competes by being narrow and specific: solving Indian deployment conditions properly, hitting price points a global major cannot profitably reach, aligning with Open RAN so that an operator can slot Tejas equipment into a multi-vendor network, and being the trusted-source option in markets where Chinese vendors are excluded and European ones are expensive. That is a viable strategy. It is not a comfortable one, and it requires the R&D spend to continue through exactly the years when the P&L makes it hardest to justify.

Technology obsolescence is the tail risk sitting underneath all of it. Radio and optical standards move; a product family that is competitive today is mid-life in three years and legacy in six. The company that survives is the one whose 5G massive-MIMO radio and 800G optical line are ready before customers ask for them. That is why the 64TR massive-MIMO launch, the 800G coherent DWDM line and the Rakuten Symphony Open RAN partnership belong in a discussion about risk as much as in a discussion about growth — they are the evidence that the R&D money spent through the loss year bought a current product portfolio rather than a maintained one.

08

The balance sheet: ₹3,531 Cr of net debt in the quiet year

What it costs to have shipped a national rollout to a government customer.

This is the part of the FY26 result that gets least attention and probably deserves the most. Tejas ended the year with gross debt of ₹4,035 Cr, cash of ₹505 Cr, and net debt of ₹3,531 Cr — against a market capitalisation of roughly ₹9,800 Cr (approx.) and annual revenue of ₹1,103 Cr. A company with a Tata parent and a technology-led business model carries more than three times its annual revenue in net borrowings.

The reason is working capital, and it is the standard hazard of selling to public-sector customers on a project basis. To supply a national rollout, Tejas had to buy components, build inventory, manufacture, ship and install well ahead of collecting payment. Government and PSU customers pay against milestones — acceptance testing, site sign-offs, commissioning certificates — and those milestones arrive on the customer's schedule, not the supplier's. The result is a balance sheet stuffed with receivables and inventory from a campaign whose revenue was recognised in a previous financial year, funded with borrowed money that keeps accruing interest into the quiet year that follows.

The good news hiding inside the bad number

Debt built out of receivables and inventory is not the same animal as debt built out of losses. Much of this borrowing is the financing cost of goods already delivered — it is, in principle, collectable. As BSNL milestones are certified and payments land, and as inventory is either consumed by follow-on orders or written down, that balance should unwind and release cash. Two things determine whether it does. The first is collection: how quickly a government customer actually pays. The second is reusability: whether the components sitting in the warehouse fit the next order or belong to a product generation that has moved on. Watch the receivables and inventory lines in the next few results alongside the order book; they are where the FY26 story either resolves quietly or turns into a second round of write-downs.

The parent is not a guarantee. Tata control gives Tejas credibility and access to capital markets, but a Tata group company is not an entity whose debts the group is contractually obliged to service. Any recapitalisation — fresh equity, a rights issue, a promoter infusion — would be a discretionary decision, and existing shareholders would carry the dilution. It is a reasonable expectation that the parent supports the business; it is not a covenant.
09

The rivals: a global oligopoly and a small domestic bench

Who Tejas is actually up against, and where it can genuinely win.

Telecom equipment is one of the most concentrated industries in the world. Building carrier-grade radios and optical systems takes a decade of R&D, a global support organisation, and a track record long enough that an operator will bet its network on you. The result is a handful of enormous vendors, a Chinese pair excluded from many Western markets on security grounds, and a very thin tail of everyone else. Tejas lives in that tail — deliberately, and with a specific angle.

Where a small vendor can actually win

Tejas does not beat Ericsson on breadth, and it will not. It wins in four specific situations. One: where a government has decided, as policy, that it wants a domestically-designed or non-Chinese, non-Western-monopoly option — the BSNL case, and the pitch to friendly emerging markets. Two: where Open RAN lets an operator buy radios from one vendor and software from another, which is precisely the wedge the Rakuten Symphony partnership targets. Three: where price matters more than brand — mid-tier operators, private networks, utilities. Four: in its own optical backyard, where its products are technically competitive and its Indian cost base is a real advantage. Every one of those is a niche. Stacked together, and with a live national deployment to point at, they are a business — just not one that gets to grow smoothly.

What the market is currently paying

  • Market capitalisation: roughly ₹9,700–9,900 Cr (approx.) in mid-2026, with the share around ₹564 in early July 2026.
  • Price/earnings: not meaningful — FY26 earnings were negative. On FY25's ₹447 Cr of profit the stock would sit near ~22×, but FY25 was a campaign year and is not a normalised base.
  • Enterprise value: market cap plus ₹3,531 Cr of net debt gives roughly ₹13,300 Cr (approx.) — about 12× FY26 revenue, or about 1.5× FY25 revenue. The gulf between those two multiples is the valuation debate, expressed in one line.
  • Book value support: the FY26 loss consumed a large slice of the equity built in FY25, and the debt load means the balance sheet offers less downside cushion than a cash-rich technology company would.
The number that settles this thesis: quarterly order inflow, split by customer

Not revenue, not EBITDA, not the loss — order inflow per quarter, and the share of the book that is not BSNL. Everything else on this company is a consequence of that one series. Three specific tests, in order of importance. (1) Absolute inflow: the book has to move from ₹1,514 Cr towards a level consistent with ₹3,000–4,500 Cr of annual revenue (approx., derived) before the P&L can clear its fixed costs. (2) Composition: what proportion of new inflow comes from BharatNet, exports, private networks and international RAN rather than the next BSNL phase — because a book refilled entirely by one government customer has re-armed the same cliff for a later year. (3) Gross margin on the new mix: optical, international and private-network business should carry better margins than price-negotiated national RAN volume; if it does not, scale alone will not fix the losses. Watch those three lines every quarter and you have the entire case; watch anything else and you are reading history.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A proven national deployment, an empty year behind it, and a book that has to do the talking.

Tejas Networks enters FY27 in a strange position: technically at its strongest — a pan-India 4G network running on its radios, a current 5G and 800G product portfolio, 371 granted patents, a first international wireless order, a Tata parent — and financially at close to its weakest, with a ₹909 Cr annual loss behind it and ₹3,531 Cr of net debt on the books. Those two facts are not in conflict. They are the same fact, seen from opposite ends of a shipment campaign.

The bull case

  • The reference now exists — a live, pan-India 4G network running on Tejas-designed RAN. No amount of R&D substitutes for a national deployment when an operator is evaluating a new vendor.
  • Order book +49% — the book grew to ₹1,514 Cr in the worst revenue year, with BharatNet Phase III (7 of 12 packages) rolling out over multiple years.
  • Diversification has actually started — first international commercial 4G/5G order, a 5G massive-MIMO contract with NEC, Rakuten Symphony Open RAN tie-up, 5G trials in the Americas.
  • Structural policy tailwind — trusted-source rules, Make-in-India and PLI incentives, and the exclusion of Chinese vendors from India and several other markets leave a gap a domestic vendor is built to fill.
  • Optical demand is real and not programme-dependent — 400G/800G coherent DWDM sells into a traffic and AI-data-centre boom, across many customers rather than one.
  • A parent that can wait — Tata control provides credibility with customers and access to capital through loss years that an independent company would not survive.

The bear case

  • Customer concentration, unresolved — the business was overwhelmingly one government-backed programme; even the most likely refill (further BSNL phases) rebuilds the same dependency rather than removing it. This is the single biggest risk on the page.
  • The cliff is proven, not theoretical — revenue has already fallen ~88% in one year and profit swung ₹1,356 Cr. It can happen again after the next campaign.
  • ₹3,531 Cr of net debt — interest accrues through the quiet quarters; collection from government customers is slow; a further equity raise would dilute.
  • Fixed R&D against giant rivals — Ericsson, Nokia, Huawei, ZTE and Samsung outspend Tejas by orders of magnitude, and telecom standards obsolete a product family in a few years.
  • Thin programme margins — even the ₹1,907 Cr peak quarter of Q4 FY25 produced a net loss; volume alone has not proven it can generate durable profit.
  • Valuation has no earnings anchor — roughly ₹13,300 Cr of enterprise value (approx.) against ₹1,103 Cr of revenue and a loss; the price rests entirely on orders that have not yet been won.
The bottom line

Very few countries have a company that designs its own 4G and 5G radios and has actually deployed them across a national network; India has exactly one, and this is it. That achievement is real, it is not reversible, and it is why the business deserves attention beyond a bad financial year. But the achievement was bought with a shape of revenue that is punishing to own: one customer, one programme, one wave of purchase orders, and a ₹7,820 Cr hole the moment the wave passed. Tejas is not currently being asked to invent anything. It is being asked to sell what it has already built to somebody other than BSNL — and the receipt for that will show up in a single, checkable line at the end of each quarterly release: the closing order book, and how much of it carries a customer name that is not BSNL. Track it from ₹1,514 Cr. If it climbs past ₹3,000 Cr with a meaningfully mixed customer list, the FY26 loss was the trough between two campaigns. If it does not, FY25 was the campaign.
