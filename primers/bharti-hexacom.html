00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're actually looking at — read this before anything else.

Bharti Hexacom is the company through which Bharti Airtel — India's premium telecom operator — runs its mobile and broadband business in two of India's ~22 telecom "circles": Rajasthan and the North-East (Arunachal Pradesh, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Tripura, plus Assam). Airtel owns ~70% of it; the customers are Airtel-branded; the network, spectrum, systems and brand are Airtel's. In plain terms, Bharti Hexacom is a focused, listed, pure-play slice of Airtel — the same business as its famous parent, but concentrated in two growing markets rather than the whole country.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a "mini-Airtel", not an independent challenger. A circle is a licensed geography; Hexacom holds Airtel's licence and spectrum for Rajasthan and the North-East and operates them under the Airtel brand. So it inherits Airtel's premium positioning, its 4G/5G network, its ARPU discipline and its balance-sheet muscle — but its fortunes rise and fall with just those two circles. That concentration is simultaneously a focused-growth advantage and a diversification limitation (§2).
  • The earnings driver is ARPU, and ARPU is rising. Indian telecom has consolidated to three private players (Jio, Airtel, Vi) plus BSNL, which has restored pricing power after a decade of ruinous price wars. Hexacom's mobile ARPU (average revenue per user) reached ₹252 in Q4 FY26 (from ₹242) and keeps climbing on tariff hikes, feature-phone-to-smartphone upgrades and data monetisation. Because a telco's network cost is largely fixed, each rupee of ARPU uplift drops heavily to profit (§1, §5).
  • The numbers already reflect it. FY26 revenue was ~₹9,354 Cr (+9.4%), EBITDA ~₹5,114 Cr at a ~53% margin, and net profit ₹1,733 Cr (+16%; +43.8% before exceptionals). Net debt (ex-leases) has fallen to a trivial ~0.44× EBITDA, and the company pays a rising dividend (₹18/share). It trades at ~41× earnings — a premium the market pays for a clean, deleveraging, ARPU-compounding Airtel proxy (§9).
In plain terms

Imagine India's mobile market as a set of ~22 regional territories, and imagine Airtel is the country's premium operator across almost all of them. In two of those territories — the big, fast-growing state of Rajasthan, and the seven states of the North-East — Airtel doesn't run the business directly; it runs it through a separately-listed company it owns 70% of, called Bharti Hexacom. Everything a customer there sees says "Airtel"; the towers, the SIM, the 5G, the app — all Airtel. So buying Hexacom is like buying a concentrated bet on Airtel's premium machine, but scoped to just those two territories. The good news: those two territories are growing, under-penetrated on home broadband, and enjoy the same rising-price tailwind as the rest of the industry. The catch: with only two territories, there's nowhere to hide if either one stumbles.

01

How a mobile operator earns — the ARPU dashboard

No jargon survives this section. Four numbers explain the whole business.

A mobile operator is, financially, a subscription business running on a largely fixed network. It builds towers, buys spectrum and lays fibre once (huge upfront cost), then earns a small monthly amount from each customer, month after month. The art is in two things: how many customers you have, and how much each pays. Four numbers tell you almost the whole story.

The simple revenue equation — and why ARPU is king

Roughly: Mobile revenue ≈ Subscribers × ARPU × 12. So an operator grows in two ways — add subscribers, or raise ARPU. In a mature, 3-player market, subscriber growth is slow and hard-won, so ARPU is where the money is made. And here's the magic: a network's costs — towers, spectrum, fibre, staff, energy — are mostly fixed. They cost roughly the same whether the average customer pays ₹200 or ₹250. So when ARPU rises, almost the entire increase drops straight to profit. That is operating leverage, and it is why a ₹10 ARPU increase matters far more than it looks — it lifts margins and cash flow, not just the top line. Watch ARPU above all else.

Mobile vs Homes — two engines

  • Mobile services — the core: prepaid and postpaid SIMs, voice and (mostly) data. This is the bulk of revenue and where ARPU lives. Mobile revenue rose ~3.8% YoY in Q4 FY26, driven by both ARPU and customer additions.
  • Homes, Office & Other services — home broadband, including fixed-wireless-access (FWA), IPTV and enterprise connectivity. Small today (~₹381 Cr in FY26) but the fastest-growing line, up ~51% — the new engine as fibre and 5G FWA reach under-penetrated homes (§6).
02

The "mini-Airtel" model — a circle-level proxy on the parent

The single idea that defines this stock. Read it slowly — everything else follows from it.

If you learn one thing about Bharti Hexacom, learn this: it is not a standalone telecom challenger — it is Bharti Airtel's own operating entity for two circles. India's mobile licences are granted circle by circle (a legacy of how spectrum was auctioned). Historically, Airtel operated most circles directly but ran Rajasthan and the North-East through this subsidiary, in which the government (via TCIL) held a legacy stake. So Hexacom is, quite literally, a geographically-carved-out piece of the Airtel franchise that happens to have its own share listing.

Hexacom is a "pure-play" on Airtel's premium model with almost none of Airtel's diversification: no Africa, no digital/enterprise conglomerate, no dozen-circle spread — just Rajasthan and the North-East.

Why the "proxy" framing is the right lens

  • Same business, same tailwinds. Hexacom benefits from exactly the forces driving the parent: the ARPU-uplift cycle, 2G-to-4G/5G upgrades, data growth and home-broadband expansion. When you read an Airtel thesis on "premiumisation and ARPU repair", it applies almost verbatim to Hexacom — just in two circles.
  • Focused-growth advantage. Rajasthan is a large, fast-growing circle where Airtel/Hexacom has been gaining revenue share for years; the North-East is a smaller but high-share stronghold. A focused operator can concentrate capital and management attention on winning these two markets — and it shows in the share-gain record (§6).
  • Diversification limitation. The flip side of focus: there are only two circles. If Rajasthan's competitive intensity spikes, or the North-East's growth disappoints, there's no third-of-the-country to cushion it. The parent Airtel spreads that risk across ~22 circles plus Africa; Hexacom cannot.
  • A governance nuance. Because Airtel controls it and provides the brand, network and services, related-party arrangements (network sharing, brand fees, management services) are a permanent feature. Minority shareholders are, in effect, along for the ride on decisions the parent makes for the whole group.
Why an investor buys the proxy instead of the parent

If Hexacom is just "Airtel in two circles", why not simply buy Airtel? Three reasons an investor might prefer the slice. (1) Purity. Airtel is a sprawling conglomerate — India mobile, Africa, enterprise, digital, data centres, DTH. Hexacom is a clean bet on Indian mobile ARPU-uplift, with none of the Africa currency risk or conglomerate discount. (2) Concentration in the right circles. Rajasthan and the North-East are under-penetrated on home broadband and still seeing 2G-to-smartphone migration — arguably higher-growth than Airtel's mature metro circles. (3) Deleveraging optionality. As a smaller, fast-deleveraging entity, its balance sheet is inflecting quickly. The trade-off is the obvious one: you give up diversification and you accept that the parent, not you, calls the shots. This is the core debate the whole stock turns on — and the reason it must never be confused with a distressed telco like Vodafone Idea (a solvency story) or a passive-infra landlord like Indus Towers (a rent-annuity). Hexacom is a growth-and-ARPU story.

03

The company, its history and who controls it

A 1990s circle licensee that became a listed slice of India's best telco.

Bharti Hexacom was incorporated in 1995 to hold telecom licences for Rajasthan and the North-East, and grew up inside the Bharti group as Airtel expanded. Its distinguishing feature was ownership: alongside Bharti Airtel, the Government of India — via TCIL (Telecommunications Consultants India Ltd) — held a large legacy stake. The 2024 IPO was essentially the government monetising that stake, not the company raising money.

Why two circles is the whole risk-and-reward story

Concentration cuts both ways. On the upside, these are two circles where the premiumisation and home-broadband runways are long — Rajasthan still has millions migrating from 2G to smartphones, and both circles have very low fixed-broadband penetration to attack with FWA. A focused operator can win here faster than a distracted national player. On the downside, there is no diversification: a price war in Rajasthan, an adverse regulatory event, or a natural disruption in the North-East hits the whole company at once. The parent Airtel absorbs such shocks across ~22 circles and two continents; Hexacom rides them bare. When you value Hexacom, you are really forecasting the fate of just these two maps.

05

The ARPU-uplift engine — the key earnings driver

The industry's structural tailwind, and where almost all of Hexacom's profit growth comes from.

India spent a decade in a brutal telecom price war. Jio's 2016 launch of near-free data forced tariffs to the floor, bankrupting weaker players and consolidating the market. But that consolidation is now the bullish twist: with only three private operators left (Jio, Airtel, Vi) plus state-owned BSNL, the survivors have regained pricing power — and are steadily raising tariffs. That structural ARPU repair is the single most important force in the sector, and Hexacom is a direct, leveraged beneficiary.

Three levers that lift ARPU — decoded

  • 1 · Tariff hikes. With three players instead of a dozen, operators can raise headline prices without losing customers to a cheaper rival (there barely is one). Industry-wide tariff hikes — the last major round in mid-2024 — flow straight into ARPU. More hikes are widely expected as operators chase returns on their 5G investment.
  • 2 · Upgrades (2G/feature-phone → 4G/5G smartphone). Every customer who moves from a ₹100-ish feature-phone plan to a ₹250+ smartphone data plan lifts the average. Hexacom's smartphone penetration rose from ~66% (FY23) to ~79% (FY26), and it added ~1.6m smartphone data customers in the year — a long migration runway remains, especially in rural Rajasthan.
  • 3 · Data monetisation. As usage rises (Hexacom's is ~34.2 GB/customer/month, +30.9% YoY), customers trade up to larger data packs and postpaid plans, and 5G opens premium tiers. More data, sold at better prices, per user.
Why ARPU uplift is so powerful — the operating-leverage multiplier

Here is the mechanism that makes ARPU the whole game. A telecom network is a fixed-cost machine: the towers, spectrum, fibre, energy and staff cost roughly the same whether the average customer pays ₹230 or ₹255. So when ARPU rises, the extra revenue lands on an already-paid-for cost base — and the majority of each additional rupee becomes EBITDA. That's why a modest ~10% ARPU increase can drive a much larger jump in profit, and why Hexacom's margin has widened toward ~53% even as revenue grows only high-single-digits. Stack this fixed-cost leverage on top of a multi-year tariff-repair cycle and a large upgrade runway, and you have the structural reason a telecom proxy can compound earnings well ahead of its revenue. ARPU × operating leverage is the entire earnings thesis.

06

Subscriber-share gains and the home-broadband / FWA optionality

The two growth engines beyond ARPU — winning premium subscribers, and wiring up homes.

Beyond the ARPU tailwind, Hexacom has two distinct growth engines. First, it keeps gaining premium subscriber and revenue share in its circles — the record above (§4) is not an accident but the payoff of Airtel-quality network in under-served markets. Second, and newer, is the home-broadband opportunity via Fixed Wireless Access (FWA) — a way to deliver broadband to homes without laying fibre to every doorstep.

Fixed Wireless Access — broadband over the air

In circles like Rajasthan and the North-East, fixed-line broadband penetration is very low — running fibre to scattered homes and difficult terrain is slow and expensive. FWA solves this: it delivers home broadband wirelessly, using the 5G network, via a simple plug-in receiver at the customer's home. The customer gets fibre-like speeds; the operator monetises spectrum it already owns, at a home-broadband ARPU far above mobile ARPU, without digging a single trench.

  • FWA / home broadband — the optionality: very low fixed-broadband penetration + owned 5G spectrum = a large, high-ARPU, capital-efficient adjacency. Early days, but the ~51% growth shows the demand is real.
  • Convergence & stickiness — bundling mobile + home broadband + IPTV in a household raises switching costs and lifetime value, echoing the "converged" strategy the parent Airtel runs nationally.
Why FWA is the perfect fit for these circles

FWA is arguably a better opportunity in Hexacom's circles than in a dense metro. In a city, fibre is already everywhere and competitive; in Rajasthan's spread-out towns and the North-East's hills, fibre-to-the-home is uneconomic — so a huge base of households has no good fixed-broadband option at all. FWA lets Hexacom reach them over the 5G airwaves it has already bought and lit for mobile, turning idle spectrum capacity into a second, higher-value subscription per household. It is capital-light growth that rides directly off the 5G capex the company is already spending. If it scales, it becomes a genuine second leg to the business rather than a rounding error.

07

The 3-player market and the rivals

Why the shape of Indian telecom — three survivors — is the backdrop for everything.

Hexacom's fortunes are inseparable from the structure of the Indian mobile market. A decade of price war left just three private operators standing plus a state-owned laggard — and that consolidated, rational structure is precisely what has restored pricing power and made telecom investable again.

Why "three players" is the whole bull-case backdrop

Market structure decides pricing power. In a fragmented market (India pre-2016 had ~12 operators), competitors undercut each other to zero and nobody makes money. In a consolidated three-player market, the survivors can raise prices in a broadly disciplined way, because a customer who dislikes a hike has few real alternatives. That is exactly what has happened: Vi's distress and BSNL's weakness leave Jio and Airtel as the two strong operators, and both want higher ARPU to earn a return on their 5G spend. Hexacom sits on the strong side of this — an Airtel-quality operator winning share from the weak while the whole industry's prices drift up. The risk to the thesis is if this discipline breaks: a well-funded Jio push, or a revived Vi, could reignite price competition in-circle. But the structural momentum, for now, favours the strong.

The Vi wildcard: Vodafone Idea's fate matters two ways. If Vi keeps losing subscribers, Airtel/Hexacom and Jio keep absorbing them — good for Hexacom. But if Vi's slow decline ever turns disorderly, or conversely if a fully-funded Vi turns aggressive on price, the competitive temperature in Hexacom's circles could shift. Watch the 3-player balance.
08

Spectrum, capex and the relationship with parent Airtel

The plumbing that makes a "mini-Airtel" work — and the deleveraging that's now underway.

A telco runs on two expensive inputs: spectrum (the airwaves it's licensed to use) and network capex (towers, fibre, 5G radios). Hexacom holds its own spectrum for its two circles, but it leans heavily on the Airtel group's scale for technology, purchasing power, tower access and systems — the essence of the proxy model. The good news of FY26 is that this capital machine is now throwing off enough cash to deleverage fast.

The three balance-sheet levers

  • Spectrum & 5G — Hexacom operates 5G across its circles using Airtel's spectrum bank and technology stack. Because the parent buys spectrum and gear at national scale, Hexacom gets group economics without group-sized standalone bargaining.
  • Capex, then deleverage — after the heavy 4G/5G build years, capex is being spent more selectively (5G densification, fibre, FWA), while rising EBITDA cash flow pays down debt. Net debt (ex-leases) fell to ~₹2,694 Cr, or ~0.44× EBITDA — a very comfortable level that funds dividends and further investment internally.
  • Parent backing — the Airtel relationship underpins the network, brand, procurement and (via joint operations) tower and fibre access. It de-risks execution enormously versus a standalone circle operator — but it is also why related-party terms matter to minority holders.
Why deleveraging changes the story

Telecom is famous for crushing debt loads — spectrum and networks are bought with borrowed money, and Vodafone Idea is the cautionary tale of what happens when the debt outruns the cash. Hexacom is the opposite picture: its debt is now tiny relative to its cash generation. That matters because as debt falls, more of the ARPU-driven EBITDA growth flows through to equity holders rather than to lenders — and it frees the company to keep investing in 5G/FWA while also raising the dividend (₹18/share in FY26, its highest yet). A fast-deleveraging, cash-generative telco with a rising ARPU is exactly the combination that re-rates. The balance sheet has gone from a constraint to a tailwind.

How to think about the ~41× multiple

At ~₹72,600 Cr market cap on ~₹1,733 Cr of FY26 profit, Hexacom trades around 41× trailing earnings — and telecom is usually valued on EV/EBITDA (enterprise value ÷ operating profit) because of its heavy fixed assets and debt. That price only makes sense if you believe the ARPU-repair cycle has years to run and that Hexacom keeps compounding EBITDA and cash. Brokers modelling the name (e.g. Elara Capital's ₹1,876 target, set on ~15× FY28E EV/EBITDA) pencil in an ~7% ARPU CAGR and mid-teens EBITDA CAGR through FY29 — the growth that a premium multiple is paying for. Bulls argue a clean, deleveraging, share-gaining Airtel proxy in two under-penetrated circles deserves a scarcity premium. Bears argue the same growth is available in the parent (with far more diversification) and that a two-circle, parent-controlled proxy at ~41× leaves no room for a tariff pause or a competitive shock. Both can be right at different points — which is why ARPU and share gains are the numbers to track each quarter.

10

The growth runway

Four stacked levers — and why the runway is longer than the two-circle map suggests.

The bear worry about Hexacom is "it's only two circles — how much can it grow?" The bull answer is that growth here is about depth, not breadth: raising the value of each existing customer and household, not conquering new territory. Four levers stack up.

Hexacom doesn't need a third circle to grow — it needs each of its existing customers and homes to pay a little more, a little more often. That is a longer runway than the map implies.
The concentration counterweight: the same "depth-not-breadth" model means the ceiling is ultimately set by the size and health of two circles. Bulls see a decade of premiumisation and FWA before that ceiling bites; bears note there is no new-geography escape valve if either circle disappoints. The runway is real — but it is a vertical runway, not a horizontal one.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely high-quality telecom proxy — priced for the up-cycle to continue.

Bharti Hexacom is one of the cleaner ways to own the Indian telecom recovery: an Airtel-quality operator, in two under-penetrated circles, riding a structural ARPU tailwind on a fast-deleveraging balance sheet. The investment debate is not about business quality — it is about price, concentration and control.

The bull case

  • Pure ARPU-repair play — a leveraged, clean bet on the 3-player market's rising tariffs and premiumisation, with none of the parent's Africa/conglomerate noise.
  • Airtel machine, focused circles — inherits the premium brand, network, spectrum and discipline; a persistent revenue-share gainer in Rajasthan and the North-East.
  • FWA / home-broadband optionality — a large, high-ARPU, capital-light adjacency in circles with very low fixed-broadband penetration; already growing ~51%.
  • Fast deleveraging — net debt ~0.44× EBITDA and falling; rising dividend; self-funded growth.
  • Premium economics — ~53% EBITDA margin, ~27% ROE, high operating leverage on ARPU.

The bear case

  • Two-circle concentration — no diversification; a shock in Rajasthan or the North-East hits the whole company with no cushion.
  • Rich valuation — ~41× earnings / premium EV/EBITDA leaves little room for a tariff pause or a competitive flare-up.
  • Parent controls the story — Airtel (70%) sets brand, network and capital terms; minorities ride along, with related-party arrangements a permanent feature.
  • Competitive risk — a well-funded Jio push, or a revived/aggressive Vi, could reignite in-circle price competition and stall ARPU.
  • Overhangs — TCIL's residual ~15% is a potential future supply of stock; regulatory/levy changes (as seen denting Q4) add noise.
The bottom line

Bharti Hexacom is a high-quality, structurally-advantaged slice of the Airtel franchise — a focused, deleveraging, ARPU-compounding operator in two growing circles. The business risk is low and the tailwinds are real; the valuation and concentration risks are what the debate turns on. Unlike Vodafone Idea (a solvency story) or Indus Towers (a rent annuity), Hexacom is a clean growth-and-ARPU proxy — its quarterly story is written in two numbers: ARPU (is the tariff-and-premiumisation engine still lifting revenue per user?) and revenue-share gains (is the Airtel machine still winning its two circles?). Get those right and the premium multiple compounds; a stumble in either, on a parent-controlled two-circle name at ~41×, and the price has room to fall.
