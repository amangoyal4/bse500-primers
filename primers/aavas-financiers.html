Aavas Financiers, the village home-loan lender with big-bank credit quality

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Aavas Financiers is a housing finance company (HFC) that makes small home loans — averaging roughly ₹9–10 lakh — to low-income, often self-employed and informal-income first-time buyers in India's smaller towns and villages, concentrated in the north and west of the country (Rajasthan, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh). It lends to the customer that big banks find impossible to score: the shopkeeper, the dairy farmer, the small contractor with no salary slip, no formal accounts, and often no credit history at all. It reaches them through a deep, branch-heavy, feet-on-street network — 435 branches across 15 states — and it assesses each borrower by hand, one relationship at a time.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It grows steadily and it grows clean. Aavas ended FY26 with an ~₹23,450 Cr loan book (AUM), up ~15% for the year, on ~₹6,780 Cr of disbursements. But the number that defines it is asset quality: gross NPAs of just ~1.05% and full-year credit costs of ~13 basis points — a loss rate so low it would flatter a prime salaried mortgage book, achieved on borrowers with no formal income proof at all.
  • The economics are the affordable-HFC template — a fat spread on secured loans. Aavas charges ~12–13% on its home loans and borrows at ~7.6%, holding a spread of ~5.2% and a net interest margin of ~7.9%. Because losses are tiny, most of that margin drops through: FY26 PAT of ~₹656 Cr (+14%) at a ~3.5% return on assets — best-in-class among Indian lenders.
  • Its "problem" is cost, not credit. The high-touch, deep-rural model is expensive to run — every loan needs a person to visit a village, verify a business by eye, and stay close to the borrower. That gives Aavas a higher cost-to-income ratio (roughly ~40%, approx.) than tech-first peers. The whole debate around the stock is whether it can digitise and get more efficient without denting the very credit quality that is its edge — and whether a pristine book on a premium valuation leaves enough return.
In plain terms

Aavas does the un-scalable-looking thing on purpose. A bank underwrites a home loan by reading a salary slip and a credit score through a computer. Aavas can't — its borrower has neither — so instead it sends a person to the borrower's shop or farm to work out, in cash terms, how much that family really earns and can repay, then keeps a relationship with them for the life of the loan. It looks slow and costly, and it is. The surprise is that this hands-on method produces better loan losses than the automated banks, not worse. The bet on Aavas is that this credit discipline is durable, that the deep-rural runway is long, and that the company can shave its costs down without loosening the underwriting that makes the whole thing work.

01

Affordable housing finance 101: lending where banks won't

No finance degree needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

A home loan is simple in principle: a lender advances money against a house, the borrower repays over 15–20 years, and the house is collateral if they don't. What makes affordable housing finance a distinct business is who the borrower is. Aavas's customer is outside the formal financial system — a family buying or building its first small home in a town or village, whose income arrives in cash, whose "accounts" are a notebook, and who cannot produce the salary slip and credit bureau score a bank's software demands. Serving that customer needs a completely different toolkit, and it comes down to five ideas.

The only terms you need

The economics that decide everything: a wide spread minus tiny losses

An affordable HFC makes money in a way that looks almost too good until you see the risk. It borrows at, say, 7.6% and lends at 12.5%, so on every ₹100 of loans it earns roughly ₹5 of gross spread. Out of that it must pay its running costs (branches, staff) and absorb its loan losses. If losses are large — as they can be when you lend to informal borrowers — the model breaks. Aavas's entire achievement is keeping those losses minuscule (~13 paise of every ₹100 lent), so almost the whole spread survives to become profit. A wide spread is common in this niche; keeping the losses tiny while earning it is the rare part.

Why banks leave this customer alone

  • No documents to score. A bank's underwriting runs on salary slips, tax returns and credit bureau scores. The rural, self-employed borrower has none of these, so the bank's model simply rejects the file — the customer is "unscoreable," not necessarily un-creditworthy.
  • Small loans, high touch, low branch density. A ₹9 lakh loan in a village 60 km from the nearest bank branch is expensive to originate and service relative to its size. Big banks chase larger, cheaper-to-serve urban mortgages instead.
  • The gap that HFCs fill. That leaves a large, underserved market for specialists willing to build the physical presence and the manual underwriting to lend safely where the formal system won't — the space Aavas has occupied longer than almost anyone.
02

Who Aavas lends to, and what it actually books

The customer, the ticket size, and the two revenue lines — read this once and the company clicks into place.

Aavas's book is built one small, secured loan at a time. The typical loan is around ₹9–10 lakh — a fraction of an urban mortgage — used to buy, build or extend a modest first home. The borrower is usually self-employed with informal income, buying in a town or village where property values are low and the family has lived for generations. Two things about this base matter enormously: it is secured (there is a house behind every loan) and it is sticky (a first-time rural home-owner rarely refinances away). Aavas earns from it in two streams.

The same branch that sourced a loan also underwrites, services and collects it — the borrower deals with people who know the local market, not a distant call-centre. That proximity is the quiet reason repayment stays high.

The two revenue lines, decoded

  • Home loans (the core) — the large majority of the book: loans to buy, build, improve or extend a home. This is the sticky, secured, long-tenor asset that Aavas underwrites by hand and holds for years. The yield here (~12–13%) against a low cost of funds is what creates the spread.
  • Other mortgage-backed loans — a smaller slice, including loans against property (LAP) to small businesses secured on a home or shop, at somewhat higher yields. It lifts the blended yield but Aavas keeps it a controlled minority of the book precisely to protect asset quality — it does not let the higher-yield product take over.
Why "secured and small" is the safety net under the whole book

Two design choices make Aavas's risky-sounding customer base far safer than it looks. First, every loan is secured on a house — if a borrower defaults, there is a real, saleable asset behind the loan, which both limits losses and, crucially, keeps borrowers paying (people fight hard not to lose the family home). Second, the loans are small and the loan-to-value conservative — the borrower has real equity of their own in the property from day one, so they have skin in the game and the collateral comfortably covers the loan. A self-employed borrower with no salary slip sounds like a credit risk; a self-employed borrower with their own savings sunk into a modest house they will not risk losing is a very different proposition.

03

The company, its heritage, and who owns it

Born out of AU, built by private equity, now professionally run without a founding family in control.

Aavas has an unusual pedigree. It began life inside AU Financiers (now AU Small Finance Bank) as its housing-finance arm, was carved out in 2016, renamed Aavas, and taken over by the private-equity firms Kedaara Capital and Partners Group. It listed in 2018. What makes its ownership distinctive is what it lacks: there is no traditional founding-family promoter running the show. It is a PE-sponsored, professionally managed institution — closer in governance to a listed subsidiary than to a family business.

Ownership & structure

  • The PE sponsors: Kedaara Capital and Partners Group have historically been the largest shareholders (via entities such as Lake District Holdings and Partners Group affiliates). Over time they have reduced their combined stake through market sell-downs — a normal PE exit path — so the free float has risen and the register is increasingly institutional (approx.; track the latest shareholding filing for exact levels).
  • No family promoter: unusually for an Indian mid-cap, there is no founder-family controlling block. Aavas is run by a professional management team and board. That removes the usual key-man and promoter-pledge risks — but it also means the sponsors can (and do) sell, which periodically weighs on the stock as blocks change hands.
  • The balance sheet: net worth of roughly ~₹4,500 Cr (approx.) supports the ~₹23,450 Cr book, funded largely by borrowings of ~₹20,400 Cr raised from banks, NHB refinance, bonds and securitisation. The leverage is modest for a lender and the capital adequacy comfortably above requirement — this is a well-capitalised, conservatively geared HFC.
The ownership quirk worth holding in mind: a PE-sponsored, promoter-less lender is a double-edged setup. On the upside, governance is institutional, incentives are professionalised, and there is no family balance-sheet entangled with the company. On the downside, the largest holders are financial owners whose job is eventually to sell — so supply overhangs and block deals are a recurring feature of the shareholding, quite separate from how the business itself is doing.
04

The feet-on-street model: underwriting a borrower with no paperwork

The core skill — and the deliberate opposite of the tech-first affordable lenders.

This is where Aavas is genuinely different from its peers, and where the whole company must be understood. Its borrower cannot be scored by an algorithm, so Aavas underwrites by hand, in person, one file at a time. A credit officer physically visits the borrower — their shop, their farm, their workshop — and reconstructs, from observation and conversation, what the family actually earns and can repay. How busy is the shop? How much stock turns over? What do neighbours say? What is the property really worth? It is slow, human, judgement-heavy work, and it is the deliberate inverse of the tech-first, salaried-heavy, app-underwritten approach that lenders like Home First have built.

The manual model costs more to run (more people, more branches, more visits per loan) but produces dramatically lower losses. The tech-first model is cheaper and faster to scale but leans on borrowers who can be scored — a different, often more salaried, customer. Aavas has chosen high-touch over high-throughput.

The manual model does more than assess income. Because the same local branch sources, underwrites, services and collects the loan, Aavas maintains a relationship with the borrower for years — not a transaction. That proximity is a second layer of credit control: local staff spot trouble early, understand the borrower's real circumstances, and can work out a repayment before a missed instalment becomes a default. In a book of informal-income borrowers, that difference between a distant lender and a present one shows up directly in the NPA line.

The device that only fits Aavas: the method that looks un-scalable IS the moat

Most competitive advantages in lending come from being cheaper or faster — better software, lower funding, quicker approvals. Aavas's advantage is the opposite: it comes from being deliberately slower and more expensive per loan than it strictly has to be. The painstaking, in-person underwriting that makes each loan costly to originate is exactly what keeps the losses near-zero, and near-zero losses on a wide spread are what produce the sector-leading returns. You cannot copy the moat by spending less; you copy it only by building the same physical network and the same underwriting culture over the same number of years — which is precisely what Aavas's longest-in-the-sector track record represents. The un-scalable-looking method is not a bug the company needs to fix; it is the asset.

05

The pristine-asset-quality surprise

A lender to informal village borrowers has some of the lowest bad loans in the entire Indian financial system.

Here is the fact that stops most people short. Aavas lends to families with no salary slip, no formal accounts and often no prior credit history, in villages and small towns far from the formal banking grid — the demographic a risk manager would instinctively call high-risk. And its gross NPA is ~1.05%, with net Stage-3 assets of ~0.68% and full-year credit costs of just ~13 basis points. That is a loss experience better than many banks' prime, salaried mortgage books. The riskiest-sounding borrower base in Indian retail lending has produced one of its cleanest loan books.

Credit cost of ~13 bps sits comfortably inside management's own <25 bps guardrail. On a ₹23,450 Cr book, that means annual loan losses measured in a couple of dozen crore — a rounding error against ~₹656 Cr of profit.

Why is it this good? Several forces stack up. The loans are secured on the family home, which both caps losses and powerfully motivates repayment. Ticket sizes are small with conservative loan-to-value, so borrowers have real equity at stake from day one. The manual, in-person underwriting screens out bad credits before they enter the book. And the local, relationship-based collection catches stress early. None of these on its own is unusual; the combination, executed with discipline over more than a decade, is what makes Aavas an outlier rather than merely good.

The genuine surprise, stated plainly

Intuition says a lender's loan losses should rise as its borrowers get poorer, more rural and more informal. Aavas inverts that intuition. Its borrowers are among the most informal in Indian retail credit, yet its losses are among the lowest — because the very difficulty of lending to them forces a discipline (visit every borrower, secure every loan, keep every ticket small, stay close for years) that a lender to easy, scoreable customers never has to build. The rural, no-documents borrower is not the risk in this story. The risk, as the next section shows, is what it costs to serve them well.

A caution on the compliment: pristine asset quality is Aavas's crown, which means the market watches it obsessively. The flip side of being prized for low NPAs is that even a small upward drift — a rise from ~1% to ~1.5% GNPA in a stress year — would be read as the moat cracking and could hit the valuation harder than the actual losses justify. Aavas is, in effect, priced for its credit quality never slipping.
06

The cost-to-income trade: the whole debate in one tension

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a high-touch rural lender.

Everything so far has a price, and this is where you pay it. The deep-distribution, branch-heavy, manual-underwriting model that produces Aavas's spotless credit is expensive to run. Every loan requires people — to travel to a village, verify a business by eye, service the account locally, and maintain a relationship for years. That labour and physical footprint show up as a higher cost-to-income ratio (roughly ~40%, approx.) and a higher operating-cost-to-AUM than the tech-first, lower-touch affordable lenders. Aavas does not have a bad-loan problem. It has a cost problem — and that reframes the entire investment question.

Aavas buys pristine credit quality with a costly, high-touch branch model. The whole debate is whether it can lift efficiency and digitise — without letting the underwriting that produces that credit quality drift.

The two levers pull against each other, which is what makes this interesting. Cut costs crudely — fewer field visits, lighter verification, more automated approvals, thinner staffing — and you might get the cost-to-income ratio down fast, but you risk loosening the manual underwriting that keeps GNPA at 1%. Let costs run — keep every visit, every relationship, every branch — and asset quality stays perfect but returns are capped by the operating drag, and the model looks structurally less efficient than peers. The prize is a narrow path between them: get more loans through the same branch and the same officer using better technology, so cost-to-income falls while the human judgement at the point of underwriting stays intact.

This is why Aavas's investment in loan-origination systems, analytics and digital servicing over the last few years matters so much — and why management frames it carefully as digitising the process, not the judgement. The technology is meant to remove the paperwork, travel and administrative cost around a loan, so the credit officer spends more time on the one thing a machine cannot do for this borrower: sizing up an informal cash business in person. If that works, operating costs fall as a share of a growing book, cost-to-income grinds down toward the peers, and the sector-leading ROA gets even better. If it goes too far and the human underwriting thins out, the crown jewel — the 1% GNPA — is what's at risk.

Why this trade is the entire thesis

Reduce Aavas to one sentence and it is this: a lender that has already proven it can keep losses near-zero is being asked to prove it can do so more cheaply. Credit quality is the settled part of the story; efficiency is the open question. That is an unusual place for a lender to sit — most are the other way round, cheap to run but fighting their loan losses. Aavas's next chapter is written not in its NPA line (already excellent) but in its cost-to-income line, and specifically in whether that line can fall while the NPA line stays flat. Both moving in the right direction at once is the bull case; the cost line refusing to fall, or the NPA line rising when it does, is the bear case.

07

North & West, and deep into the villages

A geographic footprint that is both a moat and a concentration risk — and the opposite of the South-heavy peers.

Where Aavas lends is as distinctive as how it lends. Its heartland is north and west India — Rajasthan (its home base), Gujarat, Maharashtra and Madhya Pradesh — reaching deep into semi-urban towns and villages rather than clustering in big cities. That footprint sets it apart from the affordable-HFC peers that are concentrated in the south (Aptus, for one) or spread pan-India with a more urban, salaried tilt (Home First). Aavas got to the deep rural north and west early and built physical density there over years — 435 branches across 15 states, many in places larger lenders never bothered to reach.

Why the footprint is differentiated, not just different

Affordable housing finance is often discussed as one homogeneous niche, but the players barely compete for the same borrower. A South-concentrated lender and a north/west deep-rural lender are, in practice, in largely separate markets — different states, different customer profiles, different languages of business. Aavas's north-and-west, deep-village positioning means much of its book faces less direct competition from the other listed affordable HFCs than a headline peer comparison suggests. The cost of that insulation is geographic concentration: a serious downturn, a bad monsoon cycle, or a property-market wobble in its core states would hit Aavas more than a lender spread evenly across India. The moat and the risk are the same fact seen from two sides.

The expansion philosophy reinforces the point. Aavas grows mostly by deepening contiguously — opening new branches adjacent to markets it already understands rather than parachuting into unfamiliar geographies. That keeps underwriting quality intact as it scales (a new branch is staffed and supervised by people who know the region), but it also means Aavas typically grows its book a little slower than the most aggressive peers. Slower, safer growth is a defensible choice for a lender whose entire brand is credit quality — but it is a choice the market prices, and part of why the AUM-growth line is watched as closely as the GNPA line.

08

The spread engine: how the profit is actually made

Yield, cost of funds, margin and leverage — the four dials that turn a clean book into a top-tier ROA.

Strip an affordable HFC down to its mechanics and it is a spread business: borrow cheaply, lend dearly, keep losses low, and use modest leverage to turn a good margin into a great return on equity. Aavas runs all four dials well. It lends at ~12–13%, funds itself at ~7.6%, holds a spread of ~5.2% and a net interest margin of ~7.9% (which expanded ~29 bps in FY26 and reached ~8.45% in Q4), and — because credit losses are trivial — converts most of that into a ~3.5% return on assets. Few lenders anywhere earn a 3%+ ROA; Aavas does it on informal rural borrowers.

The wide spread survives almost intact because credit cost (~13 bps) barely touches it. Net interest income rose ~17% in FY26 to ~₹1,356 Cr as margins expanded and the cost of funds fell ~62 bps — a rare year where both yield and funding moved the right way.

Two moving parts drove FY26. On the asset side, disciplined, risk-adjusted pricing and a stable mix held the yield up. On the liability side, Aavas's cost of funds fell ~62 bps as it raised ~₹6,705 Cr of fresh borrowing at ~7.61% from a diversified base — banks, NHB refinance (a cheap, sector-specific source for HFCs), bonds and securitisation. A strong credit rating lets Aavas borrow at rates its own borrowers could never access; the gap between the two is, quite literally, the business. Because the company runs modest leverage, that ~3.5% ROA translates into a ~14–15% return on equity — good, though held below the very highest HFC ROEs precisely because Aavas is under-levered and carries a lot of capital.

Why funding cost is the second half of the story

Asset quality gets all the attention, but a spread lender's fortunes swing just as much on the borrowing side. If cost of funds rises — because rates go up, or because a credit event makes lenders wary of HFCs — the spread compresses even if the loan book is flawless. Aavas's ability to keep lowering its funding cost (a strong rating, NHB refinance access, a diversified borrowing mix) is therefore as central to the thesis as its NPA line. The FY26 combination of expanding NIM and falling cost of funds is close to ideal and unlikely to repeat every year; a sober forecast assumes the spread normalises rather than keeps widening.

09

Why the runway is long

The structural tailwinds under rural affordable housing — the reason a mid-teens grower can keep growing.

Aavas's opportunity is not a cyclical upswing; it is a long, structural under-penetration. India's mortgage-to-GDP ratio is far below developed and even many emerging economies, and that gap is widest exactly where Aavas operates — among low-income, self-employed, semi-urban and rural first-time buyers who have historically been shut out of formal home finance. Several forces are slowly pulling that population into the credit system.

The tailwind that also happens to lower Aavas's costs

Most growth tailwinds only add customers. This one does something extra for Aavas. As rural India financialises — as more borrowers acquire bank accounts, digital payment trails and thin credit records — the informal borrower who once could only be assessed by an in-person visit becomes gradually easier and cheaper to underwrite. In other words, the same structural shift that expands Aavas's market also, over time, chips away at the cost problem that defines its investment case. It does not eliminate the need for feet-on-street judgement, but it means the high-touch model may cost a little less to run each year as the borrower base slowly formalises around it.

The catch is that a long runway does not guarantee Aavas walks down it fastest. The same under-penetrated market that gives Aavas decades of demand is drawing in a growing crowd of competitors, from other affordable HFCs to banks moving down-market. A long runway is necessary for the thesis; it is not sufficient. Which is the subject of the next section.

10

The industry and the rivals

A crowding niche of specialist lenders — each with a different edge — plus banks pressing down-market.

Affordable housing finance has become one of the most competitive corners of Indian lending, and the listed peer set is unusually rich. What is important is that these are not clones: each specialist has carved out a different edge — a different geography, a different product, a different underwriting style. Aavas competes less by beating a rival head-on and more by being the deep-rural, north/west, manual-underwriting incumbent while others occupy adjacent ground.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two forces matter more than the individual names. First, the specialists mostly do not fight over the same borrower — geography, product and underwriting style keep Aptus, Home First, India Shelter and Aavas in largely separate lanes, so a rising market can lift several of them at once. Second, the real long-term pressure comes from above, not beside: large banks and low-cost lenders like Bajaj Housing edging down-market with cheaper funding. Their advantage — cost of funds — is exactly where Aavas is weakest, and their weakness — the inability to underwrite a no-documents rural borrower by hand — is exactly Aavas's moat. The competitive question is whether that moat holds as more of Aavas's borrowers slowly become formal enough for a bank to score. The more rural and informal the customer stays, the safer Aavas is from the banks; the faster they formalise, the more the fight comes to Aavas's door.

Financials & valuation

A steady compounder with best-in-class credit — priced at a premium for exactly that.

FY26 was a clean, all-round-solid year: AUM up ~15%, NII up ~17%, PAT up ~14%, margins expanding, funding cost falling, and asset quality holding near its historical best. There is nothing broken here to fix. The question the valuation poses is different — whether a business this steady and this pristine, but growing in the mid-teens and carrying a higher cost base than peers, earns the premium multiple the market attaches to it.

Aavas trades at roughly 3× book and ~23× earnings (approx., on a ~₹15,000 Cr market cap) — a clear premium to the market and to some peers, though not the richest in the affordable-HFC pack. That is the price of predictability: investors pay up for a book that has proven it can grow mid-teens for years while keeping losses near zero. The bear reading is that a mid-teens grower with a ~40% cost-to-income ratio, geographic concentration and a slowly crowding niche should not command a premium multiple, and that PE sponsor sell-downs cap the upside. The bull reading is that best-in-sector credit quality, a decades-long runway and a genuine efficiency lever still to be pulled justify it — and that if cost-to-income falls while ROA holds, the earnings compound faster than the headline growth suggests.

The one number that settles the thesis

For most lenders you would point to asset quality as the metric that makes or breaks the case. For Aavas, that number is already settled — GNPA near 1% is the given, not the variable. The metric that actually decides this stock is the combination: AUM compounding in the mid-teens, GNPA staying near ~1%, cost-to-income grinding down toward the tech-first peers, and ROA holding around ~3.5% while it happens. Isolate the swing factor and it is cost-to-income falling without GNPA rising — efficiency improving while credit quality stays pristine. If both lines move the right way together, the premium multiple is earned and the earnings compound ahead of AUM. If cost-to-income refuses to fall — or worse, if it only falls because underwriting loosened and GNPA starts to drift up — the premium is the first thing the market takes back.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A proven credit machine asked to prove it can also be a cheaper one.

Aavas enters FY27 as the quality benchmark of its sector: a long track record, pristine asset quality, a wide spread, a falling cost of funds and a decades-long rural runway. The fair debate is not whether the lending works — a decade of ~1% GNPA settles that — but whether it can be run more efficiently, grown a little faster, and defended against banks pressing down-market, all without loosening the underwriting that is the entire point.

The bull case

  • Best-in-sector asset quality — ~1.05% GNPA and ~13 bps credit cost on informal rural borrowers; a low-loss moat built over the longest track record in affordable HFCs.
  • Top-tier returns — ~3.5% ROA on a wide, well-defended spread; funding cost falling ~62 bps in FY26.
  • Efficiency lever still to pull — a ~40% cost-to-income ratio with a real path toward sub-3% opex-to-AUM as tech and scale bite; every point out flows to ROA.
  • Long structural runway — deep housing under-penetration, PMAY support and financialisation across an under-served north/west rural base.
  • Differentiated, insulated franchise — deep-rural, north/west, manual-underwriting positioning with less head-to-head overlap than the peer list implies.

The bear case

  • Cost-to-income drag — the high-touch model is structurally expensive; cutting costs risks the very credit quality that is its edge.
  • Premium valuation — ~3× book / ~23× earnings on a mid-teens grower leaves limited margin if growth or efficiency disappoints.
  • Geographic & borrower concentration — heavy north/west, rural, self-employed exposure is vulnerable to monsoon, local shocks and regional property wobbles.
  • Competition closing in — Aptus, Home First, India Shelter, Aadhar and, above all, banks and Bajaj Housing pressing down-market with cheaper funding.
  • Rate, regulatory & ownership risks — funding cost and RBI/NHB HFC norms can move the spread; PE sponsor sell-downs are a recurring supply overhang.
The bottom line

Aavas has already won the argument that a lender to no-documents village home-buyers can keep its loan losses near zero — a decade of ~1% GNPA has settled it, and that is a genuinely rare and durable thing to have proven. What it has not yet fully proven is that the painstaking, people-heavy machine which delivers that credit quality can also be made materially cheaper to run without blunting it. The next few years turn on a single, checkable print each quarter: the cost-to-income ratio falling toward its tech-first peers while GNPA holds near 1% and ROA stays around 3.5%. Watch those three lines move together — clean book, lower cost, steady return — and Aavas is a quality compounder growing into the runway beneath it. Watch the cost line refuse to budge, or the NPA line twitch upward when it finally does, and the premium the market pays for perfection is the first thing to go.
