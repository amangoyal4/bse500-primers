00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Devyani International runs the restaurants you already know — KFC, Pizza Hut and Costa Coffee — but here is the fact that reframes the whole company: it owns none of those brands. Devyani is a franchisee. The brands belong to Yum! Brands (KFC, Pizza Hut) and The Coca-Cola Company (Costa Coffee); Devyani pays them a royalty for the right to build and operate their outlets across India and a few overseas markets. It is India's largest operator of these Yum brands, with over 2,250 stores across 280-plus cities. Its business is not inventing food — it is opening stores and running them well.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It grows by building stores with its own money. Every KFC or Pizza Hut Devyani opens is funded by Devyani — the fit-out, the kitchen, the staff, the rent — while a slice of every rupee of sales flows back to Yum as royalty. In FY26 it added a net ~217 stores to reach ~2,256. This is a capital-intensive, expansion-led model: the growth engine is net store additions, and the health-check is whether existing stores keep selling more.
  • It loses money at the bottom line — and that is partly the point. FY26 revenue rose ~13% to ~₹5,612 Cr, operating EBITDA margin held near 16%, yet the company posted a small net loss of ~₹42.5 Cr. Rapid expansion front-loads costs (pre-opening, rent, and — crucially — lease depreciation and interest under Ind-AS 116 accounting) while young stores are still maturing. A restaurant company can be healthy at the store level and red at the company level at the same time.
  • Chicken is winning; pizza is not. KFC delivered its best same-store sales growth in 14 quarters (+4.9% in Q4 FY26), while Pizza Hut's same-store sales fell ~3.7% and management froze its expansion — cutting Pizza Hut openings from 63 the prior year to just 9. One franchisee, two completely opposite verdicts on Indian fast food.
In plain terms

Devyani is a property-and-operations machine bolted onto someone else's brands. Yum! and Coca-Cola supply the logo, the recipe and the playbook; Devyani supplies the capital, the real estate, the kitchens and the people — and keeps the store profit after paying royalty. So the company you are analysing is really a bet on two things: how fast it can open good stores, and how much each store earns once it is running. The reported net loss looks alarming until you realise a fast-expanding chain deliberately spends today (new outlets, leases, depreciation) to own a bigger, more profitable network tomorrow. The catch is that "tomorrow" has to actually arrive — and Pizza Hut is the reminder that it doesn't always.

01

The franchisee model, in plain English

No jargon needed — just the handful of ideas that separate a brand-owner from a brand-operator.

To understand Devyani you have to understand what it is not. It is not Yum! Brands. Yum! is the American company that owns KFC and Pizza Hut worldwide — the trademarks, the recipes (the eleven herbs and spices), the global marketing, the standards. Devyani is a franchisee: it signs a long-term agreement with Yum! for the right to build and run KFC and Pizza Hut outlets in specific territories, invests its own capital in each one, and keeps the profit that outlet makes — after handing Yum! a royalty (a percentage of sales) for the use of the brand. Costa Coffee works the same way, except the brand-owner there is The Coca-Cola Company. Five terms carry the entire business.

The economics that decide everything: build cost vs. store payback

A single QSR outlet is a small capital project. Devyani spends a lump sum to fit out a KFC — kitchen equipment, seating, signage, the lease deposit — then that store generates sales day after day. The magic number is how quickly the store's operating cash flow pays back the money sunk into it (the "cash payback period"). A strong KFC can pay itself back in a couple of years and then compound; a weak Pizza Hut in a poor location may never earn its cost of capital. So the company is really a portfolio of hundreds of these little projects, and its value depends on the average quality of the stores it keeps opening. Grow the count with good stores and value builds fast; grow it with mediocre ones and you have simply bought yourself a bigger loss.

Why be a franchisee at all?

  • You skip the hardest part — building a brand. KFC and Pizza Hut already have decades of global recognition, supply chains and marketing. Devyani plugs into a proven machine rather than gambling on an unknown name.
  • You take the capital and execution risk. In exchange, the franchisee funds every store and lives or dies on operations — site selection, kitchen throughput, service, and cost control. The brand-owner's downside is capped; the franchisee's is not.
  • You are one of several. Yum! deliberately uses more than one franchisee in India. Devyani's peer Sapphire Foods runs KFC and Pizza Hut in other territories under the same brands — so the two are not head-to-head rivals for customers so much as two bets on the same franchisee playbook.
02

What Devyani actually runs: three brands, three different fights

One company, but the portfolio splits into pieces with very different economics.

Devyani's revenue comes from operating a portfolio of brands, and they are not created equal. The engine is KFC; the problem child is Pizza Hut; the quiet third leg is Costa Coffee; and around the edges sit an international arm and a handful of owned brands. Read this map once and the rest of the primer clicks into place.

The portfolio, decoded

  • KFC — the profit engine. Fried chicken is Devyani's largest and healthiest brand, with 700-plus outlets in India (approx.) and the strongest unit economics. KFC drove Q4 FY26 revenue up ~14.6% to ~₹586 Cr and delivered positive same-store growth. When people say the Devyani story is "working," they mean KFC.
  • Pizza Hut — the drag. Around 639 outlets in India (approx.), but caught in a brutal pizza fight against Domino's and delivery-first rivals. Q4 FY26 India revenue slipped ~3.5% to ~₹169 Cr and same-store sales fell ~3.7%. Management has frozen new Pizza Hut openings (down to ~9 in FY26 from 63) while it resets the brand's marketing and menu.
  • Costa Coffee — the option. Around 220-plus cafés (approx.), Devyani's play on India's fast-growing café-and-coffee habit. Smaller, still scaling, and pitched directly against Starbucks (run in India by Tata) and a wave of home-grown chains.
  • International & owned brands. Devyani also runs KFC/Pizza Hut outlets in Nepal and Nigeria (and has expanded into Thailand), plus its own brands such as Vaango (South Indian food) and other formats. These are the smaller balance of the ~2,256-store network beyond the three flagships.
Why the mix matters more than the total

It is tempting to look at "2,256 stores, +13% revenue" and file Devyani as a single growth story. But the three brands are pulling in different directions, and the blended numbers hide it. A rising KFC and a sinking Pizza Hut can average out to a mediocre-looking company-wide same-store figure — which then flatters the weak brand and understates the strong one. To judge Devyani you have to un-blend it: KFC's trajectory is the asset, Pizza Hut's is the liability, and Costa is the wildcard. The single most useful habit when reading a Devyani result is to ignore the consolidated line first and read each brand's same-store growth and store adds on its own.

03

RJ Corp, the Jaipuria family, and who controls it

A cola-empire family that also happens to run India's KFC.

Devyani International is the food-services arm of RJ Corp, the sprawling group built by Ravi Kant Jaipuria. RJ Corp holds roughly 59% of Devyani, so this is a firmly promoter-controlled company. And the promoter's other business is the tell that makes Devyani far more interesting than a standalone restaurant chain.

The surprise hiding in the ownership

Ravi Jaipuria's flagship is Varun Beverages — one of the largest PepsiCo bottlers in the world outside the United States, the company that makes and distributes Pepsi, Mountain Dew and Sting across India and a swathe of Africa and South Asia. In other words, the family that controls Devyani is one of the biggest PepsiCo partners on earth. Now look at Devyani's third brand: Costa Coffee, which is owned by The Coca-Cola Company — PepsiCo's arch-rival. The same promoter house pours Pepsi through one listed vehicle and pulls Coca-Cola's coffee through another. It is a neat illustration of what a pure franchisee really is: an operator agnostic to whose brand it carries, as long as the store economics work.

A genuine surprise: the people who built India's Pepsi bottling giant (Varun Beverages) also run Coca-Cola-owned Costa Coffee inside Devyani — and they own none of KFC, Pizza Hut or Costa outright. Devyani is not a brand company wearing an operator's hat; it is a capital-and-operations company that will happily carry a competitor's brand if the unit economics stack up.

Ownership & structure — what to keep straight

  • Promoter: RJ Corp (Ravi Kant Jaipuria & family) holds ~59%, giving the family clear control. Track the promoter stake and any pledge levels directly in the shareholding filings.
  • Not the same as Sapphire Foods. Sapphire Foods is a separately listed company that also franchises KFC and Pizza Hut — in different territories, and not controlled by RJ Corp. The two are often compared but are distinct owners.
  • Not the same as Jubilant FoodWorks. Jubilant runs Domino's in India — Pizza Hut's biggest enemy, and a different promoter group entirely. Devyani competes against Jubilant in pizza while sharing the broader QSR space.
  • The Varun Beverages link is a relationship, not a merger. Varun Beverages and Devyani are separate listed companies under the same promoter family; they are not consolidated into one another. The shared parent explains the group's distribution and real-estate muscle, not a single balance sheet.
04

The store-count engine: how Devyani grows

The primary lever on the whole business — and the reason it spends so much.

Because Devyani rents its brands, it cannot grow by inventing new products the way a brand-owner might. It grows by opening stores — and by getting the stores it already has to sell more. Net store additions are therefore the single most important operating number in the model: each new outlet is a fresh stream of future sales (and royalty, and rent, and depreciation). India's QSR market is still strikingly under-penetrated versus richer economies, which is the structural bet under the whole expansion.

The strategy is deliberately lopsided. Devyani pours its capital into the brand that is working — KFC — and starves the one that isn't. FY25's roughly 235 new stores skewed toward KFC and Costa; in FY26 that tilt became explicit, with Pizza Hut openings slashed to single digits. This is the discipline the model demands: capital is finite, each store is a bet, and a franchisee should feed its winners. The risk is the mirror image — over-build in a downturn and you have signed thousands of multi-year leases just as footfall softens.

The cost that comes with every new store

Opening a store is not free growth — it is a burst of expense that lands before the sales do. There are pre-opening costs (hiring and training staff, trial runs, launch marketing), and from day one the outlet carries rent, depreciation on its fit-out, and the accounting cost of its lease. A brand-new store typically drags on profit in its first months and only turns nicely positive once footfall matures. So a company adding 200-plus stores a year is perpetually carrying a cohort of young, still-ramping outlets that depress the blended margin. Slow the openings and reported profit can actually improve in the short run — which is exactly the trade-off management weighs every year.

Why "under-penetration" is the core bull argument: India has a tiny number of organised QSR outlets per million people compared with markets like China or the US, and eating out is still a small share of the average household's food spend. If that gap narrows even part-way as incomes rise and cities grow, a franchisee with a proven brand and the capital to build has years of store additions ahead. The entire expansion case rests on that runway being real and durable — not a one-cycle sugar rush.
05

SSSG and unit economics: the demand health-check

Store count tells you how big; same-store sales tell you how healthy.

If net store additions are the growth engine, same-store sales growth (SSSG) is the diagnostic that tells you whether the engine is running on real demand or just on more outlets. SSSG measures the change in sales at stores that have been open at least a year — so it strips out the flattering effect of simply adding locations. A company can grow total revenue by opening stores while its existing stores quietly shrink; SSSG catches exactly that. For a fixed-cost, lease-heavy business, it is the difference between operating leverage working for you and against you.

Here is the mechanism, and why it matters so much for Devyani. A store's rent, its core staffing and its equipment depreciation are largely fixed — they cost the same whether the store is busy or quiet. So when same-store sales rise, that extra revenue drops through to profit at a high rate (the fixed costs are already paid for): positive SSSG is a margin tailwind. When same-store sales fall, the same fixed costs eat a bigger share of a smaller top line, and store-level margins compress fast. That is why a swing of a few percentage points in SSSG matters more than it sounds — it moves profitability with leverage, in both directions.

Alongside SSSG, analysts track average daily sales (ADS) per store — literally how much a typical outlet takes in a day — and the store-level (restaurant) EBITDA margin, the operating profit of the outlets before head-office and lease-accounting effects. These are the numbers that reveal whether the units are good, independent of how many there are. Devyani's gross margin runs high (roughly the high-60s percent — normal for food service, where raw ingredients are a modest share of the menu price), so the real battleground is further down: staff, rent, energy, and the royalty and marketing spend that sit between gross profit and store EBITDA.

The trap in reading a blended same-store number

Devyani reports same-store growth per brand, and you should insist on that granularity. A single consolidated SSSG figure can look "fine" while masking a dangerous divergence — a strong KFC lifting the average just enough to hide a Pizza Hut that is sliding. The two brands are not one demand signal; they are two. KFC's positive turn is evidence that the chicken format is resonating with Indian eaters at current prices; Pizza Hut's negative print is evidence that the pizza format, at Devyani's price points and against Domino's, is not. Blend them and you learn nothing; separate them and you see the entire investment case in two numbers moving in opposite directions.

06

KFC carries Pizza Hut: the divergence inside one company

The single most important idea in the whole primer — and it is unique to this company.

Most restaurant companies you can size up with one demand number. Devyani needs two, and they point in opposite directions. Inside the same listed entity, under the same management, funded from the same balance sheet, one brand is having its best run in years while the other is in outright retreat. That split — not the consolidated result — is the real Devyani story, and reading the average instead of the two halves is the most common way to misjudge this stock.

Why the gap? KFC's fried-chicken format hits a sweet spot in India: a distinctive product with few direct chain competitors at its price, strong dine-in and delivery appeal, and a value architecture (buckets, combos, snack-price entry points) that keeps footfall resilient even when wallets tighten. Pizza Hut, by contrast, is fighting a losing positional battle: Domino's (run by Jubilant FoodWorks) dominates delivered pizza with unmatched scale and speed, while cloud kitchens and aggregators have commoditised the category. Pizza Hut is squeezed between Domino's on value-delivery and casual-dining on experience — an uncomfortable middle. So Devyani is doing the rational thing: pour capital into the winner, freeze the laggard, and try to fix Pizza Hut's proposition before spending another rupee expanding it.

The device that only fits Devyani: KFC's surplus versus Pizza Hut's deficit

Picture Devyani's profit-and-loss as a single ledger with two very different tenants paying into it. KFC is the tenant whose business is booming — best same-store growth in 14 quarters, fed with fresh stores, generating the store-level cash that keeps the whole enterprise moving. Pizza Hut is the tenant behind on rent — negative same-store sales, expansion switched off, absorbing management attention and marketing spend for no growth in return. The company-level result you see is the net of those two: a strong KFC quietly subsidising a struggling Pizza Hut while the group still slips to a small loss. The thesis question is therefore precise and non-generic — can KFC grow its surplus faster than Pizza Hut drains it, and can the Pizza Hut reset turn the second tenant back into a payer? Nearly every other Devyani debate is downstream of that one split.

This is what makes the "one company" framing misleading. A generalist glancing at "+13% revenue, small loss" sees a mediocre grower. The reality is a very good chicken business stapled to a struggling pizza business, and the stock will ultimately track whether that staple can be undone — KFC compounding, Pizza Hut either fixed or de-emphasised until it stops mattering. The pizza reset (menu, pricing, marketing, and eventually reopening the expansion tap) is the concrete milestone to watch; it is a live experiment with a checkable result each quarter.

07

Why a growing restaurant chain loses money — by design

The genuinely counter-intuitive part: healthy stores, red company.

Here is the fact that trips up almost every first-time reader of Devyani: the company can be profitable at the store level and loss-making at the company level at the same time — and that is not a scandal, it is the arithmetic of fast expansion under modern lease accounting. Understand this section and you will stop mis-reading the headline PAT.

Two forces push reported profit below what the stores actually earn. The first is expansion front-loading: a chain adding 200-plus outlets a year is always carrying a cohort of young stores that cost money before they mature, plus the pre-opening and launch expenses of the ones just opened. The second, and less intuitive, is an accounting standard called Ind-AS 116, which governs how leases hit the books.

What Ind-AS 116 does to the numbers

Under the old rules, a store's rent was simply an expense — you paid it, it reduced profit, done. Ind-AS 116 changed that. Now, when Devyani signs a multi-year lease, it must put the whole lease on its balance sheet: a "right-of-use" asset (the value of occupying the space) and a matching lease liability (the future rent owed). The single rent expense is then replaced by two charges — depreciation of the right-of-use asset, and interest on the lease liability. Crucially, that interest is front-loaded: it is higher in a lease's early years and tapers later, exactly like the interest on a home loan. So a company signing hundreds of fresh leases every year is perpetually loading its P&L with the high-interest early phase of all those leases — depressing reported profit even when the underlying stores are doing fine.

The single most important reading habit for this stock

Do not judge Devyani on its headline net profit or loss. In a rapid-expansion phase, that number is dominated by how many young stores it is carrying and how much fresh lease interest it is front-loading — both of which are consequences of growth, not signs of a broken business. The numbers that tell you whether the machine is actually working are further up and more granular: store-level (restaurant) EBITDA margin (are the outlets themselves profitable?), SSSG by brand (is real demand rising?), and net store additions of good stores (is the network compounding?). A healthy Devyani will show strong store economics and positive KFC same-store growth while still printing a slim company loss — and that combination is a feature of the expansion phase, not a contradiction.

The flip side — when the loss stops being "by design": the by-design defence only holds while the store-level economics are genuinely good and the maturing cohort is on track to turn profitable. If same-store sales stay weak (as at Pizza Hut) or new stores keep disappointing, then the losses are no longer a timing artefact of growth — they are evidence the units don't work. The honest task for an investor is to tell those two situations apart, because they look identical on the headline PAT line and completely different underneath it.
08

Costa Coffee and the international arm

The two smaller legs — one a growth option, one a source of diversification and risk.

Beyond the KFC/Pizza Hut core sit two smaller pieces that shape the edges of the story: Costa Coffee in India, and an international arm running Yum! brands in Nepal and Nigeria (with expansion into Thailand). Neither moves the needle the way KFC does, but both change the risk profile — and Costa in particular is where a new leg of growth could come from.

Costa Coffee — the café bet

Costa gives Devyani exposure to one of India's most talked-about consumer shifts: the rise of the organised café and premium-coffee habit. As disposable incomes climb and "third places" to sit, work and meet proliferate in Indian cities, branded coffee is a structurally growing category. With roughly 220-plus outlets, Costa is still sub-scale — but that is precisely the point of an option: it is small enough that rapid growth could matter, and Devyani has the capital and the real-estate relationships to expand it. The competition is stiff — Tata's Starbucks is the marquee rival, and a wave of home-grown chains (Third Wave, Blue Tokai and others) is crowding the space — so Costa's payoff depends on Devyani carving a distinct position rather than simply adding cups.

The international arm — diversification with a catch

Running KFC and Pizza Hut in Nepal and Nigeria spreads Devyani across markets that can grow when India is soft — useful diversification. But international QSR carries its own hazards: currency swings (a devaluing naira can wipe out reported earnings from Nigeria), local inflation, political and regulatory risk, and supply-chain complexity far from the Indian base. The international book is a genuine second front, but it is also a place where a good operating year can be undone by a macro shock nobody in the restaurant controls.

How to weigh the two smaller legs

Treat Costa and the international arm asymmetrically. Costa is best read as upside optionality — if India's café boom is real and Devyani executes, a sub-scale format can become a third meaningful brand; if not, it stays a rounding error and does little harm. The international arm is best read as a diversifier that adds volatility — it can quietly help in a good year and just as quietly sting in a currency crisis. Neither is the reason to own or avoid the stock; both are adjustments to a thesis that still lives or dies on KFC India and the Pizza Hut fix.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, capital-hungry QSR arena where brand and unit economics decide who wins.

India's branded quick-service market is a scrum of well-capitalised operators, most of them — like Devyani — franchisees of global brands rather than brand-owners. The competitive question is rarely "who has the better logo" (the logos are all famous); it is "who builds the best stores, in the best sites, with the best unit economics." Here is the field Devyani plays on.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two features define this arena. First, Sapphire Foods is the mirror you should always check Devyani against: because it runs the identical KFC and Pizza Hut brands in other regions, the gap between the two franchisees' same-store growth and store margins is a clean read on execution, stripped of brand differences. If Devyani's KFC out-comps Sapphire's, that is genuine operating skill; if it lags, the brand — not the operator — is doing the work. Second, the fiercest competition is category-specific: Devyani's KFC fights burgers and other chicken formats, its Pizza Hut fights Domino's, its Costa fights Starbucks — and behind all of them sits the delivery aggregators whose commissions set a floor under everyone's costs. There is no single "QSR rival"; there are three separate fights, and Devyani is winning one clearly and losing another.

10

Financials & valuation

Real store growth and a healthy KFC — priced as a compounder while still posting a loss.

FY26 was a year of steady top-line growth, resilient store-level economics, and a small company-level loss — the classic signature of a chain still in heavy expansion. The market values Devyani not on this year's (negative) earnings but on the network and the future profit pool it is building. That is why the stock carries a rich multiple on sales and on stores rather than a normal P/E — there often isn't a positive "E" to divide by.

A word on debt: Devyani's reported net debt looks meaningful (roughly ₹800 Cr, approx.), but a large chunk of what shows up as "debt" on a modern restaurant balance sheet is lease liabilities capitalised under Ind-AS 116 — the future rent on all those stores, not conventional borrowing. That is worth separating: the operating (non-lease) leverage is far lighter than the headline suggests, though the lease obligations are real cash commitments and do constrain flexibility if sales soften. Treat the number with that nuance rather than reading it as a heavily indebted balance sheet.

The one number that settles the thesis: the first clean year of company-level profit

At roughly ₹13,700 Cr of market value on a business that just posted a net loss, Devyani is priced as a store-count compounder that will eventually earn real money, not as a value stock. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is blunt: the point at which Devyani prints a full year of positive company-level PAT — and does so while still growing stores. That is the moment the "loss by design" story is vindicated, because it shows the maturing store base and operating leverage have finally out-run the expansion and lease drag. The leading indicators that tell you it is coming are specific: KFC same-store growth staying positive, net additions of stores that hit their unit economics, and the Pizza Hut same-store line clawing back toward zero. If those three trend the right way, the profit follows and the multiple is defensible. If KFC's SSSG rolls over or Pizza Hut keeps bleeding, the company keeps spending without the payoff, and a stock valued for a profitable future is left owning a loss-making present.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A strong chicken franchise in an under-penetrated market — asked to prove it can make money while it grows.

Devyani enters FY27 with a genuinely good asset (KFC), a genuine problem (Pizza Hut), a long structural runway (India's under-penetrated QSR market), and a valuation that already assumes the good outweighs the bad. The debate is not whether India will eat more branded fast food — it will — but whether Devyani converts that into per-share profit before its expansion-and-lease costs, and its pizza drag, eat the reward.

The bull case

  • Under-penetrated runway — India has far fewer organised QSR outlets per person than peer economies; years of store additions ahead as incomes and cities grow.
  • KFC is firing — best same-store growth in 14 quarters, strong unit economics, a value-led menu that holds up in a slowdown; the profit engine is healthy.
  • Store economics beat the headline — restaurant-level margins are well above the reported company margin; the loss is largely an expansion-and-lease artefact, not broken units.
  • Optionality in Costa — a sub-scale café brand riding India's coffee boom; small enough that success would genuinely add a third leg.
  • Promoter muscle — RJ Corp's scale, capital and real-estate reach (the same group behind Varun Beverages) support aggressive, funded expansion.

The bear case

  • When does it actually earn? — the company has yet to convert healthy stores into consistent company-level profit; the "loss by design" defence only holds if it ends.
  • Pizza Hut drag — negative same-store sales, expansion frozen, losing the pizza war to Domino's; a whole brand that isn't working.
  • Rich valuation — priced as a compounder on EV/sales and EV/store while still loss-making; any wobble in KFC's SSSG can re-rate it sharply.
  • Cyclical, discretionary demand — eating out is among the first things households cut; a consumption slowdown hits a fixed-cost, lease-heavy store base hard.
  • Rented brands & input costs — dependence on Yum!/Coca-Cola, royalty outflows, rent inflation, and volatile chicken, cheese and edible-oil costs all sit outside Devyani's control.
The bottom line

Devyani is two businesses wearing one ticker: a very good fried-chicken franchise compounding through store growth, and a struggling pizza franchise being nursed back to health — with the whole thing running a small loss while it builds. The bet is that the KFC engine and India's widening appetite for branded QSR eventually overwhelm the expansion costs, the lease drag and the Pizza Hut deficit, and the company crosses into durable profit. The two checkpoints that will tell you it is happening are concrete and datable: KFC same-store sales holding positive quarter after quarter, and the Pizza Hut reset dragging that brand's same-store line back toward zero so the expansion tap can reopen. Watch those two prints each quarter; between them, they will tell you whether the "loss today for profit tomorrow" trade is on schedule — or whether tomorrow keeps getting pushed out.
