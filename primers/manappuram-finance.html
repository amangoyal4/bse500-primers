Manappuram Finance, the gold-loan house Bain now co-owns

A complete walk through India's second-largest gold-loan NBFC — how lending money against a family's gold jewellery actually works, why a 75-year-old Kerala pawnbroking business now runs on ₹64,000 crore of assets, how a diversification into microfinance backfired, and what it means that private-equity giant Bain Capital has taken joint control.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Manappuram Finance is a non-bank lender (NBFC) that lends cash against gold jewellery — a customer walks into a branch with gold bangles or chains, Manappuram values them, hands over a short-term loan worth a portion of that gold, locks the jewellery in the branch vault, and charges interest until the customer repays and takes the gold back. It is India's second-largest gold-loan NBFC after Muthoot Finance, run from Valapad in Kerala's Thrissur district by the Nandakumar family, and its roots as a village pawnbroker go back to 1949.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • A gold franchise that ballooned in FY26. Consolidated assets under management (AUM) reached ~₹63,798 crore at March 2026, up ~48% in a year; the core gold-loan book jumped ~99% to ~₹50,953 crore — now ~80% of the whole company — carried by a once-in-a-generation surge in the gold price. It holds ~63 tonnes of customers' gold across ~4,000 branches.
  • The diversification cut both ways. To reduce its dependence on gold, Manappuram pushed into microfinance (Asirvad), vehicle finance, home loans and small-business lending. When India's microfinance sector blew up in 2024–25, Asirvad lost ~₹579 crore for the year and the RBI briefly barred it from lending — dragging consolidated FY26 profit down ~18% to ~₹993 crore even as the gold engine roared.
  • New owners in the chair. In the defining corporate event of its history, US private-equity firm Bain Capital took joint control in April 2026 — investing ~₹4,385 crore for an initial ~18% stake, launching a 26% open offer, and reconstituting the board. A family-run South-Indian lender has become a professionally-backed, PE-co-controlled platform.
In plain terms

Think of Manappuram as a giant, organised pawnshop with a stock-market listing. Its bread-and-butter is beautifully simple and very old: people who need cash today — a farmer before harvest, a shopkeeper short on working capital, a family with a medical bill — pledge the one asset almost every Indian household owns, gold, and walk out with money in minutes, no credit score required. Because the loan is fully backed by gold sitting in the vault, it is low-risk for the lender and priced at a high interest rate, which is why gold lending is so profitable. The complications in the Manappuram story are the two things it did around that simple core: it tried to grow beyond gold (and got burned in microfinance), and it just sold control to a global buyout firm.

01

Gold-loan economics 101: how lending against jewellery works

No finance degree required — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

A gold loan is one of the oldest and safest forms of lending there is: the borrower hands over physical gold as collateral, and the lender holds it until the loan is repaid. If the borrower vanishes, the lender still has the gold and can sell it to get its money back. That single feature — a fully-secured loan you can grant in minutes — is what makes the business tick. Five terms tell you almost the whole story.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and Manappuram's number)
AUM"Assets Under Management" — the total value of loans outstanding. Manappuram's consolidated AUM is ~₹63,800 Cr; the gold-loan slice alone is ~₹50,950 Cr.
LTV"Loan-to-Value" — how much you lend against the gold's worth. Lend ₹75 against ₹100 of gold and LTV is 75%. RBI caps it (see §08); Manappuram's average LTV runs ~57% — a deliberate cushion below the ceiling.
YieldThe average interest rate earned on the gold book. Gold NBFCs charge high rates — Manappuram's gold yield is guided toward ~17.5–18% — because the loans are small, short and convenient.
TonnageThe physical weight of gold held in vaults as collateral — ~63 tonnes. When gold prices rise, the same tonnage is worth more, so the loanable amount (and AUM) rises with it.
AuctionThe safety valve. If a borrower doesn't repay (or the gold's value slips below the loan), the lender auctions the pledged gold to recover principal and interest — the reason losses are rare.
Why gold lending is such a good business

Three things make it unusually attractive. (1) It's secured. Unlike a personal loan, there is real, liquid, easy-to-sell collateral locked in the vault from day one — so credit losses are tiny. (2) It's fast and needs no history. The lender assesses the gold, not the borrower — no salary slip, no credit score, no paperwork — so a first-time or informal borrower who a bank would reject gets money in minutes. (3) It's high-yield. Because the customer is paying for speed, convenience and access, gold NBFCs charge 18–24% — far above a bank's rate — while taking almost no credit risk. The catch: it's a short-tenure product (loans roll over every few months), so the entire book has to be constantly re-lent, and the value of the collateral rides on one thing the lender can't control — the price of gold.

Who actually takes a gold loan?

  • The cash-strapped and the unbanked — households with no formal credit access but with gold in the cupboard (India's households own an estimated 25,000+ tonnes of gold, the largest private stock on earth).
  • Small businesses & farmers — needing quick working capital for inventory, seeds or a lean month, against jewellery rather than property.
  • Emergency borrowers — a medical bill, a wedding, a school fee — who need money the same day and will redeem the gold within weeks.

Crucially, these customers want their gold back — it is often sentimental, family jewellery — so redemption rates are very high and outright default is the exception, not the rule.

02

The business model: the branch, the vault & the lifecycle of one loan

Follow a single gold bangle through the machine and the whole model clicks.

Manappuram's engine is a dense branch network — ~4,000 outlets, each a small, secured strong-room with an appraiser and a couple of staff. The physical presence is the moat: gold has to be handed over in person, valued by a trained eye, and stored somewhere the customer trusts. Here is the journey of one loan, start to finish.

The whole cycle can take as little as a few minutes at Step 3, and most customers reach Step 4a — they want their family gold back. Step 4b (auction) is the backstop that keeps credit losses minimal.

The three levers that grow the gold book

  • Tonnage — pledge more gold by winning more customers and bigger tickets. Manappuram has been deliberately pushing higher-ticket loans (above ₹5 lakh), lifting its average ticket size to ~₹1.28 lakh (FY26) from ~₹68,000 (FY25).
  • Gold price — even with flat tonnage, a rising gold price means each gram backs a bigger loan, so AUM swells on its own (this is most of the FY26 story — see §07).
  • Yield — the interest rate charged. Competition from banks and Muthoot has pressured yields down from the ~20%+ of a few years ago toward the ~17.5–18% guided range, a trade-off between growth and margin.
The "Online Gold Loan" twist — why it matters

Manappuram was an early mover in digital gold loans: once a customer has pledged gold at a branch, they can top-up, repay, or re-borrow against that same stored gold from their phone, without visiting again. A remarkable ~92% of its gold AUM is now transacted online. This does three things: it lowers cost-to-serve, it makes the customer stickier (their gold is already in the vault), and it turns a one-time branch visit into a recurring digital relationship — a genuine efficiency edge over a purely physical rival. The gold still lives in the branch; only the money moves online.

03

The company and who built it

From a one-branch Kerala pawnbroker to a listed, ₹30,000-crore lender.

Manappuram is one of Indian finance's great family stories. It began in 1949 in the coastal village of Valapad, Thrissur (Kerala), as a small pawnbroking and money-lending firm founded by V.C. Padmanabhan. His son, V.P. Nandakumar, took over the single branch in 1986 after his father's death, incorporated Manappuram Finance Ltd in 1992, and turned a traditional lender into a technology-driven, pan-India NBFC — the first gold-loan company in India to list on the stock exchange (1995).

  • 1986

  • 2024

  • Mar 2025

  • Apr 2026

Ownership & management

  • Founder-CEO: V.P. Nandakumar is Managing Director & CEO — the architect of the modern company, and (with family) a continuing promoter even after the Bain deal.
  • New co-controller: Bain Capital (via BC Asia Investments entities) took joint control in April 2026. Post-deal, Bain holds anywhere from ~18% up to ~41.7% depending on the open-offer take-up; the existing promoter family holds ~28.9% (fully-diluted basis). Combined promoter shareholding rose from ~31.8% to ~41.7% as the preferential shares were allotted.
  • Subsidiaries: Asirvad Micro Finance (microfinance — majority-owned), Manappuram Home Finance, and the vehicle/MSME businesses run under the parent — a spread of secured and unsecured lending around the gold core.
  • Balance sheet: well-capitalised, with a consolidated capital adequacy ratio (CRAR) of ~21.3% — comfortably above the regulatory minimum — and cost of borrowing easing to ~8.6% as rates and its own standing improved.
04

The Bain Capital deal: a family lender changes hands

The single most consequential event in Manappuram's history — read this to understand where the company is headed.

For 75 years Manappuram was a founder-controlled business. In 2025–26 that changed: US private-equity heavyweight Bain Capital agreed to buy joint control, injecting capital and management muscle and turning a family gold-lender into a professionally-backed platform. This is a genuine ownership transformation, not a passive stake — and it is the reason the stock re-rated.

Why a PE firm wants a gold lender — and what changes

Bain isn't buying Manappuram for the gold in the vaults; it's buying an underperforming franchise it thinks it can fix and re-rate. The logic: Manappuram has a strong, cash-generative gold business but has traded at a persistent discount to Muthoot, partly because of governance concerns, the microfinance mess, and a founder-dominated structure. Bain brings capital, professional management, and a mandate to clean up — tighten the non-gold businesses, sharpen the gold engine, improve disclosure and returns, and close the valuation gap with Muthoot. For shareholders it means professionalisation and fresh strategic direction; the risk is execution and the usual tensions of a two-headed (family + PE) control structure. It is, in short, the market's single biggest source of optionality in the Manappuram story.

05

The AUM mix: gold, microfinance & everything else

One company, five loan books — and a deliberate bet on not being only about gold.

Manappuram is best understood as a gold core with satellites. Over the last decade it deliberately built non-gold businesses — microfinance, vehicle finance, home loans, and SME/on-lending — to reduce its exposure to gold-price cycles and gold-specific regulation. In FY26 that strategy looked lopsided: a gold-price boom swelled the gold book to ~80% of AUM, while the troubled microfinance arm shrank. Here is the split.

The non-gold satellites, decoded

  • Asirvad Micro Finance — tiny, unsecured group loans to low-income women (India's "microfinance" model). The biggest non-gold bet, and the one that hurt (see §06). AUM ~₹4,636 Cr, down sharply.
  • Vehicle & two-wheeler finance — loans for scooters, cars and commercial vehicles. AUM ~₹2,991 Cr — but with a high GNPA of ~10.4%, a book still being cleaned up.
  • MSME & on-lending — small-business and secured/loan-against-property lending. AUM ~₹3,351 Cr (GNPA ~7.1%).
  • Manappuram Home Finance — affordable home loans; the cleanest non-gold book (GNPA ~2.6%), AUM ~₹1,852 Cr.
The diversification paradox

Manappuram diversified for a good reason — a business that is 80–90% gold is hostage to the gold price and to gold-specific RBI rules, so spreading into other lending should smooth the ride. But the execution exposed a hard truth: the non-gold books are riskier than the gold core. Gold loans are secured by metal in a vault (GNPA is low); microfinance, vehicle and SME loans are unsecured or thinly-secured, and when a credit cycle turns they go bad fast — which is exactly what happened in FY26. So the very diversification meant to de-risk the company became its main source of losses, while the "risky" gold business quietly carried the group. The strategic question Bain now owns: how much non-gold to keep, fix, or shed.

06

Asirvad and the microfinance blow-up

The story that turned a gold-boom year into a profit decline — told honestly.

The reason Manappuram's FY26 profit fell while its gold book nearly doubled sits in one subsidiary: Asirvad Micro Finance. Microfinance — tiny, unsecured, joint-liability loans to low-income borrowers — went through a nationwide stress cycle in 2024–25 (over-lending, borrower over-leverage, rising defaults). Asirvad was hit hard, and the regulator stepped in.

What went wrong

  • The RBI ban (Oct 2024). The Reserve Bank barred Asirvad (and three other NBFCs) from sanctioning and disbursing loans from 21 October 2024, citing concerns over usurious pricing and non-compliance with fair-practice norms. For a lender, being ordered to stop lending is about as serious as it gets.
  • The remediation. Asirvad revamped its pricing, systems and processes; the RBI lifted the restrictions in January 2025, allowing it to resume — but the interruption plus the broader MFI downturn had already done damage.
  • The financial hit. Asirvad's AUM shrank ~36% to ~₹4,636 Cr as it deliberately ran down and wrote off bad loans; it posted a full-year FY26 loss of ~₹579 Cr. Group provisions ballooned (~₹1,556 Cr consolidated), which is what pulled consolidated PAT down ~18% despite the gold surge.
  • The turn. The clean-up appears to be working: Asirvad's gross NPAs fell from ~8.5% (FY25) to ~4.8% (FY26), and it swung back to a small quarterly profit of ~₹13 Cr in Q4 FY26 from a ₹156 Cr loss the prior quarter — the drag easing, if not gone.
Why this matters beyond one year: the Asirvad episode is a governance and diversification warning. It showed that (1) Manappuram's unsecured books can inflict real losses and regulatory scrutiny that the gold core never would; and (2) the RBI is willing to name-and-act on pricing and conduct at a Manappuram subsidiary. Both feed directly into the "governance discount" the stock has carried — and into Bain's case for a professional clean-up.
The honest read

Don't gloss over this: a regulator ordering your subsidiary to stop lending is a serious black mark, and the ~₹579 Cr loss is real money. But two things temper it. First, it was largely an industry-wide MFI cycle, not solely a Manappuram failure — peers across microfinance took similar hits. Second, the gold core was never in question: it kept growing and generating cash right through the storm. The investment debate is whether Asirvad is a one-off wound now healing (GNPA falling, back to quarterly profit) or a structural reason to distrust management's push beyond gold. Bain's arrival is, in part, the market's answer: bring in new owners to make sure it's the former.

07

Gold price: the lever that giveth (and can taketh)

Why a ~99% jump in the gold book is mostly about the price of a metal, not the number of customers.

To understand FY26 you have to understand that a gold lender's AUM is tonnage × gold price × LTV. Manappuram's gold tonnage rose only modestly (to ~63 tonnes); the reason the gold book nearly doubled is that gold prices surged to record highs through 2025–26. The same jewellery in the vault suddenly backed far larger loans. Gold price is the most powerful — and least controllable — variable in the whole model.

The two-way blade

When gold rises (as in FY26): existing collateral is worth more, so customers can borrow more against the same jewellery; new AUM flows in; the cushion between the loan and the gold's value fattens; auctions are rarely needed. It is a pure tailwind — the gold financier's best friend. When gold falls sharply, the machine runs in reverse: the collateral backing existing loans loses value, some loans breach their LTV limits, borrowers (whose gold is now worth less than what they owe) may walk away, and the lender is forced to auction gold into a falling market — crystallising losses and denting asset quality. Manappuram manages this by keeping average LTV conservative (~57%, well below the 75% cap) so there's a big buffer before any loan goes underwater. But make no mistake: a large, sustained gold-price crash is the classic tail-risk for every gold NBFC, and the flip side of FY26's windfall.

08

Under the RBI's eye: the regulatory grip on gold lending

Gold NBFCs live and die by rules they don't write — here's the framework that matters.

Because gold loans touch millions of small, financially-vulnerable borrowers, the RBI watches the sector closely. Regulation is a permanent, defining force for Manappuram — it sets the LTV ceiling, polices conduct and pricing, and can (as Asirvad learned) halt a business overnight. In 2025 the RBI overhauled the entire rulebook.

The RBI gold-loan directions, 2025

  • One unified rulebook. The RBI (Lending Against Gold and Silver Collateral) Directions, 2025 — notified June 2025, effective April 2026 — consolidated 30+ scattered circulars from three decades into a single harmonised framework for banks and NBFCs alike.
  • Tiered LTV — and a relief. The market feared a flat 75% cap; instead the RBI set a tiered ceiling that was friendlier for small loans: up to 85% LTV for loans ≤₹2.5 lakh, 80% for ₹2.5–5 lakh, and 75% above ₹5 lakh. Gold-NBFC shares, which had sold off on the draft, rallied on the final version.
  • Tighter plumbing. Standardised gold valuation, a ~12-month cap on bullet-repayment loans, mandatory return of pledged gold within 7 days of repayment (with penalties for delay), a ~1 kg per-borrower pledging limit, and stricter, more transparent auction rules.
  • Cash-disbursement limits. RBI has separately tightened how much of a gold loan can be paid out in cash (versus bank transfer) — pushing the sector toward the digital disbursement Manappuram already leans on.
The double-edged reality: tighter rules raise compliance cost and can slow growth in the near term — but they also professionalise the sector, squeeze unorganised and aggressive lenders, and ultimately favour large, compliant players like Manappuram and Muthoot. The tail-risk is always a surprise tightening (a lower LTV, a cash-payout clampdown, or a conduct action) that hits growth or margins overnight — which is why gold NBFCs trade with a permanent regulatory-overhang discount.
Why Manappuram is relatively well-placed

Three reasons the 2025 regime is more friend than foe for Manappuram: it keeps its average LTV conservative (~57%), so higher permitted LTVs are headroom, not a constraint; ~92% of its gold lending is already digital, so cash-disbursement limits barely bite; and as a large, listed, now-Bain-backed player it has the compliance infrastructure that smaller lenders lack, so a stricter regime should shift share toward it. The Asirvad ban is the cautionary counter-example — proof that when Manappuram strays from its disciplined gold core into conduct-sensitive lending, the same regulator can bite hard.

09

The industry and the rivals

A big, growing, organising market — with one clear leader ahead of Manappuram.

India's organised gold-loan market has surged past ₹3.5 lakh crore, powered by rising gold prices, formalisation away from local pawnbrokers, and banks piling in. It is a duopoly at the top — Muthoot Finance the clear leader, Manappuram the strong number two — flanked by banks, IIFL and diversified NBFCs. Understanding the competitive set explains Manappuram's persistent valuation discount.

The Muthoot comparison — the frame for everything

Muthoot is the yardstick, and it wins on almost every operating metric: roughly three times Manappuram's gold AUM, a purer gold focus (~90% vs ~80%), higher returns, and a cleaner record — so it earns a premium valuation. Manappuram has historically traded at a discount for the mirror-image reasons: a smaller, lower-return gold book, the distracting and loss-making non-gold ventures (Asirvad above all), and lingering governance concerns. The whole bull case for Manappuram is that this gap can close — that Bain's clean-up, a re-focused gold engine, and a healed microfinance arm let a cheap stock re-rate toward its bigger rival. The bear case is that the gap is deserved and persistent. Watching Manappuram is, in large part, watching whether it can become "more like Muthoot."

Banks are the slow structural threat: banks fund themselves far cheaper than NBFCs, so as they lean into gold loans they can undercut on rate. NBFCs like Manappuram defend with speed, reach, convenience and a customer who values minutes over basis points — but the long-run pressure on yields is real, and part of why gold-NBFC yields have drifted down from ~24% a decade ago toward ~18% today.

How to think about a ~1.9× book, ~20× earnings gold lender

For a lender, Price-to-Book (P/B) is usually more telling than P/E, because a bank/NBFC's earnings swing with provisions (as Asirvad just proved). At ~1.9× book and ~20× earnings, Manappuram is demonstrably cheaper than Muthoot — and that is the whole debate in one number. Bulls say: a conservatively-run, digital-led, well-capitalised gold franchise, with a healing microfinance arm and a professional new owner (Bain) to close the gap, is cheap optionality. Bears say: the discount is earned — lower returns than Muthoot, a lossmaking non-gold portfolio, governance history, and an entire book whose value rides on a gold price now at record highs. Both are looking at the same ~1.9× multiple and drawing opposite conclusions; the resolution will come from whether the RoA/RoE recover and whether Bain delivers the clean-up.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, high-yield gold franchise with fresh owners — and a genuine list of things that could go wrong.

Manappuram enters its next chapter as a re-shaped company: a booming gold core, a shrinking-but-healing set of non-gold books, a conservative balance sheet, and — for the first time — a global PE firm in joint control. The investment case is a tug-of-war between a cheap, cash-generative franchise with a clear re-rating catalyst and a pile of structural sensitivities that have long kept the discount in place.

The bull case

  • Cheap vs the leader — ~1.9× book and ~20× earnings, a clear discount to Muthoot that Bain's clean-up could narrow.
  • Bain optionality — capital, professional management, better governance and disclosure; a credible catalyst to re-rate.
  • Gold tailwind — record gold prices lift AUM, income and collateral cushions; the core is highly profitable and cash-rich.
  • Digital & conservative — ~92% online gold loans and ~57% average LTV mean efficiency and a big safety buffer.
  • MFI healing — Asirvad's GNPA falling (8.5%→4.8%) and back to quarterly profit; the worst of the drag may be passing.

The bear case

  • Gold-price dependence — a sustained gold crash would reverse the AUM boom and force loss-making auctions.
  • Non-gold drag — Asirvad lost ~₹579 Cr in FY26; vehicle/SME GNPAs still high (~10%/~7%); execution unproven.
  • Regulatory overhang — LTV caps, cash-payout limits and conduct actions (the Asirvad ban) can hit growth or margins overnight.
  • Yield pressure — banks entering gold loans keep squeezing NBFC yields down over time.
  • Governance & control risk — a two-headed family-plus-PE structure is untested; the discount to Muthoot has been persistent for a reason.
The bottom line

Manappuram is a solid, cash-generative gold-lending franchise that has spent years trading at a discount to its bigger rival — held back by a loss-making diversification and governance doubts. The FY26 numbers capture the paradox perfectly: the gold engine had a boom year, yet consolidated profit fell because of Asirvad. What's genuinely new is the Bain Capital ownership — the first serious external force with both the incentive and the muscle to fix the non-gold mess, sharpen the gold core, and close the valuation gap. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers: the gold price (does the tailwind hold?), Asirvad's asset quality (does the drag keep healing?), and RoE / the P/B multiple (does Bain deliver the re-rating?). Get those right and a cheap stock compounds; get them wrong and the discount was deserved all along.

The gold in the vault was never the problem. The next chapter is about everything Manappuram built around it — and whether new owners can make the sum worth as much as the core.
