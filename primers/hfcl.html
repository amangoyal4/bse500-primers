HFCL Limited, the fibre-and-defence infrastructure bet

A full walk through India's leading optical-fibre-cable maker turned digital-infrastructure and defence-electronics company — how it earns from fibre, telecom gear, turnkey networks and military electronics, why its profits swing so hard with the fibre cycle and its lumpy project book, and why a business earning a ~7% return on equity trades at ~100× earnings on a "digital-infra + defence" story.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

HFCL Limited — once called Himachal Futuristic Communications — is an Indian company that builds the physical plumbing of digital networks. It manufactures optical fibre and optical-fibre cable (OFC), makes its own telecom equipment (routers, switches, Wi-Fi, 5G radios), builds and integrates whole networks as a turnkey contractor (BharatNet, telecom rollouts, railways, smart cities), and increasingly designs defence electronics (electronic fuzes, surveillance radars, thermal sights). It is the promoter vehicle of Mahendra Nahata, and it has been one of the Indian market's most volatile, boom-and-bust stocks for three decades.

Three facts frame everything:

  • FY26 was a sharp recovery on a wildly cyclical base. Revenue rose ~22% to ~₹4,949 Cr, EBITDA margin expanded to ~16.7%, and profit (PAT) nearly doubled to ~₹329 Cr — but that came straight after FY25, when revenue fell ~9% and profit halved to ₹173 Cr. Over five years revenue has essentially gone sideways (~2% CAGR). This is not a smooth compounder; it is a cyclical, project-heavy business.
  • The story has shifted from commodity cable to products, exports and defence. "Product" revenue (fibre cable + connectivity + telecom gear + defence) is now ~62% of sales, up from ~42% in FY24; exports jumped to ~41% of revenue (₹2,047 Cr) from 12% a year earlier; and the order book hit an all-time-high ~₹21,200 Cr (about USD 2.36 bn), of which ~58% is export orders — anchored by a single landmark ~₹10,159 Cr (~USD 1.1 bn) multi-year cable export contract.
  • The valuation is a narrative, not the numbers. At ~₹215 a share and ~₹33,000 Cr market cap, HFCL trades near ~100× trailing earnings on a business that earned a ~7% return on equity and ~11% return on capital in FY26. The share is up roughly 140% in a year. The market is paying for the future — India's fibre build-out, 5G, data centres and defence indigenisation — not for what the P&L looks like today.
In plain terms

Think of HFCL as a "picks-and-shovels" supplier to India's digital build-out, wrapped around a promoter family with a colourful market history. When the government wires up villages (BharatNet), when telcos fiberise for 5G, when a data centre needs connecting, or when the army wants an Indian-made artillery fuze instead of an imported one — HFCL wants to sell the cable, the box, the whole network, or the electronics. The upside is that all of those things are genuinely happening. The catch is that it's a cyclical, competitive, working-capital-hungry, execution-dependent business whose biggest customers are the government and a handful of telcos — and the stock has priced in years of the story going right.

01

What HFCL actually does

Four businesses under one roof — and they are not equally good.

HFCL is not one company; it is four different businesses with very different economics, stitched together by a common theme: the equipment and infrastructure of communication networks. Understanding the primer means understanding that these four have wildly different margins, capital needs and competitive dynamics.

The four businesses at a glance
BusinessWhat it isEconomics
Optical fibre & OFCMaking optical fibre and cabling it — the glass strands and the armoured cables that carry internet traffic. Backward-integrated (fibre + cable).Manufacturing; capital-intensive; margins swing hard with the global fibre price cycle. HFCL's historical core.

The one distinction to hold onto: product vs project

The single most important mental split is "product" versus "project." A product business (cable, telecom gear, defence electronics) makes a thing in a factory and sells it — you get gross margin, you can build a brand, and if it's differentiated you keep the pricing power. A project (EPC) business wins a tender to build a network, then spends its own cash on materials and labour for months or years before the customer pays — you get revenue and scale, but thin margins and a balance sheet clogged with receivables and inventory. HFCL's whole strategic pivot is away from low-margin projects and commodity cable, toward higher-margin owned products, exports and defence. FY26's margin jump — and the rising "product mix" — is the first real evidence that pivot is working.

02

Fibre economics 101: from glass to gigabits

No engineering degree required — just how a fibre business makes (and loses) money.

Optical fibre is a hair-thin strand of ultra-pure glass that carries data as pulses of light. It is the backbone of the entire internet: every mobile tower, every home broadband line, every data centre ultimately connects back through fibre. HFCL's oldest business is making that fibre and turning it into usable cable — and the economics have a specific shape worth understanding.

The further up the chain (toward preform), the scarcer the capability and the fatter the margin. HFCL is investing (~USD 64 mn) to build its own preform capacity — the one stage it doesn't fully control — to capture more of the value and de-risk supply.

Why the fibre business is so cyclical

  • It's a semi-commodity. Below the preform stage, cable is increasingly a scale-and-cost game. When everyone builds capacity at once, prices crater; when demand outruns supply, prices spike. HFCL is a price-taker on the commodity end.
  • Demand is capex-driven and lumpy. Fibre buyers are governments (BharatNet) and telcos (Jio, Airtel, Vodafone Idea, BSNL). When a big rollout pauses — as Indian telco capex did after the 5G spectrum splurge — fibre demand falls off a cliff, exactly what hurt FY25.
  • China sets the world price. China is the swing producer of fibre. A glut there (2024–early 2025) drags global prices down; a shortage there (late 2025 into 2026, driven by AI data centres) sends them sharply up. HFCL's margins ride that wave — see Section 07.
Why "backward integration" is the whole game

HFCL makes both the fibre and the cable — it doesn't just buy fibre and sheath it. That matters because in a downturn, a pure cabler gets squeezed from both sides (fibre cost up, cable price down), while an integrated player captures the fibre margin too. Building its own preform plant is the next step up: preform is the scarcest, highest-margin part of the chain and is largely imported. Owning it would let HFCL make money on the full "glass rod → gigabit" journey and insulate it from import shocks — the difference between being a commodity cabler and a genuine fibre technology company. It's a multi-year, capital-hungry bet, but it's the right direction.

03

The company and who controls it

A 1987 telecom pioneer, a legendary crash, and a promoter with a Jio-sized side story.

HFCL was founded in 1987 by Mahendra Nahata, an early entrepreneur in India's newly opening telecom sector. Its history is inseparable from one of the most famous episodes in Indian stock-market lore — and understanding that history explains both the stock's volatility and the market's lingering wariness about the promoter group.

Timeline

  • 1987
    Mahendra Nahata founds Himachal Futuristic Communications Ltd (HFCL) — an early private player in Indian telecom equipment and infrastructure.
  • 1990s
    Rides the first Indian telecom boom; bids aggressively for basic-telephony licences and becomes a market darling.
  • 1999–2001
    Becomes a flagship "K-10" stock in the Ketan Parekh scam: the price is ramped from ~₹40 toward ~₹2,300, then collapses — famously crashing ~₹2,300 to ~₹90 as the manipulation unwinds. Retail investors are devastated; SEBI later settles the rigging case (consent order, 2010).
  • 2000s
    Years of debt, restructuring and survival follow the crash; the company slowly rebuilds around optical-fibre manufacturing and telecom infrastructure contracting.
  • 2010s
    Re-emerges as India's #1 OFC supplier; wins large BharatNet and defence-comms EPC work; begins an in-house R&D-led push into telecom products.
  • FY24–FY25
    Telecom capex slump + global fibre glut hit hard: FY25 revenue falls ~9% to ₹4,065 Cr and PAT halves to ₹173 Cr. The company leans into products, exports and defence to de-risk.
  • FY26
    Sharp recovery: revenue ~₹4,949 Cr, PAT ~₹329 Cr, EBITDA margin ~16.7%; a landmark ~USD 1.1 bn cable export order and a record ~₹21,200 Cr book; product mix ~62%, exports ~41%. The "digital-infra + defence" narrative re-rates the stock ~140% in a year.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter: Mahendra Nahata is Managing Director and the driving force; the promoter group holds ~28% of the company (Mar 2026). That is a relatively low promoter stake for an Indian family business — a structural point bears watch.
  • Pledge history — much improved. HFCL's promoters historically pledged a large chunk of their shares (at points around half of the promoter holding), a classic red flag. That has fallen dramatically — to roughly ~1% pledged by late 2025. The de-pledging is a genuine positive, but the long pledge history is part of why the market applies a "promoter discount" to the group's credibility.
  • The Jio side-story. Nahata has long been associated with the Ambani circle and was an early figure around Reliance Jio; reporting in 2026 highlighted that a small HFCL/Nahata-linked early bet on Jio (~₹48 Cr) could be worth thousands of crore in a Jio listing — an off-balance-sheet sweetener occasionally cited by bulls, though it is not HFCL's core business.
  • Institutions: FIIs ~7% and domestic institutions ~9% (Mar 2026); the rest is a large retail public float — which is part of why the stock trades on momentum and narrative as much as fundamentals.
The promoter question, stated plainly: HFCL carries a genuine "governance memory." The Ketan Parekh association, the historical pledging, and a modest ~28% promoter stake mean the market has, for decades, demanded a discount for trust — and the stock has repeatedly rewarded and then punished believers. The de-pledging and the cleaner product/defence strategy are real improvements; whether they permanently change the market's view is one of the open questions in the story.

The four engines, in detail

Where the money comes from, and where it's trying to go.

HFCL reports a rising "product" mix and a shrinking reliance on pure EPC. Here is what each engine actually is, how it earns, and why management is steering the mix the way it is. The order book split (below) is the clearest picture of where the business is heading.

Engine 1 — Optical fibre & OFC (the historical core)

Making fibre and cable is HFCL's foundation and it remains the #1 domestic OFC supplier. The FY26 twist is that the growth is now export-led: the giant ~USD 1.1 bn cable order and a series of smaller export wins have turned a domestically-focused, cyclical business into a global supplier at a moment when world fibre prices are rising. It's still the most cyclical engine — but it's the one that landed the transformational order.

Engine 2 — Telecom products (the value-chain climb)

This is HFCL's most strategically important bet: designing and selling its own telecom gear rather than reselling others'. The portfolio spans unlicensed-band backhaul radios, Wi-Fi access points, routers, managed switches, network-management software, and a growing 5G transport/optical range — including India's first indigenously developed 5G Fixed Wireless Access (FWA) CPE, now in large-scale telco deployment. Owned products carry better margins and can be exported; the challenge is competing on R&D against global vendors with far bigger budgets.

Engine 3 — EPC & system integration (the volume, the working capital)

HFCL builds networks end-to-end: laying fibre for BharatNet, deploying telecom networks for freight corridors and metros, wiring railway stations with surveillance and communications, and building defence and smart-city networks. It's a real capability and a big revenue source — but it's the low-margin, working-capital-heavy engine. Management has been deliberately selective on new EPC tenders ("revenue quality over volume"), which is why the EPC book is being managed down as a share of the total.

Engine 4 — Defence electronics (the optionality)

The newest and most exciting engine — covered in full in Section 06. In short: HFCL has used its R&D base to design indigenous military electronics — electronic fuzes for artillery, ground surveillance radars, thermal weapon sights, high-capacity radio relays — riding India's "Aatmanirbhar" (self-reliant) defence-indigenisation drive. Small today, but high-margin, sticky, and exportable, with a new Hosur manufacturing facility and first meaningful revenues arriving from FY26.

05

The digital-infrastructure capex story

Why bulls own HFCL — India is under-fibered, and everyone is building at once.

The core bull thesis on HFCL is simple: India is in a multi-decade digital build-out, and HFCL sells into almost every part of it. Whatever the exact vehicle — government broadband, 5G, home fibre, data centres, railways, defence — the common ingredient is network infrastructure, and HFCL makes the cable, the boxes and (often) does the building. India remains badly "under-fibered" compared with China, Korea or the US — a smaller share of its mobile towers and homes are fibre-connected — so there is a long runway of catch-up spending.

Why this is a real tailwind — and why it isn't a smooth one

Every driver above is genuine and long-lived; the under-fiberisation gap alone implies years of catch-up. But notice the common thread: HFCL's customers are governments and a handful of large telcos. That means demand arrives in waves tied to policy and capex cycles, not a steady stream. When telcos paused after the 5G spectrum auctions, or when a BharatNet phase got delayed in tendering, HFCL's revenue dropped — that's exactly what produced the FY25 slump. So the tailwind is real, but it blows in gusts. The order book smooths some of this, but the timing of large government and telco spend is outside HFCL's control — a permanent feature of the business.

The export pivot changes the picture. The most important development in FY26 is that HFCL stopped being purely a bet on Indian capex. Exports leapt to ~41% of revenue, and the giant ~USD 1.1 bn cable order is a foreign contract. Selling globally — into a world where AI is tightening fibre supply — diversifies HFCL away from the stop-start rhythm of Indian government and telco spending. If it sticks, it's a structural improvement to the business, not just a good year.
06

Defence electronics: the high-margin option

Small today, but the piece of HFCL that could justify a premium.

HFCL has quietly turned its telecom R&D muscle toward defence electronics — and this is the segment the market is most excited about, because it is the opposite of commodity cable: high-margin, technology-gated, sticky, and exportable. India's "Aatmanirbhar Bharat" drive to indigenise defence procurement is a direct, policy-backed tailwind.

Why defence could re-rate the whole company

Cable is worth a low single-digit P/E in a downturn; a proven defence-electronics franchise can be worth 30–50× because the margins are fat, the customers are captive, the products are consumable or upgrade-cyclical, and export markets are large. If HFCL converts its R&D into a durable stream of fuze, radar and sight orders — Indian and export — defence could become a disproportionate share of profit and the intellectual justification for the stock's premium multiple. That is the bull's real prize. The caveat: defence is slow, lumpy and trials-gated — orders take years to convert, and "developed a product" is not the same as "shipping at scale profitably." Today it's optionality, not yet an earnings engine.

07

The OFC cycle, China, and the price whipsaw

The single biggest swing factor in HFCL's fibre margins — and it just flipped.

If Section 06 is the bull's dream, this is the section that explains HFCL's volatility. The fibre business lives or dies by the global price of optical fibre, and that price is set largely in China, the world's swing producer. In the space of about eighteen months, the market has whipsawed from a brutal glut to a sharp shortage — and HFCL's fortunes have ridden the whole arc.

Left bar: the glut that crushed FY25 margins (China Mobile's big tender cleared ~35% cheaper than two years earlier). Right bars: the reversal — Chinese bare-fibre prices jumped ~80% into early 2026 as AI data-centre demand tightened supply, pushing prices above Europe and India for the first time in years.

How the cycle flows through HFCL

  • The glut (2024–early 2025): Chinese overcapacity + a pause in Indian telco capex collapsed fibre prices and volumes. HFCL's FY25 EBITDA fell ~26% and PAT halved — a textbook cyclical trough.
  • The turn (late 2025 → 2026): the AI data-centre boom sent global fibre demand up ~76% in 2025 and Chinese prices spiking ~80% into early 2026. Supply tightened (even preform availability got scarce). Fibre stocks re-rated hard — peer Sterlite Technologies rose several-fold — and HFCL rode the same wave to its FY26 recovery.
  • Why it's a double-edged sword: rising prices lift HFCL's fibre margins and its share price on the way up — but the same mechanism means the next glut (China always eventually over-builds) would compress them again. This is a price-cycle business, and the current up-leg is doing a lot of the work in the FY26 numbers and the valuation.
Read the headwind honestly: the brief asked us to weigh "the OFC price/China glut." The truthful picture in mid-2026 is nuanced — the glut that caused FY25's pain has, for now, reversed into an AI-fuelled shortage that is helping FY26. That's great news for the near term, but it's a warning about the medium term: HFCL's margins are structurally hostage to a Chinese-led commodity cycle it cannot control. Some of today's rosy numbers are cyclical, not structural — separating the two is the analyst's real job here.
08

Order book & execution

A record book is only as good as the cash it eventually turns into.

HFCL's headline strength is its ~₹21,200 Cr order book (about USD 2.36 bn) — an all-time high, more than double a year earlier, and ~4× annual revenue. That's real forward visibility. But an order book is a promise, not a profit, and HFCL's history is a reminder that execution and working capital are where telecom-infra stories are made or broken.

Exports quadrupled in a year. Of the ~₹21,200 Cr order book, roughly ~58% (~₹12,250 Cr) is export orders — a genuine shift from a domestically-dependent business to a global supplier.

The working-capital reality

  • EPC ties up cash. Turnkey projects mean buying materials and paying labour long before the customer (often a slow-paying government body) settles the bill. That inflates receivables and inventory and can swallow the cash that the P&L says the company "earned."
  • Debt has crept up. Gross borrowings rose to ~₹1,744 Cr (FY26) from ~₹1,522 Cr — modest against a ~₹33,000 Cr market cap, but a reminder that growth here is funded partly by working-capital debt, not just internal cash.
  • Customer concentration. The biggest orders come from the government (BharatNet), a few telcos, and now one very large export customer. Losing or delaying any one is material — the flip side of a big, chunky order book.
  • Execution risk on the giant order. The ~USD 1.1 bn cable export contract is transformational if delivered on schedule and at the assumed margin — but a multi-year order of that size carries real ramp, capacity and forex execution risk.
What to actually watch each quarter

Two numbers tell you whether the story is converting: the order-book-to-revenue conversion (is the ₹21,200 Cr book turning into sales on schedule, or slipping?) and working capital / operating cash flow (is reported profit becoming real cash, or getting stuck in receivables?). A record order book with slow conversion and ballooning receivables is the classic trap in Indian infra names. HFCL's deliberate shift toward higher-margin products, exports and "quality over volume" EPC is precisely an attempt to earn better rupees, not just more of them — the FY26 margin expansion is the first sign it may be working.

How to think about a ~100× multiple on a 7%-ROE business

On the face of it, the valuation is hard to defend: a company earning a ~7% return on equity and ~11% on capital, in a cyclical, competitive, working-capital-hungry industry, should not trade near 100× earnings. What the market is buying is a forward story — that the ₹21,200 Cr book, the export pivot, the product-mix shift and the defence optionality drive several years of much faster, much higher-quality earnings growth, so today's 100× becomes tomorrow's 25×. Bulls say you're paying up for an inflection: a commodity cabler becoming a fibre-technology-plus-defence company at the exact moment global fibre tightens. Bears say you're paying a compounder multiple for a cyclical whose FY26 profit is partly a China-price artefact — and that a stock up 140% in a year, on a group with this governance history, has priced in perfection. Both are looking at the same numbers; the disagreement is entirely about how much of the future to believe.

10

The competitive set

HFCL competes on several fronts at once — and rarely as the biggest player on any one.

Because HFCL straddles four businesses, it faces a different set of rivals in each — from focused fibre majors to networking-equipment specialists to diversified cable giants. It is India's #1 OFC supplier, but in most of its arenas it is a strong contender rather than a runaway leader.

The competitive read

HFCL's breadth is both its edge and its problem. The edge: few Indian companies can offer fibre, cable, telecom products, turnkey build and defence electronics under one roof — that's a genuine one-stop appeal for a government or telco customer, and it lets HFCL cross-sell. The problem: breadth means it's rarely the sharpest specialist. STL out-invests it in fibre technology; Tata-backed Tejas has deeper equipment R&D; Polycab and Finolex have stronger balance sheets in cables. HFCL's answer is the pivot itself — climb into owned products and defence where competition is thinner and margins richer, rather than slugging it out at the commodity end. Whether it can out-innovate better-funded specialists on multiple fronts at once is the strategic question underneath the stock.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely improving business — wrapped in a genuinely demanding valuation.

HFCL in 2026 is a better company than it has been in years: a record book, a real export breakthrough, a rising product mix, cleaner promoter pledging, and a defence option that could re-rate everything. It is also a cyclical, competitive, working-capital-heavy business with a colourful governance history, trading at a multiple that assumes the good news keeps compounding. The investment debate is almost entirely about whether the pivot is structural or the numbers are cyclical — and about price.

The bull case

  • Structural digital build-out — India is under-fibered; BharatNet, 5G, FTTH and data centres imply years of catch-up fibre demand.
  • Export breakthrough — exports at ~41% of revenue and a ~USD 1.1 bn cable order diversify HFCL away from stop-start Indian capex.
  • Mix shift to products & margins — ~62% product revenue and a ~470bps EBITDA-margin jump show the value-chain climb is real.
  • Defence optionality — high-margin, indigenous fuzes, radars and sights riding Aatmanirbhar; a potential re-rating engine.
  • Cleaner promoter optics — pledge cut to ~1%; a big record order book gives multi-year visibility.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — ~100× earnings on a ~7% ROE, cyclical business; up ~140% in a year and priced for perfection.
  • Cyclicality — margins hostage to a China-led fibre price cycle; part of FY26's strength is a cyclical up-leg, not structural.
  • Working capital & concentration — EPC ties up cash; reliance on government/telco and one huge export order; rising debt.
  • Execution — delivering a ~USD 1.1 bn multi-year order and scaling defence profitably are both unproven at this size.
  • Governance memory — Ketan Parekh legacy, historical pledging, modest ~28% promoter stake keep a trust discount alive.
The bottom line

HFCL is a real, improving, well-positioned player in a structurally growing industry — the digital-infrastructure and defence-indigenisation themes are among the most durable in India, and HFCL sells into nearly all of them. The business risk has genuinely fallen as the mix shifts toward products, exports and defence. But the valuation risk is severe: at ~100× earnings the stock is discounting years of flawless execution from a group whose earnings have historically been cyclical and whose stock has repeatedly whipsawed believers. The story to watch is written in a few numbers each quarter — order-book conversion, working capital / cash flow, product-and-defence margin, and the fibre price cycle. Get those right and the pivot justifies a premium; a cyclical downturn or an execution stumble, and a 100× multiple has a very long way to fall.

The next chapter is less about winning orders and more about converting them — into cash, into margin, and into a business that no longer moves with the price of Chinese glass.
