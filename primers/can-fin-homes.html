00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Can Fin Homes is a housing finance company (HFC) — a specialised lender that does one thing: it gives people money to buy or build a home and gets repaid, with interest, over 10–20 years. It was founded in 1987 in Bengaluru and holds a distinction no rival can copy: it was the first HFC in India promoted by a nationalised bank — Canara Bank, which still owns just under a third of it. Its borrowers are, overwhelmingly, salaried, prime customers: people with documented monthly incomes and clean credit, buying their first or second home. That single fact shapes everything about the business.

Three facts frame the whole company:

  • It lends cheap and safe, not dear and risky. Because its borrowers are creditworthy salaried buyers, Can Fin charges relatively low interest — a yield around 10% (approx.), versus the 13–17% that "affordable" and informal-income lenders charge. Lower rate means lower risk: its gross bad-loan ratio is just 0.85%, among the very lowest in the entire HFC/NBFC universe. It competes on safety and price, not on yield.
  • Its edge is the cost side, not the rate side. A low yield would crush most lenders. Can Fin survives — and earns a solid ~2.4% return on assets and ~18% return on equity — because it borrows unusually cheaply (Canara-bank pedigree plus top credit ratings) and runs an exceptionally lean shop (cost-to-income just ~18.8%, one of the sector's best). Its profit comes from what it doesn't spend.
  • It grows quietly and just crossed a milestone. FY26 loan book reached ~₹42,209 Cr; disbursements hit a record ~₹10,531 Cr; and net profit crossed ₹1,000 Cr for the first time (~₹1,086 Cr, +27%). Management guides to ~14% loan growth in FY27. Yet the stock trades at a modest ~10–11× earnings and ~2× book — cheaper than the glamorous affordable-HFCs it is often lumped with.
In plain terms

Most exciting lenders make money by charging high interest to riskier borrowers and hoping the extra yield outruns the extra defaults. Can Fin does the opposite. It lends to the safest customers in housing — salaried people with payslips and clean records — at ordinary bank-like rates, and accepts a thin margin on each loan. It then wrings a good return out of that thin margin by two unglamorous disciplines: getting its own money cheaply, because a Canara-Bank-promoted HFC with strong ratings can borrow at low cost, and keeping its running costs tiny. The result is a lender that looks dull on the surface but delivers steady, high-quality returns with almost no bad loans. The entire investment question is whether that thin margin can hold as banks and giant rivals fight over the exact same prime borrowers.

01

Housing finance 101: how a home lender actually earns

No finance degree needed — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

A housing finance company is, at heart, a margin business built on other people's money. It doesn't have its own cash to lend; it borrows money (from banks, bond markets and refinancing bodies) at one interest rate, and lends that money out as home loans at a higher rate. The gap between the two is where the entire profit lives. To follow Can Fin, you need five terms — and once they click, the whole company clicks.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
YieldThe average interest rate a lender charges borrowers on its loans. Can Fin's is relatively low — around 10% (approx.) — because its salaried, prime customers are safe and can shop around for cheap rates.
Cost of fundsThe average interest rate a lender pays to borrow the money it lends out. Roughly 7.5% (approx.) for Can Fin. The lower this is, the more the lender keeps. This is Can Fin's secret weapon.
SpreadSimply yield minus cost of funds — the raw gap. Can Fin's FY26 spread was 2.86% (up from 2.55% a year earlier). Thin, but stable. Everything in this primer comes back to this number.
NIM"Net Interest Margin" — net interest income as a share of assets. A close cousin of spread, but a touch higher (because part of a lender's assets are funded by its own equity, which costs nothing). Can Fin's FY26 NIM was 4.19%.
GNPA"Gross Non-Performing Assets" — the share of loans where the borrower has stopped paying (90+ days overdue). The single best gauge of asset quality. Can Fin's is 0.85% — pristine.
The economics that decide everything: spread × book, minus what leaks

A lender's profit is easy to picture as a simple sum. Take the spread (yield minus cost of funds) and multiply it by the size of the loan book — that's the gross earnings. Then subtract two leaks: the running costs of the business (salaries, branches, systems — measured as cost-to-income) and the credit costs (money lost when borrowers default). Whatever survives is profit. There are only three ways to make more money: charge a wider spread, grow the book, or plug the two leaks. High-yield lenders chase the first. Can Fin has built its whole identity around the last two — a modest spread on a steadily growing book, with both leaks kept unusually small.

Who borrows from a housing finance company

  • Salaried home-buyers — people with a monthly payslip, a bank statement and a credit score. The safest, most sought-after borrower, and Can Fin's core: about 68% of its book.
  • Self-employed / non-professional borrowers — shopkeepers, small-business owners, professionals whose income is real but harder to document. Higher rate, slightly higher risk. About 32% of Can Fin's book and deliberately growing.
  • Loan-against-property (LAP) and top-up — mortgages taken against a home already owned, for business or personal needs. A small, higher-yielding sliver of most HFC books.
02

What Can Fin actually sells: the prime, salaried home loan

One product, done conservatively — read this once and the whole company makes sense.

Can Fin does not do anything exotic. Its product is the ordinary individual home loan — money to buy, build or improve a house, secured against that same house, repaid in monthly instalments (EMIs) over 10–20 years. What makes it distinctive is not what it lends but whom it lends to and how carefully. Roughly 90% of the book is pure home loans; the rest is loan-against-property and top-ups. And the borrower is, by design, the low-risk kind.

Why "prime and salaried" is the entire personality

  • Salaried borrowers are predictable. A payslip and a bank statement let Can Fin verify income precisely, so it can underwrite confidently and price finely. These customers rarely default — hence the 0.85% GNPA.
  • Prime borrowers demand cheap rates. A creditworthy salaried buyer can walk into any bank and get a competitive rate, so Can Fin has to price low to win them. That is the trade: safety in exchange for a thin yield.
  • Small-ticket, South-anchored, granular. Its loans are modest in size (average ticket in the low ₹20-lakh range), spread across a large number of borrowers — historically concentrated in South India but expanding into the North and West through steady branch additions (~200+ branches and outlets).
The identity in one line

If the flashy affordable-housing lenders are the corner shop that charges more for lending to customers the big banks won't touch, Can Fin is the sober municipal building society: it lends to the customers everyone wants, accepts the skinny margin that comes with them, and makes its living by being cheaper-funded and leaner than the next lender. Its competitive weapon is not a clever product or a high rate — it is a low cost base and a spotless loan book. That is a genuinely different way to run a mortgage business, and it is why Can Fin behaves so differently from the affordable-HFC cohort it is usually filed alongside.

03

The company, the parent, and who controls it

A Canara Bank creation — promoted by, but not a subsidiary of, a public-sector bank.

Can Fin Homes was set up in 1987 by Canara Bank, one of India's large public-sector banks, as the country's first bank-promoted housing finance company. Nearly four decades on, Canara Bank remains the promoter and single largest shareholder — but with a stake of just 29.99%, Can Fin is deliberately not a subsidiary. It is a professionally run, independently listed company that carries the credibility of a state-bank parent without being consolidated into it.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter: Canara Bank holds 29.99% (unencumbered, per its FY26 disclosure). The stake sits deliberately just below 30% — enough for promoter status and board influence, not enough to make Can Fin a consolidated subsidiary of the bank.
  • The rest of the register: the balance is held by public and institutional investors — domestic mutual funds, foreign portfolio investors and retail. There is no second promoter; management is professional, led by a managing director drawn from the banking system.
  • What the parent gives — and doesn't: Canara Bank lends credibility, a strong credit rating halo, and comfort to lenders, which helps Can Fin borrow cheaply. But it does not guarantee Can Fin's debt or run its book. Can Fin raises its own funds, sets its own underwriting, and lives or dies on its own spread.
The parentage nuance most people get wrong

People assume a "Canara Bank company" is a sleepy arm of a state bank. It isn't. Canara owns less than 30% and does not consolidate Can Fin, yet the association is worth real money: a mortgage lender whose promoter is a large nationalised bank is treated by bond investors and rating agencies as a safer credit, which shaves its borrowing cost. So the parentage matters most not through control but through funding — it is a big reason Can Fin can borrow so cheaply, which is the pillar its whole low-yield model stands on. The flip side, covered later, is that the very same sub-30% stake keeps alive a recurring market whisper: that a public-sector bank under capital pressure could one day sell down or exit.

04

The low-cost engine: how a thin margin still makes money

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — and it is the inverse of every affordable-lender primer.

Here is the puzzle at the centre of Can Fin. It lends at a low rate to safe borrowers, which means a thin spread of just 2.86%. A high-yield affordable lender earns twice that spread. And yet Can Fin's return on assets — the cleanest measure of how much profit a lender squeezes from each rupee it holds — is a healthy ~2.4%, right up with, and often above, lenders that charge far more. How can a thin-margin business out-earn fat-margin rivals? The answer is that a lender's return is a subtraction problem, not a multiplication problem — and Can Fin has mastered the subtractions.

The device that fits only Can Fin: returns from the cost side, not the yield side

Line up the affordable-HFC playbook next to Can Fin's and they are mirror images. An affordable lender starts with a fat yield (13–17%), then hands a big chunk back to high operating costs (feet-on-street collections, cash handling — cost-to-income often 30–40%) and to higher credit losses. It earns its return on the revenue side and defends it against leakage. Can Fin starts with a thin yield and protects it fanatically: it borrows near the cheapest rates any HFC can get, runs a cost-to-income around 18.8% — roughly half an affordable lender's — and loses almost nothing to defaults. It earns its return on the cost side. Two lenders can post the same ~2.4% ROA by walking in from opposite ends of the income statement. Can Fin's ROA is a story of subtraction kept small; the affordable lender's is a story of a big number shared out. Understand that inversion and you understand why Can Fin is not "a worse affordable lender" but a fundamentally different animal.

05

Cost of funds and the spread: where the whole game is won

Half of the spread is set by how cheaply Can Fin can borrow — its true moat.

A home loan yield of ~10% is roughly what any prime lender can charge; there is no magic on the lending side of a commoditised product. The differentiation is on the funding side. If two HFCs both lend at 10% but one borrows at 7.5% and the other at 8.5%, the first earns a spread half again as large — from nothing more than cheaper money. Can Fin has spent its life engineering the lowest funding cost it plausibly can, and that is the real reason its thin-yield model works.

The spread is not static — it breathes with the interest-rate cycle, and that is the daily drama of the business. When the Reserve Bank raises rates, Can Fin's cost of funds climbs quickly (much of its borrowing reprices fast), while its loan yields lag, because home-loan rates reset more slowly — so the spread gets squeezed. When rates fall, the reverse. To fight this, Can Fin has shifted roughly 85% of its loan book to quarterly interest-rate resets, so its lending rates now move much closer in step with its funding costs. That single plumbing change is a big reason the FY26 spread widened to 2.86% (from 2.55%) even in a choppy rate environment.

Why cheap funding is a moat, not just a nice-to-have

In a commoditised product like a prime home loan, the lender with the lowest cost of funds can always undercut on rate and still earn the same spread — or match on rate and earn more. That is a durable structural edge, and Can Fin's flows from three things that are hard for a newcomer to replicate quickly: a large public-sector bank as promoter, a long track record of pristine asset quality, and the top-tier credit ratings those two things earn. A new affordable lender can copy Can Fin's product overnight; it cannot copy a decade of AAA-grade funding access. The 85%-quarterly-reset shift is the tactical layer on top — it doesn't lower the cost of funds, but it stops a rising rate cycle from opening a hole in the spread before the loan book catches up.

06

The pristine book: asset quality as a business model

A 0.85% bad-loan ratio isn't luck — it's the point of the whole design.

For a thin-spread lender, a bad loan hurts twice as much as it does for a fat-spread one, because there is less margin to absorb the loss. So Can Fin cannot afford to be sloppy — and it isn't. Its gross NPA of 0.85% and net NPA of 0.37% (March 2026) are among the lowest anywhere in the HFC and NBFC sector. This is not incidental to the model; it is the model. Lending to salaried, prime borrowers against their own homes is a deliberate choice to keep credit losses near zero, which is what makes a thin spread viable.

Three habits keep the book clean. Underwriting discipline — verified salaried income, conservative loan-to-value ratios, and a preference for self-occupied homes the borrower will fight to keep. Granularity — a very large number of small loans, so no single default can dent the book. And the collateral itself — an owner-occupied house is the one asset a family will do almost anything to protect, which makes home loans structurally the safest form of retail credit in India.

The two-edged truth about a spotless book

A 0.85% GNPA is a genuine achievement and a real comfort. But it also sets a trap for the analyst: when asset quality is this good, it can almost only get worse. There is no room for a pleasant surprise — the book is already about as clean as a book gets — but there is room for an unpleasant one, especially as Can Fin deliberately grows its riskier non-salaried segment. For a lender earning a thin 2.86% spread, even a modest drift in bad loans — say GNPA creeping toward 1.5% — would eat a disproportionate slice of profit, because there is so little margin cushioning it. So the pristine book is both the model's proudest feature and its most-watched vulnerability: it has far more to lose than to gain on this line.

07

The non-salaried push: buying a little yield, carefully

The one growth lever that can widen a structurally thin spread.

Can Fin knows the arithmetic of its own model: with a prime salaried yield capped by competition, the only ways to lift the spread are to borrow even cheaper (hard) or to lend a bit more to higher-yielding borrowers. That is why the company has been deliberately, cautiously growing its self-employed / non-professional segment — now about 32% of the book, up over time from a smaller share — alongside a measured expansion into affordable and semi-affordable housing loans that carry a fatter rate.

The logic is straightforward. A self-employed borrower — a shopkeeper, a small manufacturer, a professional with lumpy income — is harder to underwrite and slightly riskier, so Can Fin charges more. Blend a growing slice of these higher-yield loans into a mostly-prime book and the overall yield edges up, widening the spread without abandoning the conservative core. It is the same instinct that led Can Fin to push loan-book growth toward the sector's higher-margin corners, while keeping its underwriting knobs turned tight.

The tightrope this push walks

This is the most consequential strategic choice Can Fin is making, and it cuts both ways. Done well, a bigger non-salaried mix is exactly the right medicine for a thin-spread lender — it lifts yield and defends margin without changing what Can Fin fundamentally is. Done carelessly, it is how a pristine book gets its first real scratches, because non-salaried lending is precisely where credit losses tend to show up first in a downturn. The whole bet rests on whether Can Fin can add the yield of a self-employed borrower without importing the losses that usually come with them. Its underwriting culture — the thing that produced a 0.85% GNPA — is exactly what is being tested by this move. Watch the non-salaried mix and the GNPA together: the first rising while the second stays flat is the model working; both rising together is the warning.

Why this matters for the whole thesis: the affordable/non-salaried push is Can Fin's answer to the structural problem that prime home loans are getting more commoditised every year. If the push succeeds, Can Fin lifts its spread and grows into a more profitable lender while staying conservative. If it stalls — or worse, if it lifts yield but also lifts defaults — Can Fin is left as a low-spread lender in the most competitive segment of Indian credit. This one lever carries much of the upside case.
08

Prime vs affordable: two ways to make the same 2.4%

Why Can Fin sits in a different box from the Aptus / Aavas / Home First cohort.

Can Fin is often bundled with the popular "affordable housing finance" stocks. It is worth being precise about how different it actually is, because the confusion drives a lot of mispricing. The affordable lenders — Aptus, Aavas, Home First, Five-Star — built their businesses on the opposite end of the market: informal-income, first-time, often rural or semi-urban borrowers whom banks won't lend to, charged a high rate to compensate for the risk and the cost of reaching them. Can Fin serves the borrowers banks compete for, at bank-like rates.

The comparison that reframes the stock

Both models can earn a ~2.4% return on assets, which is why they get lumped together — but they are exposed to completely different risks. The affordable lender's nightmare is a credit shock: its informal-income borrowers default in a downturn and the fat spread suddenly isn't fat enough. Can Fin's nightmare is a competition shock: banks and giant HFCs price its prime borrowers away and its already-thin spread narrows further. So the two aren't better-or-worse versions of one thing; they are bets on different futures. If you fear a credit cycle, the prime lender is the safer harbour. If you fear margin competition in commoditised prime lending, the affordable lender's fat cushion looks more comfortable. The market pays up for the affordable growth story and marks Can Fin cheaper — which is the whole valuation debate in a sentence.

09

The industry and the rivals

Prime home lending is the most crowded room in Indian credit.

Here is the uncomfortable truth about Can Fin's chosen turf: the prime, salaried home loan is the single most competitive product in Indian retail credit. Every large bank wants it, because it is safe, secured, long-duration and cross-sellable. So Can Fin competes not against a few specialist HFCs but against the full weight of the banking system and the biggest mortgage names in the country — all fighting over the same creditworthy borrower with rate as the main weapon.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two forces define Can Fin's position. First, it is a small fish in a pond full of whales: HDFC Bank, SBI and Bajaj Housing are vastly larger and, crucially, can fund even more cheaply than Can Fin can. In a straight rate war on prime loans, scale wins, and Can Fin is not the biggest. Second, its defence is not size but discipline — a leaner cost base and a cleaner book than most rivals its size, plus the granular, relationship-driven, South-anchored franchise the giants find fiddly to serve loan-by-loan. Can Fin doesn't need to beat SBI on rate; it needs to keep enough of a cost-and-quality edge that a thin spread on a growing book still compounds. That is a narrower path than the affordable lenders walk, but a well-trodden one for Can Fin over nearly four decades.

The risks that matter

A safe lender is not a risk-free stock — and its risks are specific.

The comforting things about Can Fin — prime borrowers, cheap funds, a spotless book — can lull an investor into thinking there is nothing to watch. There is. The risks here are not the dramatic blow-up kind; they are the quiet, grinding kind that erode a thin-spread lender over time. Five deserve attention.

  • Spread compression / competition. This is the central risk. Prime home loans are the most commoditised, most fought-over product in Indian credit. Banks (SBI, HDFC Bank) and giant HFCs (LIC Housing, Bajaj Housing) compete hard on rate, and a sustained rate war squeezes Can Fin's already-thin spread. There is little margin to give up before profitability suffers.
  • Rate-cycle & balance-transfer / prepayment. Prime borrowers are rate-shoppers: when someone offers a cheaper loan, they refinance and leave, so Can Fin faces high prepayment — it must keep re-lending just to stand still. And when funding costs rise faster than loan yields reset, the spread narrows even without a price war.
  • Asset-quality drift. The book is pristine now, which means it can realistically only get worse — especially as the higher-yield non-salaried segment grows. For a thin-spread lender, even a modest rise in bad loans bites harder than it would for a fat-spread rival.
  • The growth-versus-margin trade. The push into non-salaried and affordable loans lifts yield but adds risk. Chase it too hard and Can Fin imports the very credit losses its model was built to avoid; hold back and the spread stays structurally thin.
  • The Canara-parentage overhang. Canara Bank's sub-30% stake keeps alive periodic speculation of a promoter sell-down or strategic change. A stake sale wouldn't touch the loan book, but it could dent the funding-cost halo and unsettle sentiment — an overhang the market prices in from time to time.
The risk that actually decides it

Strip the five down and one dominates: spread compression from competition. Can Fin's entire model rests on a thin margin surviving all the way to profit. The affordable lenders can lose a chunk of their fat spread and still be fine; Can Fin cannot. So the risk that matters is not a credit shock — the book is too clean and too granular for that to be the first domino — but the slow bleed of a prime spread ground down by bigger, cheaper-funded rivals. That is why the non-salaried push and the cost-to-income discipline matter so much: they are Can Fin's two defences against exactly this. If both work, the thin spread holds; if the competition wins, the leanest cost base in the sector only slows the bleed, it doesn't stop it.

12

The future, and the bull/bear

A well-run, cheap, conservative compounder — asked to prove a thin spread can survive a crowded room.

Can Fin Homes enters FY27 as what it has long been: a steady, conservative, bank-backed prime lender guiding to ~14% loan growth, a ~3.75% NIM, ~2.4% ROA and 18%+ ROE, at a valuation that reflects the market's doubt rather than its record. The debate is not whether the business is good — its numbers say it is — but whether a low-yield lender in India's most competitive credit segment can keep its spread intact as the giants press.

The bull case

  • Structurally cheap funding — Canara pedigree plus AAA-grade ratings give a durable cost-of-funds edge that is hard to replicate.
  • Sector-leading efficiency — a ~18.8% cost-to-income lets a thin spread survive to a competitive ~2.4% ROA.
  • Pristine, granular book — 0.85% GNPA and small-ticket diversification make credit shocks unlikely to be the first domino.
  • Spread-lift optionality — the measured non-salaried / affordable push can widen a structurally thin margin without abandoning the conservative core.
  • Cheap on the multiple — ~2× book and ~11× earnings for a steady 18%+ ROE compounder, at a discount to the affordable cohort.

The bear case

  • Commoditised, crowded segment — prime home loans pit Can Fin against banks and Bajaj Housing that fund even cheaper; a rate war compresses an already-thin spread.
  • High prepayment — rate-shopping prime borrowers refinance away, forcing constant re-lending just to hold the book.
  • Thin cushion — a 2.86% spread leaves little room; small moves in funding cost or bad loans hit profit hard.
  • Growth-vs-risk tension — the non-salaried push could import the credit losses the model was built to avoid.
  • Promoter overhang — recurring speculation about a Canara stake sell-down can weigh on sentiment and the funding halo.
The bottom line

Can Fin Homes has spent nearly four decades proving that the dull way to run a mortgage lender — cheap funds, a lean shop, a spotless book, and a thin margin defended fiercely — compounds quietly and rarely blows up. Nothing in FY26 broke that record; the year set new highs on profit, disbursements and spread. What the next few years will decide is whether that thin spread can hold its ground in the most contested corner of Indian credit, as banks and a new generation of giant HFCs bid for the very same salaried borrower. The number to watch each quarter is not the loan book and not the profit — it is the spread: if it sits at 2.8–2.9% while the book compounds and GNPA stays under 1%, the cheap multiple is a gift; if it slides toward 2.5% and keeps going, the market's caution was the right read. For a company whose whole identity is being cheaper-funded and leaner than the next lender, the spread is the scoreboard, and it prints four times a year.
