The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

NTPC is India's largest power-generating company — a state-owned ("Maharatna") utility with about 89 GW of installed capacity, generating roughly a sixth of all the electricity India consumes. Historically a coal-fired giant, it is now in the middle of a deliberate pivot toward renewables, hydro, storage and even nuclear — while still building new coal to keep India's grid stable.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It earns a regulated, near-guaranteed return. Most of NTPC's profit comes from a cost-plus tariff set by the regulator (CERC) that allows it a ~15.5% post-tax return on the equity it invests in each plant, plus pass-through of fuel and assured payment for being available. That makes its earnings unusually predictable — closer to a bond than a typical company.
  • Its profit compounds even when revenue doesn't. FY26 consolidated revenue was roughly flat at ~₹1.87 lakh crore (because coal cost is just passed through), yet net profit rose ~15% to ₹27,546 Cr. The reason: profit tracks the growing "regulated equity" base (~₹1.16 lakh crore), which expands every time NTPC commissions a new plant.
  • It's the rare utility growing fast. NTPC added a record 9,619 MW in FY26 and targets 149 GW by 2032, including 60 GW of renewables — much of it housed in its separately-listed arm, NTPC Green Energy (which just crossed 10 GW). A steady annuity with a genuine growth runway — at barely 13× earnings and a ~2.3% dividend.
In plain terms

Think of NTPC as a toll road for electricity. For every power plant it builds, the regulator guarantees it can charge customers enough to recover its costs plus a fixed ~15.5% return on the money it put in — almost regardless of how much power actually flows. So NTPC doesn't really compete on price or worry much about demand; it grows its profit simply by building more plants, each of which adds another slice of guaranteed return. Coal is its workhorse and will be for years (India needs the reliable power), but the new building is increasingly solar, wind, hydro and batteries. The result is one of the market's most predictable profit machines — which is also why it's cheap and slow: you're buying a regulated annuity, not a rocket.

01

How a regulated utility earns

The single most important concept — get this and NTPC makes complete sense.

NTPC does not earn like a normal company that sells a product at whatever price the market bears. It earns under a cost-plus regulated framework set by the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC). Understanding this "regulated return" model is the key to everything about the company.

The CERC two-part tariff — how NTPC gets paid
ComponentWhat it covers
Capacity charge (the profit)A fixed charge that recovers depreciation, interest, O&M and — crucially — a ~15.5% post-tax Return on Equity on the equity NTPC invested in the plant. NTPC earns this as long as the plant is available to run, regardless of how much it actually generates.
Energy charge (fuel pass-through)Recovers the cost of fuel (coal) — a straight pass-through to the buyer. NTPC makes no profit on fuel, so coal-price swings barely touch its bottom line (they move revenue, not profit).
Availability incentiveNTPC is rewarded for keeping plants available (Plant Availability Factor), not just for output — incentivising reliability, its core job for the grid.
Why this model is so powerful — and so boring (in a good way)

Three consequences flow from cost-plus regulation, and they define NTPC. (1) Predictable profit: because the return is a regulated ~15.5% on invested equity for being available, NTPC's earnings are remarkably stable and visible — they don't swing with electricity demand or fuel prices the way a merchant generator's would. (2) Fuel risk is neutralised: since coal is passed through, a spike in coal prices inflates revenue but leaves profit largely intact — which is exactly why NTPC's revenue can be flat while profit grows. (3) Low business risk, capped upside: the trade-off for that safety is that NTPC can't earn windfall profits — its return is regulated. It's a low-risk, annuity-like business: the bond-like utility of the Indian market.

How NTPC grows: capacity → regulated equity → profit

If the return is fixed, how does it grow? By getting bigger.

Here's the elegant logic of a regulated utility's growth. If NTPC earns ~15.5% on the equity invested in each plant, then the way to grow profit is simply to invest more equity in more plants — to enlarge the "regulated equity" base on which that return is earned. NTPC's entire growth model is a capital-deployment machine.

03

The company and who controls it

A government-owned national champion — and the grid's anchor.

NTPC is a "Maharatna" central public-sector enterprise, majority-owned by the Government of India (~51%). Founded in 1975 to build India's thermal power base, it grew into the backbone of the national grid and remains the country's single most important generator.

Ownership & structure

  • Government of India ~51%: NTPC is a strategic national asset; the state's control brings policy alignment, low-cost funding and stability — and the usual PSU constraints.
  • Listed subsidiary — NTPC Green Energy: the renewables business is itself separately listed, creating a visible value pool and a dedicated vehicle to raise green capital (see §05).
  • Other arms: NTPC also has coal-mining, hydro (incl. via group entities), and joint ventures across the power value chain — a vertically-aware utility.

The coal backbone

The unglamorous engine that still pays for everything — and keeps India's lights on.

For all the talk of green energy, the truth is that coal remains NTPC's core — the majority of its capacity, the bulk of its generation, and most of its profit. And in a country where electricity demand is growing fast and renewables are intermittent, reliable coal "base-load" power is still essential — which is why NTPC is, controversially to some, still building new coal plants.

Why coal is both the strength and the controversy

NTPC's coal fleet is genuinely excellent — it runs at far higher utilisation (~72% load factor) than the national average (~63%), reflecting efficient, well-run plants. And it serves an indispensable role: solar and wind only produce when the sun shines and wind blows, but the grid needs power 24×7, so a backbone of dispatchable "base-load" capacity (coal, hydro, nuclear, storage) is essential for stability — especially as India's electricity demand keeps climbing. That's the case for NTPC's plan to add ~15 GW of new thermal even while greening. The controversy: new coal is a long-lived, carbon-heavy asset in a decarbonising world, carrying future stranded-asset and ESG risk if the transition accelerates faster than expected. NTPC's bet is that India needs both for decades — the subject of §06.

05

The green pivot & NTPC Green Energy

The growth story the market is most excited about — spun into its own listed company.

NTPC's most important new chapter is its renewables push, housed largely in NTPC Green Energy (NGEL) — a dedicated, separately-listed subsidiary building solar, wind, storage and green-hydrogen capacity at speed. The ambition is large: 60 GW of renewables by 2032, as part of a group target of 149 GW and ~44% non-fossil capacity.

Why spin renewables into a separate listed company?

Housing renewables in NGEL does three smart things. (1) Funding: a pure-play green company can raise dedicated green capital (equity and cheap green debt) from ESG-focused investors who won't touch a coal-heavy parent. (2) Value visibility: renewables are valued at much higher multiples than thermal, so a separate listing crystallises that value rather than burying it inside a coal utility — a clear sum-of-the-parts benefit for NTPC shareholders, who own the majority of NGEL. (3) Focus & speed: a dedicated team and balance sheet can move faster on the land-grab for renewable projects. The risk: renewable returns are competitively bid (not cost-plus regulated like thermal), so margins are thinner and depend on execution, tariffs and storage economics — it's a genuinely different, more contested business than NTPC's regulated core.

The "both-and" transition strategy

Building coal and renewables at the same time — the central strategic tension.

NTPC is doing something that looks contradictory: aggressively adding renewables and building new coal, simultaneously. This "both-and" approach is the defining strategic choice — and the heart of the bull/bear debate about the company's future.

Why "both-and" is the rational (if uncomfortable) answer

The logic is grid physics plus growth maths. India's power demand is rising rapidly, and renewables — however fast they grow — can't yet provide round-the-clock reliability without enormous (and still-maturing) storage. So to keep the lights on and meet new demand, India needs to add clean capacity and retain/expand firm base-load. NTPC, as the system's anchor, is tasked with both. For investors this creates a split personality: a stable, regulated thermal annuity (predictable, cheap, ESG-stigmatised) bolted to a fast-growing, higher-multiple renewables business (NGEL). The bull sees the best of both — a funded growth engine on a rock-solid base. The bear sees a company hedging its bets, pouring capital into coal that could strand if the transition accelerates, while its renewable returns face fierce competition. How that tension resolves is the long-term question for the stock.

07

Discom dues & payment security

The classic risk for any Indian generator — who actually pays the bill?

A regulated return is only as good as the customer who pays it. NTPC sells most of its power to state-owned electricity distribution companies ("discoms") — and India's discoms have a long, troubled history of financial weakness and delayed payments. This counterparty risk is the most important practical risk in NTPC's otherwise-safe model.

Why this matters — and why it's improved

NTPC's tariff may guarantee a return on paper, but if the discoms that buy its power are bankrupt and slow to pay, that return turns into a mountain of receivables rather than cash. For years, ballooning discom dues were a real drag on the whole power sector. The position has materially improved, thanks to two reforms: a tripartite mechanism (Government of India + state governments + RBI) that lets NTPC recover dues directly from a defaulting state's central allocations, and the Late Payment Surcharge (LPS) scheme that forced discoms onto structured repayment plans and penalised fresh delays. Together these have tightened payment discipline and shrunk overdues — strengthening NTPC's cash conversion and credit profile. The residual risk remains, though: NTPC's customers are structurally weak public utilities, and a fresh deterioration in discom finances (or a populist tariff freeze) would flow straight back to NTPC's working capital.

Why it's still worth watching: the regulated model protects NTPC's profit recognition, but discom health drives its cash collection. The reforms have de-risked it considerably, but the underlying fragility of state distribution finances is a permanent feature of the Indian power sector — and the thing that can turn a "guaranteed" return into a slow-paying one.

The industry and the rivals

The dominant incumbent in a market with a powerful demand tailwind.

NTPC is the giant of Indian power generation, but it operates alongside large private players and a fleet of state generators. The structural backdrop is hugely favourable: India's electricity demand is growing strongly with incomes, industrialisation, air-conditioning, EVs and data centres — a multi-decade tailwind for everyone who can build reliable capacity.

In regulated thermal, NTPC's scale and low cost of capital make it nearly unrivalled; in renewables, it's one strong competitor among several in a fiercely-bid market.

Financials & valuation

Steady, visible, bond-like earnings — at a utility's modest multiple.

How to think about the valuation

A regulated utility is best valued on price-to-book against its regulated return, plus the growth in its regulated-equity base, plus a sum-of-the-parts credit for its listed renewables arm. NTPC's ~13× earnings / ~1.7× book is cheap in absolute terms — but utilities are cheap, because their returns are regulated (capped) and their earnings, while safe, grow only as fast as they can deploy capital. The bull case has three legs: (1) the regulated thermal base is a rock-solid, growing annuity; (2) the NTPC Green Energy stake is worth a high multiple and may be under-credited inside the parent (SOTP upside); and (3) a record capex cycle drives years of visible profit growth. The bear case: it's a capital-hungry, government-controlled coal-heavy utility facing ESG exclusion and transition risk, whose returns are regulated and whose customers (discoms) are weak — so it deserves to stay cheap. The dividend and the predictability are what you're paid for the wait.

The growth runway

A rare utility with a genuine, multi-year growth pipeline.

Unlike most mature utilities, NTPC has a long, visible runway — a function of India's surging power demand and its own ambitious build plan. Growth comes from stacking capacity (and therefore regulated equity and renewable assets) for the better part of a decade.

  • The headline target — 149 GW by 2032 (from ~89 GW today), a ~60%+ capacity expansion driving years of regulated-equity and renewable growth.
  • Renewables acceleration — NTPC Green scaling toward 60 GW, adding ~8 GW/year — the fastest-growing, highest-multiple piece.
  • New thermal — ~15 GW of fresh coal tenders to anchor base-load and grow the regulated thermal annuity.
  • Firming & new clean tech — ~22 GWh of storage (BESS + pumped hydro), green hydrogen pilots, and a nuclear push — future-proofing the portfolio.
NTPC's growth isn't about winning customers — it's about how fast it can build, fund and commission capacity. With India short of power for a decade, the constraint is execution, not demand.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A predictable annuity with a real growth engine — and a transition to navigate.

NTPC is among the most predictable large businesses in India: a regulated, government-anchored utility earning a guaranteed return on a steadily growing asset base, with a fast-scaling renewables arm on top. The debate is about the energy transition, capital intensity and the regulated ceiling, not about earnings safety.

The bull case

  • Regulated, visible earnings — ~15.5% RoE on a growing ~₹1.16 L Cr equity base; bond-like predictability.
  • Genuine growth — 149 GW by 2032 from ~89 GW; record capex driving years of profit compounding.
  • Green optionality (SOTP) — the listed NTPC Green stake is high-multiple and may be under-credited in the parent.
  • Demand tailwind — India is power-short for a decade; the constraint is build speed, not demand.
  • Cheap & income-paying — ~13× earnings, ~1.7× book, ~2.3% growing dividend, lowest cost of capital.

The bear case

  • Capped returns — regulated ~15.5% RoE limits upside; it can't earn windfalls like a merchant generator.
  • Coal & ESG risk — still building new coal; stranded-asset and ESG-exclusion risk if the transition accelerates.
  • Capital intensity & debt — heavy, continuous capex and large borrowings; execution and funding risk.
  • Discom counterparty risk — weak state distribution finances can slow cash collection despite the reforms.
  • PSU constraints & regulation — government control and CERC tariff revisions can cut both ways.
The bottom line

NTPC is a low-risk, regulated power annuity with an unusually long growth runway — a rare combination of safety and expansion, plus a fast-growing green arm that adds optionality. The business risk is genuinely low; the questions are about the transition and the ceiling. The story over the coming years will be written in three things — the pace of capacity additions (does it hit its build targets?), the scaling and value of NTPC Green (does the SOTP get recognised?), and how the coal-vs-clean balance ages (is the base-load bet right, or a stranded-asset risk?). For investors who want a steady, cheap, dividend-paying compounder with a clear runway, NTPC is one of the market's most dependable; for those wanting high returns or zero coal exposure, the regulated ceiling and the thermal core are the catch.
