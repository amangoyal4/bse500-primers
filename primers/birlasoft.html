Birlasoft, the mid-cap IT house that fixed its margin before its growth

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Birlasoft is a mid-tier Indian IT-services company — it earns money by putting its engineers to work building, modernising and running the software that big companies depend on. Application development and maintenance, ERP implementation (its historic strength is SAP and Oracle / JD Edwards), cloud migration, data and analytics, and increasingly AI. It is part of the CK Birla group, runs mostly out of Noida and Pune with delivery weighted to the US mid-market, and concentrates on four industry verticals: Manufacturing (its largest), BFSI, Life Sciences & Healthcare, and Energy & Utilities.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a two-company mash-up, and only half of it is "KPIT." Today's Birlasoft was formed in the 2019 merger of the old Birlasoft with KPIT's enterprise-IT business. KPIT's automotive-engineering arm split off at the same moment and lists separately as KPIT Technologies. So the "KPIT" that investors picture doing car software is a different listed company; Birlasoft inherited the enterprise-applications half and the legacy listing.
  • The turnaround is currently one-sided: margin is fixed, growth is not. FY26 revenue actually fell ~1.2% to ~₹5,310 Cr (~$597.5M), dragged by soft discretionary demand and a deliberate exit from unprofitable work — yet EBITDA margin expanded 333 bps to 16.3%, and the Q4 exit margin hit 18.5%. Management has rebuilt profitability; it has not yet restored the top line.
  • The market prices it as an underdeliverer. Birlasoft trades near 15–16× earnings — roughly half the ~30× median of steadier mid-cap peers like Coforge and Persistent. That discount is not about capability; it is a verdict on years of leadership churn and uneven quarters. The re-rating question is whether new management can string together enough consistent quarters to earn the peer multiple back.
In plain terms

Birlasoft rents out skilled software engineers by the hour and by the project. A client — say a US industrial-equipment maker — needs its ancient ERP system upgraded, its factory data plumbed into a cloud dashboard, or a new customer portal built. Birlasoft supplies a team, onshore and offshore, that does the work and often stays on to run it. The economics are entirely about people: how many engineers are billing, at what rate, how many sit "on the bench" between projects, and whether pay rises faster than what clients will pay. FY26 showed this company can run those levers well — margins jumped. What it has yet to prove is that it can do that and grow the number of billable engineers at the same time.

01

IT services 101: how a company like this earns its keep

No jargon needed — just the handful of levers that decide whether the business makes money.

An IT-services firm is, at its core, a labour-arbitrage and expertise business. It hires engineers — many in lower-cost Indian delivery centres — and sells their time and skill to companies in the US, Europe and elsewhere that would rather not build and run their own software teams. Revenue is a headcount-times-billing story; profit is what's left after wages, and it lives or dies on a few operational ratios. Learn these five and the whole sector clicks into place.

The five levers that drive an IT-services P&L
LeverWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Billing modelTwo flavours. Time-and-materials (T&M): the client pays per engineer per hour — low risk, low margin. Fixed-price: Birlasoft quotes a price for a whole outcome and keeps any efficiency it wrings out — higher risk, higher margin if delivered well. The mix shifts profitability.
UtilisationThe share of billable engineers actually on a paying project versus sitting "on the bench." Every idle consultant is a salary with no revenue. A point or two of utilisation is worth real margin.
Offshore mixThe proportion of work done from low-cost India vs pricier onsite in the US/Europe. More offshore = lower cost = better margin, provided the client accepts remote delivery.
PyramidThe ratio of junior (cheap) to senior (expensive) engineers on a project. A "fat pyramid" of freshers under a few leads is cheaper to run. Managing the pyramid is one of the biggest margin levers in the business.
Wage vs pricingSalaries rise with inflation and attrition; billing rates rise only when clients allow it. When wages outrun pricing, margins compress — the perennial squeeze of Indian IT.
The number that tells you it's a people business: revenue per employee

Divide FY26 revenue (~$597.5M) by year-end headcount (11,363) and you get roughly $52,000 of revenue per employee. That single figure is the whole model in miniature: a services company doesn't own factories or patents that scale on their own — it scales by adding billable people, and it gets more profitable by making each person more valuable (higher-end work, better utilisation, a leaner pyramid) faster than their pay rises. When you hear an IT firm talk about "moving up the value chain" or "non-linear growth," it is trying to break the arithmetic that ties revenue to bodies.

How growth is measured: constant currency and TCV

  • Constant-currency (cc) growth — because most revenue is billed in dollars but reported in rupees, a weaker rupee can flatter the reported number. Analysts strip out the currency swing and watch constant-currency revenue growth to see the real, underlying demand. This is the truest read of whether the business is expanding.
  • TCV — Total Contract Value — the headline size of new deals signed in a quarter. It is the pipeline turning into a promise: a rising TCV suggests future revenue, though the value gets recognised only as the work is actually delivered over the contract's life.
  • Book-to-bill — new orders signed versus revenue billed in the same period. Above 1.0 means the order book is growing faster than it is being consumed; below 1.0 means the pipeline is thinning. It is the early-warning gauge for growth two to four quarters out.
02

What Birlasoft actually sells: from ERP roots to AI edges

The service lines, and the enterprise-applications DNA that defines them.

Birlasoft is best understood as an enterprise-applications specialist that has extended into the modern digital and data layers around those applications. Its heritage — reinforced by the KPIT enterprise-IT half it absorbed — is deep implementation and management of the big back-office systems that run a company: ERP, in particular SAP and Oracle / JD Edwards. Around that core sit the newer, faster-growing services.

Stages 1–3 are project revenue: lumpy, discretionary, sensitive to client budgets. Stage 4 — running what was built — is the calmer, recurring layer that keeps billing after the project ends.

The service lines, decoded

  • Enterprise applications / ERP — implementing, upgrading and running SAP and Oracle/JD Edwards systems: the operational backbone of a manufacturer or distributor. This is Birlasoft's identity and its strongest calling card, especially in the US mid-market where JD Edwards remains widely used.
  • Digital, cloud & data — moving workloads to cloud platforms, building customer and operations portals, and turning scattered enterprise data into analytics and dashboards. The growth-y layer that sits on top of the ERP core.
  • Application development & maintenance (ADM) + managed services — the long tail: writing new software and, crucially, keeping the lights on for systems already built. Maintenance and managed-services work is stickier and less discretionary than project work — it is what a client cannot easily switch off in a downturn.
  • AI & automation — the newest edge: embedding generative-AI copilots, automation and AI-led delivery into all of the above. Management has made "AI-led" a headline strategic theme (see §08), both as an opportunity and as a defence against AI compressing traditional effort-based work.
Why the ERP heritage cuts both ways

Being an ERP-and-JD-Edwards house gives Birlasoft a defensible niche: these systems are mission-critical, expensive to rip out, and the pool of firms that can competently run them is limited — so the work is sticky and the client relationships are long. The flip side is that a heavy tilt to legacy ERP maintenance is exactly the kind of standardised, effort-based work that generative AI most threatens to make cheaper and less labour-intensive. Birlasoft's strongest moat and its sharpest disruption risk are the same asset viewed from two angles — which is why "AI-led delivery" is not a marketing slogan here but a survival strategy.

03

The company, the CK Birla group, and the merger that built it

A CK Birla group company whose corporate DNA is genuinely two firms fused into one.

Birlasoft belongs to the CK Birla group — the ~$3-billion industrial house (autos components, building products, healthcare, technology) controlled by Chandrakant Birla, a branch of the wider Birla family distinct from Aditya Birla and Hindustan Times' KK Birla lines. The IT arm's modern shape was set by a single 2019 transaction that is essential to understand.

Timeline

  • 1990s
    Birlasoft established as the CK Birla group's IT-services arm, growing as an offshore application-development and enterprise-solutions provider with a US mid-market tilt.
  • Jan 2019
    The defining merger. KPIT Technologies' IT-services (enterprise) business merges with Birlasoft to create a ~$500M listed enterprise-digital & IT-services company; simultaneously, KPIT's automotive-engineering business demerges into a separate listed company, KPIT Technologies. The combined enterprise-IT entity takes the Birlasoft name — and, importantly, the legacy KPIT stock-exchange listing (BSE scrip 532400).
  • 2019
    First CEO of the merged company, Anjan Lahiri, exits within months; Dharmender Kapoor takes over as MD & CEO to lead post-merger integration and brand consolidation.
  • 2020–2022
    Integration and rebranding; pandemic-era digital demand lifts the sector. Birlasoft consolidates its four-vertical focus and cleans up its go-to-market, but growth is uneven relative to peers.
  • Jan 2023
    Angan Guha (ex-Wipro) appointed CEO & MD — the third chief executive in roughly four years — tasked with re-accelerating growth and steadying delivery.
  • FY24–FY25
    Demand softens across mid-cap IT; some large accounts ramp down. Birlasoft's quarters turn choppy again, and the stock de-rates relative to steadier peers.
  • FY26
    Revenue ~₹5,310 Cr (down ~1.2%), but EBITDA margin expands 333 bps to 16.3% and the Q4 exit margin reaches 18.5%. Fresh senior appointments (new CFO Aug 2025; CEO-Americas Oct 2025) reshape the leadership bench.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the CK Birla group holds roughly 40.4% — a controlling but far-from-dominant stake, leaving a large free float held by domestic and foreign institutions. That means public-market shareholders have a real voice, and the stock trades on its operating quarters rather than on promoter fiat.
  • Balance sheet: Birlasoft is debt-free and net-cash, carrying roughly ₹2,000 Cr of cash and short-term investments. Like most asset-light IT-services firms, it funds itself from operating cash and returns surplus via dividends (a ₹4/share final dividend was declared for FY26). Balance-sheet risk is essentially nil; the entire debate is operational.
  • Not to be confused: KPIT Technologies (NSE: KPITTECH) is a separate listed company — the automotive-engineering arm that demerged in 2019. It is not part of Birlasoft, does different work (software for cars and mobility), and is not owned by the CK Birla group. The shared "KPIT" name is a frequent source of investor confusion.
The structural point that shapes everything: a promoter holding of only ~40% and a professional-CEO model means Birlasoft is run like a public operating company, not a founder's fiefdom — good for governance, but it also means the CK Birla group has repeatedly reached outside for chief executives, and each transition has cost the company momentum. The controlling shareholder's steadiest contribution is the balance sheet and patient capital; the operating consistency has had to come from hired managers who kept changing.
04

The four verticals — and how much rides on manufacturing

Where the revenue comes from, and the concentration that makes this a manufacturing-plus-ERP story.

Birlasoft organises itself around four industry verticals. This matters more than it does for a generalist IT firm, because Birlasoft's revenue is unusually tilted toward one of them — manufacturing — and that vertical is precisely where its ERP heritage is strongest. Read the mix and you can see both the specialisation that wins deals and the concentration that adds risk.

Manufacturing alone is close to two-fifths of revenue — the single vertical where Birlasoft's SAP and JD Edwards depth is most valued. The other three are meaningful but none rivals it; the company's fortunes swing disproportionately with US mid-market manufacturing budgets.

Each vertical plays a distinct role. Manufacturing is the anchor — industrial, automotive-adjacent and discrete-manufacturing clients that run heavy ERP estates, exactly Birlasoft's sweet spot. BFSI brings scale and steadier spend but pits Birlasoft against far larger banking-IT specialists. Life Sciences & Healthcare is a higher-value, compliance-heavy field (validated systems, regulated data) where relationships are sticky. Energy & Utilities rides the modernisation and transition spend of a slower-moving but durable customer base.

Why the concentration is the quiet risk

A firm that draws nearly 38% of revenue from one vertical, and much of that from the US mid-market, is doubly exposed: to the health of manufacturing capex cycles and to a client base — mid-sized companies — that cuts discretionary IT spend faster and deeper than blue-chip enterprises when the economy wobbles. In a good manufacturing cycle this focus is an edge, because specialist depth wins over generalist breadth. In a downturn it is a magnifier, because the very clients most likely to pause projects are over-represented in the book. Diversifying growth into life sciences and utilities is not just ambition; it is risk management.

A genuine surprise: for a company routinely bucketed with generic "Indian IT," Birlasoft is closer to a specialist manufacturing-and-ERP shop than a broad digital generalist — nearly two-fifths of revenue is one vertical, and its identity rests on legacy enterprise applications (SAP, JD Edwards) more than on the fashionable cloud-native work. Its fate is tied less to the abstract "IT-spending cycle" and more to whether US mid-market industrial firms keep upgrading their ERP estates.
05

The consistency gap: why the market pays half-price for the same assets

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to this company.

Here is the puzzle at the centre of Birlasoft. It owns a genuinely useful business: a sticky client roster, real vertical depth in manufacturing, one of the deeper ERP practices in the mid-cap pack, and a spotless, net-cash CK Birla balance sheet. On paper it has most of what its admired peers have. Yet the market values it at roughly half their multiple. That gap is not a capability discount. It is a consistency discount — and you can very nearly measure it.

Birlasoft trades at roughly a 47% discount to its peer group's median multiple. The peers are not obviously more capable — several sit in the same verticals with similar service lines. What they have that Birlasoft lacks is a record of stable, sequential quarters under stable management.

The evidence for calling it a consistency discount rather than a quality discount is that the pieces of the business are sound while the sequence is not. Since the 2019 merger the company has cycled through three chief executives, absorbed a complex integration, and delivered quarters that lurch — a strong one followed by a soft one, a margin beat alongside a revenue miss. Investors do not pay premium multiples for potential; they pay for predictability. Every reset in the corner office and every choppy quarter re-teaches the market to underwrite Birlasoft cautiously, and the multiple compresses to match.

The device that only fits Birlasoft: same assets, half the multiple

Put two mid-cap IT firms side by side — Birlasoft and a peer like Coforge or Persistent — with comparable verticals, comparable service lines, comparable client geographies. Now note that one trades at ~30× and the other at ~15.6×. Almost none of that ~47% gap can be explained by what the companies do; it is explained by how reliably they do it. Birlasoft's entire re-rating case therefore reduces to a single, unusually clean proposition: close the consistency gap and the multiple has room to roughly double toward peers; leave it open and the discount is permanent. Very few stocks let you frame the bull and bear thesis around one measurable spread. This one does — because the assets are not in dispute, only the execution is.

06

The margin rebuild that's already happened

Half of the turnaround is done — and FY26 is the proof.

Before dwelling on what Birlasoft hasn't fixed, give it credit for what it has. FY26 is a textbook margin-first turnaround year: revenue was flat-to-down, but profitability climbed hard. That combination is deliberate, and it tells you the operating levers from §01 are being pulled competently.

EBITDA margin expanded 333 bps for the full year and the Q4 exit rate hit 18.5% — even as revenue dipped ~1.2%. That is margin bought with discipline (better utilisation, pyramid management, exiting loss-making work), not with volume.

The mechanics behind the margin gain are exactly the levers a services firm controls: tighter utilisation, disciplined pyramid management, a firmer stance on pricing, and a willingness to walk away from unprofitable accounts even at the cost of reported revenue. Attrition — the wage-inflation pressure valve — sat at a comfortable 13.0%, low enough that the company wasn't bleeding people or paying up to replace them. Q4 PAT jumped ~44% year-on-year on the margin expansion, and full-year PAT held roughly flat at ~₹518 Cr despite the softer top line.

Why margin-first is the right sequence — and the trap in it

Fixing margin before chasing growth is defensible: it proves operational control, throws off cash, and lifts earnings even in a weak demand year — which is why FY26 profit held up while revenue fell. But margin gains from utilisation and pyramid discipline are finite. You can only run engineers so hot and thin the pyramid so far before quality and morale suffer. Beyond a point, the only durable way to grow profit is to grow revenue. Birlasoft has harvested the self-help margin; the next leg has to come from the top line, and that is the harder, less-controllable half — because it depends on client demand, not internal discipline.

07

Leadership and the search for stability

The defining issue — because the discount is priced on it.

If §05 established that Birlasoft's problem is consistency, this is where that problem is manufactured: at the top. A services business is, more than most, a people-and-relationships machine, and it runs on continuity of leadership — the CEO who owns the big client relationships, sets the delivery culture, and holds the strategy steady long enough for it to compound. Birlasoft's post-merger history has been the opposite.

Three CEOs in roughly six years, layered on top of a complex merger integration, is a lot of turbulence for a business whose competitive edge is stable client relationships. Each transition risks stalled decisions, restarted strategies and unsettled clients — and the market has learned to expect exactly that friction, which is a large part of why the multiple sits where it does.

Why the 2025 bench-building matters more than it looks

The recent senior appointments — a new CFO, a dedicated Americas CEO — read like routine HR notes, but they address the core fragility directly. By building a deeper leadership layer, Birlasoft is trying to make itself less dependent on the person in the CEO chair, so that the next transition, whenever it comes, doesn't reset the company again. If it works, the single most important thing the company can do to close its valuation discount is not a product launch or a big deal — it is simply for the current team to still be running the place, delivering, four and eight quarters from now. Continuity is the product here.

08

The AI question: threat and toolkit at once

The structural force hanging over all effort-based IT — and Birlasoft's most exposed and most opportune flank.

Every Indian IT-services firm now faces the same existential question, and for Birlasoft it is sharper than most. Generative AI is very good at exactly the kind of work that fills a services company's timesheets: writing and testing code, maintaining applications, handling routine ERP support tickets. If a task that took ten engineers can be done by three engineers plus an AI copilot, the billable hours — and the revenue tied to them — compress. For a firm with a large legacy-ERP maintenance tail, that is a direct hit to the core.

The real test AI sets for Birlasoft

The AI question is not "will Birlasoft use AI" — every firm will. It is whether the company can convert AI from a revenue-shrinking force into a margin-and-relevance-expanding one faster than it erodes the legacy base. That means re-pricing work away from billable hours toward outcomes, retraining a delivery force around AI-assisted engineering, and selling clients the harder task of embedding AI into their own enterprise systems. Management has planted the "AI-led" flag; the flag is easy, the transition is not. For a company whose bread-and-butter is precisely the effort-based ERP work AI targets, getting this right is not optional upside — it is the price of staying relevant this decade.

The honest read is that no one — not Birlasoft, not its larger rivals — yet knows the net effect of AI on services revenue. It could compress the industry's top line, or it could expand the pie by making enterprise transformation cheaper and therefore more common. What is clear is that a mid-cap with a legacy-ERP tilt has less margin for a slow adaptation than a larger, more diversified peer. This is a force to watch quarter by quarter in the revenue-per-employee and pricing commentary, not a settled story.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded mid-cap field, squeezed from above by giants and from within by captives.

Birlasoft competes in one of the most contested tiers of Indian IT: the mid-cap services pack, sitting below the TCS/Infosys/Wipro/HCL giants and above the small players. Its natural comparison set is a group of similarly-sized firms — and, tellingly, several of them are the "steadier" names whose multiples Birlasoft is trying to earn back.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Birlasoft is pinched from three directions. Above it, the large-caps have the scale and R&D budgets to lead the AI transition and win the biggest deals. Beside it, steadier mid-caps like Coforge and Persistent have already earned the premium multiples by delivering consistent growth — so they attract the capital and the talent. And underneath the whole industry, the rise of client-owned GCC captives quietly removes work that used to be outsourced. Birlasoft's defence is specialisation — being the best ERP-and-manufacturing partner for a US mid-market client is a narrower but more defensible position than trying to be everything. The risk is that a niche, without consistent execution, is not enough to hold the multiple.

At ~15.6× earnings with a fortress balance sheet, Birlasoft is not priced for disaster — it is priced for a company that keeps failing to grow. The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, clean, capable mid-cap asked to do the one thing it has struggled to do: be consistent.

Birlasoft enters FY27 with a rebuilt margin, a debt-free balance sheet, a specialist niche, and a valuation that already assumes it will disappoint. That is an unusual setup: the downside is arguably priced, and the upside rests almost entirely on a single, checkable behaviour — delivering steady, growing quarters under a stable team. The business case is not in doubt; the execution record is.

The bull case

  • Margin already proven — 333 bps of FY26 expansion to 16.3% and an 18.5% Q4 exit show the operating levers work; profitability is banked.
  • Cheap with optionality — ~15.6× and net cash; if growth returns, the ~47% discount to peers can close and roughly double the multiple.
  • Defensible niche — deep SAP/JD Edwards and manufacturing expertise in the US mid-market is sticky, specialist work.
  • Healthy deal flow — Q4 TCV of $208M (up QoQ) and low 13% attrition suggest the pipeline and delivery bench are intact.
  • Bench-building — a deeper senior team (new CFO, CEO-Americas) aimed at making the company less hostage to any one departure.

The bear case

  • Growth still absent — revenue fell in FY26 and Q4 dollar revenue was down 4.5% YoY; the top line, the thing that matters now, hasn't turned.
  • Leadership-stability risk — three CEOs since 2019; another reset would re-open the very discount the story needs to close.
  • Concentration — ~38% of revenue from manufacturing and a US mid-market client base that cuts discretionary IT spend fast in a downturn.
  • AI disruption — a legacy-ERP maintenance tilt is exactly what generative AI can compress; adaptation must outrun erosion.
  • Squeezed competitively — larger, better-resourced rivals above; steadier peers beside; GCC captives insourcing work below.
The bottom line

Birlasoft has spent the years since its 2019 merger being a company that could clearly do the work but couldn't reliably string the quarters together — and the market has paid it accordingly, at roughly half the multiple of peers built from the same raw materials. FY26 answered the first half of the doubt: the margin is fixed, decisively. The unanswered half is the one that now moves the stock — whether revenue can grow, quarter after quarter, without the leadership turning over again. The next few results settle it in one line on the fact sheet: the constant-currency revenue-growth number, and how many quarters in a row it stays positive under the same team. Four consecutive positive quarters under one CEO would do more for this stock than any deal or product launch — because for Birlasoft, consistency is not a footnote to the thesis; it is the thesis.
