00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

RateGain Travel Technologies sells software and data to the travel industry — to hotels, airlines, online travel agents (OTAs), car-rental firms, cruise lines and the big travel brands. It is, by its own description, the largest travel-and-hospitality SaaS company headquartered in India, but almost all of its customers are abroad: it serves roughly 3,000-plus direct clients across 100-plus countries, and earns the overwhelming bulk of its money in US dollars, euros and pounds. Its work is the kind you never see as a traveller but rely on every time you book: making sure a hotel's rooms appear, correctly priced and available, across every website that sells them, and helping that hotel decide what to charge and how to find the guest in the first place.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It runs on three distinct engines, not one. RateGain is really three businesses stitched together: Distribution (the plumbing that pipes a hotel's rooms into hundreds of booking channels), DaaS / Rate Intelligence (selling competitors' price and demand data so a client can price smartly), and MarTech / demand generation (marketing technology that helps a travel brand actually attract and convert travellers). Each earns differently, grows at a different pace, and carries a different margin.
  • Its revenue is part subscription, part travel-volume. Some of what RateGain earns is steady software subscription — a hotel pays whether or not it sells a room. But a large slice is usage-linked: it rises and falls with how many bookings flow through the pipes and how much travel advertising the world is buying. That is what makes it attractive (recurring, sticky, global) and vulnerable (travel is cyclical and shock-prone) at the same time.
  • FY26 was transformed by one acquisition. Revenue jumped ~69% to ~₹1,824 Cr — but that leap was powered chiefly by Sojern, a US demand-generation company RateGain bought for $250 million (~₹2,223 Cr), completed in November 2025. The deal roughly doubled the top line, tilted the company decisively toward marketing technology, and — the detail most people miss — pushed reported profit slightly down, from ~₹209 Cr to ~₹194 Cr, as integration and interest costs bit. The stock nonetheless trades on a rich multiple (~50× earnings), which tells you the market is pricing the combined company's future, not its past.
In plain terms

A modern hotel doesn't sell most of its rooms at its own front desk — it sells them through Booking.com, Expedia, MakeMyTrip, Google, dozens of others, each demanding up-to-the-second prices and availability. Keeping all those channels correct, watching what rivals charge, and finding travellers to fill the rooms is a full-time engineering and data problem no single hotel wants to build itself. RateGain builds it once and rents it to thousands of them. The business earns a little every month in subscriptions and a little more every time travel actually happens — so it compounds quietly in good years and stalls when the world stops moving. In FY26 it made its boldest move yet, spending a quarter-billion dollars to buy a US company and turn itself, in large part, into a travel-advertising firm. Whether that bet earns its keep is now the whole story.

01

The distribution problem: why a hotel needs RateGain at all

No tech background needed — just the mess that RateGain exists to tidy up.

Start with one hotel and one room-night to sell tonight. In the old world it sold that room at its own reception. In today's world the same room is offered simultaneously across a sprawl of digital shop windows: global OTAs like Booking.com and Expedia, regional ones like MakeMyTrip or Agoda, metasearch sites like Google Hotels and Trivago, the old airline-era GDS networks that travel agents still use, plus the hotel's own website. Each of those channels needs three things kept perfectly in sync, in real time: Availability (is the room free?), Rates (what does it cost?) and Inventory (how many are left?). The industry shorthand for the trio is ARI.

Now multiply. A hotel has many room types, many rate plans, and dozens of channels — and the numbers change every few minutes as bookings land. Doing this by hand means a revenue manager logging into forty different extranets to retype prices, a slow and error-strewn ritual. Get it wrong and you either oversell a room you no longer have (an angry guest, a forced walk to another hotel) or leave money on the table with stale, mispriced inventory. That coordination headache — done reliably, instantly, across thousands of hotels and hundreds of channels — is the core problem RateGain was built to solve.

The structural reason this is a good business to be in

Once a hotel wires its property-management system into RateGain's plumbing, ripping it out is painful and risky — every channel connection, every rate rule, every integration would have to be rebuilt, and a mistake means lost bookings. That switching cost is why travel-distribution software enjoys the stickiness it does: RateGain's gross revenue retention has consistently run above 90%, meaning it keeps more than nine of every ten revenue-rupees from existing customers year after year. A business that seldom loses a customer, and adds new ones on top, compounds. The catch — which the rest of this primer keeps returning to — is that a slice of that revenue doesn't just depend on keeping the customer; it depends on how much travelling the world is doing.

Who buys, and what they are buying

  • Hotels & hotel groups — from independents to enterprise chains. The largest customer set, buying distribution, pricing intelligence and marketing tools.
  • Airlines, car rentals, cruises, ferries, package providers — other travel suppliers with the same need to price against rivals and reach travellers.
  • OTAs and metasearch companies — the big travel-selling platforms themselves buy RateGain's data (for example, competitive rates to power their own displays), which is why an OTA can be a customer and a rival at once.
02

The three engines: what RateGain actually sells

Distribution, Rate Intelligence and MarTech — one company, three different machines.

RateGain is best understood not as a single product but as three complementary engines, each attacking a different part of a travel supplier's problem: getting the room seen, priced right, and filled. They were built and bought over two decades, and after the FY26 Sojern deal the third engine has become the largest of the three.

A single hotel can buy one engine or all three — and the more it buys, the deeper it is embedded and the more each customer is worth. Cross-sell across the three engines is the growth flywheel.

Engine 1 — Distribution (the plumbing)

This is RateGain's connectivity backbone, marketed as the UNO channel manager. It links a hotel's inventory to 700-plus demand partners — OTAs, metasearch, wholesalers — plus direct GDS connectivity that reaches over 600,000 travel agents. A single database pushes ARI to every channel at once, processing (the company says) billions of ARI updates a year at near-total uptime, and it has held Booking.com's "Premier Connectivity Partner" status for eight straight years. Distribution is the oldest, most mission-critical, and stickiest engine — but also the slowest-growing, and the one most exposed to the whims of a big channel partner.

Engine 2 — DaaS / Rate Intelligence (the data)

Here RateGain sells data as a service: it continuously scrapes and structures the prices, availability and demand signals of the whole travel market, then sells that back to clients so they can price dynamically. Its Optima platform gathers competitive pricing from hundreds of sources so a hotel or airline can see, live, what everyone else is charging and adjust. This is a classic subscription business with high gross margins — you build the data pipeline once and sell the same intelligence to many buyers — and it is the engine that gives RateGain a genuine, hard-to-replicate data moat.

Engine 3 — MarTech / demand generation (the marketing)

The newest and now-largest engine helps travel brands find and convert travellers. It was assembled by acquisition: BCV (2019, social-media management for hotels), Adara (2023, traveller-intent data), and then the transformational Sojern (2025, digital demand generation and advertising). Together these turn RateGain into a travel-advertising and audience-intelligence platform: it knows, from vast pools of travel-intent data, who is likely about to book a trip, and it places the marketing that captures them. RateGain has folded Adara and Sojern into a single unit under the Sojern brand, pitching it as one of the world's largest sources of travel-intent data.

Why owning all three engines is more than the sum of the parts

Each engine is useful alone, but the real prize is the loop between them. Distribution shows RateGain where rooms are being sold and at what price; Rate Intelligence tells it what the market is charging and where demand is heading; MarTech uses travel-intent signals to generate the bookings that then flow back through distribution. A hotel that buys one engine is a candidate to be sold the next two — and because the underlying data feeds each other, RateGain can credibly claim to help a client across the entire journey from "unknown traveller" to "checked-in guest." That cross-engine data flywheel is the strategic argument for stapling three quite different businesses onto one platform.

How each engine earns: subscription, usage, and the retention math

The money model — and why "recurring" and "travel-linked" are both true at once.

To value RateGain you have to see how the cash actually arrives, because the three engines are paid for in different ways — and the blend of "steady subscription" versus "moves-with-travel-volume" is the single most important thing about the quality of its earnings.

How the three engines are paid

The pattern that emerges is a barbell. At one end, Rate Intelligence is beautifully recurring — data subscriptions renew, margins are high, revenue is predictable. At the other, MarTech (now the biggest engine) is the most exposed to travel-advertising budgets and therefore to the cycle. Distribution sits in between: sticky subscriptions plus a booking-linked tail. RateGain's overall revenue is thus mostly recurring but with a meaningful volume-sensitive component that swells the upside in boom years and stings in bad ones.

The retention number to watch, and why it is the quiet engine of the whole model

A SaaS company lives or dies on whether last year's customers stay and spend more. RateGain reports gross revenue retention consistently above 90% — it rarely loses a customer outright — and its net revenue retention runs higher still when existing clients buy more engines or more volume. That combination, low churn plus expansion, is what lets a business grow without constantly refilling a leaking bucket. But note the asymmetry the segment mix creates: the more the revenue base tilts toward advertising-linked MarTech, the more of that "retention" quietly depends on clients keeping their marketing budgets intact — which, unlike a mission-critical distribution connection, is exactly the line item a nervous hotel trims first when travel softens.

04

The founder, the group, and who controls it

A founder-led company, still run by the man who started it in 2004.

Unlike many listed Indian companies, RateGain is not a family conglomerate or a private-equity roll-up dressed for market — it is a founder-led technology business, still chaired and managed by Bhanu Chopra, who started it in 2004 as a rate-intelligence tool for hotels and built it, engine by engine and acquisition by acquisition, into today's platform. That founder continuity matters: the strategy of buying capabilities (BCV, Adara, Sojern) and integrating them is a single, consistent bet made by the same person over twenty years.

Minority investors are betting that the same founder who bought well and cheaply before has not overpaid — or over-borrowed — for his biggest prize.
05

The Sojern bet: a quarter-billion-dollar reinvention

The single most important thing that happened to this company — and the fact about it that surprises everyone.

For most of its life RateGain was pitched as a capital-light, net-cash, high-margin Indian SaaS exporter. In late 2025 it did something that looked out of character: it spent $250 million (~₹2,223 Cr) to buy Sojern, a US demand-generation company larger, by revenue, than RateGain's entire MarTech engine — and it borrowed to do it, flipping the company from a net-cash to a net-debt position for the first time. To understand RateGain today, you have to understand this one decision.

Sojern, founded in 2007, is a US travel-advertising and audience-intelligence business that serves some 13,000 travel and hospitality customers and turned over about $172 million in 2024. It does the "get it filled" job — using vast pools of travel-intent data to find and convert travellers — at a scale RateGain's own MarTech engine could not match organically. Buying it did three things at once: it roughly doubled RateGain's revenue base, it made MarTech/demand-generation the dominant engine of the combined company, and it gave RateGain a far deeper foothold in the Americas. Management's stated ambition for the combined group is north of $310 million in revenue and over $50 million in EBITDA.

The genuine surprise: revenue up 69%, reported profit down

Here is the fact that trips up nearly everyone reading RateGain's FY26 numbers cold. The company grew revenue by about 69% — a headline any growth investor would kill for — and yet its reported net profit went down, from roughly ₹209 Cr to ~₹194 Cr. That is not a contradiction; it is the visible cost of the Sojern bet. Acquiring and folding in a company of that size brings one-off integration expenses, higher interest on the new debt, and the amortisation of what was bought — all of which land on the P&L before the synergies show up. Strip those out and adjusted PAT rose about 20% to ~₹250 Cr. So FY26 is a year with two fair readings: a company transformed and scaled, and a company whose bottom line temporarily shrank while it digested its biggest meal. Which reading proves right depends entirely on whether Sojern earns its keep from FY27 onward.

Why this reframes what RateGain even is: before Sojern you could describe RateGain as a data-and-distribution SaaS firm that also did some marketing. After Sojern, more than half the revenue is marketing technology and travel advertising — a business with a different rhythm, more exposed to advertising budgets and the travel cycle, and carried on a balance sheet that now has debt on it. The bull sees a bolder, bigger, more complete platform. The bear sees a high-multiple SaaS story that used leverage to buy a lower-multiple ad business, and now has to prove the two halves are worth more together than apart. Both are watching the same thing: the integration.
06

A dollar business run in rupees: the global base and FX

Where the customers are, why that is a strength, and the currency twist that comes with it.

RateGain is an unusual listed-in-India company: it reports in rupees but earns almost everywhere except India. Its customers are concentrated in the Americas and Europe — the Americas is its single largest region, and Europe alone contributed around a third of revenue in recent years — with the balance spread across the Middle East, Africa and Asia Pacific. India is a small slice of sales. After the Sojern acquisition, the US weight increases further still.

This global footprint is a genuine competitive strength. Travel is a worldwide industry, and a hotel-tech vendor that can serve a chain across dozens of countries, in local channels and currencies, is far more valuable than a domestic point-solution. It also means RateGain competes for the world's best travel customers directly against the biggest global players, and wins a good share of them — a real validation of the product built out of India at a fraction of Western engineering cost.

The currency twist a first-timer should price in

Because RateGain earns in dollars, euros and pounds but reports and is valued in rupees, its results carry a foreign-exchange overlay that has nothing to do with how the business performed. A weakening rupee flatters reported revenue and margins even in a flat operating quarter; a strengthening rupee does the reverse. Over the long run this tends to be a mild tailwind — the rupee generally drifts weaker against the dollar — but in any single period it can add or subtract growth that isn't "real." When you read RateGain's rupee numbers, the cleaner signal is the constant-currency growth the company also discloses: it strips out the FX noise and shows how the underlying business actually moved. Judge the operating story on constant currency; let the rupee flatter or flatten the reported line as it will.

07

The value equation: subscriptions × travel × cross-sell

The one construct that captures how RateGain actually compounds — and where it is fragile.

Strip away the product names and RateGain's economic value can be written as a single product of three terms. It is worth memorising, because every quarter's result is really a report card on these three numbers moving:

Value ≈ (recurring software base) × (how much travel is actually happening) × (engines sold per customer)

Take them one at a time. The recurring software base is the sticky, subscription core — the data feeds and the mission-critical distribution connections that renew above 90% each year. This is the floor, the part that survives a bad travel year. The travel-volume multiplier is the cyclical amplifier — the booking-linked distribution fees and, above all, the advertising-linked MarTech revenue that swell when the world travels and shrink when it doesn't. And engines-per-customer is the growth flywheel — every hotel that buys distribution is a candidate for rate intelligence and demand generation too, and each additional engine both raises the revenue per customer and deepens the switching cost.

The floor keeps RateGain solvent and sticky through a downturn; the travel-volume amplifier decides whether a given year is spectacular or merely solid; the flywheel is what turns a one-engine customer into a three-engine one and drives the long-run compounding. All three are RateGain's to work on — but only two are truly within its control, because no software vendor can conjure travel demand out of a grounded fleet or an empty lobby.

The construct that fits only RateGain: what would survive a name-swap, and what wouldn't

Most SaaS companies you could describe with "sticky subscriptions and cross-sell" — that half of the equation is generic. What is not transferable is the middle term. RateGain's revenue is uniquely bolted to the physical fact of people getting on planes and into hotel rooms: its distribution fees scale with bookings, and its now-largest engine, MarTech, scales with how much hotels and airlines choose to spend advertising those rooms and seats. A software company selling to banks or factories has no such term. That is why RateGain can post a stellar year when travel is booming and marketing budgets are fat, and a flat one when a pandemic, a recession or a shock empties the lobbies — the same product, the same customers, but the travel-volume multiplier collapses. The equation is the business: a resilient recurring floor, a cyclical travel-linked amplifier, and a cross-sell flywheel — and the whole investment debate is which of those three terms is doing the work in any given year.

08

The industry and the rivals

A fragmented travel-tech landscape where RateGain competes engine-by-engine against very different giants.

Because RateGain spans three engines, it has no single competitor — it faces a different set of rivals in each. That is both a strength (few peers do all three) and a challenge (each engine is contested by specialists, some of them enormous). The competitive picture also contains an unusual tension: some of RateGain's biggest customers, the OTAs, are also the players most able to build this technology in-house.

The competitive shape to hold in mind — and its hidden dependency

RateGain's defensible position is breadth built cheaply: few rivals span distribution, data and demand generation, and India-based engineering lets it undercut Western specialists on cost while serving a global base. But two vulnerabilities sit inside the picture. First, the demand-generation engine it just made its largest now competes against Google and Meta — an ocean of ad money where RateGain is a minnow, not the category leader it is in hotel connectivity. Second, and more subtle, is the OTA dependency: a handful of giant booking platforms are simultaneously RateGain's channels, customers and biggest potential competitors, and a single large OTA account winding down (as one was, dragging on distribution growth) can dent a whole segment. Breadth is the moat; concentration among a few powerful counterparties is the crack in it.

The honest inventory of what could break the case — worth reading before the financials.

The dependency that stacks the risks together

Read the stack and one thread runs through it: the same event — a travel downturn — makes several risks fire at once. A slump doesn't just shrink the travel-volume multiplier; it prompts hotels to cut the marketing spend that feeds the newly-dominant MarTech engine, it pressures the cash flow that is supposed to repay the Sojern debt, and it does all this while the stock carries a growth multiple that assumes none of it. In a boom the three engines and the leverage all pull in RateGain's favour; in a genuine travel shock they can pull the other way together. That correlation — not any single line in the table — is the real risk to size.

10

Financials & valuation

A fast-compounding SaaS record, a transformative acquisition year, and an expensive price — the question is durability of margin, not richness of the multiple.

RateGain's five-year record is the kind that earns a premium rating: revenue has grown from ~₹367 Cr in FY22 to ~₹1,824 Cr in FY26, with operating margins climbing from single digits to the low-twenties before the Sojern deal diluted them back. The market prices it accordingly — around 50× earnings, a full growth-SaaS multiple, on a market capitalisation near ₹11,400 Cr. At that price the debate is not whether the business is good; it is whether the combined RateGain-plus-Sojern can rebuild its margin and keep compounding fast enough to justify the rating.

At ~50× earnings, RateGain's price already assumes years of fast, profitable growth — so the metric that proves or breaks the case is not revenue (Sojern flatters that) and not the multiple (it can only compress). It is organic, constant-currency revenue growth — the underlying business ex-acquisitions and ex-FX — sustained in double digits, read alongside the EBITDA margin rebuilding toward the low-twenties as Sojern is integrated. Track those two together, against the >90% gross retention that shows the base is intact and against the raw fact of travel volume that drives the cyclical half. If organic growth stays strong and margin recovers as the debt is repaid, the rich multiple is earned and earnings compound into it. If organic growth slips into single digits, or margin stays stuck in the high-teens because ad-linked revenue is structurally thinner, the price has to come to the business rather than the business growing into the price. Underlying growth times recovering margin, minus what the world's travel appetite gives or takes — that is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A fast-growing global travel-SaaS platform mid-way through its biggest bet — asked whether the bet pays.

RateGain enters FY27 as a bigger, bolder company than it was a year earlier: three engines instead of a scattered product set, a doubled revenue base, a far larger US presence, and — for the first time — debt to work off. Its founder is still at the helm, its retention is intact, and travel is, structurally, a growing global industry. What it must now prove is that the Sojern bet compounds rather than merely enlarges. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Complete platform — after Sojern, RateGain spans the whole "seen, priced, filled" journey; few rivals offer all three engines, and cross-sell deepens every customer.
  • Sticky, global, recurring — >90% gross retention, 100+ countries, a dollar-earning base built cheaply out of India; the recurring core is a genuine floor.
  • Structural travel tailwind — global travel and travel-digitisation grow for years; RateGain's volume multiplier works powerfully in its favour in the good times.
  • Synergy & deleveraging — the combined group targets >$310m revenue and >$50m EBITDA, with debt repaid to net cash by ~FY28 if cash flow holds.
  • Founder alignment — a founder-CMD who has compounded the business for two decades and is buying more stock personally.

The bear case

  • Expensive, unforgiving multiple — ~50× earnings assumes years of fast, profitable growth; any stumble in growth or integration re-rates the stock hard.
  • Cyclical, ad-heavy tilt — the now-dominant MarTech engine rides advertising budgets that hotels cut first in any downturn; earnings quality softened, not hardened, with Sojern.
  • Integration & leverage risk — a $250m acquisition to digest while carrying debt; a travel shock mid-integration is the dangerous scenario.
  • Giants & concentration — competing for ad money against Google/Meta, and reliant on a few powerful OTAs that are customers, data-buyers and potential in-housers at once.
  • Reported profit already dipped — FY26's revenue leap came with a fall in reported PAT; the synergy has to show up before the rating is vindicated.
The bottom line

RateGain is a rare thing — a genuinely global software platform built out of India, embedded in the plumbing and pricing of the world's travel industry, with a sticky recurring core and a founder who has compounded it patiently for twenty years. In FY26 that founder made his largest and boldest bet, spending a quarter-billion dollars and taking on debt to turn RateGain into a demand-generation heavyweight — a move that doubled the company and, for one year, shrank its reported profit. The recurring floor and the retention are real; the travel tailwind is real; the price assumes both keep delivering. The next test is concrete and datable: whether, through FY27 and FY28, organic constant-currency growth holds in double digits and the EBITDA margin climbs back toward the low-twenties as Sojern is absorbed and the acquisition debt is repaid. Deliver that, and a bigger, more complete RateGain grows into a rating that looks demanding today; miss it — on integration, on margin, or on a travel wobble — and FY26 will read as the year the company got larger faster than it got better.
