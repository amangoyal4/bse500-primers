00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Godfrey Phillips India (GPI) is a cigarette company — India's second-largest by volume, after ITC. It makes and sells home-grown brands like Four Square, Red & White, Cavenders and Stellar, and — crucially — it is the licensed manufacturer and distributor of Marlboro in India under an agreement with Philip Morris International (PMI), which also owns roughly a quarter of the company. GPI belongs to the Modi Enterprises / K.K. Modi group, one of India's older business families.

Four facts frame everything:

  • It's a cash machine with pricing power. Cigarettes are addictive, habitual, repeat-purchase products with strong brand loyalty. In FY26 GPI grew domestic cigarette volumes ~20% — far above ITC — lifted net profit ~32% to ~₹1,526 Cr, and earns a ~33% return on capital (ROCE) on a near debt-free balance sheet. Reported gross sales value (including all the taxes it collects for the government) was ~₹18,379 Cr.
  • The government is its silent, dominant partner. Cigarettes carry a punishing stack of 40% GST plus a large per-stick excise/cess — legal cigarettes are barely ~10% of India's tobacco consumption but pay ~80% of tobacco taxes. Every Union Budget is a heart-in-mouth event for the sector.
  • "What else is there?" got smaller. GPI exited its 24Seven convenience-store chain (shut in 2024, sold to start-up New Shop), leaving a much simpler company: cigarettes at home, a sizeable unmanufactured-tobacco export business (~₹1,945 Cr, ~21% of net sales), a small confectionery/other line, and a large treasury/investment book (~₹3,300 Cr).
  • Governance is the overhang. A long-running, very public Modi family control battle (Bina Modi vs her son Samir Modi) hangs over the stock, on top of the permanent "sin stock" ESG discount that attaches to any tobacco name. Yet the stock has been one of the market's big winners — up several-fold in five years.
In plain terms

Think of GPI as the clear #2 in a two-horse, heavily-taxed, quasi-legal-monopoly industry. ITC is the elephant; GPI is the fast-growing challenger that also happens to hold the keys to Marlboro, the world's most valuable cigarette brand, inside India. The business itself is wonderful — customers come back every day, brands are sticky, and the balance sheet is stuffed with cash. The complications are all around the business: a government that taxes it harder each year, a huge illegal-cigarette market undercutting it, a "should I even own this?" ESG stigma, and a feuding promoter family fighting in court over who runs the show. That tension — great business, ugly wrapper — is the whole story.

01

Cigarette economics 101: how a tobacco company earns

No finance degree required — just why this is one of the best business models ever taxed to the hilt.

A cigarette business is, at its heart, an extraordinarily good consumer franchise wrapped inside an extraordinarily heavy tax. Strip away the regulation and you have a product that is cheap to make, addictive to consume, bought daily out of habit, and sold under brands customers rarely switch. Understand five ideas and you understand the whole economic engine.

The five levers that drive a cigarette maker
IdeaWhat it means (and why it matters for GPI)
Repeat & addictive demandNicotine creates a habitual, near-daily purchase. Demand is inelastic — it barely falls when prices rise — which is exactly why governments tax it so heavily and why makers keep pricing power.
Volume × mixEarnings grow by selling more sticks (volume) and by shifting smokers to pricier, longer, filter/premium variants (mix). GPI grew domestic volumes ~20% in FY26 — unusually fast for the industry.
Pricing power vs taxMakers raise prices to protect margins and pass on tax — but every rupee of price also nudges some smokers toward cheaper illegal sticks. The art is raising price enough to cover tax, not so much that volumes leak away.
Distribution moatCigarettes reach millions of tiny paan shops and kiosks. A dense, decades-old distribution network is a real barrier — you can't sell a cigarette brand nationally without it.
Low capital intensityMaking cigarettes needs little ongoing capital, so the business throws off huge free cash flow and earns very high returns on capital. The cash then piles up (see the treasury book in §07).
The tax sandwich — who really gets the money

Here is the single most important thing to grasp. When you buy a pack, the lion's share of the price is tax, collected by the maker and handed to the government. That's why GPI reports two very different "revenue" numbers: a Gross Sales Value of ~₹18,379 Cr (what customers paid, taxes included) versus net revenue of ~₹6,391 Cr (what GPI keeps after excise/levies). The gap — roughly ₹12,000 Cr — is essentially the government's cut. So a cigarette company is partly a tax-collection agency that keeps a slice. The slice is still very profitable because the underlying product costs so little to make — but it means the state, not the smoker, is the company's biggest financial counterparty.

Why the model is both loved and loathed by investors

  • Loved: predictable, cash-gushing, high-return, recession-proof demand, genuine pricing power, and a distribution moat that keeps new entrants out.
  • Loathed: a shrinking-by-design social licence, relentless tax risk, ESG exclusion by many big funds, and a product whose long-term volume ceiling is capped by public-health policy. The valuation you pay reflects that tug-of-war.
02

The company and who controls it

A colonial-era tobacco name, an Indian business dynasty, and an American partner.

Godfrey Phillips traces its roots to a 19th-century London tobacco house and set up in India decades ago. Its modern identity, though, is that of a Modi-family company: the late K.K. Modi built it into the country's #2 cigarette maker, in a long partnership with the American giant that became Philip Morris International. Today control rests with the Modi family and their trust, alongside PMI's ~25% stake.

Timeline

  • 1844 / early 1900s
    The Godfrey Phillips tobacco name originates in London; the Indian company is established and grows a home-grown cigarette portfolio (Four Square, Red & White).
  • 1960s–1980s
    The K.K. Modi (Modi Enterprises) group takes the helm; Philip Morris becomes a long-standing technology and equity partner. GPI cements its place as India's clear #2 cigarette maker behind ITC.
  • 2000s
    GPI begins manufacturing and distributing Marlboro in India under licence from Philip Morris International — bringing the world's best-known cigarette brand into its stable.
  • 2011
    Ownership is restructured: Modi Enterprises takes majority control; PMI's direct stake steps down (to ~21% at the time), while the Marlboro licence and a wholesale JV keep the two sides tightly linked.
  • 2019
    K.K. Modi dies. A ferocious succession dispute erupts among the heirs — widow Bina Modi and sons Samir and Lalit — over an estate reported at ₹11,000–30,000 Cr. The battle for control of GPI begins.
  • 2024
    GPI exits its 24Seven retail chain (~145 stores shut, sold to start-up New Shop). Samir Modi is removed from the board; daughter Charu Modi is appointed director.
  • FY25–FY26
    A blockbuster stretch: ~20% domestic cigarette volume growth, record profits, a 2:1 bonus issue (2025), and the stock hitting all-time highs before pulling back. Bina Modi re-elected Managing Director with ~87% shareholder support.

Ownership & control (mid-2026)

  • Promoters ~72.6% — the controlling block. Within it, the K.K. Modi family & their trust hold ~47% and Philip Morris (PMI) holds ~25% — so the American partner is embedded inside the promoter group, not a mere outside investor.
  • Public / institutions ~27% — FIIs ~8%, DIIs ~3%, retail and other public ~16%. A relatively thin free float, which amplifies price moves.
  • Management: Bina Modi is Chairperson & Managing Director; daughter Charu Modi is an executive director. Son Samir Modi — once seen as a successor and the driving force behind 24Seven — has been ousted from the board and is litigating.
Why the ownership structure matters: two outside forces sit inside GPI's control room. PMI's ~25% stake and the Marlboro licence make the world's largest cigarette multinational both a shareholder and a supplier — a source of strength and dependency at once (§04). And the Modi family's internal war (§08) means the ~47% family block is itself contested, which is the governance cloud over everything else.

The brand ladder

How GPI covers the smoker from the value end to the premium tip.

A cigarette company grows by owning a brand at every price point and then nudging smokers up the ladder as incomes rise. GPI's portfolio spans the mass/value segment through the mid-market to a genuinely premium top — capped by Marlboro. Getting a smoker to "trade up" one rung is one of the most reliable ways to grow profit without selling a single extra stick.

Why "premiumisation" is the growth story

India's smokers are gradually moving from cheap, unbranded or value sticks toward branded, filtered, longer, premium cigarettes as incomes rise. Each step up the ladder earns GPI more margin per stick. GPI's fast recent growth has come from a mix of share gains from ITC in certain segments, aggressive expansion of premium own-brands like Stellar, and the halo of Marlboro at the top. The catch: the pricier the stick, the closer its price gets to a smuggled illegal one — so premiumisation and the illicit-market problem (§06) are two sides of the same coin.

04

Marlboro & the Philip Morris relationship

The single most distinctive — and most double-edged — feature of GPI.

No other Indian cigarette maker holds what GPI holds: the right to make and sell Marlboro, the world's most valuable cigarette brand, inside the world's second-largest population. That licence, plus Philip Morris International's ~25% shareholding, is a genuine competitive asset — and, in the same breath, a genuine dependency the market never fully forgets.

How the relationship is wired

  • The licence. GPI manufactures and distributes Marlboro in India under a licence agreement with PMI. GPI runs the factories and the distribution; PMI owns the brand. GPI earns the margin on Indian Marlboro sales, but only for as long as the licence runs — it does not own the brand it profits from.
  • The equity. PMI holds ~25% of GPI, sitting inside the promoter group. So the global tobacco leader is simultaneously GPI's brand-licensor and one of its largest owners — deeply aligned, but also deeply influential.
  • The wholesale JV. A separate joint venture, IPM India Wholesale Trading, is co-owned by Philip Morris (~50%), the K.K. Modi group and GPI — the vehicle through which the international brand is taken to market.

The value — and the risk — in one idea

The value: Marlboro gives GPI an unbeatable premium anchor, global marketing know-how, and a technology/quality relationship with the world's largest cigarette company. It's a moat ITC can't replicate and VST can't dream of. The risk: the crown jewel is borrowed. The licence is a contract that must be renewed, on terms PMI can influence, and PMI's own global strategy is pivoting hard toward smoke-free products (IQOS, heated tobacco) that it has historically kept outside the GPI relationship. If PMI ever changed the licence terms, declined to renew, or restructured its India approach, a slice of GPI's premium franchise and prestige would be at stake. This dependency is a permanent, if low-probability, tail risk — and one reason the market applies a discount even to GPI's best asset.

The strategic question: is PMI a loyal partner or a landlord who could one day raise the rent? For now the alignment is real — PMI owns a quarter of GPI, so it profits when GPI profits. But investors should treat the Marlboro licence as a valuable asset with a counterparty, not a permanent birthright, and watch any news on licence renewal or PMI's India intentions closely.
05

The industry and the rivals

A near-duopoly at the top of a tightly-controlled market.

India's legal cigarette industry is concentrated, regulated and effectively closed to newcomers — advertising is banned, distribution is entrenched, and taxes are enormous. At the top it looks like a lopsided contest: ITC is the dominant #1 with roughly three-quarters of the legal market, and everyone else fights over the rest. GPI is the clear #2; VST Industries and Golden Tobacco trail well behind.

Why #2 is a genuinely good place to be here

In most industries, being a distant second to a 75%-share leader is uncomfortable. In cigarettes it can be excellent. The market is closed to new entrants (no advertising, entrenched distribution, no appetite for new tobacco licences), so the competitive set is frozen. That means GPI faces essentially one serious legal rival (ITC) and a fragmented tail — and it has been taking volume share and growing faster than the leader. A stable duopoly-plus structure, inelastic demand, and a challenger that's gaining ground is a rare combination. The threat isn't a new competitor; it's the government and the smugglers.

06

Tax and the illicit-cigarette problem

The permanent overhang every Budget re-lights — and the shadow market it feeds.

If you own a cigarette stock, you own a tax story. Cigarettes in India carry one of the heaviest tax loads of any consumer product on earth, and it ratchets up almost every year. That does two things at once: it hands the state most of the revenue, and — perversely — it fuels a massive illegal-cigarette market that steals volume from the very legal companies the tax is aimed at.

The tax stack

  • 40% GST on cigarettes — the top slab, reserved for "sin" goods.
  • Plus a large per-1,000-stick excise / cess on top — the 2025 changes set basic excise in a band of roughly ₹2,050–₹8,500 per 1,000 sticks depending on length, and India is moving toward a new health/national-security cess framework to replace the GST compensation cess.
  • Result: tax is the majority of a pack's price. Legal cigarettes are ~10% of tobacco consumed but ~80% of tobacco tax collected — the government's most efficient tobacco revenue source, and thus a perennial target for "just one more" hike.
Budget-day risk is structural, not occasional. Cigarette stocks — ITC, GPI, VST — routinely swing several percent on Budget days and on any tax-hike proposal (as they did around the 2025–26 excise increases and the 40% GST/sin-tax discussions). This is not noise you can wish away; it is a permanent feature of owning the sector. The bet a shareholder makes is that pricing power outlasts the tax — that GPI can keep raising prices enough to protect margins without losing too much volume.

The illicit market — the tax's own worst enemy

Here is the paradox at the centre of Indian tobacco policy. The higher the tax, the wider the price gap between a legal pack and a smuggled or duty-evaded one — and the more smokers defect to the illegal product. The numbers are startling:

Why this cuts both ways for GPI

The illicit market is a drag — every smuggled stick is a legal sale GPI (or ITC) didn't make, and it caps how far legal players can push prices. But it's also, oddly, part of the bull case: because roughly a quarter of the market is illegal, there's a large pool of volume that could return to the legal industry if taxes ever stabilised or enforcement tightened. GPI's own recent ~20% volume growth suggests it has been winning back and gaining share in the legal market even in a punishing tax environment. The industry's constant lobbying line — "stop raising taxes; you're just feeding smugglers and losing revenue" — is self-interested but not wrong.

07

Beyond cigarettes: what else is GPI?

After the 24Seven exit, a simpler company — and a big pile of cash.

For years the bull-and-bear debate on GPI included a fourth character: its 24Seven convenience-store chain, a rare Indian attempt at branded, always-open urban retail. That experiment is over. What remains beyond the cigarette cash cow is a sizeable tobacco-export arm, a small confectionery/other line, and a large treasury book — giving GPI a faint holding-company flavour.

1 · The 24Seven exit — the retail dream that ran out of road

  • What it was: ~145 24Seven convenience stores across Delhi-NCR, Punjab and Hyderabad — many open 24 hours — championed by Samir Modi as GPI's consumer-facing future.
  • Why it died: chronic losses, capital tied up in leases and inventory, and then the quick-commerce wave (Blinkit, Zepto, Swiggy Instamart) that delivered the midnight-craving purchase to the doorstep in minutes and destroyed the convenience-store economics.
  • How it ended: GPI announced the exit in April 2024, shut the stores, and sold the business to start-up New Shop (deal finalised late 2024). The move simplified the company and stopped a cash drain — a decision investors broadly welcomed, even as it was entangled in the family feud.

2 · Unmanufactured tobacco exports — the quiet second business

GPI is a real player in the global leaf-tobacco trade: it buys, processes and exports unmanufactured tobacco to buyers in 30–35 countries. In FY26 these exports were about ₹1,945 Cr — roughly 21% of net sales (international sales overall ~25%). It's a lower-margin, more commoditised business than branded cigarettes, but it's a genuine, dollar-earning second leg that diversifies GPI away from pure domestic tobacco taxation.

3 · Confectionery & other FMCG — small and non-core

GPI has dabbled in confectionery, chewing products and other consumer lines over the years. These remain small relative to cigarettes and are not the profit engine; think of them as legacy/optional bets rather than a second pillar.

4 · The treasury / investment book — cash on cash

Because cigarettes generate far more cash than the business needs to reinvest, GPI has accumulated a large investment/treasury book — on the order of ₹3,300 Cr in financial investments, against negligible net debt (borrowings ~₹234 Cr). This is what gives the company a mild holding-company character: a cash-gushing operating business with a substantial financial-assets pile sitting on the side of the balance sheet.

The sum-of-parts angle — and its limits

Some investors like to value GPI as "cigarette business + a bag of cash and investments." With a ~₹3,300 Cr treasury book and a near debt-free balance sheet, a chunk of the market value is backed by financial assets rather than operating goodwill — historically this made the stock look cheap on a "core cigarette P/E" basis, and it cushions downside. But be careful: since the 2024–26 re-rating, GPI's market cap (~₹32,500 Cr) is now many times its investment book, so the "you're getting the cigarette business for free" argument that once applied no longer does. The cash pile is a comfort and a capital-allocation question (buybacks? dividends? another retail adventure?), not a hidden free lottery ticket.

08

The Modi family control battle

A public, bitter succession war — the governance cloud that won't lift.

You cannot understand GPI as an investment without understanding that its controlling family is at war with itself. Since the death of patriarch K.K. Modi in 2019, his heirs have fought — in boardrooms, courtrooms and the media — over who controls a group and a company worth many thousands of crore. This is not gossip; it is a live governance overhang that the market prices into the stock.

The cast and the conflict

  • Bina Modi — K.K. Modi's widow, now Chairperson & Managing Director of GPI and head of the family trust. She has consolidated control, winning re-election as MD with ~87% shareholder support.
  • Samir Modi — son, once the group's consumer-facing entrepreneur (the force behind 24Seven and other ventures). He has been removed from the GPI board (August 2024, when directors declined to reappoint him) and is embroiled in litigation — and, separately, in serious personal legal troubles reported in the press.
  • Charu Modi — daughter, appointed executive director in Samir's place, aligned with Bina.
  • Lalit Modi — the more famous (IPL-founder) son, a party to the broader estate dispute.
  • The prize: control of the K.K. Modi estate, reported at anywhere from ₹11,000 Cr to ₹30,000 Cr, of which GPI is the crown jewel.

Why a family feud is a real investment risk

In a promoter-controlled company, the controlling family is the governance. When that family is fighting, several concrete risks follow: (1) distraction — management attention and board energy consumed by litigation instead of the business; (2) unpredictable control — the outcome of court cases could reshape who runs GPI; (3) capital-allocation & related-party worries — minority shareholders must trust that decisions (dividends, the treasury book, related entities like the PMI JV) are made in the company's interest, not one faction's; and (4) headline risk — lurid personal and legal news around family members can hit sentiment regardless of the underlying numbers. The counter-point: the operating results through the feud have been excellent, and Bina's camp now has a firm grip — so the market has, for now, chosen to look past it. But the discount is real, and any escalation is a genuine downside catalyst.

The honest read: GPI is a case where the business quality and the governance quality point in opposite directions. Volumes and profits say "buy"; the family war and tobacco stigma say "be careful what you're getting into." Neither cancels the other — they coexist, and the share price is the running scoreboard of which one investors weight more on a given day.

Financials & valuation

A cash machine, firing on all cylinders — at a re-rated (but still tobacco-discounted) price.

How to think about the ~21× multiple

At ~₹32,500 Cr on ~₹1,526 Cr of profit, GPI trades around 21× trailing earnings. That's a striking place to sit: above slow-growing VST, roughly in line with or below ITC on some measures, and at a clear discount to the 40–60× multiples of premium consumer-staples names with similar returns on capital. Why the discount despite faster growth than ITC? Three reasons, all covered in this primer: the tobacco/ESG stigma (§10) that keeps many funds out, the tax/regulatory risk (§06) that caps how much certainty the market will pay for, and the Modi family governance overhang (§08). The bull says: a #2 cigarette maker growing volumes ~20%, with Marlboro, net cash and ~33% ROCE, is too cheap at 21×. The bear says: those discounts exist for good reasons and won't fully close. The stock's wild 52-week range shows how violently the market oscillates between the two views.

10

The sin-stock discount

Why a cash machine trades cheaper than its cash flows deserve — and whether that ever changes.

Tobacco companies live under a permanent valuation cloud that has nothing to do with their financials: the "sin stock" discount. A large and growing pool of global capital — ESG funds, pension mandates, ethical screens — simply will not own a cigarette maker, no matter how good the numbers. Fewer eligible buyers means a structurally lower multiple. For GPI, this ESG discount stacks on top of the tax and governance discounts, and it is unlikely to disappear.

The forces holding the multiple down

  • ESG exclusion. Many institutional investors are contractually barred from tobacco. That permanently shrinks GPI's buyer base and its multiple, regardless of growth or returns.
  • Volume ceiling by design. Public-health policy wants cigarette volumes to fall over the long run. A business whose social licence is to slowly shrink can't command a growth multiple forever — even if GPI is gaining share today.
  • Tax unpredictability. You can't confidently model earnings when a Budget can lift the tax by double digits overnight (§06). Uncertainty = discount.
  • Governance overhang. The Modi family feud (§08) is a company-specific extra discount most peers don't carry.

The forces that can lift it anyway

  • Cash returns. Tobacco's answer to a low multiple is to pay it back in cash — dividends and buybacks. GPI already pays a healthy dividend and sits on a large treasury book that could fund more.
  • Share gains & premiumisation. ~20% volume growth and a premiumising mix can grow earnings fast enough that even a flat multiple delivers strong returns — which is exactly what happened during the 2024–26 re-rating.
  • Illicit-market reversion. If tax policy ever stabilises and enforcement improves, legal players like GPI could reclaim a chunk of the ~1/4 of the market that's currently illegal.
  • The Marlboro halo. A unique premium asset (§04) supports a modest premium over a pure-play like VST.
The honest verdict on the discount

Don't expect the sin-stock discount to vanish — it is structural, and betting on ESG funds suddenly embracing cigarettes is a poor thesis. The realistic bull case is narrower and sturdier: a heavily-taxed, ESG-shunned, family-controlled cigarette maker can still be a very good investment if it keeps growing volumes, defends margins against tax, and returns cash — because you're buying durable cash flows cheaply and being paid to wait. The discount is the price of admission, not necessarily a value trap. The risk is that one of the overhangs — a punitive tax, a governance blow-up, or a Marlboro licence problem — turns the discount into a justified permanent haircut. This is a stock for eyes-open investors comfortable holding a "sin" asset, not for anyone who needs the story to be clean.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A wonderful business in an ugly wrapper — the whole debate in one panel.

GPI is one of the more interesting risk/reward puzzles in the Indian market: a genuinely excellent cigarette franchise — fast-growing, cash-rich, Marlboro-armed, #2 in a closed duopoly — carrying an unusually heavy load of tax, ESG and governance baggage. The debate isn't about business quality; it's about whether the price adequately pays you for the baggage.

The bull case

  • Best-in-class growth — ~20% domestic cigarette volume growth, far outpacing ITC; premiumisation and share gains compounding earnings.
  • Marlboro & PMI — a unique super-premium licence and a global partner that owns ~25% and is aligned with GPI's success.
  • Fortress balance sheet — net cash, ~₹3,300 Cr treasury book, ~33% ROCE, capital-light cash machine.
  • Cheap for the quality — ~21× earnings, a clear discount to premium FMCG with similar returns; healthy dividend, room for buybacks.
  • Simpler post-24Seven — the cash-draining retail experiment is gone; the story is cleaner.

The bear case

  • Tax at the mercy of every Budget — 40% GST plus rising excise/cess; earnings can be hit overnight by policy.
  • Governance overhang — the Modi family control battle is unresolved and can flare into headline and control risk.
  • Marlboro is borrowed — the crown jewel depends on a PMI licence GPI doesn't own and must renew.
  • Sin-stock/ESG discount — a structurally shrunken buyer base and a socially-shrinking product; long-run volume ceiling.
  • Illicit market & volatility — ~1/4 of the market is illegal and undercuts legal players; the stock's huge 52-week range shows how skittish sentiment is.
The bottom line

Godfrey Phillips is a high-quality, fast-growing #2 cigarette maker that happens to sit inside three overlapping discounts — tax, ESG and family governance. The operating business has rarely looked better: volumes up ~20%, profit up ~32%, net cash, Marlboro in the stable, and 24Seven's drag gone. The reasons it's cheap are also real and permanent: the government can tax it harder any year, a chunk of the world won't touch a tobacco stock, and the controlling family is fighting in court. Owning GPI is a bet that durable, growing cash flows bought at a discount beat a clean story bought at a premium — with your eyes fully open to the "sin", the tax, and the feud. Watch three things each year: the Budget (how hard is tax rising?), volume growth (is GPI still gaining share?), and the family/court news flow (is the governance cloud lifting or darkening?).
