00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Usha Martin makes steel wire ropes — the thick, engineered steel cables that do the heavy, dangerous lifting of the industrial world. When an elevator carries you up a tower, when a crane swings a container onto a ship, when a cage of miners is lowered a kilometre down a shaft, when an oil rig is moored against the North Sea, or when a cable-car crosses a valley, there is a Usha Martin-type rope taking the load. It is one of the largest wire-rope manufacturers on earth, run from Ranchi and Kolkata by the Jhawar family, and it sells to thousands of customers across dozens of countries. Alongside rope it makes specialty wire, LRPC strand (the pre-stressing cable inside concrete bridges and flyovers), and downstream wire products and end-fittings.

Three facts frame everything:

  • These ropes are engineered products, not commodity steel. A mining hoist rope or an offshore mooring line is certified, application-specific and safety-critical — designed for a job where failure is not an option. That is why Usha Martin competes on engineering, quality and global certification, not price, and earns a materially better margin than the steel inside the rope would suggest. FY26 operating EBITDA margin was ~19.1% — a world away from commodity steel-making economics.
  • It sold its steel mill and became a different company. Until 2019 Usha Martin ran a big, debt-laden specialty-steel business that made the raw wire rod — and nearly sank under the borrowing it took to build it. It sold that steel business to Tata Steel and used the proceeds to erase its debt. What remained is a focused, cash-rich, high-margin pure-play wire-rope specialist. FY26 ended with ~₹332 Cr net cash — debt-free — versus net debt as recently as FY25.
  • It is really a global exporter that happens to be Indian. Roughly 57% of FY26 revenue came from outside India, sold through a worldwide web of distribution and service branches. Revenue rose ~6.2% to ~₹3,691 Cr; PAT rose ~21% to ~₹491 Cr — profit growing three times faster than the top line as the mix shifts to higher-value rope. The stock trades near 32× earnings on a ~₹13,500 Cr market cap.
In plain terms

Usha Martin is a specialty-engineering exporter wearing a steel company's overalls. The product looks like a coil of steel cable, so the instinct is to value it like a steel firm — cyclical, thin-margin, capital-hungry. But the rope that lifts a mine cage is a certified, life-safety component that a customer will not switch on price, and the buyer keeps coming back for replacements every few years as ropes wear out. The real story here is a company that spent the 2010s nearly buried by the steel-making half of its business, sold that half to Tata, paid off the debt with the cash, and was left holding the good half: a globally certified, replacement-cycle rope franchise that now throws off free cash instead of consuming it.

01

Wire ropes 101: why a cable is not just steel

No engineering degree needed — just the handful of ideas that make this a good business.

Start with the object itself. A wire rope is not one steel bar — it is a precisely engineered assembly: many thin steel wires are twisted into strands, and several strands are laid helically around a core to form the finished rope. That geometry is the whole point. By choosing the wire grade, the number of wires, the lay pattern, the coating and the core, an engineer can tune a rope for a specific job — enormous crushing loads for a mine hoist, corrosion resistance for a seawater mooring, flexibility and fatigue life for an elevator that starts and stops a thousand times a day. Two ropes of identical weight can be completely different products at completely different prices.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Wire rodThe raw input: a coil of plain steel a few millimetres thick. This is the commodity Usha Martin buys (it used to make its own — see §04). The rod is the cost; everything after it is where the value is added.
WireRod drawn thinner and stronger. Usha Martin sells wire on its own (for springs, ropes, tyres, fencing, cables) and also uses it as the building block for rope.
Strand & ropeWires twisted into strands; strands laid around a core into a rope. The rope is the flagship, highest-value product — engineered for a named application and certified for it.
LRPC strand"Low-Relaxation Pre-stressed Concrete" strand — high-tensile cable embedded in concrete to pre-stress bridges, flyovers, sleepers and buildings. An infrastructure-linked product, a step above commodity wire.
End-fittings & accessoriesThe sockets, clamps, connectors and hardware that terminate a rope so it can actually be attached to a crane or a rig. Selling the fitted, ready-to-use assembly earns more than selling bare rope by the metre.
CertificationThird-party approvals (classification societies, standards bodies, OEM qualifications) that a rope is fit for a life-safety job. Winning and keeping these is the moat — a customer cannot legally hang a mine cage on an uncertified rope.
The economics that decide everything: value added per tonne

A commodity steel mill sells tonnes and lives or dies on the spread between the price of a tonne of steel and the cost of making it — a thin, brutal, cyclical margin. A wire-rope specialist sells engineering by the tonne: the same tonne of steel, once drawn into wire, laid into a certified deep-mining rope and fitted with sockets, can fetch a multiple of the plain-steel price. So the metric that matters for Usha Martin is not "how many tonnes did it ship?" but "how much value did it add to each tonne?" A year where rope and value-added products grow faster than plain wire is a better year even if total tonnage barely moves — because the average rupee-per-tonne rises, and with it the margin. Hold that idea; it is the key to the whole primer.

Why the rope wears out — and why that is good for the maker

Here is the quiet feature that makes rope a nicer business than most heavy engineering: ropes are consumables. A crane rope, an elevator rope, a mining rope all fatigue with use and must be inspected, retired and replaced on a schedule — long before the crane or the elevator itself is scrapped. That means a large installed base of equipment worldwide generates a steady, recurring pull for replacement rope, largely independent of whether anyone is building new cranes this year. The maker sells the rope once with the new machine, then again and again over the machine's life. It is not a subscription, but it rhymes with one.

What Usha Martin actually sells: one coil, several grades of value

The product ladder — read this once and the margin story clicks into place.

Everything Usha Martin makes starts from the same drawn steel wire and climbs a ladder of value. At the bottom is plain wire sold by the tonne; at the top is a certified, application-specific rope shipped with its fittings and, increasingly, with service. The higher you climb the ladder, the better the margin and the stickier the customer.

The three revenue families, decoded

  • Wire ropes — the flagship and the reason the company is world-famous. Usha Martin makes ropes for cranes, mining (hoisting and drag ropes), elevators/lifts, oil & offshore (mooring, drilling, anchor-handling), fishing and marine, aerial ropeways and cable-cars, structural and bridge applications, general engineering and forestry. These are engineered to specification and certified — the highest realisation and margin in the portfolio. In FY26, rope revenue grew ~8%.
  • Wire & wire products — specialty wire (bright, galvanised, coated) sold to industry, plus a growing range of downstream wire products. This is the higher-volume, somewhat lower-margin base of the ladder. In FY26 the wire segment grew strongly — up ~24% — a reminder that the "boring" rung can still add real revenue.
  • LRPC strand & downstream / services — pre-stressing strand tied to India's infrastructure build-out, plus the fast-growing business of selling fitted, serviced rope: end-fittings, on-site inspection, and the global distribution-and-service branch network that turns a product sale into a relationship. This rung is small today but strategically the most important, because it lifts the whole mix upward.
Why selling the fitting matters more than it sounds

A coil of certified rope is already a good product. But a rope cut to length, spliced, fitted with the right sockets and delivered ready to bolt onto a specific crane is a solution — and a customer will pay more for the solution than for the raw rope, because it removes their risk and their labour. It also locks them in: once your engineers have specified and certified a rope-and-fitting assembly for a mine or a rig, switching to an unproven supplier to save a few percent is not worth the safety headache. Every rung Usha Martin climbs — from wire, to rope, to fitted-and-serviced rope — trades a little volume simplicity for a lot more margin and a lot more customer stickiness. That upward drift is the strategy.

03

The company, the family, and who controls it

A Jhawar-family business from Ranchi and Kolkata, now focused and unpledged.

Usha Martin traces back to 1960s eastern India and has been controlled through its life by the Jhawar family, with Rajeev Jhawar as Managing Director and the founding B.K. Jhawar lineage as its industrial roots. Its heartland is Ranchi (Jharkhand) — where its flagship rope plant sits on around 100 acres, one of the largest single-site wire-rope facilities in the world — with corporate roots in Kolkata. What makes the company interesting today is not just what it makes but what it stopped making: it deliberately shed the steel-making empire that once defined and nearly destroyed it.

  • 1990s–2000s

  • 2010s

04

The pivot that made the company: selling the steel, keeping the rope

The single most important thing that ever happened to Usha Martin — and it is unique to this company.

For most of the 2010s, Usha Martin was two businesses stapled together, and one of them was slowly killing the other. There was the good business: a globally respected, high-margin, export-led wire-rope franchise. And there was the heavy business: a specialty-steel plant it had built to make its own wire rod, financed with a mountain of debt just as the steel cycle soured. The steel arm's interest bill and capital hunger meant the whole company was permanently short of breath — a fine rope maker shackled to a struggling steel mill. In 2019 management made the decision that defines the company today: it sold the specialty-steel business to Tata Steel and used the proceeds to pay down the debt.

Read that trade carefully, because it is counter-intuitive. Backward integration — owning your own raw-material supply — is usually sold as a strength. Here, giving it up was the making of the company. The steel mill tied up enormous capital and exposed the group to the commodity-steel cycle it was trying to escape, all to save a margin on wire rod it could simply buy from someone else. By selling the mill, Usha Martin swapped a capital-heavy, low-margin, cyclical input business for a clean balance sheet and the freedom to focus every rupee on the part that was always good: engineered rope.

The device that only fits Usha Martin: the same brand, minus the anchor

The whole modern investment case can be written as a subtraction. Take the Usha Martin of the mid-2010s. Keep the rope franchise, the certifications, the export branches, the Ranchi plant, the brand — all unchanged. Now remove one thing: the specialty-steel mill and the debt it carried. What is left is a company that earns a ~19% EBITDA margin instead of a diluted low-teens one, sits on net cash instead of net debt, and generates ~₹457 Cr of free cash a year instead of consuming it — without having changed a single thing about the product it is admired for. That is the rare corporate story where value was created not by building something, but by cleanly amputating the part that was doing the harm. The trade-off it accepted: Usha Martin now buys its wire rod on the open market, so a spike in rod prices flows straight into its costs — the price of a lighter, cleaner balance sheet.

A genuine surprise: the household-name "steel company" reflex about Usha Martin is now almost entirely wrong. The business that carries the name today does not make steel at all — it buys it. It is a specialty-engineering exporter that draws bought-in steel into certified rope and sells more than half of it abroad. The steel-maker Usha Martin was, and the rope-maker Usha Martin is, are effectively two different companies wearing the same logo — and the market only started paying for the good one after 2019.

Rupees per tonne, not tonnes: how the profit really grows

The one lens that explains why PAT can grow far faster than volume — and it is specific to this company.

If you value Usha Martin like a steel company, you will watch tonnage and get the story wrong. The right lens is realisation and value added per tonne. The company's profit growth comes less from shipping more steel than from making each tonne of steel worth more — by shifting it up the value ladder from wire, to rope, to fitted-and-serviced rope, and into higher-spec end-markets. FY26 is the proof: revenue grew only ~6%, but PAT grew ~21% and the EBITDA margin expanded from ~17.2% to ~19.1%. Volume did not carry that; mix did.

Each rung up the ladder — modest revenue growth, faster EBITDA growth, faster PAT growth, and a step-change in cash — is the signature of a company whose engine is mix and margin, not tonnage. This is the pattern to watch every quarter.

Where the value-added mix can still climb

The reason this lens matters for the future, not just the past, is that Usha Martin is nowhere near the top of its own ladder. Plenty of its volume is still relatively standard rope and wire; the highest-spec segments — deep-mining hoist ropes, offshore mooring and anchor-handling lines, elevator ropes for tall buildings, and the fitted-and-serviced solutions sold through overseas branches — are the ones it is deliberately pushing into. Every point of margin from here is a bet that the company can keep tilting its book toward those premium applications and away from the commodity end. If it can, revenue and margin can both keep rising; if the mix stalls, the company drifts back toward looking like the steel processor the market half-suspects it of being.

The global export engine

Why a "Ranchi company" is really a worldwide business — and what that gives it.

Here is the fact that reframes Usha Martin: in FY26, roughly 57% of its revenue came from outside India. This is not an Indian company that occasionally exports a container; it is a global wire-rope supplier with a genuine international sales-and-service footprint, that happens to manufacture in India. Understanding that changes how you read both its opportunity and its risks.

A majority-export revenue base gives Usha Martin access to the world's mining, offshore, crane and elevator markets — but also exposes it to global industrial cycles and to the rupee. Split is approximate, from FY26 commentary.

Being a real global supplier confers advantages that a domestic-only rope maker cannot match. Certifications and approvals earned for demanding markets — European mining, North Sea offshore, international classification societies — become a passport that lets it sell the highest-spec, highest-margin ropes anywhere. Overseas service branches mean it can inspect, replace and support ropes on-site, turning one-off sales into ongoing relationships and capturing the fitted-and-serviced margin. And geographic spread means a downturn in one region or one end-market can be offset by strength elsewhere — a mining boom in one continent, an offshore recovery in another.

The export edge cuts both ways

A 57%-export book is a strength and an exposure at once. On the upside, it plugs Usha Martin directly into global mining, offshore-energy, port and construction demand, and it lets the company earn foreign-currency margins on ropes that are certified for the toughest markets — which is exactly where the pricing power lives. On the downside, it means the company's fortunes ride on global industrial cycles, not just India's growth story, and its reported profits swing with the rupee: a stronger rupee can erode export realisations, a weaker one flatters them. For an Indian investor used to thinking of "Make in India" infrastructure plays, the mental adjustment is that most of Usha Martin's demand is set in mines, ports and oilfields far from home.

Where the ropes go — and why the demand swings

The end-markets that drive the order book, and the cyclicality baked into them.

A wire rope is only as good a business as the industries that buy it, so it is worth walking the end-markets one by one. They share a common shape: each is a large, global pool of equipment that needs certified rope both when it is built and, repeatedly, as the rope wears out. But they do not move together — and their capex-driven swings are the main source of cyclicality in Usha Martin's demand.

ApplicationWhat the rope doesDemand driver & cycle
Elevators & liftsHoist ropes that raise and lower the cab; high fatigue-life, safety-critical.Urbanisation and high-rise construction ("elevatorisation"); relatively steady, replacement-heavy, less violently cyclical.
Cranes & material handlingLifting and luffing ropes for port, construction and industrial cranes.Ports, construction, logistics capex; moves with industrial and trade cycles.
MiningHoisting ropes that lift cages of ore and miners; drag/dragline ropes; the most demanding, high-spec segment.Global mining capex and commodity prices — can swing hard with the mining cycle. High value, high margin.
Oil & gas / offshoreMooring lines, drilling and anchor-handling ropes for rigs and platforms.Offshore energy capex and oil prices; deeply cyclical, but very high-spec and high-margin when it runs.

Marine, fishing, structuralMooring and fishing ropes; structural strand for bridges and towers.Fishing fleets, shipping, and infrastructure/bridge construction; mixed cycles.

Two features stand out across this list. First, the replacement pull: much of the demand is not new-build but re-roping — swapping worn ropes on equipment that already exists. That gives the aggregate a steadier floor than any single end-market's capex cycle would suggest. Second, the diversity of drivers: elevators follow construction, mining follows commodities, offshore follows oil prices, ropeways follow tourism and public projects. Because these cycles rarely peak or trough together, a spread across all of them smooths the whole — but it does not remove cyclicality. In a synchronised global industrial downturn, mining, offshore and crane demand can all soften at once, and rope orders soften with them.

The structural tailwinds worth naming

Several of these end-markets are not just cyclical — they are structurally growing. Elevatorisation: as cities build taller, the installed base of elevators (and their hoist ropes) compounds for decades. Offshore energy: not only oil & gas but increasingly offshore wind, which needs mooring and installation ropes. Infrastructure: bridges, metros and ropeways lift LRPC strand and structural rope demand, especially in India. And the world's mining of the metals needed for electrification keeps demand for high-spec hoist rope alive. Usha Martin's bet is that these secular pulls — infrastructure, elevatorisation, offshore energy, mining for the energy transition — keep the underlying rope market growing through the cycles, while it climbs the value ladder within it.

Capacity, capex, and the push downstream

How the cash-rich balance sheet is being put back to work — deliberately in rope.

A net-cash company generating ~₹457 Cr of free cash a year has a pleasant problem: what to do with it. Usha Martin's answer is telling. It has announced a capex programme of roughly ₹300 Cr over about two years, with the large majority — around 70–75% — earmarked specifically for expanding rope capacity (by around ~6,000 tonnes, approx.). That allocation is a strategy statement in itself: the money is going into the top of the value ladder, not the bottom.

Two things make this capex low-risk relative to Usha Martin's own history. First, it is self-funded — paid for out of net cash and free cash flow, not new debt, so it cannot recreate the leverage trap of the steel years. Second, it is modest and incremental — a few hundred crore against a company already generating that much in annual free cash — so it grows the rope franchise without betting the company on a single mega-project. This is capacity growth done the way a burned company does it: cautiously, in the high-margin product, with cash it already has.

The downstream is the quiet compounding lever

Beyond raw rope capacity, the more valuable long-run investment is in the downstream: end-fittings, ready-to-use assemblies, on-site inspection and the overseas service branches. This is where a rope sale becomes a service relationship, and where margins are structurally higher because the customer is buying certainty, not steel. It also builds the replacement annuity — the more of the world's cranes, mines and rigs Usha Martin services, the more of their re-roping it captures automatically. Rope-capacity expansion grows the volume you can sell; the downstream grows the value and stickiness of each sale. Watching how fast the value-added and serviced share of revenue climbs is the best gauge of whether the strategy is actually compounding.

The industry and the rivals

A consolidated global niche, contested by a few scaled specialists.

High-spec wire rope is not a commodity anyone can make. Certifying a mining hoist rope or an offshore mooring line, and building the customer trust to sell it, takes decades of engineering and track record — which is why the top of the market is held by a small number of scaled, global specialists. Usha Martin competes in that top tier, against names most Indian investors have never heard of, and — at the lower-spec end — against a wave of cheaper producers.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

The market splits cleanly in two, and Usha Martin's whole strategy is about which half it lives in. At the commodity end — plain wire, standard rope — competition is global, price-driven, and increasingly Chinese; margins are thin and there is little to defend. At the high-spec end — certified mining, offshore and elevator rope, fitted and serviced — the competitors narrow to a handful of trusted global specialists (Bridon-Bekaert, WireCo, Kiswire, Usha Martin), switching costs are high, and margins are healthy. Every rung Usha Martin climbs up its own value ladder is also a move away from the crowded, price-fighting half of the market and toward the defensible, few-player half. The competitive question is not "can it beat China on price?" — it should not try — but "can it keep winning the certified, high-spec work against the three or four global names that also can?"

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A repaired, cash-rich niche champion — asked to keep tilting its mix upward through the cycles.

Usha Martin enters the second half of the 2020s in the strongest shape of its listed life: debt-free, generating real free cash, dominant in a defensible global niche, and deliberately steering its book toward higher-value rope. The genuine debate is no longer whether the business is sound — the 2019 steel-sale settled that — but whether a cyclical, export-led specialty engineer can keep improving its mix fast enough to justify a growth multiple, and how it weathers the next global industrial downturn.

The bull case

  • Global niche leadership — one of the world's largest wire-rope makers in a market only a handful of specialists can serve at the high-spec end; a real, certifiable moat.
  • Clean, cash-rich balance sheet — net cash after the steel exit; ~₹457 Cr free cash flow funds capex and dividends without leverage.
  • Mix-up margin engine — profit grows faster than revenue as the book tilts to rope, fittings and services; FY26 margin already ~19.1% and climbing.
  • Structural end-market pulls — elevatorisation, infrastructure/LRPC, offshore energy and mining for the energy transition support underlying rope demand.
  • Replacement annuity — a large global installed base of equipment needs recurring re-roping, giving demand a steadier floor than pure capex would.

The bear case

  • End-market cyclicality — mining, oil & gas/offshore, cranes and construction are capex-driven; a synchronised downturn cuts rope demand across markets at once.
  • Lost backward integration — now buys wire rod, so a spike in steel/rod prices flows straight into costs, with no captive mill to cushion it.
  • FX & global exposure — ~57% exports mean rupee strength and foreign demand swings move reported profit; the story rides world industry, not just India.
  • Global competition — Bridon-Bekaert, WireCo and Kiswire contest the high-spec work; Chinese producers pressure the commodity end.
  • Valuation & legacy overhangs — a low-30s P/E on a cyclical prices in continued mix-improvement; plus legacy regulatory/investigative matters worth tracking in disclosures.
The bottom line

Usha Martin is the rare industrial that got better by getting smaller — selling the steel mill that was dragging it down and keeping the certified rope franchise that was always the good part. What remains is a debt-free, cash-generative, export-led global niche leader whose profit grows less by shipping more steel than by making each tonne of steel worth more. The open question is durability of the mix-up through a cycle: whether Usha Martin can keep pushing the serviced, high-spec rope share higher — and hold its ~19% margin — even when mining, offshore and crane capex all cool at once. The next milestones to watch are concrete and checkable: the ~₹300 Cr rope-led capacity coming on stream, the value-added and export share of each quarter's revenue, and — above all — whether the EBITDA margin keeps grinding up from FY26's 19.1% rather than settling back. Those prints, not the tonnage headline, tell you whether the mill-to-rope transformation is still compounding.
