00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Hindustan Zinc Limited (HZL) is India's only fully integrated zinc-lead-silver producer and a near-monopoly in domestic zinc — and globally it is the second-largest integrated zinc producer. From a cluster of mines and smelters concentrated in Rajasthan, it digs zinc and lead ore out of the ground, smelts it into refined metal, and recovers silver as a byproduct along the way. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) it produced a record 1,114 kilotonnes of mined metal and 1,048 kt of refined metal, booked record revenue of ₹40,844 crore (up 20%), and earned an all-time-high ₹22,162 crore of EBITDA at a 54% margin — a level of profitability most manufacturers can only dream of.

Five facts frame everything:

  • It is a price-taker on a commodity it cannot control. Zinc sells at a single world price set on the London Metal Exchange (LME). HZL doesn't set that price — it takes it. So its profit per tonne is simply the gap between the LME zinc price and HZL's own cost of production ($959/tonne in FY26, among the lowest on earth). Widen that gap and profits soar; squeeze it and they shrink. Everything here is, ultimately, about that gap.
  • Its moat is being a lowest-cost producer. HZL sits in the first decile of the global zinc cost curve — i.e. among the cheapest ~10% of producers worldwide. That isn't a marketing line; it's existential. When zinc prices crash, high-cost mines elsewhere lose money and shut, but HZL keeps earning. Low cost is what lets it survive — and stay hugely profitable — through any cycle.
  • Silver has become a second engine. HZL is one of the world's largest silver producers — silver comes out of the same ore as a byproduct. With the silver price up ~75% to $53/oz in FY26, silver has quietly become a large, ultra-high-margin profit contributor on top of the zinc business. This is the "kicker" (§03).
  • It is a dividend machine — and that's the controversy. HZL throws off so much cash that it pays out enormous dividends. The catch: parent Vedanta (~61%) uses those dividends to service the heavy debt sitting above it — making HZL's payout policy a governance flashpoint (§06–§07).
  • The Government of India still owns ~28–29%. HZL was privatised in 2002–03 but the state never fully exited. That residual stake is both a structural overhang (a future stake sale could pressure the price) and a governance counterweight to Vedanta (§08).
In plain terms

Think of Hindustan Zinc as a money-printing mine wrapped in a corporate-governance puzzle. The mine half is genuinely world-class: it owns India's biggest zinc deposits, digs the metal up more cheaply than almost anyone alive, smelts it itself, and — because zinc ore happens to carry silver — gets a giant silver business thrown in for free. With both zinc and silver prices firm, it earns a 54% EBITDA margin and gushes cash. The puzzle half is who that cash belongs to and where it goes: the company is controlled by Vedanta, a group carrying a lot of debt one level up, which leans on HZL's dividends to stay current — while the Government of India still owns roughly a quarter and acts as a check (and a potential future seller). So the investment question isn't really "is this a good mine?" — it plainly is — but "how much of this superb cash machine's output actually reaches an ordinary shareholder, and on what terms?" Master that tension and you understand Hindustan Zinc better than most headlines will tell you.

01

Low-cost miner economics — the LME price you can't control

No metallurgy degree required — just the one idea that drives every commodity miner's year.

A zinc miner is, financially, a price-taker with a cost it can control. The selling price of zinc is set globally on the London Metal Exchange and is identical for everyone — HZL, a Canadian mine, an Australian mine. No single producer can move it. So a miner cannot win on price; it can only win on cost. The profit on each tonne is the gap between the world price (which the market hands you) and your own cost of digging it up and smelting it (which you control). Master that one idea and the whole business clicks into place.

The miner's margin — why cost is the only lever

The left side is given to HZL by the market — it can't change it. The right side is the only thing HZL controls. That is why "lowest-cost producer" isn't a slogan for a miner; it is the entire competitive strategy. In FY26, with LME zinc near $2,970/t and HZL's cost ~$959/t, the gap was vast.

Why the zinc price is cyclical — and beyond HZL's control

Zinc is mostly used to galvanise steel — coating it to stop it rusting — so zinc demand rides on construction, infrastructure, autos and appliances, especially in China, which dominates world metal demand. When the global economy and Chinese construction boom, zinc demand outruns mine supply and the LME price spikes; when growth slows or new mines flood the market, the price sags. On the supply side, prices also swing with mine closures, smelter outages and the cost/availability of zinc concentrate. The upshot: the price half of a miner's margin heaves around year to year, on forces — Chinese demand, global mine supply, the dollar — that no single producer can influence. A zinc miner's annual profit is therefore a leveraged bet on a price it does not set.

Why low cost = survives any cycle — the cost-curve logic

Picture every zinc mine in the world lined up cheapest-to-most-expensive — that line is the global cost curve. World demand has to be met by enough mines that the most expensive one still needed sets a rough "marginal cost", and the price tends to gravitate toward what that marginal producer needs to keep going. Now place Hindustan Zinc in the first decile — the cheapest ~10%. Two powerful things follow. First, HZL is always profitable when the marginal mine is. Since the price is anchored to the high-cost producer's economics, a low-cost mine pockets a fat spread in good times. Second, HZL survives the busts. When zinc prices crash, the expensive mines at the right of the curve start losing money and shut down — which removes supply and helps the price recover — but HZL, far to the left, is still in the black even at prices that bankrupt others. So being lowest-cost does double duty: it maximises profit in the good years and guarantees survival (and market-share gains) in the bad ones. For a commodity producer with no pricing power, that is the only durable moat there is — and HZL has one of the strongest versions of it on the planet. The mechanics of how it stays so cheap are §02.

In FY26 it did exactly that — a near-$2,000/tonne zinc margin, plus a silver bonanza on top. The price is the cycle; the cost is the company.
02

The cost moat & the mine-to-metal model

How HZL stays in the cheapest 10% of producers on earth — the source of all the cash.

If the only lever a miner controls is cost (§01), then HZL's defining feature is how it stays so cheap. The answer is a combination of exceptional ore bodies, full vertical integration from mine to metal, scale, captive power, and constant cost-cutting. HZL doesn't buy zinc concentrate on the market and smelt it (a thin-margin business); it owns the entire chain — the ore in the ground, the mines, the smelters, the refineries, and much of the power — so it captures the full value from rock to ingot.

The crown jewels — world-class ore bodies

  • Rampura Agucha (Rajasthan) — the world's largest underground zinc mine, with an exceptionally rich, high-grade ore body. High grade is decisive in mining: the more zinc per tonne of rock, the less rock you have to dig, haul, crush and process per tonne of metal — which directly lowers cost. This single asset anchors HZL's place at the cheap end of the cost curve.
  • Sindesar Khurd — among the top-five silver-producing mines in the world, and a major zinc-lead mine in its own right; the heart of the silver story (§03).
  • Rajpura Dariba, Zawar, Kayad — the other underground mines that round out a deep, long-life reserve and resource base concentrated in one geography in Rajasthan, which keeps logistics tight and costs low.

Mine to metal — owning the whole chain

HZL is India's only fully integrated zinc-lead-silver player: it operates the mines, the concentrators, and its own zinc and lead smelters and refineries at Chanderiya, Dariba and Debari, with refined zinc-lead capacity of roughly one million tonnes a year. Because it controls every step, it isn't exposed to the volatile treatment charges a standalone smelter pays, and it captures margin at the mine and the smelter. It also runs significant captive power (including a growing renewable share), insulating it from grid-power swings — energy being one of a smelter's biggest costs.

Because HZL owns every stage from the rock to the ingot to the silver, it captures the full value chain — and isn't squeezed by the third-party charges a standalone miner or smelter faces. That integration, plus high-grade ore and captive power, is the cost moat.
Why HZL's cost keeps falling — the cost-reduction flywheel

A great ore body gives you a low starting cost; staying lowest-cost is a constant grind, and HZL has been winning it. In FY26 the all-in zinc cost of production fell ~9% to $959 a tonne, and in the March quarter it hit a 16-quarter low of $903. Several levers drive that: cheaper, greener power (shifting from costly grid/coal power toward captive solar and wind cuts the energy bill, a smelter's largest variable cost); better grades and recoveries from the high-quality mines; digitalisation and automation across the underground mines; and plain operating scale — spreading fixed costs over record 1,114 kt of mined metal. The reason this matters so much: every dollar shaved off the cost of production drops straight to profit, because the selling price is fixed by the LME and doesn't move when HZL gets more efficient. A standalone smelter trying to cut costs is fighting over a thin margin; HZL, owning the whole chain and the cheap ore, turns each efficiency gain into pure spread. This is the quiet, unglamorous engine beneath the 54% EBITDA margin — and it is the most controllable, most durable part of the entire investment case.

03

The silver kicker — a byproduct that became a profit engine

The single most underappreciated thing about Hindustan Zinc right now.

Here is the surprise inside the zinc miner: Hindustan Zinc is also one of the world's largest silver producers — and silver has become a large, fast-growing, ultra-high-margin part of its profit. Silver isn't mined on its own here; it comes out of the same zinc-lead ore as a byproduct. That single fact changes the economics dramatically, and with silver prices at multi-decade highs, the silver business is now a genuine second engine bolted onto the zinc machine.

Why byproduct silver is so unusually profitable — the credit mechanism

This is the key idea, and it's subtle. When you mine zinc-lead ore, the cost of digging, hauling and processing that rock is incurred to get the zinc and lead — that's the primary product the mine is built for. The silver tucked inside the same ore comes up for almost no extra cost: you've already paid to extract and process the rock. So the revenue from selling that silver is nearly all profit. (Miners formally treat it as a "byproduct credit" — silver revenue is often netted off to make the zinc cost of production look even lower, which is part of why HZL's reported zinc cost is so competitive.) Now layer on the price move: silver has surged to multi-decade highs, and HZL's realised silver price jumped ~75% to $53.1/oz in FY26. Because the cost base barely moves when silver rises, almost the entire price increase falls to the bottom line. The result is that a "byproduct" has quietly become one of HZL's most valuable profit streams — and a reason HZL's earnings can climb even in a year when zinc itself is only modestly higher. In effect, owning HZL gives you a large, low-cost zinc miner plus a free, leveraged bet on the silver price. That optionality is a big part of why the stock re-rates when precious metals run.

For HZL, that is a windfall on metal it produces anyway. The flip side (§11): if silver mean-reverts, this kicker shrinks just as fast as it grew.
04

Lead & the product mix — the quieter third metal

Rounding out the integrated mine-to-metal picture.

Zinc is the star and silver is the kicker, but HZL is formally a zinc-lead-silver producer, and lead is the third leg. Lead comes out of the same mines and is smelted alongside zinc; it is smaller and lower-margin than zinc, but it is part of what makes HZL a multi-metal integrated producer rather than a pure zinc play.

  • Lead — the battery metal. HZL produced 197 kt of refined lead in FY26 (down ~13% YoY). Lead's biggest end-use is lead-acid batteries — the batteries in cars, trucks, inverters and backup power. It's a mature, steady market: less exciting than zinc, but a reliable contributor that shares the mines and smelters, so it adds volume at low incremental cost.
  • Refined zinc — the bulk. 851 kt in FY26 (+3%), the dominant product and the main driver of revenue. Sold mostly to galvanisers (who coat steel) and to die-casting and alloy makers.
  • Silver — the high-value byproduct. 627 t in FY26 (§03), small in volume but, at $53/oz, large in value.
  • Value-added & specialty products. HZL also sells higher-margin zinc alloys (e.g. for die-casting) and is building out downstream and recycling capacity (§09) to capture more of the chain and reduce raw cyclicality.

In plain terms — one ore, three revenue streams

The elegance of HZL's model is that a single mining and smelting operation produces three saleable metals at once. The mine is built and paid for to extract zinc; lead and silver ride along in the same rock. That's why HZL can report a low "cost of production" for zinc — the lead and silver revenue effectively subsidise it — and why a year like FY26, where zinc is flat-ish but silver soars, can still be a record. The diversification is modest (all three metals are still commodities, and zinc demand dominates), but it does mean HZL isn't a one-trick pony: when zinc is soft, a strong silver or lead market can cushion the blow, and vice versa.

05

The company & its owners — privatised, but never fully

A former state miner, now Vedanta-controlled, with the government still in the room.

Hindustan Zinc's ownership is the key to understanding it. It began as a government-owned company, was privatised to the Vedanta group in 2002–03, and yet the Government of India never fully exited — it still owns roughly a quarter. The result is an unusual three-way structure: a controlling private promoter (Vedanta) with strong incentives to extract cash, a large minority government shareholder acting as a check, and public shareholders caught between them. This ownership triangle drives the dividend, governance and overhang stories that follow (§06–§08).

In plain terms — why the ownership is the whole story

Most companies you can analyse purely on their business. With Hindustan Zinc, the business is the easy part — it's a superb, low-cost, cash-rich miner. The hard part is who controls the cash and what they do with it. Vedanta, the controlling shareholder, is itself part of a group (ultimately Vedanta Resources, the London-based parent) that carries substantial debt and has a strong appetite for the dividends HZL can pay. The Government of India, still owning ~28%, has every incentive to resist anything that transfers value from HZL to Vedanta at minority shareholders' (and the state's) expense. And public shareholders effectively ride along, benefiting from the same fat dividends but exposed to the governance risk that the parent's needs, not the company's, end up steering policy. So when you read about HZL, you're really reading about a tug-of-war over an exceptionally productive cash machine — which is exactly what §06–§08 unpack.

06

The dividend cash-cow — gushing payouts

Why HZL pays out enormous dividends, and why that is both the attraction and the catch.

Hindustan Zinc is, by design, a dividend cash-cow. A low-cost, integrated miner with a near-net-cash balance sheet and a 54% margin generates far more cash than it needs to run and grow — so it returns a large share of it as dividends. For income-seeking shareholders that's a feature; the catch is why the payouts are so aggressive (the parent's needs — §07).

Just how much cash

  • Record cash generation. FY26 free cash flow before growth capex was about ₹13,337 crore — money available to pay out or reinvest.
  • A near-net-cash balance sheet. HZL ended FY26 with roughly ₹13,846 crore of cash & investments against just ₹8,252 crore of borrowings — i.e. essentially net cash, unusual for a heavy industrial. It doesn't need to hoard cash, so it returns it.
  • Big, frequent dividends. In FY25 HZL paid out ₹29 per share in total across multiple tranches; it then declared a fresh ₹11/share interim dividend (~₹4,648 crore) — a payout that, at a ~2 face value, represents a 550% dividend. These are among the largest dividend streams in the Indian market.

Why a miner makes a great dividend stock — and where the catch hides

A mature, low-cost mine is close to an ideal dividend machine. The big upfront capital — sinking the shafts, building the smelters — is largely spent; the ore body lasts decades; and once running, the operation throws off cash well in excess of maintenance needs. With nowhere better to deploy all of it, returning cash to owners is rational, and a tax-efficient way to reward shareholders in a business that can't compound at high rates forever. That's the genuine attraction: own HZL and you get a slug of India's zinc-and-silver cash flow handed to you regularly. But here's the catch, and it's specific to HZL: the scale and timing of the dividends aren't set purely by what's best for HZL — they're heavily influenced by what the controlling shareholder, Vedanta, needs to service debt sitting above it (§07). Sometimes that means HZL pays out so aggressively it even raises debt to fund a dividend — borrowing to pay a payout, which is the opposite of conservative finance and a red flag minority investors watch closely. So the dividend is simultaneously HZL's biggest attraction and the channel through which the governance risk flows. A high yield is lovely; a high yield engineered to feed a parent's debt is something to scrutinise.

07

Vedanta & the governance angle

The single most important risk to understand. Read this section twice.

The defining risk in Hindustan Zinc is not the zinc price — it is governance. HZL is controlled by Vedanta Ltd, which in turn sits under the heavily indebted Vedanta Resources in London. That parent structure has strong, structural incentives to pull cash out of HZL — through dividends and through fees — to service its own debt. Because HZL has large minority shareholders (the government and the public), every such transfer raises the question of whether it's fair to all owners or just convenient for the controlling one. This is the issue that hangs over the stock.

How cash flows up — the two channels

  • Dividends (the legitimate channel). Vedanta's ~61% stake means it receives ~61% of every rupee HZL pays out — close to ₹3,000 crore from a single ₹10/share dividend. This is normal and pro-rata: every shareholder gets the same per share. The concern is the aggressiveness — pushing HZL to pay out so much (even funding payouts with debt) that the company's own flexibility and balance sheet are stretched to meet the parent's needs.
  • Related-party fees (the contested channel). More controversially, HZL has paid Vedanta a "brand and strategic services" (BSS) fee — reportedly around ₹1,060 crore in a single quarter of FY25-26, and over ₹1,560 crore across FY23–FY25 — which is then routed up toward Vedanta Resources. Unlike a dividend, this is not pro-rata: it goes only to the parent, so minority shareholders (and the government) pay their share of the fee but receive none of it. A US short-seller (Viceroy Research) has flagged such payments and questioned whether material related-party transactions received the required disinterested-shareholder approval under SEBI's rules.
Why related-party transactions (RPTs) are the red flag: when a controlling owner can make the company it controls pay it for brands, services or loans, value can quietly leak from all shareholders to just one. India's securities rules (SEBI LODR Reg. 23, plus the Companies Act) require "material" RPTs to be approved by disinterested shareholders — i.e. the related party can't vote on its own deal. The dispute around HZL is precisely whether large fees to Vedanta cleared that bar. For a minority investor, the question isn't whether HZL can earn — it plainly can — but how much of that earning is siphoned via fees and forced payouts before it reaches them.
In plain terms — the parent-debt machine

Picture three boxes stacked vertically. At the bottom is Hindustan Zinc — the cash machine. In the middle is Vedanta Ltd — the listed Indian parent that controls HZL. At the top is Vedanta Resources — the London holding company that controls Vedanta Ltd and carries a lot of debt that has to be repaid in hard currency. The top box's debt has to be serviced, and the cash to do it ultimately has to be pumped up from the bottom box, because HZL is the group's crown-jewel cash generator. Two pumps move the cash upward: dividends (fair, pro-rata, but pushed aggressively) and fees (not pro-rata, and contested). The governance risk is that decisions at HZL — how much to pay out, when, whether to borrow to do it, what fees to pay the parent — get made to suit the top box's debt calendar rather than the bottom box's long-term interest. The two things that keep this in check are (a) the Government of India's ~28% stake and board presence, which can resist value transfers (§08), and (b) SEBI's RPT rules and public scrutiny, including activist short-sellers. For an investor, the bottom line is blunt: you are buying a wonderful asset attached to a parent with a powerful, ongoing reason to extract its cash. That's not a reason to dismiss HZL — the dividends are real and you receive your pro-rata share — but it is the lens through which every capital-allocation decision should be read.

08

The Government's ~29% — the privatisation overhang

A leftover stake that is both a check on the parent and a cloud over the share price.

The flip side of Vedanta's control is the Government of India's residual ~28–29% stake — never sold after the 2002–03 privatisation. It plays two opposite roles at once: it is a governance counterweight that helps protect minority shareholders from value extraction (§07), and it is a structural overhang on the stock, because if the government ever sells that block, the sheer supply could weigh on the price.

The overhang — a large block that could come to market

  • The government wants to monetise it. Successive governments have flagged selling the residual HZL stake to raise disinvestment proceeds — a stake worth tens of thousands of crores. Any sale would likely come via an offer-for-sale (OFS), putting a big slug of shares onto the market at once, which typically requires a discount and can cap the share price near a planned sale.
  • It recurs as a headline. The market periodically reacts to reports of a government stake sale; HZL has at times had to clarify such reports as speculative, noting it had received no formal communication. The uncertainty itself is the overhang — investors don't know the timing or price.
  • Vedanta also trims its own stake. Separately, Vedanta has at times sold small slices of HZL (e.g. a ~1.59% offer-for-sale) to raise cash and meet public-shareholding norms — another source of supply.
Why a big government stake cuts both ways — in plain terms

As a check (good for minorities): the government isn't a passive holder — it has board representation and a strong interest in not letting Vedanta transfer value out of HZL via fees or excessive payouts, because that would dilute the value of the state's own ~28% holding. So the government's presence is one of the few forces that can genuinely restrain the controlling shareholder. In disputes over related-party fees, the government's stance and its directors' votes matter (critics have at times argued the government directors should be more assertive). As an overhang (a cloud on the price): the same stake is a large, known block of stock that the state has signalled it may eventually sell. Markets hate that kind of uncertainty — a future OFS means a predictable wave of supply, usually at a discount, which tends to keep a lid on the share price until it's resolved. There's also a structural tension: the government wants the best price for its stake, which can clash with Vedanta's desire to control HZL cheaply and extract its cash. So the ~29% is simultaneously HZL's best governance safeguard and a recurring weight on its valuation. Watch for two things: any concrete move toward a government OFS (a near-term price negative but a long-term clarifier), and how forcefully the government's directors push back on related-party deals (the real test of the "check" function).

Net of it all: the Government's stake is the reason HZL isn't only a Vedanta story. It's the institutional brake on the parent's cash extraction — and the asterisk on the share price. A clean resolution (a well-priced OFS, or clear rules on RPTs) would remove an overhang; a messy one (a forced sale at a discount, or governance disputes) is the recurring risk.
09

Capacity expansion & diversification — Vision 2.0

From a 1.1 MT zinc miner toward a 2 MT multi-metal enterprise.

HZL isn't just milking the existing mines — it has an ambitious growth plan, branded "HZL Vision 2.0", to roughly double production from ~1.1 to ~2.0 million tonnes of mined metal over about five years, backed by a large ₹40,000–50,000 crore capex programme. Alongside the volume growth runs a deliberate diversification beyond raw zinc — into fertiliser, recycling, downstream zinc products and new critical minerals — aimed at extracting more value per tonne and reducing exposure to the bare zinc cycle.

The growth pillars

  • Double the metal — toward ~2 MTPA. The headline ambition: lift mined-metal capacity from today's record ~1.1 MT toward ~2 MT, mostly through brownfield expansion and debottlenecking (squeezing more from existing mines and smelters) plus new mine and smelter capacity — including a planned ~250 kt integrated zinc smelter at Debari (targeted ~FY29). Brownfield growth is cheaper and lower-risk than greenfield, and protects the cost advantage.
  • Fertiliser — a new revenue stream. A ~510 ktpa fertiliser plant is targeted for completion around Q2 FY27. Zinc and sulphuric acid (a smelting by-product) are feedstocks for certain fertilisers, so this turns a waste/by-product stream into a saleable product — classic integration economics, and a step into a non-cyclical, agriculture-linked market.
  • Recycling & tailings reprocessing. A ~10 Mtpa tailings-reprocessing plant (targeted ~Q4 FY28) aims to extract metal from old mine waste — recovering value from material already mined, lowering effective cost and improving sustainability. Recycling (secondary zinc) also hedges against ore depletion.
  • Critical minerals & downstream. Through an exploration arm, HZL is pursuing blocks in tungsten, potash and rare earths — riding India's "critical minerals" push — and building a zinc industrial park for downstream zinc-based manufacturing, capturing more of the value chain.

Why the expansion is the long-term value lever — and its tension

For a price-taking miner, there are only two ways to grow profit over time: cut cost further (§02) or sell more tonnes — because you can't raise your price. Vision 2.0 is the "sell more tonnes" lever, and doubling volume from a first-decile cost base is genuinely powerful: if HZL can add ~900 kt of low-cost metal and keep its cost advantage, profit scales with it. The diversification (fertiliser, recycling, critical minerals, downstream) layers on a second benefit — more value per tonne and a bit less dependence on the bare zinc cycle, smoothing the commodity swings that otherwise dominate. The tension is capital allocation: HZL generates a lot of cash, but it can't simultaneously pay maximal dividends to feed the parent and fund a ₹40–50k crore expansion and stay net-cash. Something gives. The bull reading is that growth capex is the better use of cash and a sign HZL is investing for the long term rather than being milked dry; the bear reading is that the parent's dividend appetite could starve or slow the expansion, or push HZL into debt to do both. How HZL splits its cash between growth and payouts over the next few years is the clearest signal of whose interests are really steering the company.

Financials & valuation

A record FY26 — read the margin, the cost, and the cash, not just the headline.

FY26 was a record year on almost every line: highest-ever revenue and EBITDA, the lowest-ever cost of production, record mined and refined metal, and a silver-price windfall — all dropping into a near-net-cash balance sheet. The right way to value HZL is to recognise it as a low-cost, high-margin, cash-generative miner whose earnings swing with the zinc and silver price, and whose valuation carries a governance discount for the Vedanta parent and the government overhang.

How to think about valuing a low-cost miner like HZL

Valuing a commodity producer is harder than a steady consumer business because the "E" in any P/E swings with metal prices the company can't control. Two adjustments matter. First, normalise the prices. FY26's record was powered by a ~75% silver surge and firm zinc; ask what HZL earns at mid-cycle zinc and silver prices, not peak. A miner valued on peak earnings looks cheap and on trough earnings looks dear — both mislead. Professionals lean on EV/EBITDA across the cycle and on the cost position (a first-decile producer deserves a premium to a high-cost one, because it earns through the cycle and survives downturns). Second, apply the governance lens. Even a wonderful asset trades at a discount when a controlling shareholder can extract value via fees and forced payouts (§07) and a large government block hangs over the supply (§08). So HZL's "fair" multiple is the quality of the mine minus a governance/overhang discount. The bull case is that the discount is too harsh for an asset this good and this cash-generative; the bear case is that the discount is warranted because minority shareholders genuinely don't fully control the cash. Watch four numbers each year: zinc cost of production (is the moat intact?), silver realisation (how big is the kicker?), the dividend-vs-capex split (growth or extraction?), and any RPT/fee to Vedanta (is value leaking?).

The key reading note: HZL's FY26 record is genuine and built on real, durable strengths (lowest-cost mine, integration, net-cash). But part of the profit jump is a price windfall — especially silver — that can reverse. The durable value is the cost moat and the ore body; the cyclical cherry is the metal price. Separate the two when you read the numbers.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A world-class mine and a high yield — guarded by a governance asterisk.

Hindustan Zinc is the clearest "quality asset" play in Indian mining: a near-monopoly, lowest-cost, fully integrated zinc-lead-silver producer with a silver kicker, a net-cash balance sheet, a huge dividend, and an ambitious low-risk expansion. The debate is less about the asset — which is superb — and more about the commodity cycle and the governance: whether metal prices (and silver especially) stay firm, and whether the controlling parent's cash needs are managed fairly to all owners.

The bull case

  • A first-decile cost moat — among the world's lowest-cost zinc producers ($959/t, heading lower), so it earns fat margins in good cycles and survives the bad ones; 54% EBITDA margin.
  • The silver kicker — one of the world's largest silver producers, byproduct silver at near-pure margin; silver realisation up ~75% in FY26 and a free, leveraged play on precious metals.
  • A dividend cash-cow — net-cash balance sheet and ~₹13,000+ Cr of free cash flow funding among India's largest dividends; a real income stream for shareholders.
  • Vision 2.0 growth — roughly doubling to ~2 MTPA, mostly low-risk brownfield, plus fertiliser/recycling/critical-minerals diversification — more tonnes and more value per tonne.
  • Near-monopoly in Indian zinc — riding India's infrastructure, construction and galvanised-steel demand, with no domestic peer at this scale or cost.

The bear case

  • Governance / parent extraction — Vedanta's debt-driven need for cash, aggressive dividends (even debt-funded), and contested related-party "brand" fees (~₹1,060 Cr) risk siphoning value from minorities; short-seller scrutiny.
  • The commodity cycle — a pure price-taker on zinc; a global slowdown or Chinese demand drop can compress the spread and profits sharply.
  • Silver could mean-revert — a big slice of FY26's record profit came from a ~75% silver-price jump; if silver falls, the kicker shrinks just as fast.
  • The government overhang — a ~28–29% state stake that may be sold via OFS, a recurring supply cloud over the share price.
  • Single-geography, depletion & ESG — concentrated in Rajasthan; mines deplete and need constant reserve replacement; mining carries water, tailings and emissions risk.
The bottom line

Hindustan Zinc is a genuinely world-class mining asset wrapped in a governance question. The asset is close to ideal: a near-monopoly in Indian zinc, one of the lowest-cost producers on earth, fully integrated from mine to metal, throwing off a 54% margin and a torrent of cash, with a large silver business riding for free on the same ore — and an ambitious, mostly low-risk plan to double in size while diversifying into fertiliser, recycling and critical minerals. If you simply want exposure to a high-quality, low-cost, dividend-rich zinc-and-silver miner levered to India's growth, few assets anywhere are better. But the company is more than its mine: it is controlled by Vedanta, a group with a powerful, ongoing need to pull cash upward to service debt, balanced (imperfectly) by a government that still owns ~28% and could one day sell it. So the honest framing is this: you are buying a superb cash machine, but you do not fully control the cash. Get firm metal prices, a fairly managed payout, and disciplined expansion, and HZL compounds India's zinc-and-silver story while paying you handsomely along the way; get a price downturn, an aggressive parent, and a messy government exit at the same time, and even a first-decile cost position can only cushion so much. It is one of the highest-quality ways to own a commodity miner in India — but the governance asterisk never goes away.

The next chapter for Hindustan Zinc is less about whether the mine keeps minting cash — it will — and more about who that cash ultimately serves: a fairly-rewarded shareholder base funding disciplined growth, or a debt-laden parent steering the payout. The ore body is settled; the governance is not.
