Indian Metals & Ferro Alloys, own the ore, own the power

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Indian Metals & Ferro Alloys — IMFA — makes ferro chrome, an alloy of iron and chromium that is the essential ingredient in stainless steel. You cannot make stainless without chromium, and chromium is added to molten steel in the form of ferro chrome. IMFA is India's largest fully-integrated ferro-chrome producer: it doesn't just run smelting furnaces, it also digs its own chrome ore out of captive mines in Odisha and generates its own electricity from captive power plants. Ore, power and furnaces under one owner — that integration is the whole story. And it sells the bulk of what it makes not in India but overseas, to the stainless-steel mills of China, Japan, Korea and Europe.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Integration is the moat. Ferro-chrome smelting is savagely power-hungry and ore-hungry — electricity and chrome ore together are the majority of the cash cost of a tonne. A converter that buys both at market prices gets squeezed hard when ferro-chrome prices fall. IMFA owns its ore leases and ~205 MW of captive power, so its cost per tonne sits well below a non-integrated rival's. That is why it stays profitable at points in the cycle where others bleed.
  • It is a commodity business, and the commodity swings hard. Ferro-chrome and chrome-ore prices rise and fall with global stainless-steel demand — above all China's, which makes more than half the world's stainless. FY25 was a down-leg (revenue fell to ~₹2,565 Cr as prices corrected); FY26 recovered to ~₹2,892 Cr revenue and ~₹425 Cr profit. The topline is only partly in IMFA's hands — a chunk of it is set in Chinese mills and the dollar.
  • It just bought a growth leg from Tata Steel. In FY26 IMFA acquired Tata Steel's 100,000-tonne Kalinganagar ferro-chrome plant (KNR-2) for a base ₹610 Cr, pushing total capacity past 0.5 million tonnes a year. Management is guiding production from ~267,000 t in FY26 to ~400,000 t in FY27 and 475,000–500,000 t in FY28 — a step-change in scale for a company that had run flat for years.
In plain terms

IMFA is a chromium-alloy factory that happens to own the mine and the power station feeding it. Most metals companies buy their raw materials and their electricity from someone else and live on the thin gap between input and output prices. IMFA controls both ends of that gap, so when ferro-chrome prices crash it still earns a spread while un-integrated rivals fall to break-even or worse. The trade-off: it makes one product, sold mostly abroad, whose price it does not set. In a good stainless year it prints cash and pays fat dividends; in a bad one, its cost floor is the only thing keeping the lights green. The 2026 acquisition of the Tata Steel plant is the first real attempt in years to grow the volume of that spread, not just wait for the price to turn.

01

Ferro chrome 101: the ingredient stainless steel can't do without

No metallurgy degree needed — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

Stainless steel is "stainless" because it contains chromium — typically 10.5% or more — which forms an invisible protective layer that stops the steel rusting. That chromium has to get into the molten steel somehow, and it can't be added as pure chromium metal (too expensive) or as raw ore (won't dissolve usefully). It is added as ferro chrome: a solidified alloy of iron and chromium, made by melting chrome ore with a carbon reductant (coke or coal) in an enormous electric furnace. IMFA sits at exactly this step — it turns rock (chrome ore) into the alloy (ferro chrome) that a stainless mill throws into its furnace. To follow the business, you need six terms.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Chrome ore / chromiteThe raw mineral dug out of the ground — chromium locked inside rock. India's chromite is concentrated almost entirely in Odisha's Sukinda valley. This is IMFA's captive raw material; it mines its own.
Ferro chrome (FeCr)The finished product — an iron-chromium alloy, usually "high-carbon" ferro chrome, sold in lumps to stainless mills. IMFA's single output. Priced per tonne, globally, in US dollars.
Smelting furnaceA giant submerged electric-arc furnace that melts ore + reductant into ferro chrome. Rated in MVA (mega-volt-amperes) — a measure of its electrical size. IMFA runs ~190 MVA of furnaces (rising past 280 MVA after the Tata Steel plant).
Captive powerElectricity IMFA generates itself (mostly coal-fired, plus a little solar) rather than buying from the grid. About 205 MW. Because smelting devours power, self-generated electricity is the single biggest reason IMFA is a low-cost producer.
ReductantThe carbon (coke, coal, char) that chemically strips oxygen off the ore so chromium can alloy with iron. A bought-in input — one of the few IMFA doesn't own.
Stainless steelThe end market. ~90% of all chromium ends up in stainless. So ferro-chrome demand is really a bet on global stainless-steel production, which China dominates.
The chain of demand, in one line

Follow the demand backwards and the business becomes obvious. People and factories buy stainless steel → mills melt steel with chromium in it → those mills buy ferro chrome to supply the chromium → ferro-chrome makers like IMFA buy (or mine) chrome ore and burn huge amounts of electricity to make it. So IMFA is three steps removed from a kitchen sink or a chemical tank — but its fortunes are chained directly to how much stainless the world, and especially China, is making this quarter. When Chinese stainless output rises, ferro-chrome prices firm and IMFA's spread widens; when China's builders stop buying, the whole chain deflates.

One feature of this chain drives the cyclicality above all: ferro chrome is essentially a one-customer commodity. Roughly 90% of all chromium ends up in stainless steel, with only a small tail going into alloy and tool steels, foundries and refractories. There is no meaningful second market to lean on — so when stainless demand weakens, ferro chrome has nowhere to hide. That single-end-market concentration is exactly what makes the product, and IMFA's revenue, swing as hard as it does.

02

The integrated machine: mine, power, furnace, market

The one diagram that makes IMFA click into place.

The reason IMFA is described as "fully integrated" is that it owns almost every link in the chain that turns rock into alloy — and it built that chain deliberately, over decades, precisely because the two costliest inputs (ore and power) are the two most dangerous to buy from someone else. Why owning each link matters

  • The mine (chrome ore) — chrome ore is not a global commodity you can buy cheaply anywhere; India's is bottled up in Odisha and access is granted through government leases. Owning its own chromite means IMFA gets its most important raw material at the cost of digging it (plus royalty) rather than at a market price that spikes exactly when ferro chrome is dear. In FY26 it raised a record 810,612 tonnes of chrome ore, over half of it from deep underground mines.
  • The power plant — a submerged-arc furnace is essentially a controlled electrical fire; making a tonne of ferro chrome consumes roughly 3.5–4 megawatt-hours of electricity. Buying that from the grid at commercial tariffs would be crippling. IMFA's ~205 MW of captive generation (largely low-cost coal, with a small solar slice) is the reason its power cost per tonne is a fraction of a grid-dependent rival's.
  • The furnaces and the market — IMFA's two complexes at Therubali (Rayagada district) and Choudwar (near Cuttack) house the furnaces that do the actual smelting, and the value-added grades (low-carbon, tailored sizes) that fetch better prices. What leaves the gate goes overwhelmingly to export mills — which is where IMFA hands control to the global price and the rupee-dollar rate.
Why "integrated" is not just a buzzword here

For most manufacturers, vertical integration is a nice-to-have. For a ferro-chrome smelter it is the difference between surviving a down-cycle and not. Consider what happens when the ferro-chrome price falls 20%: a converter that buys ore and grid power watches its entire margin evaporate, because its costs don't fall with the selling price. IMFA's costs are largely internal — the ore comes from its own pit, the power from its own boilers — so a big share of its cost base doesn't move with the market at all. That converts a wild price swing into a merely uncomfortable one. Integration doesn't make IMFA immune to the cycle; it makes IMFA the last one standing when the cycle turns ugly.

03

The company, the Panda family, and Odisha roots

A six-decade Odisha industrial house, tightly family-controlled.

IMFA is not a recent listing riding a theme — it is one of eastern India's oldest private industrial enterprises, built by the Panda family of Odisha. Founded in 1961 by Dr. Bansidhar Panda and his wife Ila Panda, it grew from a single ferro-alloy plant into the country's largest integrated chrome producer. The founder — a metallurgist trained at Michigan Tech and Harvard — returned to Odisha specifically to build a ferro-alloy business on the state's chromite reserves. That founder's DNA, a technical family running an integrated resource business from Bhubaneswar, still defines the company.

Ownership & control

  • Promoters: the Panda family and associated group entities hold the controlling majority (broadly ~55–60%, approx. — verify against the latest shareholding filing). This is a founder-family business with tight promoter control and continuity of management, not a widely-held professional company.
  • Leadership: Subhrakant Panda is Managing Director — a notable industry figure who has served as president of FICCI (India's apex business chamber) and headed the International Chromium Development Association, giving IMFA an unusually senior voice in the global chrome industry for a company its size.
  • Where it sits: headquartered in Bhubaneswar, Odisha, with operations concentrated in the same state — close to the chromite it mines. IMFA is essentially a pure-play: it does one thing, ferro chrome, and does it fully integrated. (Note: it is unrelated to any similarly-named entity — this is the Odisha Panda-family chrome company.)
Why the founder-family nature matters: IMFA's edge — captive mines, captive power, a single integrated product — was built patiently over sixty years and is very hard for a newcomer to replicate, because mining leases and power plants aren't things you can simply buy your way into quickly. The flip side of a tightly family-run commodity company is that capital-allocation decisions (a big acquisition, dividend policy, how much debt to carry) rest with a small group; minority holders are along for the ride on the family's judgement.
04

The cost-floor moat: why IMFA earns when rivals don't

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it lives in the cost sheet.

Every ferro-chrome producer sells into the same global price. So in a commodity where everyone gets the same revenue per tonne, the only durable edge is a lower cost per tonne — and cost is where integration cashes out. Break down what it actually costs to make a tonne of ferro chrome and the picture is stark: two inputs, power and chrome ore, dominate everything else. IMFA owns both.

The device that only fits IMFA: the two costs that matter are the two it owns

Here is the number that does not survive a find-and-replace with any non-integrated ferro-alloy converter. Roughly 3.5–4 MWh of electricity and a couple of tonnes of chrome ore go into every single tonne of ferro chrome — and those two inputs alone are around 60% of cash cost. A converter buys that power from the grid at commercial tariff and that ore from a miner at market price; when the ferro-chrome price falls, its costs stay stuck and its margin is gone. IMFA generates the power in its own boilers and digs the ore from its own leases, so ~60% of its cost base is effectively internal and largely insensitive to market spikes. The result: IMFA has a cost floor a grid-and-market rival simply cannot reach, and that floor is what keeps it in the black at the bottom of the cycle. A ferro-chrome business, stripped to its essence, is a captive power company that happens to smelt chrome — and IMFA built the power company first.

At the trough of a ferro-chrome down-cycle, the converter's cost can meet or exceed the selling price — it stops or bleeds. IMFA, with a structurally lower cost, still earns a spread. The gap is the moat; it widens exactly when times are hard, which is when moats are worth the most.

None of this makes the cost advantage automatic or permanent. Reductants (coke and coal) are still bought at market and can spike; captive coal linkages, mining costs and royalty all move; and the deeper the underground mining goes, the more each tonne of ore costs to raise. But the structural point holds: on the two inputs that decide a smelter's fate, IMFA has taken the market price out of the equation. That is why, through a cycle that has broken plenty of ferro-alloy converters, IMFA has kept making money.

05

The commodity cycle: living with a price you don't set

The force that dominates every year — and it is largely made in China.

If the cost floor is IMFA's defence, the ferro-chrome price is the weather it lives in — and the weather is set overseas. Ferro chrome is a global commodity quoted in US dollars, and its price is pulled up and down by global stainless-steel demand, of which China alone is more than half. When Chinese mills run hot, ferro-chrome and chrome-ore prices firm and IMFA's spread fattens; when China's construction and consumer economy sag, the whole chain deflates — as it did through late 2024 and into 2025, when ferro-chrome bidding prices in China fell sharply and chrome-ore piled up in ports.

IMFA's FY25 revenue fell to ~₹2,565 Cr as ferro-chrome prices corrected, then recovered to ~₹2,892 Cr in FY26 — a textbook illustration that the topline follows a price the company doesn't control. Note that profit held up better than revenue in the dip, because the cost floor cushioned it.

What makes this cycle so violent is chrome ore moving in the same direction at the same time. Ore and alloy prices tend to rise and fall together, so a downturn hits both what IMFA sells (ferro chrome) and, in principle, the value of what it mines (chrome ore). For most producers that is a double blow. For IMFA it is partly a hedge: because it consumes its own ore rather than selling it, a cheap-ore environment simply lowers its own input cost, softening the hit from cheaper ferro chrome. The integration that lowers the cost floor also dampens the cycle's amplitude.

The surprise most Indian investors miss

Here is the genuinely counter-intuitive fact about IMFA: it is an "Indian" metals company whose profits are barely an Indian story at all. It sells the majority of its ferro chrome abroad, so its realisations are set in the stainless mills of China, Japan and Korea and denominated in US dollars. That means the two biggest swing factors in IMFA's P&L — the global ferro-chrome price and the rupee-dollar exchange rate — are both decided outside India. When an investor buys IMFA, they are, in economic substance, buying a leveraged bet on Chinese stainless demand and a weaker rupee, wrapped in an Odisha mining-and-power cost structure. It is one of the more China-exposed names on the Indian exchanges that almost nobody thinks of as a China play.

06

The export engine and the dollar

Where the ferro chrome actually goes — and why forex is part of the thesis.

India makes far more ferro chrome than its own small stainless industry can absorb, so producers like IMFA are structurally exporters. IMFA targets roughly a 60:40 export-to-domestic split of its sales, shipping to the stainless heartlands of East Asia and Europe. That export orientation is both an opportunity and a dependency: it plugs IMFA straight into the world's largest stainless markets, but it also hands the top line over to foreign demand and the exchange rate.

That targeted ~60:40 tilt to exports means IMFA's realisations are largely dollar-linked and set by foreign demand: a weaker rupee flatters export earnings, a stronger rupee erodes them. Forex is not a footnote here — it is a real driver of the margin. Two consequences flow from being an exporter. First, currency is a live variable: with revenue in dollars and much of the cost base in rupees (Indian mining, Indian power, Indian labour), a depreciating rupee is a genuine tailwind to margins, and a sharply appreciating one is a headwind. Over the long run the rupee has tended to drift weaker against the dollar, which has quietly helped Indian ferro-chrome exporters — but it is not something IMFA controls, and it can whipsaw a given quarter.

Second, trade and demand policy abroad matters. Because China is both the largest stainless producer and a large ferro-chrome producer and importer, shifts in Chinese smelting economics, port inventories, winter production curbs and property-sector health ripple straight back to Indian exporters' order books and prices. IMFA's job is to be the reliable, low-cost supplier that mills keep buying from across the cycle — which is exactly what its cost floor lets it be.

The nuance on the export tilt: selling mostly abroad diversifies IMFA away from India's still-small stainless demand and into the far larger global market — a strength. But it also means IMFA cannot simply lean on a booming domestic economy to save a bad export year; if global stainless is weak, IMFA's biggest market is weak, full stop. The domestic ~40% is a useful ballast and a growth avenue as India's own stainless consumption rises, but the swing factor remains offshore.

07

Mines, leases and the royalty backdrop

The captive-ore advantage — and the Odisha auction context that governs it.

The captive chrome ore that anchors IMFA's cost advantage doesn't come free of politics or expiry dates. In India, mineral resources belong to the state, and companies mine them under leases granted (increasingly, auctioned) by the government, paying a royalty on what they extract. IMFA's competitive position therefore rests not just on geology but on continuing to hold the right to mine — which makes the Odisha lease-and-royalty backdrop a core part of the story, not a technicality.

The captive-ore base

  • The leases: IMFA operates captive chromite mines in Odisha's chrome belt — names to know are Sukinda (the heart of India's chromite), Mahagiri and Nuasahi. Between them they feed the furnaces; in FY26 IMFA raised a record 810,612 tonnes of chrome ore, with over 536,000 tonnes coming from underground mining.
  • Underground is the tell: that heavy underground share is significant — India tightened rules against easy open-cast chromite mining in the ecologically sensitive Sukinda valley, so extending mine life increasingly means going deep. Underground ore costs more per tonne to raise than open-cast, a slow upward pressure on IMFA's single biggest owned input.
  • Mine life & renewal: the durability of the cost moat depends on lease tenure and reserves — how many years of chromite IMFA has the right to mine. Investors should track lease-renewal timelines and reserve disclosures directly; a captive-ore producer's most important long-term asset is the runway of ore it controls.
Why the auction regime cuts both ways

India moved to auctioning mineral blocks, and in Odisha several chromite leases have come up for competitive bidding — sometimes at eye-watering premiums, where winners commit to paying the state a large share of the ore's value. For a captive producer this is a double-edged sword. On one hand, holding long-dated existing leases is hugely valuable, because acquiring fresh chromite now can mean bidding away much of the ore's margin. On the other, when IMFA's own leases eventually come up for renewal or it wants to add reserves, it faces that same expensive auction market. The captive-ore moat is real and rare — but it is a moat with a clock on it, governed by Odisha's mining policy as much as by IMFA's drilling.

The royalty and levy structure adds a further layer: producers pay royalties, district mineral-fund contributions and other levies on chromite, so a portion of the "captive" ore advantage is handed back to the state on every tonne. It is still far cheaper than buying ore at market — but the net advantage is the gap between the all-in cost of raising ore (mining + royalty + levies + going deeper) and what non-integrated rivals pay in the open market. That gap, not the gross saving, is the number that matters.

08

The growth leg: the Tata Steel plant and the push past 0.5 MTPA

After years of running flat, a real step-up in volume.

For much of the last decade IMFA's story was about defence — deleveraging, riding the cycle, protecting the cost floor. FY26 added something new: offence. The company acquired Tata Steel's ferro-chrome plant at Kalinganagar (KNR-2) — a 100,000-tonne-per-annum facility with 99 MVA of furnace capacity — for a base consideration of ₹610 Cr, and had it fully operational by March 2026. In one move, IMFA lifted its total installed capacity past half a million tonnes a year and set up a multi-year volume ramp.

From ~267,000 t in FY26 to a guided ~400,000 t in FY27 and ~475,000–500,000 t in FY28 — roughly a doubling of output in two years, driven mainly by the KNR-2 plant plus debottlenecking. This is the first genuine growth leg in years; treat FY27/FY28 as targets, not results.

In furnace terms, the deal added 99 MVA to IMFA's pre-existing ~190 MVA base (Therubali plus Choudwar), taking installed smelting capacity to roughly 289 MVA — half again as much, in a single step. That is the kind of scale jump that would take years to build greenfield, and it arrived as a running plant rather than a construction project. Two things make the acquisition strategically sharp. First, it is brownfield, not greenfield — IMFA bought a working plant, sidestepping the multi-year delays and cost overruns of building new furnaces from scratch. Second, it fits IMFA's model like a glove: additional smelting capacity is most valuable to the producer that can feed it with captive ore and captive power, because more tonnes only compound the advantage if the input cost stays low. The open question is whether IMFA's owned ore and power can scale to feed a 0.5-MTPA machine, or whether the extra tonnes increasingly rely on bought-in ore and grid power — which would dilute the very cost moat the growth is meant to leverage.

The other quiet build-out: renewable power

Alongside the capacity jump, IMFA has been nudging its power mix greener — its ~205 MW captive base already includes a small solar slice (~4.55 MWp), with further solar under development at Therubali. It is modest today, but the direction matters for two reasons: stainless buyers increasingly want lower-carbon ferro chrome, and cheaper renewable power over time chips at the one input cost (electricity) that dominates the whole cost sheet. A ferro-chrome maker that can green and cheapen its own power is defending its moat and its market access at once.

09

The balance sheet and the dividend record

A deleveraged company that re-geared, on purpose, to grow.

A commodity producer's balance sheet is its shock absorber — it decides whether a down-cycle is survivable or fatal. IMFA spent the 2010s learning that the hard way, carrying heavy debt from power and expansion capex into a weak ferro-chrome market. The multi-year project since has been deleveraging, and by FY23–FY24 it had reached a low-gearing, near net-cash position. That repaired balance sheet is exactly what let it write a ₹610 Cr cheque for the Tata Steel plant.

The FY26 picture reflects that choice: IMFA took on borrowings of roughly ₹958 Cr (as of March 2026) to fund the acquisition and the growth ramp — a deliberate, value-accretive re-gearing off a clean base, not a return to distress.

Why the timing of the debt is the whole point

For a cyclical commodity producer, when you carry debt matters far more than whether you carry it. Borrowing to buy cheap, ready-built capacity on the way into a recovery — as IMFA has done with the Tata Steel plant — is the opposite of being forced to service heavy debt on the way down, which is what nearly broke the old IMFA in the 2010s. The same balance-sheet leverage that is dangerous at a cycle top is an accelerant off a repaired base near a cycle bottom. The judgement being tested is not the amount of the debt; it is whether IMFA has read the ferro-chrome cycle correctly in taking it on.

The dividend record is real and meaningful — IMFA declared a total of ₹12.50 per share for FY26 (a ₹5.00 interim plus a ₹7.50 final, on a ₹10 face value) — but it is emphatically cyclical: it rises in strong ferro-chrome years and gets trimmed in lean ones, tracking the commodity price rather than a fixed formula.

Financials & valuation

A cash-generative cyclical, growing volume into a recovering price.

The stock trades around ~₹7,600 Cr of market value, a modest single-digit-to-low-teens earnings multiple typical of a cyclical commodity producer rather than a growth compounder.

The one number that settles the thesis: EBITDA per tonne, across the cycle

For a single-product commodity producer, valuation is not a mix-shift or a re-rating story — it comes down to how many tonnes IMFA makes and the cash margin it earns on each one, averaged across good years and bad. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is EBITDA (or cash margin) per tonne of ferro chrome, through the cycle, multiplied by the volume ramp. The bull case is arithmetically simple: if the captive ore-and-power cost floor holds the per-tonne margin resilient and the KNR-2 plant lifts volumes from ~267,000 t toward ~400,000+ t, then mid-cycle earnings step up structurally — more tonnes at a protected margin. The bear case attacks the same number from two sides: a deep, prolonged China-led price trough that compresses per-tonne margin, or a scaling problem where the extra tonnes increasingly need bought-in ore and grid power that erode the very cost floor. Watch two things each result: the realised margin per tonne, and whether captive ore and power are keeping pace with the higher volumes. Everything else — the dividend, the multiple, the currency — is downstream of that.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A low-cost integrated producer, growing volume into a price it doesn't control.

IMFA enters FY27 in a stronger shape than it has held in years: a deleveraged-then-purposefully-re-geared balance sheet, a record ore-raising year behind it, a bought-in plant taking capacity past half a million tonnes, and the same captive ore-and-power moat that has carried it through every prior down-cycle. The debate is not whether the business works — sixty years of integrated ferro chrome says it does — but whether the volume ramp lands while the ferro-chrome cycle behaves.

The bull case

  • Structural cost floor — captive chrome ore and ~205 MW of captive power keep cost per tonne below non-integrated rivals, so IMFA earns a spread even at the bottom of the cycle.
  • Real volume growth — the Tata Steel KNR-2 plant lifts capacity past 0.5 MTPA; guided output roughly doubles to ~400,000 t (FY27) and ~475–500k t (FY28).
  • Cycle leverage — in a stainless up-cycle, more tonnes at a protected margin compound into a sharp earnings step-up; the Q4 FY26 profit jump is a preview.
  • Clean, generous returns — ~17% ROE, a meaningful dividend, and a modest cyclical multiple leave room if mid-cycle earnings re-base higher.
  • Scarce, hard-to-copy assets — captive chromite leases and integrated power can't be quickly replicated by a new entrant; the moat has a long history.

The bear case

  • Price-taker, China-hostage — the ferro-chrome price is set by global (largely Chinese) stainless demand and quoted in dollars; a prolonged China slump hits revenue and margin regardless of how well IMFA runs.
  • Single product, single market — one alloy, ~90% of it into stainless; no second end-market to cushion a downturn.
  • Scaling could dilute the moat — feeding a 0.5-MTPA machine may increasingly need bought-in ore and grid power, eroding the captive cost advantage.
  • Ore cost creep & lease risk — deeper underground mining raises ore cost per tonne; lease renewals and Odisha auctions could get expensive.
  • Acquisition debt into a cyclical — ~₹958 Cr of borrowings taken on to grow; well-timed if the cycle firms, a burden if it rolls over. Forex adds another uncontrollable swing.
The bottom line

IMFA has done the two things a cyclical commodity producer can actually control: it built the lowest-cost position it can — owning the ore and the power — and it repaired its balance sheet so it could grow from strength rather than distress. What it cannot control is the price of what it sells, set in Chinese stainless mills and in dollars, and that will always be the weather it works in. The next two years turn on one checkable arc: can the Tata Steel plant lift output toward 400,000 tonnes while captive ore and captive power keep pace, so that IMFA is selling more tonnes at a still-protected margin? Watch the FY27 production number against the ~400,000 t target, and watch the cash margin per tonne alongside it — commissioning the volume without cracking the cost floor is the whole game. Get both, and a deleveraged low-cost producer re-bases its mid-cycle earnings higher; miss on either, and IMFA is once again a well-run company waiting on a price it does not set.
