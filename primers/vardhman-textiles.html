00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Vardhman Textiles takes raw cotton and man-made fibre and spins it into yarn — then weaves and processes a big chunk of that yarn into fabric, and also runs sewing-thread, acrylic-fibre and garment businesses. It is India's largest producer and exporter of cotton and blended yarn, spinning on over 1.25 million spindles, and among the country's top-three woven-fabric makers. It is run from Ludhiana, Punjab, by the founding Oswal family, and sells into 75+ countries.

Three facts frame the whole business:

  • It sits at the front of the textile chain, where the commodity swings live. Yarn spinning is capital-heavy and cyclical: cotton is roughly 60% of the cost of a kilo of yarn, so earnings ride on the gap between what cotton costs and what yarn sells for — the "cotton-to-yarn spread." Widen it and margins soar; crush it and they collapse.
  • The same mills earn wildly different profits year to year. In the FY22 cotton boom, Vardhman earned a record profit near ₹1,700 Cr at a ~27% EBITDA margin. Two years later, on almost identical revenue, profit fell to roughly ₹600 Cr. FY26 landed at ₹740 Cr. Same spindles, same people — the spread did the moving.
  • A cyclical business on an un-cyclical balance sheet. Textiles are usually leveraged and fragile; Vardhman runs the opposite — net debt to equity of ~0.07, a large treasury, and enough self-funded firepower to keep adding spindles and looms through a downturn. That balance-sheet quality is the reason it survives cycles that sink weaker spinners.
In plain terms

A yarn spinner is a commodity converter: it buys a farm crop (cotton), turns it into an industrial input (yarn), and pockets the margin in between. That margin is not something Vardhman fully controls — it is set by cotton harvests, global apparel demand and Chinese and Bangladeshi competition. What Vardhman does control is being the biggest, most integrated and best-financed player in the room: it spins more yarn than anyone in India, weaves and dyes a large slice of it into higher-value fabric, and carries almost no debt into the bad years. So the yearly profit is a cyclical number the market can't forecast — but the survival and the share-gain are structural.

The textile value chain, in five steps

No industry knowledge assumed — just follow a cotton boll to a shirt.

Everything Vardhman does lives on one production line that turns fibre into cloth. Understanding this chain — and knowing exactly where Vardhman sits on it — is most of the analysis. There are five stages, and value (and difficulty) rises at each step.

The further right you travel, the less commodity-like and the higher the margin — but also the more design, branding and labour it takes. Vardhman's chosen ground is the capital-intensive middle: yarn and fabric.

Reading the chain

  • Stage 1 — Fibre. Cotton (natural) or man-made fibre like polyester and acrylic. Vardhman is a huge buyer of cotton, and — unusually — a maker of one man-made fibre (acrylic). It does not farm cotton, so the crop's price is an input it must live with.
  • Stage 2 — Spinning (yarn). Machines draw and twist fibre into a continuous thread. This is Vardhman's heartland: 1.25 million+ spindles producing cotton, blended and specialty yarns. It is a scale-and-efficiency game measured in spindles, utilisation and power cost.
  • Stage 3 — Weaving (greige fabric). Yarn is interlaced on looms into raw, undyed "greige" cloth. Vardhman runs ~1,300 shuttleless looms and is a top-three Indian woven-fabric maker with grey capacity around 220 million metres a year.
  • Stage 4 — Processing. Greige fabric is bleached, dyed, printed and finished into sale-ready cloth (processed capacity ~180 million metres a year). This is where a spinner starts to look like a branded fabric supplier — higher margin, more repeat customers.
  • Stage 5 — Garments. Cutting and stitching finished fabric into apparel. Labour-intensive and design-led; Vardhman has a modest garment arm but this is mostly the domain of dedicated apparel exporters and brands.
Why the chain shape matters for the shares

Two rules govern this industry. First, margin and stability rise as you move right — a commodity yarn deal is re-priced every month against cotton; a processed-fabric programme for a named apparel brand is stickier and better-paid. Second, capital intensity peaks in the middle — spinning and weaving mills swallow enormous investment in machinery and power, which is exactly why they are cyclical and why scale wins. Vardhman deliberately owns the capital-heavy middle (Stages 2–4) rather than the commodity crop or the fashion-risk garment end. That choice is the whole strategy: be the low-cost, integrated supplier of yarn and fabric that the garment makers and brands buy from.

02

Spinning economics: the four levers of a yarn mill

The handful of numbers that decide whether a spinner prints money or bleeds it.

A spinning mill is, financially, a spread business wrapped around a fixed-cost factory. It buys cotton, converts it to yarn, and keeps the difference — minus power, labour and depreciation. Because the factory costs roughly the same whether it runs flat-out or half-empty, four levers explain almost every rupee of profit.

The four levers of spinning profitability
LeverWhat it means (and why it swings the P&L)
Cotton-to-yarn spreadThe gap between yarn selling price and cotton cost. Cotton is ~60% of yarn cost, so a small move in the spread is a huge move in margin. This is the master lever.
Capacity utilisationWhat share of the 1.25 M spindles are actually spinning. Fixed costs don't shrink when demand does, so idle spindles bleed money; full mills earn operating leverage.
Power & energy costSpinning is power-hungry. Cheap, captive or renewable power can be the difference between a competitive mill and a loss-maker — a permanent line item, not a one-off.
Product / value-add mixFiner counts, blended and specialty yarns, and processed fabric earn more and are less commoditised than plain grey cotton yarn. Mix lifts the average realisation per kilo.
The spread, made concrete

Picture a mill that buys cotton at ₹100 and sells the yarn made from it at ₹140 — a ₹40 spread. If cotton stays at ₹100 but yarn weakens to ₹125, the spread halves to ₹25, and because conversion costs (power, wages, interest, depreciation) are fixed at, say, ₹20, the profit per kilo collapses from ₹20 to ₹5 — a 75% fall in profit from a ~11% fall in yarn price. Reverse it in a shortage year and profit multiplies. That leverage — tiny price moves, giant profit swings — is the single most important thing to understand about a spinner. Volume barely explains the earnings; the spread explains almost all of it.

Why cotton is the plot, not the backdrop

  • Cotton is a farm commodity — its price rides on monsoon rains, acreage, global harvests, export policy and speculation. Vardhman is a price-taker on its biggest cost.
  • India prices cotton its own way. A government Minimum Support Price (MSP) sets a floor, and an import duty on cotton (a long-running ~11% levy, periodically debated and relaxed) can push domestic cotton above world prices — a structural cost handicap versus duty-free Bangladeshi or Vietnamese rivals. When Indian cotton trades expensive, Indian spinners' spreads suffer even if global yarn demand is fine.
  • Timing risk is real. Spinners hold months of cotton inventory. If cotton falls sharply after they've bought high, they take inventory losses even before selling a kilo of yarn — a hidden way the cycle bites.
The generalist's trap: a spinner's revenue line looks stable and boring, which tempts you to value it on steady sales. That is exactly the mistake. Revenue is roughly flat across the cycle because volume is capped by capacity — but profit can triple or third on the spread alone. Value a spinner on its sales and you will call it cheap at the top and expensive at the bottom, every single time.
03

The company and the Oswal family

From one Ludhiana spinning mill in 1965 to India's yarn leader.

Vardhman is a founder-family enterprise, built and still steered by the Oswals. S.P. Oswal started with a single spinning unit in Ludhiana in 1965 and spent six decades forward-integrating — into fabric, processing, sewing thread, acrylic fibre and, once, steel — turning a small mill into a ~$1.4-billion textile group. The promoter family holds a firm ~65% of the equity, much of it through the listed holding company Vardhman Holdings.

Timeline

  • 1965
    S.P. Oswal founds Vardhman in Ludhiana, Punjab — a single cotton-spinning mill (Mahavir Spinning Mills), the seed of the group.
  • 1980s–90s
    Adds scale in spinning, then forward-integrates into weaving and fabric processing — moving up the chain from pure yarn toward finished cloth.
  • 1990s
    Sewing-thread joint venture with American & Efird (A&E) of the US — the future Vardhman Yarns & Threads — puts a consumer-facing thread business inside a B2B yarn group.
  • 1999
    Sets up Vardhman Acrylics at Bharuch with Japan's Marubeni & Exlan — a rare Indian foray into making man-made (acrylic) fibre.
  • 2010
    Demerges the steel business into a separately listed company, Vardhman Special Steels — untangling the legacy diversification from the textile core.
  • FY22
    The cotton boom delivers a record profit near ₹1,700 Cr at a ~27% EBITDA margin — the high-water mark of the cycle.
  • FY24
    The downcycle: on almost unchanged revenue, profit falls to roughly ₹600 Cr as the spread compresses.
  • FY26
    Revenue ~₹9,652 Cr, PAT ₹740 Cr; ~50,000 spindles and ~100 looms under addition; the India–UK FTA reshapes the export outlook.

Ownership & control

  • Promoters (~65%): the Oswal family, chiefly via Vardhman Holdings Limited (which alone owns ~28.85% of Vardhman Textiles). A high, stable promoter stake means the family carries the cyclical pain and the cyclical reward alongside minority shareholders.
  • Leadership: S.P. Oswal as Chairman & Managing Director has run the group for essentially its entire history — a conservative, low-leverage, long-horizon operator, honoured with the Padma Bhushan for industry.
  • Balance sheet: very lightly geared (net debt/equity ~0.07, debt/EBITDA ~1.2×) with a sizeable investment book — the financial fortress that lets a cyclical business keep investing through downturns.
The surprise hiding in the structure

People often describe Vardhman as "the textile company that also makes steel." That is out of date, and the correction is instructive. Vardhman demerged its steel arm in 2010 into a separately listed firm, Vardhman Special Steels — which serves the auto industry with alloy steel (with Japan's Aichi Steel as a technical partner). Today Vardhman Textiles simply holds ~24% of that company as an associate; steel is not a division of the textile business at all. So the group's real complexity isn't steel — it's the quieter fact that a giant B2B yarn maker also owns a consumer-recognised sewing-thread brand (via the A&E venture) and one of India's few acrylic-fibre plants. The diversification is on the fibre-and-thread side, not the metal side.

Five businesses, one dominated by yarn — with fabric the growth story.

Under the single listed company sit several businesses. Yarn is the core — roughly half of revenue — with fabric the second engine and a growing share, plus smaller sewing-thread, acrylic-fibre and garment lines. (Steel, as noted, is now an outside associate, not a segment.)

The businesses, one by one

  • Yarn — the flagship. India's largest producer and exporter, spinning cotton, blended (poly-cotton, poly-viscose) and specialty/dyed yarns across a wide count range on 1.25 M+ spindles. This is the commodity-cyclical heart of the P&L — and the base that everything else is built on.
  • Fabric — the value-add engine. Weaving (~1,300 shuttleless looms) plus processing (bleaching, dyeing, printing, finishing) turns yarn into sale-ready woven fabric. Top-three in India, with grey capacity ~220 mn metres and processed ~180 mn metres a year. Fabric is stickier and better-margin than commodity yarn — the direction Vardhman is deliberately pushing.
  • Sewing thread — the hidden consumer brand. Through its venture with American & Efird (one of the world's biggest thread makers), Vardhman is among India's largest sewing-thread manufacturers — the industrial thread that stitches garments, shoes and upholstery worldwide. A rare piece of brand and pricing power inside an otherwise B2B group.
  • Acrylic fibre — the man-made outlier. A Bharuch plant (JV heritage with Marubeni/Exlan of Japan) making acrylic staple fibre — used in sweaters, blankets and blended yarns. Small, but it makes Vardhman a fibre producer, not just a fibre buyer, on one product line.
  • Garments — the toe in Stage 5. A modest apparel-making arm; a foothold in finished product rather than a major profit centre today.
Why the mix moves: when you read that "yarn is ~52% of revenue," remember that share is not fixed. In a boom year yarn prices spike and yarn's revenue share swells; in a soft year fabric and thread hold up better and their share rises. The direction of travel Vardhman wants is clear — more fabric and processed, value-added products, less pure commodity grey yarn — because that is what dampens the cycle and lifts the average margin.
05

Scale and integration as the edge

In a commodity business, being the biggest and the most joined-up is the moat.

Yarn is a near-commodity: a kilo of 30s cotton yarn from Vardhman is broadly interchangeable with one from a rival. So how does the biggest spinner earn a durable edge? Not through a secret product, but through scale, vertical integration and cost discipline — the three things that let it undercut smaller mills and out-last them through downturns.

Three sources of durable advantage

  • Scale economics. Buying cotton by the shipload, running the newest high-speed machinery, spreading fixed overheads and R&D across the largest volume in India — all of it lowers cost per kilo. In a spread business, the low-cost producer keeps earning at prices that push marginal mills into losses.
  • Vertical integration. Owning the chain from yarn to processed fabric means Vardhman captures margin at each step and can flex between selling yarn or converting it into higher-value fabric depending on which spread is better that quarter. It also insulates fabric customers from yarn-supply risk — Vardhman is its own supplier.
  • Cost and power discipline. Decades of investment in captive and renewable power, modern equipment and efficient plants keep the conversion cost low — the quiet, unglamorous line that decides who survives when the spread thins.
The integration payoff, in one idea

A pure spinner lives and dies on one spread (cotton-to-yarn). Vardhman lives on several — cotton-to-yarn, yarn-to-greige, greige-to-processed — and can lean toward whichever is healthiest. When commodity yarn is weak but branded apparel demand is firm, it pushes more output into processed fabric and thread; when yarn spreads are fat, it sells more yarn. That optionality doesn't eliminate the cycle, but it smooths it — and it is only available to a player big enough to own every stage. Scale buys the integration; integration buys the resilience.

The cotton cycle — the earnings driver that dwarfs everything else

The single most important idea for anyone valuing this stock.

If you learn one thing about Vardhman, learn this: its profit is a cotton-cycle number, not a growth number. The revenue line barely moves across years, because volume is capped by installed capacity. What moves — violently — is the profit, and it moves on the cotton-to-yarn spread. Between FY22 and FY24, Vardhman's revenue moved by low single digits — but its profit fell roughly two-thirds and its EBITDA margin halved from ~27% to ~13%. Almost none of that was volume. All of it was the spread.
The device that separates this business from a "grower"

Take FY22 and FY24 side by side. Nearly identical revenue (around ₹9,400–9,600 Cr each). Same 1.25 million spindles, same looms, same plants, same management. Yet FY22 earned a ~27% EBITDA margin and a record profit, while FY24 earned ~13% and roughly a third of that profit. The entire gap is the cotton-to-yarn spread widening in a shortage-driven boom and then collapsing as cotton normalised and global yarn demand softened. That is why you cannot extrapolate a Vardhman year: a great year and a poor year are the same company at two points on a commodity cycle. The correct mental model is a refiner or a metals producer, not a consumer compounder — steady tonnage, wildly swinging margin per tonne.

What actually drove the recent cycle: the FY21–22 boom came from a global cotton and yarn shortage (COVID-era supply disruption, strong pipeline restocking, firm apparel demand) that sent yarn prices — and spreads — to unusual highs. It reversed in FY23–24 as cotton prices stayed elevated in India while global apparel demand cooled and buyers de-stocked, squeezing the spread from both ends. FY25–26 stabilised at a middling, "mid-cycle" spread — better than the trough, far below the boom.
07

China+1, exports and the FTA opening

The structural tailwind that could reshape demand for Indian yarn and fabric.

Layered on top of the cotton cycle is a slower, structural story: global apparel sourcing is shifting toward India. As brands de-risk supply chains away from an over-concentration in China ("China+1"), and as trade deals cut tariffs on Indian textiles, the addressable export market for Indian yarn and fabric widens. Vardhman — with 75+ export markets and ~43% of sales already overseas — is one of the most direct listed ways to play it.

The three forces pulling demand toward India

  • China+1 diversification. Rising Chinese labour costs and buyers' wish to avoid single-country concentration are steering sourcing toward India, Bangladesh, Vietnam and others. India's edge: a full domestic cotton-to-garment chain, of which Vardhman owns the yarn-and-fabric spine.
  • Trade deals. The India–UK FTA removes tariffs across almost all textile and apparel categories, handing Indian exporters a real price advantage in a major market; an EU deal is the larger prize still in negotiation. FTAs turn a cost handicap into a cost edge overnight for the covered products.
  • Policy support. Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) schemes and textile-park initiatives are nudging investment into man-made-fibre and value-added capacity — the parts of the chain where India has historically lagged China.
Where the tailwind helps Vardhman specifically

The China+1 shift mostly lands first on garment exporters — the cut-and-sew factories that fill brand orders. Vardhman sits one step upstream, as their yarn and fabric supplier. That is both a limitation and a strength: it doesn't capture the apparel margin directly, but every incremental garment India exports pulls through more Indian yarn and fabric, and Vardhman is the largest, most reliable domestic source of exactly that. An FTA that boosts UK-bound Indian apparel raises demand for the greige and processed fabric those garments are cut from — Vardhman's Stage 3–4 output. The catch is competition: Bangladesh and Vietnam enjoy duty-free cotton and preferential apparel access of their own, so the India tailwind is real but contested, not automatic.

The honest caveat on exports: export demand is itself cyclical. A US or EU retail slowdown, a strong rupee, or fresh tariff turbulence can stall the order book regardless of the structural China+1 story. The tailwind sets the direction over years; it does not smooth the quarters.
08

The move up the chain — from commodity yarn to value-added fabric

The strategy meant to tame the cycle: sell less plain yarn, more finished cloth.

Vardhman's long-run answer to the cotton cycle is not to escape spinning — it is to convert more of its yarn into higher-value, stickier fabric and specialty products. Every metre of processed fabric sold to a named apparel programme is a metre less exposed to the naked cotton-to-yarn spread, and a step toward the steadier, better-paid end of the chain.

The levers of the up-chain move

  • More fabric, less loose yarn. Growing greige and processed-fabric volumes (top-three Indian scale already) so a larger share of yarn is captured internally and sold as finished cloth.
  • Specialty and blended yarns. Finer counts, dyed yarns, and blends (poly-cotton, viscose) that command better realisations than plain grey cotton yarn and are harder to commoditise.
  • Sustainability and compliance credentials. Certifications (OEKO-TEX, sustainability indices) that global brands demand — a passport into premium, long-term programmes that low-cost commodity rivals can't easily supply.
  • Measured capacity growth. The current ~50,000-spindle and ~100-loom addition (targeted by around FY27) tilts toward value-added output rather than plain-vanilla commodity expansion.
The realistic version of this promise

Mix-shift is a genuine strategy, not a cure. Even at its best, Vardhman remains a spinner at heart — fabric and thread dampen the cycle, they don't delete it, and processing carries its own dye-cost and demand risks. The right expectation is a business whose trough gets shallower and whose average margin drifts up over a decade as value-added share rises, while the yarn core still swings with cotton. The single clearest signal that the strategy is working: the blended EBITDA margin holding a higher floor in soft years than the ~13% FY24 trough — evidence that fabric and specialty are carrying more of the load when commodity yarn is weak.

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, fragmented sector where Vardhman is the yarn heavyweight.

Indian textiles is large, fragmented and intensely competitive, spanning thousands of spinners plus a handful of scaled, listed integrated players. Each rival occupies a slightly different slot on the value chain — pure spinning, home textiles, knits/garments, denim. Vardhman's slot is the biggest yarn maker with deep woven-fabric integration.

How to place Vardhman among these

Two axes separate these companies: how far down the chain they go (yarn → fabric → finished goods → brands), and how much cotton-cycle beta they carry. Vardhman sits as the low-cost, deeply integrated yarn-and-fabric supplier — less finished-goods and brand exposure than Welspun or KPR, but more scale and integration than a pure spinner like Nahar. Its distinguishing feature versus every domestic peer is the fortress balance sheet: near-zero net debt and a big investment book let it keep spending on modernisation and capacity through the exact downturns that force weaker, leveraged mills to cut. In a commodity sector, out-lasting rivals through the trough is itself a competitive weapon.

The permanent external pressure: India's cotton import duty and MSP can leave domestic cotton dearer than the global price, handicapping Indian spinners against Bangladesh and Vietnam, who source cotton duty-free. Policy relief on the duty is a recurring sector ask — a swing factor on competitiveness that sits outside any single company's control.

The metric that settles the case

Do not anchor on the P/E in isolation — for a cyclical, the P/E is highest at the bottom (depressed earnings) and lowest at the top (peak earnings), which is precisely backwards for a naive buyer. The number that actually settles the Vardhman thesis is the cotton-to-yarn spread, expressed as the blended EBITDA margin. At the FY24 trough it was ~13%; in the FY22 boom, ~27%; FY26 sits at ~15%. The entire question is where that margin normalises: a spinner that can hold, say, a high-teens blended margin through the mix shift to fabric and specialty is worth far more than one stuck at 13%. So the disciplined way to value Vardhman is on a mid-cycle margin and a mid-cycle profit — not on this year's, and certainly not on the boom year's. Track the blended EBITDA margin quarter to quarter against the cotton price, and you are watching the only variable that matters.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A best-in-class spinner on a fortress balance sheet — priced against an unforecastable spread.

Vardhman is the highest-quality operator in a low-quality-of-earnings business: the biggest, most integrated, best-financed cotton spinner in India, run conservatively by the family that built it. The investment argument is not about whether it is well-run — it is — but about the two forces it cannot control: the cotton cycle and the global export cycle.

The bull case

  • Scale & integration — India's largest, most vertically integrated yarn-to-fabric maker; the low-cost survivor of every downturn.
  • Fortress balance sheet — near net-cash, large treasury; self-funds capacity through troughs that break leveraged rivals.
  • China+1 & FTA tailwind — structural sourcing shift plus the India–UK FTA widen the export runway for Indian yarn and fabric.
  • Mix-shift to value-add — more processed fabric, specialty yarn and thread should lift the average margin and shallow the troughs.
  • Cyclically cheap optionality — bought on a mid-cycle margin, a spread recovery toward boom levels is pure upside the current price doesn't assume.

The bear case

  • Spread cyclicality — profit can two-thirds in a year on the cotton-to-yarn spread alone; earnings are genuinely unforecastable.
  • Export & demand risk — US/EU retail slowdowns and de-stocking hit the order book regardless of the structural story.
  • Cost handicaps — India's cotton duty/MSP and power costs can leave Indian spinners dearer than Bangladesh/Vietnam.
  • Commodity core — despite the mix shift, most of the business is still near-commodity yarn with limited pricing power.
  • Modest through-cycle returns — ROCE dips to single digits at the trough; the capital-heavy model earns its keep only across a full cycle.
The bottom line

Vardhman rewards a buyer who understands what they own: not a compounder that grows every year, but the strongest, cheapest, most durable spinner in India, whose profit breathes in and out with the cotton cycle and whose balance sheet ensures it is always still standing when the spread turns back up. The way to be wrong is to buy it as a steady grower on a boom-year margin; the way to be right is to buy the scale and the balance sheet at a mid-cycle price and let the cycle do its work. Two concrete markers will tell you which world you're in: the blended EBITDA margin rebuilding above its ~13% FY24 floor, and the ~50,000-spindle, ~100-loom expansion coming on stream around FY27 tilted toward value-added output. Watch the spread and the spindle count in Ludhiana — everything else is commentary.
