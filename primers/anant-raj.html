00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Anant Raj Limited is a Delhi-NCR real-estate developer — the Sarin family's 55-year-old house that has built homes and commercial property across Delhi, Gurugram and Haryana since 1969. That is the base business. What has transformed the stock is a second business bolted onto the first: data centres. On land it already owned, Anant Raj is building power-and-cooling-dense buildings that it leases to cloud and enterprise customers under the "Anant Raj Cloud" brand — riding the same AI-and-digital-infrastructure demand wave as the hyperscalers.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A profitable, near-debt-free developer that quietly turbo-charged. FY26 revenue was ~₹2,512 Cr (up ~22%) and net profit ~₹557 Cr (up ~31%), on a ~29% EBITDA margin. Net debt has been driven from over ₹1,600 Cr a few years ago to near zero, helped by residential cash flows and a ₹1,100 Cr equity raise (QIP). Promoters (the Sarins) hold ~60%.
  • The data-centre arm is the whole re-rating story — and it is still tiny. Data-centre and cloud services earned ~₹176 Cr in FY26 — about 7% of revenue — from 28 MW of live IT capacity. Yet management has laid out a build-out toward 63 MW by December 2026, 117 MW by FY28 and ~307 MW by 2031-32, with a cumulative spend around ₹20,000 crore. The market is paying for that future, not the present.
  • The valuation prices the pivot, not the P&L. At ~₹20,600 Cr market cap on ~₹557 Cr of profit, the stock trades near ~37× trailing earnings — a rich multiple for a real-estate company, and one that only makes sense if the data-centre megawatts get built and filled. The board has even formed a committee to study splitting the two businesses apart.
In plain terms

Anant Raj does two very different things with the same asset — land in and around Delhi. It can sell a plot of that land once, as apartments or floors, and book a one-time profit; or it can keep the land, build on it, and rent it forever — as offices, IT parks, and now as data centres leased by the megawatt. For decades it was mostly the first kind of company: a sleepy land-holder. The bet the market is now making is that Anant Raj becomes the second kind — a long-lease digital-infrastructure landlord — fast enough to justify a price that its current earnings, still overwhelmingly from selling homes, do not yet support.

01

How a property developer actually earns

No real-estate background needed — two engines, and the numbers that measure each.

A real-estate developer is, at heart, a business that turns land into buildings and buildings into money — but it can do that in two structurally different ways, and Anant Raj does both. Understanding the difference is the key that unlocks the whole company.

Engine one — development (sell it once)

The developer acquires land, obtains approvals, builds apartments / floors / plots, and sells them to buyers. The cash comes in as customers book units and pay in instalments as construction progresses. This is lumpy, one-time revenue: once a project is sold and handed over, that income is gone and the developer needs the next project. The metrics that matter here:

  • Pre-sales / bookings (booking value) — the rupee value of units sold in a period, whether or not the revenue is recognised yet. This is the truest read on demand.
  • Collections — the cash actually received from those buyers as construction hits milestones.
  • Saleable area (msf) — million square feet of product available to sell; Anant Raj has ~11.4 msf of ongoing and planned residential projects.
  • Land bank — the acres held in reserve. It is the raw material; a developer with cheap, well-located, freehold land has a decades-long head start.

Engine two — annuity (keep it, rent it)

Instead of selling, the developer holds commercial property — offices, IT parks, retail, hotels — and earns rent, year after year. This is recurring, predictable revenue: lower upside per building than a sale, but it never stops and it compounds. The metric here is simply the rental / annuity income and the occupancy of the leased assets. Data centres, as we'll see, are an annuity business in a digital wrapper.

The developer's core tension

Development throws off big, irregular profits but forces you to keep finding and selling new land — you are always running to stand still. Annuity throws off smaller, steady rent but builds a base that grows every year and smooths out the property cycle. Most Indian developers lean heavily on development because it recycles cash faster; the mature ones build an annuity book alongside it for stability. Anant Raj's strategic move is to convert a chunk of its land bank from the "sell once" bucket into the highest-value annuity asset available today — a data centre, which rents for far more per square foot than an ordinary office.

Why net debt is the developer's vital sign: building is capital-hungry and revenue is lumpy, so developers routinely carry heavy borrowing — and a downturn that stalls sales can turn that debt lethal (India's 2018–2020 property shakeout buried several over-leveraged builders). A developer that has driven net debt to near zero, as Anant Raj has, has bought itself the freedom to fund an expensive new business without betting the company. Keep one eye on net debt throughout this primer.
02

The company and the Sarins

From the Asiad Village to a data-centre park — one family, three chapters.

Anant Raj is a promoter-run family business, controlled by the Sarin family for its entire history. It began not as a glamorous listed developer but as a construction contractor, and the shift into data centres is the third act of a story that has already reinvented itself twice.

Timeline

  • 1969
    Ashok Sarin founds the Anant Raj Group — named after his father, Lala Anant Ram Sarin — as a construction firm taking on contracts for the DDA and government departments in Delhi.
  • 1969–1990
    Builds and delivers ~30,000 homes across Delhi — Rohini, East of Kailash, Sheikh Sarai, Katwaria Sarai — and the landmark Asiad Village Complex. Establishes a large, low-cost freehold land bank in and around the capital.
  • 2000s
    Second act: shifts from contractor to developer and landlord — builds IT parks, commercial buildings, hospitality and townships across Delhi and Haryana, accumulating annuity assets on owned land.
  • ~2018–2021
    Carries meaningful debt (net debt ~₹1,626 Cr in FY21) and trades as a low-profile, land-heavy stock near book value. Begins a deliberate de-leveraging and asset-monetisation drive.
  • 2021–2022
    Third act begins: enters data centres, commissioning its first facility at Manesar and launching the "Anant Raj Cloud" digital-infrastructure business on existing land.
  • FY24
    Reports PAT of ~₹266 Cr — its highest in 15 years; the data-centre narrative takes hold and the stock begins a dramatic multi-year re-rating.
  • FY25
    Revenue ~₹2,060 Cr, PAT ~₹426 Cr; raises ₹1,100 Cr via QIP and prepays debt, moving toward a net-cash balance sheet.
  • FY26
    Revenue ~₹2,512 Cr, PAT ~₹557 Cr; 28 MW of data-centre capacity live earning ~₹176 Cr; sets up a Singapore cloud unit, signs a ₹4,500 Cr Andhra Pradesh MoU, and forms a board committee to weigh a demerger of realty and data centres.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters — the Sarin family — hold ~60%. A high, aligned stake: the family's own wealth rides on the same shares outside investors own. The flip side is a classic promoter-controlled firm where minority holders must trust the family's capital-allocation calls.
  • A next-generation management bench. Amit Sarin leads the company (three decades in real estate, infrastructure and finance); Ashim Sarin oversees construction, IT parks, hospitality and the data-centre build; Aman Sarin and, more recently, Anish Sarin (appointed a whole-time director) round out the operating team. Founder Ashok Sarin remains the group's patriarch.
  • Balance sheet: near-zero net debt after the QIP and years of de-leveraging — the platform that lets a mid-cap developer contemplate a ₹20,000 Cr data-centre programme without cratering its credit.
03

The base business: homes and annuity

The still-dominant engine — what pays the bills today and funds the pivot.

Strip away the excitement and Anant Raj is still, in FY26, mostly a real-estate developer: the great majority of its ~₹2,512 Cr revenue comes from selling and renting property in the Delhi-NCR / Haryana region. This is the cash engine that both feeds shareholders and quietly bankrolls the data-centre build.

The land bank — the family silver

Anant Raj holds one of the more valuable freehold land portfolios in Delhi-NCR — roughly 300 acres across prime pockets, including the Golf Course Extension Road corridor in Gurugram and parcels in South Delhi. Much of this was acquired decades ago at a tiny fraction of today's prices. Two things make this land the strategic centre of the whole company: it is largely freehold (owned outright, not leasehold, so cleaner to develop or convert), and it sits where both premium homebuyers and data-centre power infrastructure want to be.

Residential development — the "sell once" engine

  • The flagship — Anant Raj Estate, Gurugram. A large gated township on Sector 63A / Golf Course Extension Road, rolled out in phases (Phase IV under way; Phase V approvals expected in FY27). Products span plots, independent floors and apartments aimed at the premium NCR buyer.
  • Recent launches. "The Estate Apartments" (launched Q1 FY26, ~0.40 msf, ~₹750 Cr estimated revenue) and "The Estate Residences" (248 four-BHK units across ~5.4 acres). In total, ~11.4 msf of ongoing and planned residential projects carry an estimated ~₹2,886 Cr of revenue potential.
  • Why premium, not mass. High-end NCR homes carry fatter margins and stronger pricing power, and they monetise the prime land bank at its highest value — which is exactly why realty EBITDA margins have widened as the mix has richened.

Commercial & annuity — the "keep it, rent it" engine

Alongside sales, Anant Raj holds and operates commercial and IT-park assets that throw off rent. This annuity base is modest today but is the conceptual bridge to data centres: the company already knows how to build, hold and lease income-producing property. The data-centre business is that same landlord instinct pointed at the most lucrative tenant of the decade — a hyperscaler that needs power and floor space, not a corner office.

The quiet cross-subsidy

Here is the mechanism that makes Anant Raj unusual among data-centre builders. Pure data-centre companies must raise huge debt or private capital to fund construction before a single megawatt earns rent. Anant Raj instead runs a profitable residential business that generates cash every quarter, and channels a slice of that cash — plus the one-time QIP — into the data-centre capex. So the developer's oldest, most cyclical activity (selling flats) is the thing keeping the new, capital-hungry activity (building data centres) off the debt market. It is a genuinely different funding model from a standalone operator's.

04

What a data centre business really is

The single most important concept in this primer — explained from scratch.

A data centre is a building purpose-built to house computer servers — the machines that run websites, apps, cloud software and, increasingly, artificial-intelligence models. But financially, a data-centre business is not a technology company at all. It is a very specialised landlord: it builds the building, feeds it enormous amounts of electricity, keeps it cool, and then rents out capacity to companies whose servers live inside.

Why it's rented by the megawatt, not the square foot

An ordinary office is priced by floor area. A data centre is priced by power — measured in megawatts (MW) of "IT load", the amount of electricity the tenant's servers can draw. That's because the scarce, expensive thing in a data centre is not floor space; it's reliable, cooled power. Racks of AI chips are astonishingly power-hungry, so a customer's needs are quoted as "I need 10 MW", and the operator earns rent per MW. This is why "28 MW live, 307 MW planned" — not square feet — is the yardstick for Anant Raj's data-centre business.

The operator's product is "powered, cooled, always-on space." The tenant supplies the IT. That is why a land-and-power-rich developer — not a software firm — can credibly build one.

Colocation vs cloud — Anant Raj does both

  • Colocation — the pure-landlord model. The customer (a bank, an enterprise, a cloud provider) brings its own servers; Anant Raj provides the building, power and cooling and charges rent per MW on a long contract. Lower risk, steadier, lower return. This is the bulk of the plan (~49 of the near-term 63 MW).
  • Cloud — a step up the value chain. Anant Raj buys the servers itself and rents out computing capacity (storage, GPUs, virtual machines) to customers — competing, in a small way, with the likes of AWS. Higher potential margin, but more capital and operating risk. A smaller slice (~6–14 MW) of the near-term plan, with a Singapore unit set up to chase global cloud demand.
Why this looks like real estate with a digital coat

A colocation data centre is, economically, an annuity asset: build once, sign a multi-year lease, collect rent per MW for a decade or more, with high operating margins once the facility fills. The revenue is recurring and contracted — closer to a toll road than to a software product. That is precisely why a company that already owns land, knows construction, and understands long-lease commercial property can move into it — and why data-centre rent per square foot dwarfs what the same land would earn as an ordinary office. The catch is the front-loaded cost: the power infrastructure, cooling plant and fit-out are enormously expensive, and the rent only starts once a tenant signs and the rent-free fit-out period ends.

Anant Raj Cloud: the megawatt ramp

The capacity build-out that the entire re-rating is priced on — and how early it still is.

Everything the market is excited about lives in one number that must climb over the next several years: commissioned data-centre capacity, in megawatts. Today Anant Raj has 28 MW live across Manesar, Panchkula and its Rai (Sonepat) campus. The published roadmap takes that up an order of magnitude — and the gap between what exists and what is promised is the whole investment debate.

Where the money and the megawatts go

  • Three Haryana campuses anchor the plan. Manesar and Panchkula are operational; the big one is Rai (Sonepat), earmarked to grow to ~200 MW — the bulk of the 307 MW vision. The company has spoken of investing up to ~₹20,000–25,000 crore in Haryana data centres over the build cycle.
  • Beyond Haryana. A ₹4,500 Cr MoU with Andhra Pradesh and a newly-formed Singapore cloud unit signal ambitions beyond the home turf — but these are early-stage intentions, not commissioned capacity.
  • Funded without wrecking the balance sheet. Roughly ₹700 Cr of capital deployed so far, drawn from residential cash flows and the QIP — which is how net debt stayed near zero even as the build began.
A device to size the bet — the "market cap per megawatt" gap

Do one blunt calculation. Anant Raj's ~₹20,600 Cr market value spread across its headline 307 MW plan is roughly ₹67 Cr of market cap for every planned megawatt. But only 28 MW actually earns rent today — so the market is, in effect, paying up front for roughly 279 megawatts that do not yet exist. Turn it around and the point is starker: at ~₹176 Cr of FY26 data-centre revenue, the live 28 MW is generating real but modest income, while the valuation leans on capacity that is years and thousands of crores of capex away. That single gap — 28 MW real versus 307 MW promised — is the clearest way to see both the opportunity and the risk in one line.

The surprise worth sitting with: this is a company that built the Asiad Village in the 1980s and then spent years as a near-book-value land stock — and it re-rated many times over not by selling more flats but by pouring concrete and power around servers on land it already held. And even after all that excitement, the data-centre business is still only ~7% of revenue. The valuation is almost entirely a claim on what the next 279 megawatts will earn, not on what the business earns now.

Why a land-rich NCR developer is well-placed to build them

The three things a data centre needs most — and the two it doesn't have yet.

A data centre needs three scarce ingredients before anything else: land, power and patient capital. It is not a coincidence that a decades-old Delhi-NCR developer is the one attempting this — Anant Raj already sits on two of the three, and its funding model supplies the third.

The land is the moat — and the constraint

Anant Raj's real advantage is unglamorous: it can put a data centre on land whose cost was locked in decades ago, in a location competitors would have to pay dearly to enter. That lowers the capital hurdle and improves the returns on the megawatts it does build. But land and power get you a building, not a business — the value only shows up when tenants sign and the halls fill. The bull case rests on Anant Raj converting a land edge into leased, cash-generating megawatts faster than the doubters expect; the bear case is that owning the land was the easy part.

07

The data-centre wave and the rivals

A genuine boom — but Anant Raj is a minnow among whales.

The demand backdrop is as strong as any theme in Indian infrastructure. India's data-centre capacity is crossing ~1.7 GW in 2026 and is widely expected to scale several-fold — toward ~8 GW by 2030 — driven by cloud adoption, AI and 5G workloads, cheap data consumption, and data-localisation rules that force more of the country's data to be stored on Indian soil. This is the same demand wave powering the hyperscalers and Netweb's AI servers; Anant Raj sells the building, power and cooling those servers sit inside.

But it is a market of giants, and Anant Raj is, for now, a small new entrant. The top operators alone run over a gigawatt of installed capacity between them.

What "competing as a minnow" actually means

Anant Raj does not need to beat STT or Adani to win — the market is growing fast enough that many operators can add capacity. What it needs is to fill its own megawatts: sign colocation tenants, keep utilisation high, and convert its land edge into contracted rent before the giants' supply floods its home region and compresses pricing. The risk is real: when much larger, better-capitalised players build aggressively, a small operator can find its halls filling slowly or at thinner rents than the plan assumed. The theme is a tailwind for everyone; whether it lifts this boat depends on execution Anant Raj has not yet demonstrated at scale.

The one metric that settles the thesis

Ignore, for a moment, every other number and watch commissioned-and-leased data-centre capacity in megawatts — and the revenue it produces as a share of the total. Today that is 28 MW live and ~7% of revenue. The bull case is only validated if commissioned MW climbs toward the 63 / 117 targets and those megawatts fill with paying tenants, pushing data-centre revenue from ~7% toward a third or more of the P&L over the coming years. Capacity that is built but empty, or targets that slip, breaks the case regardless of how the residential book performs. Rising home sales can carry the earnings; only rising leased megawatts can justify the multiple. That is the number to track every quarter.

At ~37× trailing earnings, Anant Raj is priced well above a typical developer — because a slice of the market treats it as an early-stage digital-infrastructure company. If the megawatts arrive and lease up, today's multiple can look reasonable in hindsight; if the ramp disappoints, the stock re-rates back toward a realty multiple on realty earnings, and the data-centre premium evaporates.

09

The demerger question

Two businesses in one shell — and a board now openly weighing a split.

In 2026 the board took a step that crystallises the whole story: it formed a committee to evaluate demerging the real-estate and data-centre businesses into separate companies. Whether or not it happens, the very fact of it tells you how differently these two engines are now viewed.

Why split them?

  • They are fundamentally different animals. Realty is cyclical, capital-recycling, valued on a modest earnings multiple or on net asset value. A data-centre business is a long-lease, capital-intensive, infrastructure asset that investors value on capacity and contracted cash flows — often at far richer multiples.
  • Value visibility. Bundled together, the market applies one blended multiple to a mix it finds hard to price. Separated, a pure data-centre company could attract dedicated digital-infrastructure investors (and capital) at its own valuation, while the realty business is valued cleanly on its own merits.
  • Focus and funding. Independent entities can pursue their own capital-allocation and growth strategies — the data-centre arm could raise infrastructure capital directly, rather than competing for the group's cash with a homebuilding pipeline.
Why this is the pivotal corporate event to watch

A demerger would let investors do explicitly what many are already doing implicitly — pay a high price for the data-centre megawatts and a normal price for the flats. It could unlock value if the parts are worth more apart than together. But it also removes the elegant cross-subsidy described earlier: today the residential cash flows quietly fund the data-centre capex inside one balance sheet. Split them, and the data-centre company must fund its enormous build on its own credit and equity — which is precisely where a capital-hungry, still-early operator is most exposed. The demerger is therefore not a footnote; it is the corporate decision that will most shape how the risk and the reward get divided between the two halves of Anant Raj.

10

Risks and execution

The honest ledger — where a good story could still disappoint.

Anant Raj carries two overlapping risk profiles at once: those of a cyclical NCR developer, and those of an unproven, capital-hungry data-centre entrant priced for success. Both deserve a clear look.

  • Valuation ahead of the P&L. The clearest risk. A data-centre business at ~7% of revenue is carrying a ~37× multiple. If the megawatt ramp slips — and data-centre builds slip often, on power, approvals or tenant timing — the premium can unwind quickly toward a realty valuation.
  • Execution & capital intensity. Going from 28 MW to 117 MW to 307 MW means thousands of crores of capex, securing hundreds of MW of reliable (ideally green) power, and building operating capability the company hasn't yet proven at scale. Commissioning capacity is one job; filling it with paying tenants is a harder, separate one.
  • Formidable, better-funded competition. STT, Nxtra, CtrlS, Sify, AdaniConneX, Reliance/Yotta and NTT are larger and deeper-pocketed. Aggressive supply from giants could slow Anant Raj's lease-up or squeeze rents in its home region.
  • Real-estate cyclicality & NCR concentration. The cash engine is premium homes in one region. An NCR property downturn would hit both the earnings and the very cash flows funding the data-centre build — a correlated double-hit.
  • Regulatory & approval dependence. Both businesses live on approvals — RERA and local sanctions for housing; land-use, power and clearances for data centres. Delays are a normal feature of Indian real estate, not an exception.
  • Promoter-controlled capital allocation. With ~60% family ownership, minority holders are trusting the Sarins' judgement on a bet-the-narrative pivot and a possible demerger — high alignment, but concentrated decision-making.
The verification caveat: the data-centre capacity targets (63 / 117 / 307 MW) and investment figures (~₹20,000–25,000 Cr) are management guidance and plans, not commissioned assets, and have shifted upward over time. Treat the 28 MW that is live and earning as the hard number, and the rest as intent that must still be executed, funded and leased. Cross-check each milestone against results and concalls as they land.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A real land edge and a real balance sheet — attached to a promise the megawatts must keep.

Anant Raj is a well-run, near-debt-free developer sitting on cheap, prime NCR land, attempting one of the most ambitious pivots in Indian mid-caps: turning that land into an AI-era data-centre platform. The business is sound; the question is whether the second engine scales fast enough to earn the price the market has already awarded it.

The bull case

  • A structural land edge — ~300 acres of prime freehold NCR land, largely bought decades ago, that can host data centres competitors would pay dearly to build near.
  • Self-funded pivot — profitable residential cash flows plus a ₹1,100 Cr QIP bankroll the data-centre capex, keeping net debt near zero.
  • Enormous demand runway — India's data-centre capacity heading toward ~8 GW by 2030 on AI, cloud and data-localisation.
  • Optionality unlock — a possible demerger could crystallise a dedicated, richly-valued data-centre company.
  • Improving fundamentals — rising revenue, ~29% margins, premium-housing mix, aligned promoters.

The bear case

  • Valuation vs reality — ~37× earnings for a business whose data-centre arm is ~7% of revenue; the price leans on capacity that doesn't exist yet.
  • Execution unproven at scale — 28 MW live against a 307 MW plan; commissioning and, harder, leasing the rest is a multi-year, thousands-of-crores task.
  • Giants next door — STT, Nxtra, CtrlS, AdaniConneX, Reliance/Yotta can out-spend and out-scale a new entrant.
  • Correlated downside — an NCR property downturn hits earnings and the cash funding the data-centre build at once.
  • Guidance, not assets — targets have been revised upward and remain plans; power, approvals and tenants can all slip.
The bottom line

Anant Raj has done the hard, unglamorous part any data-centre dream needs: it owns the land, it fixed the balance sheet, and it has a cash engine to fund the build. What it has not yet done is prove that the megawatts arrive on schedule and fill with tenants — and that is exactly what the ~37× multiple is asking it to do. The nearest checkpoint is concrete and dated: 63 MW of commissioned capacity by December 2026, and behind it the Rai (Sonepat) campus scaling toward 200 MW and the board's decision on the demerger. Watch those live-and-leased megawatts climb — the day data-centre revenue stops being a footnote to the housing book is the day this reinvention is real.
