Polycab India, the wire that runs through everything

A complete walk through India's largest wires-and-cables maker — the company whose copper and aluminium run inside almost every new home, factory, metro line and data centre in the country. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Polycab makes the wires and cables that carry electricity and data — from the thin building wires inside your walls to the heavy power cables that feed a factory, a metro line, a solar farm or a data centre. It is India's largest wires-and-cables company, with roughly a 26–27% share of the organised market — about twice the size of its nearest rival — and it has bolted a growing consumer-electrical brand (fans, lights, switches, appliances) on top.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A dominant, debt-free compounder. Revenue grew from ₹12,204 Cr (FY22) to ₹28,884 Cr (FY26) — more than doubling in four years. It earns a ~13.9% EBITDA margin, made ₹2,708 Cr of profit in FY26 (+32%), holds ₹4,190 Cr of net cash (no meaningful debt), and earns a ~33% return on capital. This is a big, high-quality industrial franchise, not a fragile one.
  • The moat is the network, not the wire. A copper cable of a given spec is, physically, a near-commodity. Polycab's edge is ~4,300 dealers/distributors reaching 200,000+ retail outlets, a trusted safety-first brand, and manufacturing scale that lets it produce cheaply and deliver in 24 hours. In wires, the electrician and the local shop pick the brand — and Polycab has spent decades earning that shelf.
  • Two engines, wildly different economics. The core Wires & Cables business (~87% of external sales, ₹25,179 Cr, +33% in FY26) is a scaled, cash-generative near-oligopoly. The FMEG consumer business (~7%, ₹2,069 Cr) is the long-game brand bet — growing fast but still low-margin, chasing an 8–10% EBITDA target by FY30. A smaller EPC (turnkey projects) segment rounds it out.
In plain terms

Think of Polycab as the "Amul of electrical wiring" — it turned a boring, invisible, seemingly-commodity product into a trusted national brand, and it wins because everyone in the chain (the electrician, the shopkeeper, the builder, the government contractor) reaches for Polycab first. It buys copper and aluminium, draws and insulates it into wire and cable, and pushes it out through the densest distribution network in the industry. Because it's genuinely hard to build that network and that trust, Polycab keeps taking share and earns fat margins on a product rivals think of as generic. The open questions: can it turn its consumer-brand ambitions (fans, lights, switches) into real profit, and can it keep its lead now that giants like Adani and the Aditya Birla group are barging into its market?

01

How a cable maker actually earns

No engineering degree required — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

At its heart, Polycab does something simple: it buys metal, shapes and insulates it, and sells it at a mark-up. A wire is a conductor (copper or aluminium) wrapped in insulation (PVC or cross-linked polyethylene, "XLPE"). A cable is many wires bundled and armoured together. The financial trick is that the metal is most of the cost — so the whole game is about volume, mix and the mark-up over metal, not the raw metal price itself.

The only concepts you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for Polycab)
ConductorThe metal that carries current — copper (better conductor, dearer, used in wires & premium cables) or aluminium (cheaper, lighter, used in bulk power cables). Metal is the dominant input cost, so revenue moves up and down with metal prices even when volume is flat.
Value-add / spreadThe margin Polycab earns on top of the pass-through metal cost — for converting metal into a branded, spec-compliant product. This spread, not the metal, is where profit lives. Higher-spec cables (EHV, special cables) earn a fatter spread.
Distribution vs institutionalTwo routes to market. Distribution/retail (dealers → shops → electricians; mostly building wires) is higher-margin and stickier. Institutional (direct to big projects, utilities, EPC contractors) is bulkier, more price-competitive and lower-margin. Mix between them swings blended margin.
Organised vs unorganisedHalf the historical market was small, unbranded, often-unsafe local players. As GST, quality norms (BIS) and safety awareness bite, that share is migrating to branded majors — a structural tailwind that adds to underlying demand growth.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ Volume × (Spread over metal) − Fixed costs. So Polycab grows in three ways — sell more tonnes (volume, driven by the construction and power cycle), improve the mix (more premium cables, more retail wires, more consumer FMEG — each carries a bigger spread), and take market share (from unorganised players and weaker branded rivals). Crucially, because the factories, brand and distribution are largely fixed costs, once a plant is full extra volume drops much more to profit — that's operating leverage. And because metal is passed through to the customer, a swing in the copper price mostly changes the revenue number, not the underlying spread. Watch the spread and the volume, not the copper headline.

02

The company and who controls it

A Partition-migrant family business that became India's cable leader — with one governance scar.

Polycab is a promoter-driven family business, built over three generations by the Jaisinghani family. It began as a single electrical store in Mumbai's Lohar Chawl wholesale bazaar and grew, plant by plant, into the country's dominant wire-and-cable maker — listing only in 2019, decades after it was founded.

Timeline

  • 1964
    Thakurdas Jaisinghani, a Partition-era migrant, opens Sind Electric Stores in Lohar Chawl, Mumbai — the family's entry into the electrical trade.
  • 1983
    Polycab is formally founded by Inder T. Jaisinghani and his brothers; the first factory is set up in Halol, Gujarat — still the manufacturing heartland today.
  • 2008–09
    JV with France's Nexans for specialised cables; enters the EPC (engineering, procurement & construction) business — turnkey electrical projects.
  • 2014
    Launches FMEG — fans, LED lighting, switches, switchgear, solar and conduits — beginning the shift from a pure B2B cable maker toward a consumer electrical brand.
  • Apr 2019
    IPO — raises ₹1,346 Cr; the issue is oversubscribed 50×+; lists on NSE & BSE. A rare, long-established family business finally going public.
  • 2021
    Completes Project LEAP planning — a 5-year plan targeting ₹20,000 Cr revenue by FY26 (achieved a year early); divests its Ryker copper-rod plant to Hindalco.
  • Dec 2023
    Income-tax search across 50+ Polycab premises; the department alleged unaccounted cash sales. The stock fell ~21% in a day. (See governance note below.)
  • FY26
    Record year: revenue ₹28,884 Cr (+29%), PAT ₹2,708 Cr (+32%); ~26–27% organised W&C share; embarks on Project Spring, the next 5-year growth plan.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Jaisinghani family, led by Chairman & MD Inder T. Jaisinghani, hold ~61.5% (as of Mar 2026). High promoter skin-in-the-game, but concentrated family control.
  • Institutions: FIIs ~18%, DIIs ~8%, public ~12% — a well-owned large-cap with meaningful institutional backing.
  • Balance sheet: effectively net cash (₹4,190 Cr net cash at FY26; borrowings only ~₹236 Cr) — self-funding a large capex programme without leverage.
The 2023 governance episode — stated plainly. In December 2023 the Income-Tax Department searched more than 50 Polycab locations and later alleged unaccounted cash sales of ~₹1,000 Cr and related irregularities; the shares dropped sharply on the news. The company denied wrongdoing and continued reporting record, audited results through FY24–FY26, and the market has largely re-rated the stock back to new highs since. For a governance-minded reader it remains a genuine caveat — a reminder that this is a founder-controlled business where the promoter's dealings warrant scrutiny — even as the operating performance has been exceptional. We flag it for completeness, not as a verdict.

The three segments, broken into parts

One dominant B2B engine, one consumer-brand experiment, and a small projects arm.

Polycab reports three businesses, but they are wildly unequal. Wires & Cables is ~87% of external sales and essentially is the company; FMEG is the growth-and-brand bet; EPC is a small, lumpy projects business the company is deliberately shrinking as a share of the mix.

Wires & Cables (W&C) — the core

  • Building wires — the retail, higher-margin product: house wiring sold through dealers to electricians. This is where brand and distribution matter most, and where Polycab is strongest.
  • Power & specialty cables — heavier LT/HT cables for factories, utilities, infrastructure, renewables and data centres, including a new extra-high-voltage (EHV) capability (cables above 220kV) for grid projects.
  • Economics: FY26 W&C EBIT margin ran in the ~13–14% band; the company guides a 12–14% range through the cycle. Q4 dipped to ~13.1% on an unfavourable mix (more low-margin institutional sales, softer exports).

FMEG (Fast-Moving Electrical Goods) — the consumer brand

  • What's in it: fans, LED lighting, switches & switchgear, conduits & pipes, small appliances, and a fast-growing solar products line (now the largest FMEG category, roughly doubling YoY in FY26).
  • Why it exists: it uses the same dealer network and brand to sell higher-frequency, consumer-facing products — extending Polycab from "inside the wall" to "on the wall and in the room."
  • Economics: historically low/volatile margins (mid-single-digit or worse); the FY30 target under Project Spring is an 8–10% EBITDA margin. FY26 growth was strong (+25%; +47% in Q4) but profitability is the unfinished job (see §06).

EPC / Others — the projects arm

  • Turnkey electrical and infrastructure projects (power distribution, transmission, etc.). It is lumpy, working-capital-heavy and lower-return, and Polycab is content to let it shrink as a share of revenue — FY26 EPC revenue fell ~13% even as the group grew 29%. It does, however, keep Polycab close to large infrastructure customers and pull-through cable demand.
04

The distribution moat — why a "commodity" isn't one

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — read this once and Polycab's returns make sense.

Here is the puzzle: a copper wire of a given specification is, physically, almost identical whoever makes it. So how does Polycab earn ~13–14% margins and ~33% returns on capital selling something that looks like a commodity — and keep gaining share year after year? The answer is that in Indian wires & cables, the product is a commodity but the route to the customer is not. The moat is the network and the brand, and it is genuinely hard to copy.

Why the network is a moat, not just a sales channel

  • The electrician decides — and trusts the brand. For house wiring, the homeowner rarely chooses the wire; the electrician or contractor does, and they default to a brand they trust not to fail in the wall (fire and safety are real risks). Decades of reliability make "Polycab" the safe default — a habit that's expensive and slow for a newcomer to dislodge.
  • Shelf space and availability compound. With 200,000+ outlets and 24-hour delivery, Polycab is there when the electrician needs it. A dealer who stocks Polycab, gets fast replenishment and reliable margins has little reason to switch — and a rival must win them one shop at a time.
  • Scale funds the brand that funds the scale. Being ~2× its nearest competitor lets Polycab spend more on advertising, dealer incentives and a 9,600-SKU catalogue — which pulls more demand, which funds more scale. It's a flywheel a sub-scale player can't spin.
  • The organised-sector shift adds a tailwind. GST, tighter BIS quality norms and rising safety awareness are pushing buyers from unbranded local wire to branded product. Polycab estimates it gained ~3–4% organised share in FY26 alone and ~8–9 points since listing — it's the biggest single beneficiary of formalisation.
In plain terms

Building this moat is like building a national road network for one product. Anyone can make a truckload of wire; almost nobody can, from a standing start, sign up 4,300 dealers, get onto 200,000 shop shelves, earn the trust of a million electricians, and deliver anywhere in India within a day. That distribution-plus-trust machine is what lets Polycab charge a premium on a "commodity" and keep taking share — and it's why the market pays up for the stock. It's also exactly what the new giants (§09) will have to replicate, and why doing so is harder than building a factory.

In cables, the factory is table stakes. The dealer, the electrician's trust, and the shelf are the moat — and Polycab has spent forty years building all three.
05

Copper, aluminium and the metal pass-through

Why Polycab's profits are far less hostage to the copper price than the headline suggests.

Copper and aluminium (plus PVC and XLPE insulation) make up the large majority of a cable's cost — roughly two-thirds or more. That sounds terrifying: aren't Polycab's profits at the mercy of the London Metal Exchange? In practice, no — and understanding why is key to understanding the business. The trick is pass-through: Polycab prices its products to move up and down in tandem with metal costs, so it protects the spread (its value-add) rather than betting on the metal.

How the pass-through works

  • Prices track inputs. When copper, aluminium or PVC rise, Polycab raises product prices; when they fall, prices ease. In FY26 it took price hikes of ~18–19% in Q4 to keep pace with input inflation (PVC alone spiked 60–80% in March 2026). The spread it earns for converting metal into a branded product is what it defends.
  • Revenue inflates/deflates with metal — profit less so. A big chunk of Polycab's ~29% FY26 revenue growth was price (metal + hikes), with volume up only low-single-digits in Q4. So high revenue growth can partly be a metal illusion — always look at volume and spread underneath.
  • Hedging & inventory. Polycab hedges copper and aluminium to lock in the spread on orders, cushioning short-term swings. The flip side: sharp metal moves can create inventory gains (when prices rise, cheap old stock sells at new higher prices — a windfall) or losses (when prices fall) — a one-off that can flatter or dent a quarter and should be stripped out to judge underlying margin.
In plain terms

Polycab is less a bet on copper and more a toll on turning copper into trusted, branded, spec-compliant cable. If copper doubles, Polycab's sticker prices roughly double too — its revenue balloons, but the margin it keeps stays broadly intact because it's charging for the conversion, not speculating on the metal. Two things can distort a single quarter, though: (1) if metal jumps faster than it can re-price, margin briefly compresses; and (2) inventory gains/losses as old stock is valued at new prices. Over a full year these wash out. So when you see "revenue up 29%!", the sober question is: how much was volume, how much was metal, and did the spread hold? In FY26 the spread held (EBITDA margin actually rose to ~13.9%), which is the real signal of pricing power.

06

The FMEG bet — building a consumer brand on a B2B chassis

The most debated part of the story: a long, expensive game to become a household electrical brand.

Since 2014 Polycab has been building FMEG — Fast-Moving Electrical Goods: fans, LED lights, switches, switchgear, appliances, conduits and now solar products. The logic is seductive; the execution is hard. It is still only ~7% of revenue and its margins are far below the cables core. FMEG is the part of Polycab that will either become a second big profit engine over the next decade — or remain a persistent margin drag. Both outcomes are live.

The thesis — why do it at all?

  • Leverage the same moat. Polycab already owns the dealer network, the brand and the electrician's trust. FMEG rides that infrastructure to sell more, higher-frequency products to the same shops — a classic distribution-adjacency play (the path Havells walked years earlier).
  • Better long-run economics. Consumer electrical goods can, at scale and with premiumisation, earn structurally higher margins than commodity cable — if the brand and product quality get there. That's the prize.
  • Diversification. It softens Polycab's dependence on the B2B construction/infra cycle by adding steadier consumer demand.

The reality — why it's hard, and the margin drag

  • FMEG is a brand-and-marketing war, fought against entrenched specialists (Havells, Crompton, Bajaj Electricals, Orient, Finolex, V-Guard). Winning shelf and mind-share costs heavy advertising and promotion — which depresses margins for years before they turn.
  • Margins have been thin and volatile. FMEG has run at mid-single-digit EBITDA margins or worse, well below the ~13–14% cables core. Under Project Spring, the guided target is 8–10% EBITDA by FY30 — an admission that today's profitability is inadequate and a multi-year climb.
  • But the top line is compounding. FMEG has grown ~25% CAGR over the past decade; in FY26 it grew ~25% (and ~47% in Q4), with solar emerging as its largest and fastest category. Scale is the precondition for margin — and scale is arriving.
In plain terms

FMEG is Polycab spending cable profits today to buy a consumer brand tomorrow. The bet is that its distribution muscle lets it do to fans, lights and switches what it did to wire — become the trusted default — and eventually earn healthy margins. The risk is that consumer categories are crowded with strong incumbents, so Polycab must keep pouring money into advertising and product, and the promised 8–10% margins keep sitting a few years out. For now, judge FMEG on two things: is revenue still compounding fast (yes), and is the margin trending up toward the FY30 goal (the number to watch each year). It's the option value in the story — potentially large, definitely not free.

07

The demand supercycle

Four structural forces are pulling on wires & cables at the same time — and they don't usually line up.

Polycab sells into almost every kind of construction and electrification in India, so its demand is essentially a bet on the country building things. What makes the current moment unusual is that four powerful drivers are firing simultaneously — housing, infrastructure, electrification and data centres — on top of the organised-sector share shift. Industry estimates put the domestic wires-and-cables market at ~₹80,000 Cr (FY25), heading toward ₹1,15,000 Cr+ by FY28 (roughly low-teens annual growth), with organised players adding it faster still.

In plain terms

Copper cable is the "picks and shovels" of India's build-out — you cannot electrify a home, run a metro, connect a solar farm or power a data centre without it. Normally these cycles ebb and flow separately; right now all four are rising together, and the shift from unbranded to branded product adds an extra few points of growth on top for a leader like Polycab. That's why industry order books and capacity are expanding hard — and also why everyone, including new giants, now wants in (§09). The supercycle is real; the question is how the spoils get divided as capacity floods in.

The cyclicality caveat: "supercycle" cuts both ways. Wires & cables demand is ultimately tied to construction and government capex, which are cyclical. A property downturn, a pause in government spending, or a global slowdown would slow volume growth — and Polycab's premium valuation assumes the good times keep running.
08

Exports and capacity — building a global powerhouse

The newest growth leg, and the capex that has to feed the whole plan.

Two supply-side levers underpin Polycab's ambitions: a growing export business (aiming to make it a global cable player, not just an Indian one) and a large capacity-expansion programme to have the cable to sell as demand rises.

The export ramp

  • What it is: Polycab exports cables internationally, with the US a particular focus — Indian cable exports to the US grew at a ~44% CAGR over FY17–24 as global buyers diversify supply chains. Exports are still a modest share (international was ~5% of consolidated revenue in FY26), so the runway is long.
  • Why it matters: export/overseas cable can carry attractive margins and diversifies away from the domestic cycle. Management frames the ambition as building a "global C&W powerhouse."
  • The watch-item: exports are lumpy and exposed to shipping, tariffs and geopolitics — Middle-East headwinds and softer exports were a drag on the FY26 margin mix in some quarters. It's a real opportunity, but not a smooth line.

The capacity & capex plan

  • Scale of spend: Polycab has guided to ~₹60–80 billion (₹6,000–8,000 Cr) of capex over five years under Project Spring — for capacity expansion, backward integration into inputs, digitisation and ESG.
  • New capacity: greenfield and brownfield expansion at Halol, Gujarat; a new extra-high-voltage (EHV) plant (cables above 220kV, revenue accruing from ~FY27) for the transmission build-out; and dedicated export/special-cable capacity coming online over the next 12–18 months.
  • Backward integration: Polycab is investing further up the value chain (having earlier exited its Ryker copper-rod JV) to secure inputs and protect the spread. Funded entirely from internal cash — no leverage needed.
In plain terms

To ride a demand supercycle you need cable to sell — so Polycab is spending heavily now to add plants (especially high-voltage and export lines) before the demand arrives. Because it's debt-free and highly cash-generative, it can fund this without borrowing, which is a real advantage over weaker rivals in a capacity race. Exports and EHV are the two "up-the-value-curve" bets: higher-spec, higher-spread products that make Polycab less of a commodity player. The risk is timing — if a lot of industry capacity lands just as a demand pause hits, spreads across the whole sector could soften.

The new-entrant shock: when UltraTech and Adani announced cable-and-wire forays in 2025, shares of Polycab, KEI and Finolex fell sharply (KEI –12%+, Polycab –6% in a day) on fears of a price war and margin compression. In plain terms

The bull answer to the giants is exactly §04's moat: a factory can be built in ~2 years, but 4,300 loyal dealers, 200,000 shelves and a million electricians' trust cannot. New entrants will likely take some institutional volume (where relationships and price matter more than brand) faster than they take retail wire (where habit and trust rule). The bear answer is that even a few years of aggressive pricing from a deep-pocketed rival could compress industry margins and slow Polycab's share gains — and a stock at ~57× earnings has priced in continued dominance, so any crack in the moat would hurt the valuation more than the profits. Both can be partly right; watch retail volumes, margins and share each quarter for the real signal.

How to think about the ~57× multiple

At ~₹1.48 lakh Cr market cap on ~₹2,708 Cr of FY26 profit, Polycab trades around 55–58× trailing earnings — a rich multiple for what is, at its core, a metals-conversion business. That price makes sense only if you believe several things at once: the demand supercycle keeps volume compounding, the distribution moat holds off the new giants, FMEG margins actually climb toward 8–10%, and the balance sheet keeps funding growth without dilution. Bulls argue a debt-free, ~33%-ROCE market leader riding a multi-year electrification boom deserves a scarcity premium. Bears argue that (a) the new entrants (Adani, UltraTech) threaten the very dominance the multiple assumes, (b) growth has been more price than volume lately, and (c) a 57× stock has a long way to de-rate on any wobble. The next few years turn on volume growth, spread stability, FMEG margins and market share — the four numbers to track every quarter.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely dominant business — priced for its dominance to last.

Polycab is one of the cleanest quality-compounder stories in Indian industrials: the clear market leader in a structurally growing category, debt-free, high-return, and run by a promoter family with deep skin in the game. The investment debate is almost entirely about price, new competition, and the FMEG payoff — not whether the core business is good.

The bull case

  • Distribution moat — 4,300 dealers, 200,000+ outlets and electrician trust that a factory can't replicate; the biggest winner of the organised-sector shift.
  • Demand supercycle — housing, infra, power grid, renewables and data centres all pulling at once; a multi-year volume runway.
  • Fortress balance sheet — net cash, ~33% ROCE, self-funding ₹6,000–8,000 Cr of capex; can out-invest weaker rivals.
  • Optionality — FMEG toward 8–10% margins, EHV cables, and an export/global ambition each add a leg if they land.
  • Proven execution — beat its Project LEAP revenue target a year early; consistent share gains.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~57× earnings leaves no margin for error; vulnerable to a sharp de-rating on any disappointment.
  • New giants — Adani and UltraTech entering with conglomerate balance sheets could trigger a price war and compress industry margins.
  • Growth quality — recent revenue growth has leaned on metal prices and hikes; volume growth was soft in places.
  • FMEG still unproven on profit — a decade in, margins remain thin; the 8–10% target keeps sitting a few years out.
  • Governance & cyclicality — the 2023 tax-search episode is a caveat in a promoter-controlled business; and demand is ultimately tied to a cyclical construction/capex cycle.
The bottom line

Polycab is a genuinely dominant, high-quality, debt-free market leader riding a real demand supercycle, protected by a distribution moat that is hard to overstate and hard to copy. The business risk is modest; the valuation and competition risks are real and rising. The story over the next few years will be written in four numbers each quarter — volume growth (is it more than metal-price inflation?), W&C spread/margin (does pricing power hold as new capacity floods in?), market share (does the moat hold off the giants?), and FMEG margin (is the consumer bet finally paying?). Get those right and the compounding justifies the premium; if the moat cracks or the metal-illusion in growth is exposed, a 57× stock has plenty of room to fall.
