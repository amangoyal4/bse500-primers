00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

C.E. Info Systems — brand name MapmyIndia, newer product brand Mappls — is India's homegrown digital-mapping and location-technology company. Over roughly three decades it has built a proprietary, deeply detailed digital map of India: every road, address, landmark, point of interest, and increasingly house-level, 3D and lane-level detail. It then licenses that map and the software around it to businesses and governments. It is, in effect, India's own answer to Google Maps — except it makes real money, because it is a business-to-business licensing engine, not a free consumer utility.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Two segments, one crown jewel. The core, high-margin engine is Automotive & Enterprise Digital Transformation (A&E) — often labelled Map & Data — which puts navigation maps inside cars, sells location APIs to enterprises, and supplies geospatial data to government. Around it sits a smaller, lower-margin IoT (Internet of Moving Things) business selling tracking hardware and telematics. A&E is roughly 85%+ of revenue (approx.) and almost all of the profit.
  • It is genuinely, unusually profitable. FY26 revenue from operations was ~₹474 Cr with an EBITDA margin near 37% and a PAT margin of ~25.5% — software-like economics, net cash of ~₹685 Cr in the bank, no meaningful debt, and returns on capital in the high teens to twenties. Among India's crop of new-age tech IPOs — many still loss-making — that combination is rare.
  • It lives under a giant's free shadow. The permanent question hanging over the stock is simple: why pay MapmyIndia when Google Maps is free? Google gives consumers maps for nothing and prices enterprise APIs aggressively; Ola has built its own Ola Maps and exited Google. MapmyIndia trades on a rich, software-like multiple (roughly 70×+ earnings, approx.) that assumes it keeps embedding its map into more of India's digital plumbing faster than free maps commoditise the base beneath it.
In plain terms

Strip the company to one sentence and it is this: MapmyIndia built a map once and sells it many times. The expensive, decades-long work — surveying India road by road and address by address, then keeping it fresh — is largely done and paid for. Every additional car that ships with its navigation, every app that calls its location API, every government department that licenses its data adds revenue at very little extra cost, which is why margins are so high. The whole investment question is whether it can keep signing up new cars, apps and departments faster than a free global map erodes what people are willing to pay for the base underneath.

01

Digital mapping 101: what a "map company" actually sells

No GIS degree needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

A modern digital map is not a picture. It is a vast, structured database: every road as a line with a name, direction, speed limit and turn restriction; every junction, flyover and one-way; millions of addresses and "points of interest" (a shop, an ATM, a hospital) each with coordinates and attributes; and layers of extra detail — building footprints, 3D shapes, lane markings, terrain. On top of that database sits software that does useful things with it: find me the fastest route, tell me where this delivery boy is, estimate this journey's time, geo-tag this bank branch. MapmyIndia builds and owns both the database and the software. To follow the business, you need five ideas.

The only terms you need

The economics that decide everything: build cost vs. sell cost

A map business has a brutal upfront cost and a wonderful marginal cost. Surveying a country, structuring the data and keeping it current takes decades of labour and money — that is the wall that keeps new entrants out. But once the database exists, selling it again to the next customer costs almost nothing: no factory, no raw material, no new survey. That gap — enormous fixed investment already sunk, trivial cost to serve the next licence — is the entire reason MapmyIndia can run a ~37% EBITDA margin. It is a software P&L wrapped around a physical-world dataset.

Who pays for maps, and why

  • Automakers — they need in-dash navigation that works offline, is India-tuned, and comes with a support and update relationship. They license an embedded map rather than depend on a phone app.
  • Enterprises — e-commerce, logistics, food/grocery delivery, ride-hailing, banks, telcos and fintechs that need routing, address verification, geo-tagging and tracking woven into their own apps and back-ends.
  • Government & PSUs — ministries, states and agencies needing authoritative national/Indian-owned geospatial data for planning, land records, utilities, disaster management and defence-adjacent uses, where a foreign consumer map is often unsuitable.
02

What MapmyIndia actually sells: two segments, very different economics

The structure — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

MapmyIndia reports in two segments, and they could hardly be more different. One is a high-margin, asset-light licensing engine built on the map database. The other is a lower-margin hardware-and-service business that rides on top of it. Getting the split straight is the single most useful thing a first-time reader can do — because the market pays for one of them far more than the other.

Segment 1 — Automotive & Enterprise (A&E) / Map & Data

This is the crown jewel: roughly 85%+ of revenue (approx.) and the overwhelming majority of profit. It bundles three ways of selling the same database — in-car navigation to automakers, map/location APIs and SDKs to enterprises, and geospatial data and services to government. The revenue arrives as licences, subscriptions and royalties — much of it recurring, sticky and paid regardless of whether MapmyIndia ships a single physical object. This is where the ~37% margin lives.

Segment 2 — IoT (Internet of Moving Things)

The smaller line: telematics and tracking — GPS trackers, fleet-tracking devices, connected-vehicle hardware and the software dashboards that go with them, sold to logistics fleets, insurers, and consumers. It is a real, growing business that deepens MapmyIndia's grip on movement data, but it involves hardware and services, so its margins are structurally lower than the licensing core. When the blended EBITDA margin wobbles, the mix between these two segments is often why.

Why the segment split is the first thing to internalise

A reader who treats MapmyIndia as "a maps app" will misjudge it completely. The consumer Mappls app is essentially free marketing and a data-collection layer — it is not where the money comes from. The money comes from A&E: a Hyundai shipping cars with MapmyIndia navigation, a delivery firm calling its routing API a million times a day, a ministry licensing its data. When you read the results, watch the A&E line and its margin. The IoT line and the app are supporting cast; the licensing engine is the star.

03

The company, the family, and who controls it

A Verma-family company that spent 30 years building before it ever listed.

MapmyIndia is not a venture-funded overnight story. It was founded in the mid-1990s by Rakesh Verma and Rashmi Verma, a husband-and-wife team who began the unglamorous, patient work of digitising India's geography long before "location data" was a business anyone valued. Their son Rohan Verma now runs it as CEO. The company came to the market only in December 2021 — after nearly three decades of building the asset — and remains firmly promoter-controlled.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Verma family — Rakesh Verma (Chairman & Managing Director), Rashmi Verma (co-founder), and Rohan Verma (CEO & Executive Director) — hold the controlling block, roughly ~53% (approx.). Founder-run, founder-owned, with the people who built the map still steering it.
  • Institutional interest: the float carries a notable roster of domestic and foreign institutions, plus early strategic investors from before the IPO (Qualcomm and Zenrin, a Japanese mapping company, were historic backers). A tightly held register with a modest free float amplifies the stock's moves.
  • Balance sheet: effectively debt-free, with ~₹685 Cr of cash and investments at FY26 year-end. This is a company that funds itself, returns cash via dividends (FY26 final dividend ~₹3.50/share), and has no financing pressure on its operating decisions.
Why the founder story matters commercially: a map is only as valuable as it is trusted and current, and trust in geospatial data is built over decades, not quarters. The Vermas' 30-year head start is the moat — you cannot venture-fund your way to a 30-year-old, continuously-updated, India-specific map database overnight. That same longevity is also the risk the bears press on: a base built patiently by hand can, in an AI-and-satellite era, be approached far faster than it once could.
04

Build once, licence many: the economics that define the company

The single most important idea in the entire investment case — and it is specific to this business.

Every mapping company reports revenue. But for MapmyIndia, the number that explains the whole P&L is not revenue — it is how many times the same map gets sold. The database is a fixed asset that has already been paid for in decades of survey work. From here, each new licensee — a carmaker, an app, a ministry — is a fresh revenue stream layered onto the identical underlying data, at almost no incremental cost. Multiplication, not addition, is the engine.

Because the build cost is already sunk and the cost to serve the next customer is tiny, incremental revenue drops to the bottom line at very high rates — which is why a big-volume quarter (Q4 FY26) can carry a ~45% EBITDA margin.

This is the whole company in one mechanism. A carmaker signing a multi-year navigation deal does not just buy the map — it agrees to a per-vehicle royalty that keeps paying as long as those cars keep rolling off the line. An enterprise wiring MapmyIndia's routing into its app does not buy once — it pays a subscription that recurs and grows with its usage. A government licensing the data commits to a multi-year project. Each of these is the same database, sold again into a new pocket of the economy, and each one — once won — is sticky because ripping out an embedded map is disruptive and risky.

The device that fits only MapmyIndia: one asset, N annuities

Most companies you value by asking "how much can they sell?" MapmyIndia you value by asking a stranger question: "how many independent, recurring annuities can they hang off a single, already-built dataset?" Every design-win — a new car model that ships with its nav, a new enterprise on its API, a new government contract — is a fresh annuity bolted onto a fixed asset, at almost no marginal cost. The order book (~₹1,754 Cr against ~₹474 Cr of annual revenue) is exactly this: years of already-contracted map-selling stacked up in advance. The thesis is not "will revenue grow?" — it is "how fast can it keep multiplying the count of licences on the one map before the base underneath gets commoditised?"

A genuine surprise: the "Indian Google Maps" is not a loss-making land-grab like most new-age Indian tech IPOs — it earns a ~25% net margin and sits on ~₹685 Cr of cash. It makes money in precisely the corner of the market where Google Maps is free. The reason is that Google's free product is a consumer utility, while MapmyIndia's money comes from businesses and governments licensing an owned, India-specific dataset — a different customer, a different sale, a different economics entirely.
05

Automotive & Enterprise: inside the profit engine

The 85%+ of revenue that actually matters — decoded into its three motors.

The A&E segment is where MapmyIndia earns its keep. It is best understood as three distinct motors running off one map: automotive royalties, enterprise APIs, and government data & services. Each has its own rhythm, and together they are what a buyer of the stock is really paying for.

Motor 1 — Automotive: the map inside the dashboard

When you sit in many new Indian cars and the built-in navigation screen lights up, there is a real chance the map underneath is MapmyIndia's, not Google's. The company supplies embedded, offline-capable navigation to a broad set of automakers — historically names such as Hyundai, Kia, MG Motor, Tata Motors, Mahindra, Honda and BMW, plus a growing list of two-wheeler and EV makers. The economics are attractive: an up-front integration fee, then a per-vehicle royalty that keeps flowing for years as those models ship. As India's car parc grows and more models add connected navigation, this royalty base compounds.

Motor 2 — Enterprise: the API business

This is the fastest-moving motor. Any company that needs to move something, verify an address, or place a pin on a map is a potential customer: e-commerce and logistics (route planning, delivery tracking), food and grocery delivery, fintech and banking (branch/ATM location, address verification, fraud geo-checks), ride-hailing and mobility, and telcos. They pay via subscriptions and usage-based API calls. It is the most recurring, most scalable slice — and the one most directly exposed to Google's aggressively priced enterprise Maps Platform.

Motor 3 — Government & data services

Here MapmyIndia sells the underlying geospatial data and bespoke services to the public sector — national and state mapping, asset geo-tagging, utility and infrastructure planning, and analytics — including its high-profile collaboration with ISRO to fuse satellite imagery with its map. This motor is lumpier and project-shaped, so it can swing quarter to quarter, but it is high-value and structurally hard for a foreign consumer app to serve, because Indian public bodies often want an Indian-owned, sovereignty-friendly data partner.

Where to look when the quarter lands

Because the three motors have different cadences, a single quarter can mislead. The soft first half and blowout Q4 of FY26 were largely this — timing of orders, not a change in the franchise. When judging the business, read the full-year A&E trajectory and margin, and treat any one government-heavy quarter with caution.

06

IoT: the Internet of Moving Things

The smaller, hardware-tinged segment — useful, growing, and margin-dilutive.

The second segment, IoT, is MapmyIndia's answer to a natural question: if you own the map, why not also sell the devices that move across it and feed data back? IoT bundles tracking hardware and telematics software — GPS trackers, fleet-management devices, connected-vehicle units, dashcams — with dashboards that turn a moving vehicle into a stream of live location data.

The customers are logistics fleets wanting to see where their trucks are, insurers wanting driving-behaviour data, and consumers wanting to track a vehicle or asset. It is a genuinely useful extension: it deepens MapmyIndia's grip on movement data (which in turn improves the map — live traffic, road conditions), and it opens a recurring device-plus-subscription relationship. But because it involves physical hardware and field services, its gross margins are structurally lower than the pure licensing core.

The margin tell hidden in the segment mix

When MapmyIndia's blended EBITDA margin ticks down, the culprit is often IoT growing faster than A&E in that period — more hardware in the mix drags the average. That is not necessarily bad news: IoT can be strategically valuable and still dilutive. The discipline for a reader is to separate the two. A quarter where IoT surged and the blended margin dipped is very different from one where the high-margin A&E core itself softened. The former is mix; the latter is the thing that would actually threaten the thesis.

The nuance: IoT is best read as an option and a data-feeder, not the core valuation driver. If it scales into a large connected-vehicle and fleet-telematics platform, it adds a second growth leg. If it stays sub-scale, it barely dents a valuation that is really being paid for the A&E licensing engine. Do not let a good or bad IoT quarter distract from where the profit — and the multiple — actually sits.
07

The free-Google-Maps shadow

The defining bear point — met head-on, because everything else rests on it.

No honest primer on MapmyIndia can dodge the one question every sceptic asks first: why would anyone pay for a map when Google Maps is free? Google's map is superb, ubiquitous, and costs consumers nothing; its enterprise Maps Platform is priced to win developers globally; and now Ola has built Ola Maps and publicly ditched Google, proving a well-funded Indian player can roll its own. This is the shadow the whole stock trades under, and it deserves a straight answer rather than a wave-away.

The distinction that resolves the "why pay?" question

The free-Google objection quietly assumes one market. There are two. In the consumer market, Google is free and dominant, and MapmyIndia does not really try to win rupees there — the Mappls app is brand and data, not revenue. In the business-and-government market, buyers want ownership, India-specific depth, localisation, control and support — and there they pay, contract, and stick. MapmyIndia's whole model is to be irrelevant to the free consumer fight and indispensable to the paid enterprise-and-government one. Whether that separation holds as Google's enterprise pricing and Ola's ambition press in is the crux of the bear case.

The bear does not need MapmyIndia to disappear. It only needs the price of a licence to fall — for Google or Ola to make the enterprise API cheap enough, or the automaker relationship contestable enough, that MapmyIndia's software-like margin slowly compresses toward something ordinary. That is the slow-squeeze risk beneath the rich multiple, and it is why the A&E margin, not the headline growth, is the number worth guarding.

08

The map, the moat, and the app

What actually protects MapmyIndia — and what the consumer app is really for.

If the map can be copied by a free giant, where is the durable advantage? It rests on four things that are genuinely hard to replicate quickly, plus one thing that is widely misunderstood — the consumer app.

  • Depth and Indian-specificity of the data. Thirty years of surveying produces address logic, unnamed lanes, local POIs and road behaviour that a globally-templated map struggles to match in India's messy, fast-changing built environment.
  • Switching costs. An embedded car navigation system or an enterprise's routing back-end, once integrated and warranted, is disruptive and risky to rip out and re-validate. Design-wins are sticky by construction.
  • Sovereignty and localisation. For government and security-sensitive work, being an Indian-owned map is not a marketing line — it is often a hard procurement requirement that structurally excludes foreign rivals.
  • The data flywheel. More devices, cars and app users generating live movement data feed back into a fresher, better map — a loop that improves the very asset being licensed.
What the Mappls app is genuinely for

Newcomers assume the consumer Mappls app is MapmyIndia's product and revenue. It is neither. The app is a shop window and a sensor. As a shop window it keeps the brand credible as a real, living map people can use — important when pitching a carmaker or a government. As a sensor it collects the anonymised movement, traffic and change-detection data that keeps the licensed database current. It is a cost centre that makes the profit centre better. Reading it as the business is the most common mistake made about this company.

The candid limit of the moat: every one of these advantages is a matter of degree, not a wall. AI-assisted mapping, cheap high-resolution satellite imagery and crowd-sourced data are all lowering the cost of building a "good enough" map. The moat is real today; the open question is how much of MapmyIndia's head start technology quietly erodes over the next decade. That, not next quarter's growth, is the debate that decides whether the multiple is deserved.

The competitive set

One free giant, one aggressive newcomer, a few global specialists, and a domestic peer.

MapmyIndia competes on several fronts at once, and no single rival threatens all of it. The map that beats it in cars is not the one that beats it in government; the API that undercuts it for a start-up is not the one that wins a defence contract. Holding the competitive map straight is essential.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two takeaways. First, MapmyIndia's safest ground is exactly where Google is weakest — Indian-owned, sovereignty-sensitive, deeply-localised government and automotive work — and its most contested ground is the enterprise API market, where Google and Ola press hardest on price. Second, the competition is not one battle but several, so the company can be losing ground in cheap consumer-grade APIs while extending its lead in embedded auto nav and government data at the same time. Judge each motor on its own front, not on a single "Google is winning / losing" verdict.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A rare profitable Indian tech asset — asked to keep multiplying its map faster than a free map commoditises it.

MapmyIndia is that unusual thing: a new-age Indian tech company that already makes money, throws off cash, and owns a genuinely hard-to-build asset. The debate is not whether it is a real business — it plainly is — but whether it can keep expanding the count of paid licences on its map, at its current margin, for the many years its valuation assumes, all while Google gives maps away and Ola builds its own.

The bull case

  • Asset-light licensing economics — ~37% EBITDA margin, ~25% net margin, net cash; incremental revenue drops through at very high rates (Q4 ~45%).
  • Build-once, licence-many runway — every new car model, enterprise and government contract is a fresh annuity on an already-built map; ~₹1,754 Cr order book underwrites visibility.
  • India digitisation tailwind — logistics, e-commerce, fintech, EVs and connected cars all need location data woven in; the addressable base keeps widening.
  • Sovereignty moat in government — Indian-ownership and data-localisation structurally favour it where foreign maps can't easily go.
  • Founder-owned, patient — 30-year-built data asset run by the people who built it; no debt, real dividends.

The bear case

  • The free-Google shadow — free consumer maps and cheap enterprise APIs cap pricing power; the "why pay?" question never fully goes away.
  • New entrant proof — Ola Maps shows a funded rival can build its own; enterprise API pricing is the most contestable rupee.
  • Concentration & lumpiness — reliance on a limited set of large auto/enterprise/government clients; renewals, royalty terms and lumpy government orders swing quarters (see soft-H1 FY26).
  • Margin dilution & commoditisation — IoT drags the blend; AI-mapping and cheap satellite data lower the cost of a "good enough" rival map over time.
  • Very rich valuation — a ~70×+ multiple on a company that grew ~2% in FY26 leaves the price highly sensitive to any A&E growth or margin wobble.
The bottom line

MapmyIndia has already answered the hardest question most Indian tech IPOs never do — it is profitable, cash-rich and built on an asset a competitor cannot conjure overnight. What it has not yet settled is whether it can keep multiplying paid licences on that map at a software margin for a decade, in the teeth of a free global map and a hungry domestic newcomer. The next few years turn on one checkable thing: the pace at which new automotive and enterprise design-wins add to the recurring royalty base while the A&E margin holds near the mid-30s%. Watch each results season for A&E growth alongside that margin line — a flat FY26 followed by a ~45%-margin Q4 is exactly the kind of split signal that will keep the map-licensing bull and the free-Google sceptic arguing, print by print, until the segment's trajectory makes the call for them.
