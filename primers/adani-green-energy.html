00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Adani Green Energy (AGEL) is India's largest renewable-energy generator and developer — solar, wind and hybrid plants that turn sunlight and breeze into electricity, almost all of it pre-sold on long-term contracts. It owns roughly 19.3 GW of operating capacity (about 1 in every 7 watts of India's utility-scale renewables), is racing toward an audacious 50 GW by 2030, and is the green-power flagship of the Adani group, controlled by the family of Gautam Adani. It is best understood as an infrastructure annuity machine running on borrowed money.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A high-margin annuity — at huge and growing scale. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) AGEL sold 37,567 million units (37.6 BU) of clean power and earned ~₹11,600 Cr of revenue from power supply, ₹10,865 Cr of core EBITDA at a remarkable ~91% EBITDA margin, and ~₹5,400 Cr of cash profit. Net profit was a much smaller ~₹2,000 Cr — because interest and depreciation on the asset base eat most of that fat operating margin.
  • The cash is contracted and predictable. Almost the entire portfolio sells power under long-term, fixed-tariff power-purchase agreements (PPAs) — mostly to sovereign-backed buyers like the Solar Energy Corporation of India (SECI) and state utilities. Once a plant is built and connected, it produces a near-fixed cheque for 20–25 years. That is the "boring, bond-like" heart of the business.
  • The whole story is build fast + manage leverage. Growing from ~19 GW to 50 GW means tens of thousands of crores of debt-funded capex every year. Net debt rose to ₹91,252 Cr in FY26 (from ₹64,462 Cr a year earlier). The shares trade on an extraordinary multiple (~130–150× earnings) that prices in flawless execution. That combination — turbo growth, heavy leverage, a sky-high valuation, and an Adani-group governance overhang — is exactly what bulls and bears argue about.
In plain terms

Think of AGEL as a company that builds enormous fields of solar panels and wind turbines, then signs a 25-year contract to sell every unit they produce at a price agreed up front. Building each field costs a fortune of borrowed money; running it costs almost nothing (the sun and wind are free, there's no fuel to buy). So once a plant is up, ~90 paise of every revenue rupee becomes operating profit — but a big slice of that then goes to pay interest on the loans that built it. The genius of the model is the predictability: contracted, fixed-price cash for two decades. The danger is the debt: to keep growing it has to keep borrowing, and the value of the whole thing rests on cheap, continuous access to capital. Strip it to one sentence: AGEL borrows heavily to build clean-power annuities — and its fate turns on how cheaply it can keep borrowing.

01

Renewable-developer economics 101: how AGEL earns

No engineering degree required — just five numbers, and one idea that flips the whole risk profile.

A renewable-energy plant is, financially, almost the mirror image of a coal plant. A coal plant has a modest build cost but a huge recurring fuel bill; a solar or wind plant has a huge up-front build cost and then almost no running cost at all — the "fuel" (sun, wind) is free. That single difference reshapes everything: the renewable developer's entire economic life is decided before the plant ever switches on, by how cheaply it built and financed the asset. Five numbers tell the story.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and AGEL's number)
Capacity (MW / GW)The physical size — how much power the plant can produce at full tilt, in megawatts (1 GW = 1,000 MW). AGEL has ~19.3 GW of operating capacity, the largest renewable portfolio in India.
CUF (%)"Capacity Utilisation Factor" — what share of the maximum the plant actually produces over a year. The sun sets and the wind drops, so a solar plant runs at only ~24% and wind at ~29% CUF. This is structurally lower than a coal plant — but it is free energy.
Energy sold (MU / BU)The electricity actually sold, in million units (MU). AGEL sold 37,567 MU (37.6 BU) in FY26, up 34% year-on-year as new capacity came online.
Tariff (₹/kWh)The price per unit, fixed for 20–25 years in the PPA the day the plant is contracted. Indian solar tariffs are very low (often ~₹2.5–3/unit) — there is no fuel cost to recover, only the plant and its financing.
Cost of capital (% interest)The interest rate on the debt that built the plant. Because there's no fuel bill, this is the real "fuel" of a renewable developer — the single biggest cost line, and the thing that decides whether the project makes money.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Revenue ≈ Capacity × CUF × 8,760 hours × Tariff, and because the tariff is fixed and the running cost is tiny, the EBITDA margin is enormous (~91%) — far above a coal plant's ~40%. But then comes the twist: a giant slice of that EBITDA goes to interest (on the loans that built the plant) and depreciation (writing down the asset). That's why AGEL can earn ₹10,865 Cr of EBITDA but only ~₹2,000 Cr of net profit. So the developer grows profit in three ways — add capacity (more MW under contract), build/finance cheaper (lower cost per MW, lower interest rate), and keep CUF and uptime high. Of these, the cost of capital is king: a renewable plant is essentially a bond, and a bond is only worth building if you can borrow cheaper than the return it locks in.

Why this flips the risk profile vs a coal plant

  • No fuel-price risk. AGEL never worries about coal or gas prices — there is no fuel. That removes the single biggest swing factor that buffets a thermal generator.
  • No merchant-price risk (mostly). Because nearly all output is pre-sold at a fixed tariff, AGEL doesn't ride the volatile day-to-day power market the way a merchant coal plant does. The cash flow is contracted and stable.
  • But total exposure to capital cost. All the risk migrates to the balance sheet: if interest rates rise, or if AGEL's own borrowing cost spikes (because of group sentiment), the economics of the next plant — and the refinancing of the last one — get squeezed. The risk didn't disappear; it moved from the fuel bill to the financing.
  • Plus intermittency & curtailment. The sun and wind aren't dispatchable, so a renewable plant can be told to stop producing when the grid is full ("curtailment"), and it can't sell at night. Storage (batteries) is the emerging fix — more on that in §07.
02

The business model: build it, contract it, collect for 25 years

Three design choices explain how AGEL earns — read this once and the strategy clicks.

AGEL's model rests on three deliberate choices: build at the lowest possible cost per megawatt (giant single-site mega-parks, vertically integrated supply), pre-sell the output on long-dated, fixed-tariff PPAs (turning a power plant into a 25-year annuity), and fund it almost entirely with debt raised against those contracted cash flows. Together they turn a capital-hungry construction business into a defensive, bond-like cash machine — provided the building never stops and the borrowing stays cheap.

The value chain — from a barren plain to a 25-year cheque

Unlike a coal plant, there is no fuel stage. The chain runs from building the plant, through generating free power, to a contracted sale, and finally — the part that matters for the balance sheet — to refinancing the debt against the now-operating, cash-producing asset.

The flywheel: contracted cash flow lets AGEL refinance built assets at lower rates and raise fresh debt — recycling capital into the next tranche of capacity. It spins fast when capital is cheap and confidence is high; it seizes up if borrowing costs spike.

The mega-park advantage — build cheaper by building bigger

  • One giant site beats many small ones. AGEL's signature is the single-location mega-park — above all Khavda in Gujarat (see §04). Concentrating tens of GW on one vast plot slashes the per-MW cost of land, transmission, roads, water and project management, and lets the same crews and supply chain work continuously.
  • Group-level integration. The Adani group's manufacturing arm (Adani New Industries) can supply solar modules, wind turbines and even the polysilicon/ingot upstream — giving AGEL a degree of supply security and cost control few rivals have.
  • Pre-contracted growth. Much new capacity is tied to PPAs (with SECI and others) before it is built, including large "manufacturing-linked" and round-the-clock tenders. That reduces the risk of building plants with no buyer.
Why the PPA-and-debt structure matters so much here

By locking output into 20–25-year fixed-tariff PPAs, AGEL converts an intermittent commodity (electricity) into a contracted annuity — a stream of near-certain payments. That predictability is precisely what lets it carry ₹90,000+ Cr of debt: lenders will fund a bond-like cash flow. The trade-off is that AGEL has, in effect, swapped commodity-price risk (which a coal plant carries) for financing risk and counterparty risk. Financing risk: it must keep refinancing and raising debt cheaply, forever. Counterparty risk: the contracts are only as good as the buyers paying them (mostly sovereign-backed SECI, which is a strength, plus some weaker state discoms). Understand those two swaps and you understand the entire investment case.

03

The company and who controls it

From one Tamil Nadu solar farm to the world's largest renewable build-out.

Adani Green Energy is the renewable-energy flagship of the Adani group, tightly controlled by the promoter family of Gautam Adani, with French oil-and-gas major TotalEnergies as a large strategic minority partner. It listed in 2018, scaled at a blistering pace through both home-grown projects and big acquisitions, and is today one arm of a sprawling infrastructure conglomerate spanning ports, airports, power, transmission and more.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Adani family / promoter group holds ~62.4%. This is a tightly held, founder-controlled business; the family's vision (and its appetite for debt-funded growth) drives the strategy, and minority shareholders are along for the ride.
  • TotalEnergies (~17%): the French super-major is a strategic partner via its ~17.25% stake and joint ventures. It provides capital and a global-energy stamp of approval — though it has begun trimming the holding (selling a ~1.7% slice in late 2025/early 2026), which some read as caution after the US case.
  • Group context: AGEL sits inside the wider Adani conglomerate. That brings scale, in-house module/turbine manufacturing and capital access — but also ties the stock's funding cost and perception to the whole group's leverage and governance reputation (see §08).
The defining duality: AGEL is simultaneously a best-in-class renewable operator (world-record build pace, 91% margins, sovereign-backed contracts) and a highly leveraged, founder-controlled entity inside a scrutinised group. Almost every debate about the stock is really a debate about which of those two faces matters more for the next rupee of value.

The 50-GW capacity engine & the Khavda mega-park

The single most important growth idea — and the world-record machine built to deliver it.

AGEL's clearest growth lever is sheer capacity addition, and its ambition is among the boldest in global energy: from ~19.3 GW today toward 50 GW of renewable capacity by 2030 — roughly a tenth of India's entire clean-energy target. In FY26 it proved it can execute, adding a record 5.1 GW in a single year (3.4 GW solar, 0.7 GW wind, 1.0 GW hybrid) — described as the highest greenfield annual addition by any company on earth outside China. The engine room of that ambition is one extraordinary site: Khavda.

Khavda — the world's largest power plant of any kind

In the barren salt plains of the Rann of Kutch in Gujarat — near-uninhabited, scorched, and exceptionally rich in both sunshine and wind — AGEL is building a single renewable-energy park across 538 square kilometres (about five times the area of Paris). Why Khavda is the whole thesis in one place

Khavda is the physical expression of AGEL's strategy: build cheaper by building bigger. Concentrating 30 GW on one site means one set of transmission lines, one logistics base, one workforce, and continuous round-the-clock construction — which is how AGEL drives its per-MW cost down toward world-best levels and adds capacity faster than anyone outside China. The site also pairs solar and wind (which peak at different times of day), then layers batteries on top, so it can move toward delivering firm, round-the-clock clean power rather than just intermittent solar. If the 50-GW story works, it works because Khavda works. If construction slips, or grid evacuation can't keep up, or financing tightens — the same site is where the risk concentrates too.

Grid evacuation is the quiet constraint: building 30 GW in a remote desert is only useful if the electricity can be carried to where demand is. That requires massive new transmission (much of it built by sister-company Adani Energy Solutions and the state grid). Transmission build-out lagging generation is a real, recurring risk for any mega-park — power you can't evacuate is power you can't sell.
05

PPA-locked annuity economics — how the cash is really earned

The idea that makes a power plant behave like a 25-year government bond.

The reason AGEL can carry so much debt and still be considered "defensive" is the power-purchase agreement. Almost every plant it owns sells its output under a long-term, fixed-tariff PPA — a contract that says, in effect: "For the next 20–25 years, we will buy your electricity at this agreed price." That turns each plant into a contracted, predictable cash stream — an annuity — and it is the single most important feature of the economics.

The PPA, decoded

Fixed tariff = predictability. Unlike a merchant coal plant that rides volatile spot prices, AGEL knows the price of nearly every unit it will sell for two decades. There is no commodity-price guesswork. That visibility is what makes the cash flow "bond-like" and bankable.

The counterparty is usually strong. A large share of contracts are with SECI (the Solar Energy Corporation of India, a central-government agency) and other public buyers — far more creditworthy than the cash-strapped state discoms that plague thermal generators. That makes AGEL's receivables risk lower than a typical Indian power company's, though not zero (some state-discom and inter-state payment delays still occur).

The catch is duration risk on the other side. Because tariffs are fixed for 25 years, AGEL captures none of the upside if power prices rise — but it also bears no fuel-cost downside. The real exposure is that it has effectively borrowed at a floating/refinanced cost to fund a fixed-return asset: the spread between the locked-in tariff and the cost of the debt is the profit, and that spread can be squeezed if borrowing gets dearer.

06

The debt-funded-capex tightrope — the key risk

Why the balance sheet, not the power plants, is where this story is won or lost.

Here is the central tension of the whole investment case. AGEL's contracted cash flows are stable and high-margin — but to build those cash-flow-producing assets at the pace it wants, it must spend tens of thousands of crores of capital it doesn't have, which means borrowing, heavily and continuously. The result is a company carrying a very large, growing debt load against an asset base that is still part-built. Manage that tightrope well and the model compounds beautifully; mismanage it — or get hit by a funding shock — and the leverage bites hard.

Why a renewable developer is so capital-intensive

  • The whole cost is up front. A solar or wind plant is essentially all capital and no fuel — you pay for it once (in steel, silicon, turbines, land and grid links) and then run it for 25 years almost for free. So growth = capex, and lots of it: AGEL spent ~₹30,000 Cr building in FY26 alone.
  • Project finance is the norm. Like most infrastructure, each project is funded with a high proportion of debt secured against its own contracted cash flow. Modest equity goes a long way — but it stacks up a large consolidated debt pile.
  • The cash-profit gap. Note that FY26 cash profit (~₹5,400 Cr) is far above net profit (~₹2,000 Cr) because depreciation is a non-cash charge. The market often watches run-rate EBITDA and cash profit, not reported PAT, to judge whether the debt is serviceable.

How AGEL manages the tightrope

  • Refinance built assets cheaper. Once a plant is operating and de-risked, its contracted cash flow can be refinanced at lower rates — freeing capital to redeploy. In March 2025, AGEL raised a ~US$1.06bn refinancing — its first major dollar deal since the US indictment — a sign markets reopened to it.
  • The move to dollar bonds. AGEL increasingly taps international bond markets (it has targeted ~US$1bn issues) to lengthen maturities and diversify away from bank debt — though a planned ~US$600m bond was shelved right after the November 2024 indictment, showing how group sentiment can shut a funding door overnight.
  • Run-rate EBITDA rising with capacity. Every new GW lifts the contracted cash flow that services the debt. The bet is that EBITDA grows fast enough to keep leverage ratios in check even as gross debt rises.
The tightrope — and why it's THE risk

AGEL is, in effect, running a spread business: it borrows money and locks it into fixed-return clean-power assets, earning the gap between the two. That works wonderfully as long as (1) it can keep raising debt to fund the next plant, and (2) it can keep doing so cheaply. The danger is a funding shock — a spike in interest rates, a downgrade, or a fresh group controversy — that simultaneously raises the cost of new debt and makes refinancing the existing ₹90,000+ Cr harder. Because the company is mid-build and the cash flow is still ramping, it has less margin for a financing accident than a mature, fully-built utility. This — not the power plants, which run fine — is the number-one thing to watch. The single most important sentence in this primer is: AGEL's fate turns on its continuous, cheap access to capital.

07

CUF, intermittency, curtailment & the battery answer

The structural weakness of solar and wind — and how AGEL is trying to engineer it away.

Renewable power has one inherent flaw a coal plant doesn't: it isn't there when you flip the switch. Solar produces only in daylight, wind only when it blows. That shows up in two ways — a low CUF (the plant sits idle much of the day) and curtailment (the grid sometimes can't absorb a midday solar surge and orders the plant to stop). AGEL's answer, and a big part of its strategy, is to combine solar + wind + giant batteries to deliver firmer, round-the-clock power.

A renewable plant produces far fewer hours than a coal plant — but with zero fuel cost. Hybrid (solar + wind on one site) lifts utilisation because the two resources peak at different times; batteries lift it further by time-shifting power.

Why this matters — and how storage changes the game

  • Intermittency caps the value. A buyer can't run a factory on power that vanishes at sunset. So pure solar/wind, however cheap, can only ever supply part of the grid — the rest needs something firm. This is the structural ceiling on renewables-only.
  • Curtailment wastes built capacity. When too much solar hits the grid at noon, the system operator can order plants to curtail (stop). The plant is built and financed but isn't allowed to sell — a direct hit to returns. Better transmission and storage reduce it.
  • Batteries are the unlock. A battery energy storage system (BESS) stores midday solar and releases it in the evening peak — turning intermittent power into firm, dispatchable, round-the-clock supply that commands better tariffs and dodges curtailment.
AGEL's battery bet at Khavda

AGEL is building grid-scale storage at Khavda at world-record scale. It has already commissioned over 3.37 GWh of BESS — described as the world's largest single-location battery installation outside China — and is targeting roughly 14 GWh by the end of the year and a staggering ~50 GWh over five years. The strategic logic is to transform Khavda from a producer of cheap-but-intermittent solar/wind into a supplier of firm, round-the-clock clean power — exactly the product that lets renewables genuinely displace coal baseload rather than merely supplement it. If it works at scale and at acceptable cost, it materially widens the moat. The risks are the cost of all that battery capex (more debt) and the still-evolving economics and tariffs for storage-firmed power.

08

The Adani-group governance & funding-cost overhang

Why a company with great assets has, at times, paid a "group" penalty on its cost of capital.

For a business whose entire model rests on cheap, continuous access to capital, the most consequential risk isn't operational — it's reputational. Twice in recent years, group-level controversy has directly raised AGEL's funding cost or shut a funding door, even when the power plants kept running flawlessly. Understanding this overhang — and its recent easing — is essential to understanding the stock.

Two episodes that defined the overhang

  • The Hindenburg report (Jan 2023). A US short-seller published allegations of stock manipulation and accounting concerns across the Adani group. Group shares — AGEL included — sold off sharply, credit spreads widened, and fundraising was disrupted. The group denied the allegations; operating businesses kept performing, and most stocks substantially recovered. But it established the pattern: group sentiment can move AGEL's cost of capital regardless of its own results.
  • The US DOJ / SEC matter (Nov 2024). The bigger, more specific event. US prosecutors indicted Gautam Adani, his nephew Sagar Adani and others, alleging a scheme to pay ~US$265m in bribes to Indian officials to secure lucrative solar contracts — and to conceal it from US investors who had bought AGEL-linked debt. The SEC filed parallel civil charges. AGEL shelved a planned ~US$600m dollar bond within days. The allegations struck especially close to home because they concerned the renewable business itself.
How to scope the US matter accurately (as of mid-2026): the charges were allegations, denied by Adani, and never tested at trial. In 2026 the situation shifted markedly: the US DOJ moved to drop the criminal charges (reported alongside a large Adani-group US investment pledge), and the SEC civil case was settled for roughly US$18m in total (Gautam ~$6m, Sagar ~$12m) without any admission or denial of wrongdoing. A US federal judge then declined to dismiss the criminal case immediately and asked the DOJ to justify the decision (further submissions expected mid-2026). So the overhang has eased substantially but is not yet fully and formally closed.
Why this hits AGEL more than most

For an ordinary company, a headline controversy dents sentiment. For AGEL, it strikes the core of the business model. Because the whole strategy is to borrow cheaply to build annuities, anything that raises the group's perceived risk directly raises AGEL's cost of capital — making every future plant a little less profitable and every refinancing a little harder. A shelved bond or a wider credit spread isn't just bad PR; it's a higher hurdle rate on a ₹2-lakh-crore growth plan. The flip side: as the US matter de-escalates and AGEL demonstrates renewed market access (the 2025 ~US$1bn refinancing, fresh dollar-bond plans), the "group penalty" on its funding can compress — which would be a direct tailwind to value. In short, the governance story and the cost-of-capital story are the same story.

09

The industry, the rivals & the energy-transition tailwind

A structurally growing market where AGEL is the scale leader — riding a once-in-a-generation tailwind.

AGEL operates in one of the most powerful structural growth themes anywhere: India's energy transition. The country has pledged ~500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030 and net-zero by 2070; electricity demand is growing ~6–7% a year as the economy industrialises and electrifies. Renewables are now the cheapest new power India can build. AGEL is the clear scale leader in this race — but it competes against well-funded rivals and the state behemoths.

The tailwind — and the competitive edge

The macro backdrop is about as supportive as it gets: India needs to roughly triple its renewable capacity by 2030, solar is now the cheapest electricity source, demand is compounding, and the policy machinery (SECI tenders, manufacturing-linked schemes, round-the-clock and storage tenders) is purpose-built to pull in developers like AGEL. Within that, AGEL's edge is scale and integration: the Khavda mega-park, in-house module/turbine manufacturing, and a proven ability to add 5+ GW a year mean it can build cheaper and faster than almost anyone. The competitive tension is twofold — (1) cost of capital, where state-backed NTPC Green and global-backed peers can sometimes borrow cheaper, and (2) tariff discipline, since aggressive bidding for tenders can compress returns if developers chase volume. AGEL's bet is that its scale-driven low build cost lets it win profitably even as tariffs fall.

The regulatory backdrop: the business is shaped by central and state policy — SECI/NTPC tenders, the ~500 GW-by-2030 target, must-run status for renewables, transmission planning, the basic-customs-duty/ALMM rules protecting domestic manufacturing, and evolving storage and round-the-clock norms. Supportive policy is the tailwind; policy or tariff shifts (or transmission bottlenecks) are the ever-present regulatory risk.

High-growth, high-margin, high-debt — and priced at an extraordinary multiple.

AGEL's financials show a business compounding revenue and EBITDA at 20%+ a year on a ~91% operating margin — but with thin net profit (interest and depreciation), heavy debt, and a valuation that prices in years of flawless growth. The debate is whether that premium is justified by a once-in-a-generation build-out, or whether it leaves no room for the funding/execution risks the model carries.

How to think about the valuation

At a ~₹2.5 lakh crore market cap on ~₹2,000 Cr of FY26 net profit, AGEL trades around 130–150× trailing earnings (and ~12× book) — a multiple far beyond any normal utility, even a fast-growing one. The market is explicitly not valuing it on current earnings; it is paying for the 50-GW build-out, the contracted-cash-flow visibility, the world-record Khavda asset, and the energy-transition tailwind — and capitalising the much larger future EBITDA the pipeline implies. Bulls argue that a developer tripling its asset base on 25-year annuities deserves a growth multiple, and that EBITDA (already ₹10,865 Cr) and cash profit (~₹5,400 Cr) are better anchors than thin PAT. Bears argue the multiple bakes in flawless, fully-financed execution for years, ignores the very real funding/leverage and group risks, and leaves a long way to fall on any stumble. With the stock's 52-week range a vast ₹765–₹1,562, the market clearly hasn't made up its mind — and the valuation leaves essentially no margin for error.

A note on which number to watch: for AGEL, reported PAT is a poor guide — it's whipsawed by interest and depreciation on a debt-funded, still-ramping asset base. The numbers that actually move the thesis are operating capacity (GW), run-rate EBITDA, net debt and net-debt/EBITDA, and the cost of new/refinanced debt. Judge the company on the annuity and the balance sheet, not on the bottom line of the P&L.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A world-class renewable annuity machine, riding a powerful tailwind — financed to the hilt and priced for perfection.

Adani Green is a genuinely exceptional operating business — the largest renewable developer in India, building at a pace unmatched outside China, on high-margin contracted cash flows, riding one of the strongest structural tailwinds in global energy. It is also a heavily leveraged, founder-controlled company inside a scrutinised group, valued at a multiple that assumes everything goes right. The debate is almost entirely about funding, leverage and valuation — not about whether the plants work, because they do.

The bull case

  • Unmatched growth runway — a clear path from ~19 GW to 50 GW by 2030, into a market that must triple renewables; each GW a new 25-year annuity.
  • World-record execution — added 5.1 GW in FY26 (a global high ex-China); Khavda is a structural, scale-and-cost moat few can match.
  • Contracted, high-margin cash — ~91% EBITDA margin on fixed-tariff PPAs, much of it sovereign-backed (SECI); bond-like visibility.
  • The storage edge — record-scale BESS at Khavda moves AGEL toward firm, round-the-clock clean power that can truly displace coal.
  • Easing overhang — with the US matter de-escalating and market access reopening, the "group penalty" on its cost of capital can compress.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — ~130–150× earnings prices in flawless, fully-funded execution for years; no margin for a stumble.
  • Leverage tightrope — net debt ₹91,252 Cr and climbing; the model depends on continuous, cheap access to capital — the key risk.
  • Funding-cost / group overhang — Hindenburg and the US DOJ/SEC matter showed group sentiment can shut funding doors and lift its cost of capital overnight.
  • Thin net profit & modest ROCE — interest and depreciation eat most of the fat margin; returns on capital are still low while capital is being deployed.
  • Execution / curtailment / tariff risk — transmission bottlenecks, aggressive tender bidding, and intermittency could compress the returns the valuation assumes.
The bottom line

Adani Green is a leveraged, world-class bet on India's energy transition — a company that borrows heavily to build the cheapest, largest fleet of clean-power annuities in the country, then collects contracted, high-margin cash for decades. The operating story is excellent and the tailwind is real; the entire risk sits on the balance sheet and the share price. The story over the coming years will be written in a handful of numbers each quarter — capacity added (GW) (is the 50-GW march on track?), net debt and net-debt/EBITDA (is the build-out affordable?), and the cost of new and refinanced debt (is the group penalty fading or biting?). Keep the build cheap and the funding cheap, and a tripling asset base compounds into something formidable; let leverage or a funding shock catch it mid-build, and a stock priced at 130×+ earnings has a very long way to fall. It is, in the end, less a story about the sun and the wind than about the price and availability of money.
