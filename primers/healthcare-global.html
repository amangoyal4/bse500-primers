Healthcare Global, a specialist bet on cancer

A full walk through India's largest dedicated cancer-hospital network — why treating only oncology, in a country where general hospitals do a bit of everything, is a deliberate and unusual strategy; how a chain of high-tech cancer centres actually earns money; why a brand-new centre loses money for years before it pays off; and why the company just changed private-equity owner — from CVC to KKR — while quietly selling off its fertility business to become a pure cancer specialist.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Healthcare Global Enterprises — everyone calls it HCG — runs India's largest network of hospitals dedicated to one disease: cancer. Where an Apollo or a Fortis is a general multi-specialty chain that treats hearts, bones, babies and tumours under one roof, HCG made the opposite choice — go narrow and deep, building specialist oncology centres that do little else but diagnose and treat cancer, packed with the expensive machines and rare specialists that cancer demands. Roughly 25 centres span India (plus one in Nairobi, Kenya), organised on a hub-and-spoke model, and sold under the trusted HCG brand.

Three facts frame the whole company:

  • It is a focused specialist, not a general hospital. Cancer care needs a different toolkit from ordinary healthcare — linear accelerators that fire targeted radiation, PET-CT and cyclotron imaging that pinpoint tumours, robotic surgery suites, and teams of surgical, radiation and medical oncologists working together on a single patient. That equipment is hugely expensive and the talent is scarce, so concentrating on oncology lets HCG build a depth of capability a general hospital cannot easily match. The specialisation is the strategy.
  • Its economics are decided one centre at a time. A HCG centre is not profitable the day it opens — it burns cash for two to four years while doctors, referrals and patient volumes build, then crosses breakeven and its margin climbs as the fixed cost of all that machinery is spread over more patients. So the network is always a mix of mature centres (throwing off healthy profit) carrying emerging centres (still ramping). The blended result in FY26 was an adjusted EBITDA margin of ~18.5% — held down by the young centres, and rising as they age.
  • FY26 was a solid year under a new owner. Revenue grew ~15% to ~₹2,545 Cr and adjusted EBITDA rose ~19% to ~₹471 Cr. In the background, ownership changed hands: private-equity firm KKR bought controlling control from CVC (which had owned HCG since 2020), and the company sold its Milann fertility arm to become a pure cancer specialist. The reported bottom-line profit is tiny — buried under heavy depreciation and interest — which is why this business is read on EBITDA and cash flow, not on net profit.
In plain terms

Picture a chain of shops that sell only one thing, but sell it better than anyone — and where each new shop needs an enormous, expensive machine installed on day one and takes years to attract enough customers to pay that machine off. That is HCG. Every centre it opens is a multi-year bet: heavy cost up front, losses while it fills up, then rising profit once the specialists and referrals arrive and the machines run near capacity. The company's value is not really "revenue" — it is the sum of centres at different points on that curve, plus the volume of cancer patients each one treats and the revenue it earns per bed. FY26 showed the mature centres pulling the average up. The question for every year ahead is whether the young centres climb the same curve fast enough, and whether HCG's new owner, KKR, can make the whole network more profitable than CVC did.

01

Why cancer care is its own kind of business

Before the company, the disease — and why treating it is unlike running a general hospital.

To understand HCG you first have to see why oncology sits apart from the rest of healthcare. A general hospital treats hundreds of conditions, most of them with relatively standard equipment and generalist-plus-specialist staff. Cancer is different on three axes at once — the technology is extreme, the talent is scarce and team-based, and the treatment is long and multi-modal. Each of those pushes toward specialisation.

The three ways doctors attack a tumour

Almost all cancer treatment is some combination of three "modalities," and a serious cancer centre must offer all three under one roof, coordinated:

  • Surgical oncology — physically removing the tumour, often with robotic or minimally-invasive systems. The most familiar, but only part of the answer.
  • Radiation oncology — killing cancer cells with precisely-targeted beams from a linear accelerator (LINAC), or systems like CyberKnife and TrueBeam. This is HCG's historical stronghold and the most capital-heavy modality: a single LINAC bunker costs many crores and needs shielded rooms, physicists and constant calibration.
  • Medical oncology — treating cancer with drugs: chemotherapy, immunotherapy and increasingly targeted biologic therapies. This is the fastest-growing, most drug-cost-driven modality and often the longest patient relationship.
Why the expensive machinery changes everything

A cardiac ward can be equipped and staffed at a manageable, repeatable cost. A comprehensive cancer centre cannot: it needs linear accelerators, PET-CT scanners (which reveal where cancer is active in the body), sometimes a cyclotron to produce the radioactive tracers those scans use, nuclear-medicine labs, molecular-diagnostics and pathology, plus surgical robotics. That is tens of crores of fixed capital per centre before a single patient is treated. Two consequences follow, and they define the whole business. First, once installed, that equipment costs almost the same to run whether it treats ten patients a day or forty — so utilisation is everything: profit lives in filling the machines. Second, no general hospital can casually replicate all of it, which is exactly why a focused chain like HCG can build a depth of cancer capability that a broad multi-specialty rival struggles to match centre-for-centre.

Scarce, team-based talent

Cancer is not treated by a single doctor but by a tumour board — surgeon, radiation oncologist, medical oncologist, radiologist and pathologist deciding a plan together for each patient. Those specialists take a decade-plus to train and are in short supply in India. A chain that already employs deep oncology teams, runs its own academic and training programmes, and carries a recognised brand can attract and retain that talent more easily than a general hospital adding a cancer wing. HCG has leaned on this from the start: it was founded by a practising oncologist and built a reputation as a place serious cancer specialists want to work — a soft asset that is genuinely hard for rivals to copy.

02

The hub-and-spoke network: how HCG is built

The structural idea that lets one company put high-end cancer care in many cities.

You cannot afford to put a cyclotron and a full molecular-diagnostics lab in every town. But you also cannot ask a cancer patient in a smaller city to travel hundreds of kilometres for every appointment. HCG's answer — and the standard model for organised cancer care — is hub-and-spoke: a few large Centres of Excellence carry the heaviest, rarest technology, while smaller spoke centres bring day-to-day treatment closer to where patients live and feed the most complex cases back to the hub.

The rarest, costliest capability sits at the hub and is shared; the spokes widen the catchment and keep the hub's expensive machines busy. The network only works if patients flow inward — which is why referrals and diagnostics matter as much as beds.

The logic is financial as much as clinical. The hub's cyclotron or robotic-surgery suite is only justifiable if enough patients use it — and the spokes are what generate that flow, funnelling complex cases upward while handling routine chemotherapy and follow-ups locally. In turn, the hub's reputation and specialists lend credibility to the spokes. Done well, the model lets HCG put a recognised cancer brand into a mid-size city at a fraction of the cost of building a full Centre of Excellence there, because that city's spoke leans on the hub for the heaviest work.

The catch built into the model

Hub-and-spoke is elegant, but it front-loads risk. Every new spoke is another fixed-cost location that must find its patients before it pays for itself, and the hub only earns its keep if the spokes actually deliver the referral flow. Open too many spokes too fast and the network fills with young, loss-making centres dragging the average down; open too few and the hubs' expensive machines sit under-used. The whole art of running HCG is pacing the network — adding centres fast enough to grow, slow enough that maturing ones can carry the newcomers. That balance, more than any single hospital, is what an investor is really underwriting.

03

What HCG actually runs — and what it just sold

The centres, the technology, and a deliberate narrowing to pure oncology.

HCG is, at its core, a network of specialist cancer hospitals plus the diagnostics that feed them. Around 25 centres — comprehensive centres, smaller units and day-care facilities — operate across India, with one international centre in Nairobi, Kenya. It describes itself as the largest provider of cancer care in India measured by the number of treatment centres licensed for radiation by the atomic-energy regulator (AERB) — a telling yardstick, because radiation capacity is exactly the scarce, licence-gated capability that defines a serious oncology chain.

The parts of the business

  • Comprehensive cancer centres — the heart of HCG: full surgical, radiation and medical oncology under one roof, anchored by the Bengaluru Centre of Excellence and major centres across South, West and East India.
  • Diagnostics & imaging — PET-CT, nuclear medicine, pathology and molecular diagnostics, both as a clinical necessity and as a feeder that pulls newly-diagnosed patients into the network.
  • Multi-specialty adjacency — a limited amount of non-cancer care attached to some hospitals, but the strategic direction is unmistakably toward focus, not away from it.
The genuine surprise: HCG is narrowing, not diversifying

Most growing hospital companies broaden — add cardiac, ortho, transplants, maternity — to fill beds and spread risk. HCG has just done the opposite. In FY26 it agreed to sell Milann, its well-known fertility (IVF) chain, run under the operating company BACC Health Care, to Inviga Healthcare Fund for ~₹37.6 Cr, with the deal signed in the March-2026 quarter and closing in early FY27. Milann was a real business — about ₹60 Cr of revenue — but it earned thin margins, sat only marginally above breakeven after tax, and would have needed fresh capital and management attention to scale. Management's verdict was blunt: that bandwidth belongs to oncology. So instead of adding a leg, HCG cut one off, choosing to become a pure-play cancer company. In a sector that instinctively diversifies, deliberately narrowing to a single disease is the contrarian move — and it is the clearest signal of what kind of company the new owner intends to run.

The technology that sets the cost base

HCG's fixed costs — and its competitive moat — sit in machines and specialists, not in bricks. Linear accelerators and radiation systems (LINAC, CyberKnife, TrueBeam), PET-CT and nuclear-medicine imaging, molecular diagnostics, robotic and minimally-invasive surgery: this is the equipment that makes a centre a genuine cancer hospital rather than a ward with an oncologist. It is why a new centre is so expensive to open, why utilisation matters so much, and why the depth of HCG's installed base is hard for a general chain to match quickly.

04

The company, and who controls it

A founder-oncologist's creation, now on its second private-equity owner in five years.

HCG has an unusually eventful ownership history for a hospital company — three distinct owners in five years — and that history is central to the investment case. It was built by a doctor, taken over by one global private-equity house, and has just passed to another. Understanding that chain explains both the discipline being imposed on the business and the risk that comes with a controlling financial owner.

Ownership & structure

  • Controlling owner: KKR (via its acquisition vehicle) is now the largest shareholder and controls the company, having bought CVC's stake at ₹445/share and run the SEBI-mandated open offer at ₹504.41. Its final holding depends on open-offer take-up, expected in the ~54%–77% range. Investors should confirm the exact post-offer promoter percentage in the latest shareholding filing.
  • The founder's role: Dr B.S. Ajaikumar, who built HCG, has stepped back to Non-Executive Chairman, concentrating on clinical excellence, academics and research while professional management runs operations — a deliberate separation of the clinical soul of the business from its financial steering.
  • The prior owner: CVC exits majority control after five years with a ~3.4× rupee return, retaining only a small residual — a clean handoff from one PE house to another rather than a return to founder or strategic ownership.
Why the ownership chain matters both ways: a disciplined global PE owner like KKR typically brings capital for expansion, a hard focus on margin and utilisation, and professional governance — genuine positives that show up in the numbers. But a controlling financial owner also runs the company for its own return horizon: it may add leverage, prioritise EBITDA and cash generation over softer goals, and will eventually seek an exit (a sale or block-sale) that can overhang the stock. The change from CVC to KKR is not cosmetic — it resets who sets HCG's capital-allocation and expansion pace for years.
05

The J-curve: why a new centre loses money first

The single most important idea in the case — and it is specific to a ramping specialist-hospital network.

The instinct is to value HCG on its total revenue or its blended margin. That misses the mechanism entirely. HCG is best understood as a portfolio of centres, each at a different age — and a cancer centre's profitability follows a pronounced J-curve: it plunges into losses when it opens, bottoms out, then climbs steadily as it matures. The company's margin at any moment is just the weighted average of where its centres sit on that curve. Get this, and everything else about the financials makes sense.

Why the J-shape? On day one a new centre carries almost its full cost — the LINAC, the PET-CT, the specialists' salaries, the building — but only a trickle of patients, because referrals, reputation and doctor relationships take years to build in a new city. So it loses money. As word spreads and the tumour board fills up, patient volume climbs while those fixed costs barely move — so each additional patient drops almost straight to profit. A mature HCG centre can run at a ~20%-plus EBITDA margin; a first-year centre can be deeply negative. The gap between them is the entire story of the blended number.

The device that only fits a ramping specialist chain: value = centre maturity × case volume × ARPOB

Forget the top line. The number that actually runs HCG is the product of three things, centre by centre: how mature the centre is (where it sits on the J-curve), how many cancer cases it treats (volume filling the fixed machines), and how much revenue it earns per occupied bed (ARPOB — richer in a metro doing complex surgery and radiation, thinner in a young spoke doing routine chemo). Multiply those across ~25 centres and you have the company. A new centre scores low on all three and loses money; a filled Bengaluru hub scores high on all three and gushes cash. This is why HCG can grow revenue and still report a thin bottom line — it is deliberately carrying a batch of low-scoring young centres in the hope they become high-scoring old ones. The bet is not "cancer care grows"; it is "our specific emerging centres climb the curve, on schedule."

This reframes what "growth" even means here. When HCG opens a centre, near-term margins and profit get worse, not better — the new location dilutes the average before it lifts it. A company aggressively adding centres can look less profitable than a static one, even though it is building more future earnings. So the honest way to read HCG is to separate the mature base (already earning) from the emerging cohort (still investing), and ask whether the young cohort is ramping on the timeline management promised. That single distinction, not the headline margin, is where the value is won or lost.

06

ARPOB, occupancy, and the profit puzzle

The operating gauges that matter — and why the reported net profit looks almost nonexistent.

If the J-curve is the shape of the story, two operating numbers tell you where HCG sits on it in any given quarter: ARPOB (average revenue per occupied bed per day) and occupancy (how full the beds are). Together they capture whether the expensive infrastructure is being used, and how richly. And they lead to a genuinely surprising place: a company this large that reports almost no net profit at all.

ARPOB — the intensity of care

ARPOB measures how much revenue each occupied bed generates a day. A high ARPOB signals complex, high-value work — major cancer surgery, advanced radiation, sophisticated drug regimens — while a low ARPOB signals routine care. HCG's overall ARPOB in FY26 was around ₹44,342 a day, but the spread across the network is enormous: its mature metro hubs in Bengaluru and Ahmedabad run at roughly ₹1 lakh a day, more than double the average, while young spoke centres sit well below it. That gap is the J-curve again, seen through a different lens — mature, complex, metro centres earn multiples of what a ramping regional centre does per bed.

Occupancy is the companion gauge: HCG's oncology centres have historically run in the mid-60s percent range, and every extra point of occupancy on that near-fixed cost base falls largely to profit. Rising occupancy at a rising ARPOB is the ideal — more beds full, each earning more — and that combination is precisely what lifts a maturing centre up its J-curve. Watching these two numbers move together is the most direct read on operating health available quarter to quarter.

The surprise that stops most first-time readers cold

Here is the fact that startles people: India's largest cancer-hospital chain, with ~₹2,545 Cr of revenue and a healthy ~18.5% EBITDA margin, reports a net profit of only single-digit-to-low-double-digit crores — a headline P/E that runs into the hundreds and looks absurd. It is not a mistake, and it is not weak operations. The gap between that fat EBITDA and that thin net profit is eaten by two things intrinsic to this business: huge depreciation on all those linear accelerators, PET-CT scanners and buildings, and interest on the debt that funded them — plus the drag of young centres and one-off items like the Milann exit. In other words, the machinery that makes HCG a real cancer hospital is exactly what buries the bottom line. That is why nobody sensible values this company on reported earnings; the business is read on EBITDA, cash flow and centre maturity, and the reported net profit is close to meaningless until the asset base is more fully depreciated and the young centres mature.

Why this reframes the valuation debate: because reported profit is near-zero, the usual P/E lens is useless here and even misleading. The market prices HCG on EV/EBITDA and on the future earnings power locked in its maturing centres — which is why the shares can trade at what looks like an insane P/E while being, on a hospital-sector basis, priced much like its peers. Read the EBITDA and the centre mix; ignore the P/E headline.
07

The demand behind it all

Why the market HCG serves grows almost regardless of the economy.

A specialist bet only pays off if the specialty keeps growing — and cancer, tragically, is one of the most reliable structural growth markets in Indian healthcare. HCG's demand does not really rise and fall with GDP; it rises with demographics, detection and the slow spread of the means to pay. Three forces push it up together.

There is a fourth, subtler tailwind: the shift of complex cancer care from scattered general hospitals to organised specialist chains. As treatment grows more technical — precision radiation, molecular diagnostics, targeted drugs — it increasingly needs the concentrated equipment and expertise that only a focused oncology centre can justify. That structural drift toward organised, specialist providers is the current HCG is designed to ride, and it is why a pure cancer chain can grow share even in a market where general hospitals are also expanding.

The demand truth to hold onto

Very few Indian businesses can say their end-market grows almost independently of the economic cycle, is under-served relative to need, and is shifting structurally toward the exact model they run. HCG can. That is the deep appeal of the specialist bet: the disease burden is rising, detection and insurance are widening the funnel, and complex care keeps migrating to focused centres. The demand is not the question. The question, always, is execution — whether HCG can open and mature centres profitably enough to convert that rising tide of patients into rising cash flow, rather than into a permanent construction site of loss-making new locations.

08

What a private-equity owner pushes for

How KKR's ownership is likely to reshape priorities — and the risk stack that comes with it.

HCG is no longer really a founder's company or a public float with dispersed owners; it is a PE-controlled asset, and that changes the objective function. A financial owner like KKR buys a business like this to make it materially more valuable over a defined horizon and then realise that value. Understanding what such an owner typically pushes for tells you a lot about how HCG will be run.

The PE playbook, applied to a cancer chain

  • Margin over vanity — relentless focus on lifting the blended EBITDA margin: maturing existing centres, improving occupancy and ARPOB, tightening procurement of expensive drugs and consumables, and cutting anything that does not serve oncology (hence the Milann exit and the pure-play focus).
  • Capital discipline — every new centre judged on its return and ramp timeline, not on empire-building; capacity added where the J-curve is most likely to pay, funded with an eye on the balance sheet.
  • Selective expansion — capital for growth (the network targets substantial new bed additions over the coming years), but paced so maturing centres can carry the new ones rather than the average collapsing under too many openings at once.
  • Professional governance & eventual exit — stronger reporting and management, and, on a multi-year view, a planned exit — a strategic sale or staged block-sales — that is the owner's ultimate goal and the market's eventual overhang.
The double edge of PE control

A disciplined owner is mostly good news for the operating numbers: it is why HCG has been narrowing to its most profitable focus, squeezing utilisation, and setting hard margin targets. But the same owner introduces risks a founder-run or widely-held company would not carry to the same degree. It may fund expansion partly with debt, adding leverage to an already asset-heavy, high-depreciation business. It runs the company for a finite horizon, which can favour near-term EBITDA and cash over patient, decade-long clinical investment. And its eventual exit — whenever KKR chooses to sell — is a known future event that can weigh on the share price regardless of how the hospitals are performing. The quality of the assets and the quality of the ownership have to be judged together.

The full risk stack, in one place

  • New-centre ramp & capex risk — if emerging centres climb the J-curve slower than planned, they stay a margin and cash drag for longer, and expansion turns from investment into a chronic overhang.
  • Specialist retention — the business runs on scarce oncologists; losing key doctors can hollow out a centre's referrals and reputation faster than any spreadsheet suggests.
  • Equipment utilisation — the huge fixed cost of LINACs and PET-CT only pays off if the machines run near capacity; under-used technology is dead weight.
  • Payer & pricing pressure — a rising share of insurance and government-scheme patients can cap pricing and squeeze margins on the very volumes that fill the beds.
  • Competition from general chains — Apollo, Fortis, Max and others are adding oncology capacity, chasing the same high-value cancer patient with deeper balance sheets.
  • PE ownership, leverage & change of control — the very thing driving discipline also brings possible added debt, a finite horizon and a future exit overhang.
  • Valuation — priced on future EBITDA and centre maturity, so any stumble in the ramp is felt sharply in a multiple that already assumes the young centres climb.

The industry and the rivals

A specialist standing among generalists — with its own strategic choice on trial.

HCG competes in an unusual position: it is the largest dedicated cancer chain, but it is far from the largest hospital company treating cancer. Its rivals are mostly big general multi-specialty groups that run oncology as one department among many. The whole investment debate is, in a sense, whether HCG's narrow-and-deep choice beats their broad-and-diversified one — for cancer patients, and for shareholders.

The competitive question that actually decides it

The threat to HCG is not that cancer care shrinks — it grows for everyone. It is that well-capitalised general chains bolt on oncology and, with deeper pockets, buy the same machines and hire the same specialists in the same rich metros. HCG's defence is genuine but must be continually earned: a brand built entirely around cancer, the deepest radiation and comprehensive-centre footprint in the country, an academic and clinical reputation that attracts oncologists, and a hub-and-spoke reach into cities a generalist may not prioritise. In a market where everyone can buy the hardware, HCG's edge has to come from focus, referral density and specialist depth — the things a part-time oncology department cannot replicate. Whether that edge holds against balance sheets several times its size is the live question a shareholder is really taking a view on.

10

Financials & valuation

Read on EBITDA and centre maturity, not on the near-invisible bottom line.

FY26 was a steady, broad-based year: revenue up ~15%, adjusted EBITDA up ~19% with margins expanding to 18.5%, and management reiterating ~15% growth guidance for FY27. The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A focused specialist, freshly owned and freshly narrowed, judged on whether its young centres grow up.

HCG enters FY27 as a sharper version of itself: pure-play oncology after the Milann sale, a disciplined new PE owner in KKR, a rising blended margin, and a structural tailwind of cancer demand it is purpose-built to serve. What it cannot yet prove is the only thing that matters — that its cohort of emerging centres will climb the J-curve to mature-centre economics on the timeline the valuation assumes. That single execution question frames both cases.

The bull case

  • The category leader in a growth market — India's largest dedicated cancer network, riding rising incidence, wider detection and spreading insurance that grows almost regardless of the economy.
  • A maturing network — the emerging-centre cohort climbing the J-curve should lift the blended margin toward the low-20s, compounding EBITDA without needing a re-rating.
  • Sharpened focus — the Milann exit frees capital and attention for pure oncology, exactly where the returns and the moat are.
  • Disciplined owner — KKR's capital, governance and margin focus should professionalise expansion and utilisation, as CVC's stewardship already did.
  • A genuine specialist moat — a cancer-only brand, the deepest radiation footprint, and specialist depth a part-time oncology department cannot easily match.

The bear case

  • Ramp risk is the whole thesis — if emerging centres mature slowly, the margin stalls and growth capex becomes a lasting drag rather than future earnings.
  • Deep-pocketed generalists — Apollo, Max, Fortis and others are pouring capital into oncology, chasing the same high-value patients with far larger balance sheets.
  • Asset-heavy, thin-profit model — huge depreciation and interest keep reported earnings near zero; any wobble in EBITDA is felt sharply.
  • PE overhang — leverage, a finite ownership horizon and an eventual KKR exit sit over the stock independent of hospital performance.
  • Payer & specialist pressure — a rising insurance/scheme mix can cap pricing, and losing key oncologists can hollow out a centre quickly.
The bottom line

Healthcare Global is a rare thing in Indian healthcare: a company that deliberately went narrow, staking everything on being the best at one disease rather than adequate at many. That focus buys it a real moat — brand, radiation depth, specialist gravity — in a market whose demand only rises. But the same focus concentrates the risk into one variable: the maturation of its emerging centres. Everything the new owner is doing — selling Milann, tightening utilisation, targeting ~20% margins — is aimed at accelerating that curve. The next test is concrete and datable: whether the blended EBITDA margin keeps climbing toward mature-centre levels through FY27 as occupancy and ARPOB rise and the young cohort crosses breakeven on schedule. Deliver that maturation and the specialist bet compounds quietly; let the young centres stall on the curve, and a business priced for them to grow up will have to explain why they didn't.
