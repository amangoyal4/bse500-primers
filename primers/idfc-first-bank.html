00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

IDFC First Bank is a private-sector bank born from two mergers and one obsession: retail. Its 2018 union of IDFC Bank (a young bank spun out of an infrastructure-financing institution) and Capital First (a fast-growing consumer & MSME lender) put Capital First's founder V. Vaidyanathan in the CEO chair — and he set about a rare kind of transformation: turning a wholesale, low-margin, loss-making infra lender into a granular, high-margin, consumer-and-small-business retail bank. With ~₹2.90 lakh crore of loans and ~₹2.84 lakh crore of customer deposits, it is now a mid-to-large private bank — but the story is not its size. It is the metamorphosis, and where it is in the journey.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A high-NIM retail machine, rebuilt from the ground up. By tilting the book to consumer, home, vehicle, credit-card and MSME lending — small, high-yield, granular loans — IDFC First earns a net interest margin of ~5.75% (FY26; 5.93% in Q4), far above the ~3–3.5% of a typical corporate-heavy bank. That fat margin is the whole point of going retail.
  • A deposit franchise conjured almost from nothing. A bank is only as good as its funding, and IDFC First had almost none in 2018. It built one at astonishing speed — customer deposits compounding ~32% a year and CASA ~58% a year since December 2018 — reaching a 49.8% CASA ratio (₹1.47 lakh crore of low-cost current/savings money). This is arguably its single greatest achievement.
  • An honest setback: the microfinance stress. Just as the model was scaling, an industry-wide microfinance (MFI) blow-up in FY25–26 spiked credit costs and flattened profit — FY25 PAT fell ~48% and RoA collapsed toward ~0.4%. Management has now shrunk the MFI book to ~2.7% of assets, GNPA/NNPA have healed to 1.61%/0.48%, and the stated goal is to normalise the lending business back toward a ~1.5–1.6% RoA. The whole investment debate lives here.
In plain terms

Picture a bank that used to lend big, thin-margin loans to power plants and highways — and lost money doing it. In 2018 it merged with a nimble consumer-finance company, took that company's boss, and flipped the strategy: instead of a few enormous loans, make millions of small ones — a bike loan here, a shopkeeper's working-capital loan there, a home loan, a credit card. Small loans to ordinary people pay a much higher interest rate, so the bank's margin roughly doubled. To fund all that lending it also had to persuade millions of ordinary Indians to open savings accounts — which it did, fast, with a customer-friendly brand and high savings rates. The catch: one slice of that small-loan lending — tiny unsecured loans to poor rural women (microfinance) — went through a bad patch across the whole industry in 2025, and the losses there wiped out most of the bank's profit for a year. The bank is now shrinking that risky slice and waiting for profitability to climb back to where the rest of the business says it should be.

01

Bank economics 101: how a bank actually earns

No finance degree required — just the six numbers that drive everything.

A bank is, at its core, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors) and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap between what it earns on loans and pays on deposits — scaled to its assets — is the engine. For IDFC First specifically, two of these numbers tell the whole tale: the margin (unusually high, because it lends retail) and the credit cost (the thing that microfinance blew up). Get those two, and you understand the stock.

The only banking units you need — with IDFC First's FY26 number
TermWhat it means (and IDFC First's number)

The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. A bank gets richer four ways — lend at a higher yield, fund cheaper, lose less, and earn fees. IDFC First is brilliant at the first (its retail book earns a ~6% margin) and has become genuinely good at the second (a ~50% CASA ratio is cheap funding). Its Achilles' heel has been the third — loan losses — because the same "lend at a higher yield" instinct pushed it into microfinance, the highest-yield and highest-risk corner of lending. The whole IDFC First story is: a bank with a superb top line (margin) whose bottom line (profit) was temporarily wrecked by loan losses in one segment — and is now recovering as that segment shrinks.

Why the margin and the credit cost are the two dials that matter

  • A high margin is earned, not given. IDFC First's ~6% NIM isn't luck — it comes from deliberately lending small, to consumers and micro-businesses, where competition is thinner and rates are higher. That's the reward for the hard work of retail underwriting and collections at scale.
  • But a high margin invites high losses. The borrowers who pay 15–24% are, by definition, riskier than a AAA corporate paying 8%. So a retail bank lives and dies on credit cost — the discipline of provisioning and collections. When it's controlled, a high-NIM book is enormously profitable; when it slips (as MFI did), the losses eat the margin.
  • RoA is the scoreboard. Margin minus costs minus losses plus fees, all over assets, is return on assets — the master number. A ~6% NIM should throw off a ~1.5%+ RoA. That it currently doesn't (~0.4%) is the gap the recovery is meant to close.
02

How the money flows: cheap CASA in, high-yield retail out

Read this once and the entire bank — and its margin — clicks into place.

A bank is a conveyor belt for money. Deposits come in one end; loans go out the other; the spread between the two is harvested as net interest income. IDFC First's belt is distinctive at both ends: it pulls in unusually cheap, sticky CASA money at the front, and lends it out as unusually high-yield, granular retail loans at the back. That combination — cheap in, dear out — is exactly why its margin runs near 6%. The one place the belt jammed was a small, high-risk section of the "out" end: microfinance.

The two levers that set the spread

A bank's margin is just (what it earns on loans) minus (what it pays for funding). IDFC First moved both levers hard after 2018:

  • Earn far more on loans. Swap the old, thin-spread infrastructure and wholesale book for high-yield retail lending — consumer durables, two-wheelers, home loans, credit cards, small-business loans. A retail book yields dramatically more per rupee than lending to a power project.
  • Pay less for funding. Build a CASA franchise from scratch so the bank isn't reliant on expensive bulk borrowings. From a near-zero CASA base in 2018 to ~50% today — this is what keeps the cost of funds contained even as the bank grows.
Why IDFC First's margin is double a corporate bank's — and why that's the design, not an accident

Lend ₹500 crore to a top-rated infrastructure company and you might earn a spread of 1.5–2%. Lend that same ₹500 crore as fifty thousand ₹1-lakh consumer and small-business loans and you earn a spread of 5–7% — because those borrowers can't get money as cheaply, and few banks want the operational grind of underwriting and collecting from them at scale. IDFC First deliberately chose the grind. That's why its NIM is ~6% while a corporate-heavy bank's is ~3%. The trade-off is written into the model: the higher yield comes with higher risk, so the bank has to be world-class at collections. Most of its book proves it can be; microfinance proved that even it has limits. The margin is the reward; the credit discipline is the price.

03

The two mergers and the retail pivot — the defining story

How an infrastructure lender became a consumer bank. This is the whole company.

If you remember one thing about IDFC First, make it this: it is a retail bank wearing the body of an old infrastructure lender, and the entire investment case is about how completely — and how profitably — that transformation lands. Two mergers built the company, and a single strategic obsession (go granular, go retail) rebuilt it from the inside.

Act one — the 2018 merger of IDFC Bank + Capital First

  • IDFC Bank was born in 2015 when IDFC Limited — a government-created Infrastructure Development Finance Company — was granted a banking licence and hived off its lending into a bank. It inherited a low-margin, wholesale, infrastructure-heavy loan book laced with legacy problem assets, and a nearly non-existent retail deposit franchise. It struggled from day one.
  • Capital First was the opposite: a nimble, profitable, fast-growing consumer & MSME finance company built by V. Vaidyanathan (who had earlier built ICICI's retail business), specialising in exactly the small, high-yield, granular lending IDFC Bank lacked — with a strong track record on growth and asset quality.
  • The 2018 merger (effective 18 December 2018) fused the two, creating IDFC First Bank and installing Vaidyanathan as MD & CEO. The logic was elegant: give Capital First's retail-lending engine a banking licence and a deposit base, and give IDFC Bank's balance sheet a profitable, high-margin lending model. On day one, retail was only ~35% of the book.

Act two — the strategy: shrink the legacy, build the retail

Vaidyanathan's plan had two moving parts, executed in parallel over five-plus years:

  • Run down the legacy infrastructure and wholesale book. The old low-yield, lumpy, problem-prone infra loans were deliberately allowed to mature and roll off — cleaning up asset quality and freeing capital for higher-return lending. The infrastructure book, once a large share, has been reduced to a small tail.
  • Build a granular retail engine at speed. Grow consumer loans, home loans, vehicle finance, credit cards, gold loans and small-business/MSME lending — the high-margin, diversified book — until it dominated. Retail, rural and MSME lending now make up the overwhelming majority of loans, and ~87% of loan growth in FY26 came from mortgages, vehicle, consumer, wholesale and business-banking segments.

Act three — the 2024 reverse merger that removed the holding company

A final structural knot remained: IDFC Limited (the old parent) still sat above the bank as a holding company, an awkward, value-trapping layer. On 1 October 2024, the long-planned reverse merger completed — IDFC Limited merged into IDFC First Bank and ceased to exist. IDFC shareholders received 155 bank shares for every 100 IDFC shares. The result: a clean, single-entity structure with no holding company and no promoter — exactly like HDFC Bank — which simplified ownership and modestly lifted book value per share.

Why this is the section that matters most: almost every other fact about IDFC First is a consequence of this transformation. The ~6% NIM exists because it went retail. The MFI stress happened because "go high-yield" pushed it into microfinance. The capital raise was needed because the transformation is capital-hungry and the MFI hit dented internal capital generation. Read the whole company as "a five-year rebuild of an infra lender into a retail bank — now in the awkward, capital-heavy, MFI-dented middle innings, waiting for its excellent margin to finally show up as excellent profit."
04

Why granular retail earns a ~6% NIM

The mechanism behind the fat margin — and why it's the prize the whole strategy chases.

IDFC First's defining financial signature is its net interest margin of ~5.75% (FY26) — close to double what a corporate-heavy bank earns, and even ahead of many peers. This is not a quirk; it is the direct, intended output of the retail transformation. Understanding why granular retail lending produces such a wide margin is the key to understanding the bank's entire earnings power.

Illustrative. The point: the more granular and consumer-oriented the loan book, the wider the spread — because small borrowers pay materially higher rates than large corporates. IDFC First sits at the high-margin end by design.

The three reasons small loans earn big margins

  • Small borrowers pay higher rates. A consumer buying a phone on EMI, a shopkeeper needing working capital, a family taking a used-car loan — none can borrow at the 8% a blue-chip company commands. They pay 12–24%. Aggregate millions of these and the average loan yield is far above a corporate book's.
  • Cheap funding on the other side. A high loan yield only becomes a high margin if the funding is cheap. IDFC First's ~50% CASA ratio (§05) means a big chunk of its money costs almost nothing — so the gap between loan yield and funding cost is wide. High yield plus cheap funding is the whole formula.
  • Granularity is a moat and a risk-spreader. A book of millions of small loans has no single exposure big enough to sink the bank — one borrower defaulting is a rounding error. That diversification is what makes high-yield lending bankable at scale, provided collections hold.
Why "high NIM" is the reward and the warning at once

A ~6% margin sounds like an unambiguous win — and structurally, it is the bank's biggest asset. But margin in banking is never free: the market pays you a wide spread precisely because the lending is harder and riskier. So a high-NIM bank must be judged on the net spread after losses, not the gross. IDFC First's own recent history is the perfect illustration: its gross margin stayed near 6% right through FY25–26, but its profit cratered, because microfinance losses ate the extra spread. The lesson for a reader: admire the NIM, but always ask "after credit costs, what's left?" When collections are clean, a ~6% NIM is a money-printing machine. When they aren't, the same NIM is just a buffer absorbing losses. The recovery thesis is entirely about restoring the net — bringing credit costs down so the fat gross margin finally drops to the bottom line.

05

The deposit franchise built from scratch

The quiet triumph — and the foundation everything else rests on.

Any bank can lend; the hard part is funding the loans cheaply and stickily. In 2018 IDFC First had almost no retail deposits — the old IDFC Bank funded itself largely through expensive wholesale borrowings, a structurally weak, low-margin way to run a bank. Building a genuine, low-cost, granular deposit franchise from a near-zero base — and doing it in barely six years — is arguably the bank's single greatest achievement, and the true foundation of the ~6% NIM.

How a young bank out-gathered deposits

  • A high savings rate, early. To attract savers with no brand loyalty, IDFC First offered market-leading savings-account interest rates in its early years — paying up to lure balances, then gradually normalising as the franchise stuck. It bought its way into customers' primary-account habit.
  • A genuinely customer-friendly brand. The bank leaned hard into consumer-friendly positioning — zero-fee banking on many services, no hidden charges, a clean app, "customer-first" messaging. In a sector famous for nickel-and-diming, that differentiation won trust and stuck accounts.
  • Branches plus digital. A rapidly expanded branch network in chosen geographies, paired with a strong digital and mobile experience, let it acquire and service accounts cheaply and at scale — turning first-time savers into sticky CASA balances.
  • The pay-off: a ~50% CASA ratio. The result is one of the highest CASA ratios in Indian banking (49.8%) — meaning roughly half its deposits are low- or no-interest current/savings money. That cheap funding is precisely what lets the high-yield retail book translate into a ~6% margin.
Why the deposit build is the real foundation

It's tempting to think a bank's edge is its lending. But lending is the easy half — anyone can give money away at a high rate. The genuinely hard, genuinely valuable thing is funding those loans cheaply, from a sticky, diversified base of ordinary savers, rather than from flighty wholesale money that vanishes when markets turn. A near-zero-to-₹2.84-lakh-crore deposit franchise, half of it low-cost CASA, is what turns IDFC First from "a high-yield lender that has to borrow expensively" into "a proper bank with a durable, self-reinforcing funding advantage". Every rupee of the ~6% margin ultimately rests on this. It doesn't grab headlines the way a big loss or a big capital raise does — but it is the deepest, most durable thing the transformation built.

06

The microfinance stress and the RoA-recovery journey

The honest setback — and the single number the whole thesis turns on.

Here is the part of the story a fair primer must not soften. Just as IDFC First's retail machine was scaling and its RoA was climbing toward ~1.2–1.5%, an industry-wide microfinance (MFI) crisis in FY25–26 — over-lending, borrower over-leverage, and rural stress across the whole sector — hit its small MFI book hard. Credit costs spiked, and because loan losses come straight off the bottom line, FY25 profit fell ~48% and RoA collapsed to around ~0.4%. The bull case is that this is a cyclical dent in one sliver, not a flaw in the model — and that RoA now normalises back toward ~1.5%+ as MFI shrinks and the rest of the book compounds.

What actually happened — and why it's (probably) a dent, not a break

  • An industry-wide event, not an IDFC First mistake. The FY25–26 microfinance stress hit the entire sector — the result of years of aggressive lending, borrowers holding loans from too many lenders at once, and rural income stress. IDFC First's MFI book soured along with everyone else's; management has been explicit that "every business is performing well except microfinance."
  • It was always a small sliver. Microfinance peaked at ~6.6% of the loan book and has been deliberately cut to ~2.7% of funded assets, with a stated aim to hold it around 3–3.5%. Even at its worst it was a minority of the book — the stress was concentrated, not systemic to the bank.
  • Provisioning was front-loaded, then fell. FY26 credit cost was ~2.13%, but the trajectory through the year tells the recovery story: provisions as a % of loans fell from 2.69% in Q1 to 1.63% by Q4. The bank recognised the pain, then watched it recede.
  • Asset quality healed. GNPA/NNPA improved to 1.61%/0.48% by March 2026, and — crucially — excluding microfinance, the rest of the book's asset quality remained solid throughout. The clean core was never in doubt.
Why one ratio — RoA — is the whole debate

Return on assets is how much profit a bank squeezes from each rupee on its balance sheet, and it drives everything the stock is valued on. IDFC First's math is stark: a bank with a ~6% NIM and a ~50% CASA ratio should earn a ~1.5–1.6% RoA once credit costs normalise — that's roughly what management guides the lending business toward. But FY26 actual RoA was only ~0.4%, because microfinance losses (and a one-off branch-fraud hit in Q4) swallowed the difference. So the entire investment question reduces to: is the ~0.4% the "real" IDFC First, or a temporary MFI-dented trough on the way back to ~1.5%? Management, the shrinking MFI book, the healing NPAs and the falling quarterly credit cost all argue "trough". If they're right, profit could roughly triple as RoA normalises — with no change to the margin at all. That asymmetry — great top line, temporarily crushed bottom line — is exactly why the stock is interesting and contentious.

The number to watch every quarter: credit cost and RoA, together. Falling credit cost + rising RoA = the recovery is real and profit is on its way back to matching the margin. Sticky-high credit cost or fresh stress in a new segment (unsecured personal loans, cards) = the market was right to worry that high yield always drags high losses. Everything else — deposits, NIM, growth — is already working; this is the pivot the whole story turns on.
07

Warburg, ADIA and the strengthened balance sheet

A ~₹7,500 crore vote of confidence — and the capital to fund the recovery.

A high-growth, capital-hungry retail bank needs a steady supply of fresh equity — and the microfinance hit, by denting internal profit generation, made that more pressing. In April 2025, IDFC First secured a landmark answer: two of the world's most respected investors, private-equity giant Warburg Pincus and sovereign fund ADIA (Abu Dhabi Investment Authority), committed a combined ~₹7,500 crore for close to a 15% stake — a major capital infusion and, just as importantly, a powerful institutional endorsement of the transformation.

Why this matters on three levels

  • Capital to grow. Every rupee a bank lends must be backed by regulatory capital. The infusion lifts capital adequacy (CRAR) from ~16% toward ~19–20%, giving IDFC First years of runway to keep growing the loan book without needing to return to the market — vital for a bank compounding advances at ~20%.
  • An endorsement that de-risks the story. Warburg Pincus and ADIA are among the most sophisticated financial investors on earth; they did deep diligence and chose to buy ~15% after the microfinance stress was visible. That's a strong external signal that serious money views the RoA recovery as real, not a hope.
  • Long-term, aligned owners. A patient PE house and a sovereign wealth fund are stable, long-horizon shareholders — the kind that back a multi-year transformation rather than trade the next quarter. They strengthen the register and the governance around the bank.
Why a capital raise is fuel, not a warning sign

When a bank raises equity, it can look like weakness — "why does it need money?" For a growing bank, the honest answer is the opposite: growth consumes capital, because regulators require every loan to be backed by a cushion of shareholder equity. A bank compounding its loan book at ~20% a year will exhaust its capital buffer unless it periodically tops up. IDFC First's raise is best read as filling the tank for the next leg of the journey — and the fact that Warburg and ADIA, not a fire-sale to whoever would take it, provided the fuel is the reassuring part. It also cushions the balance sheet precisely when the MFI hit had slowed internal capital generation. The mild downside for existing holders is dilution — more shares outstanding — but that's the ordinary price of funding growth with quality partners.

"Ex-microfinance" — the two words that reframe the risk

The single most useful lens on IDFC First's asset quality is to mentally split the book in two: microfinance, and everything else. "Everything else" — mortgages, vehicles, consumer, business banking — has behaved well throughout, with contained overdue loans and steady collections. Microfinance was the one segment that blew up, and it was always a small minority of the book that the bank has now cut back hard. This is why management, investors like Warburg/ADIA, and the healing headline ratios all point the same way: the problem was concentrated and identified, not diffuse and hidden. A bank whose bad news is confined to one shrinking 2.7% sliver is in a very different place from one whose whole book is deteriorating.

09

The company and who controls it

A merged, professionally-run bank with no promoter and marquee institutional owners.

IDFC First Bank is, like HDFC Bank, a professionally-managed bank with no promoter — a feature reinforced by the 2024 reverse merger that removed the old IDFC holding company. The animating figure is CEO V. Vaidyanathan, the architect of the retail transformation, backed increasingly by heavyweight institutional shareholders.

Ownership & management

  • No promoter. Following the reverse merger, the bank has zero promoter holding — a widely-held, board-run institution. There is no controlling family or group. (A residual government stake of ~7.8%, a legacy of IDFC Ltd's government origins, sits in the register but is not a promoter holding.)
  • Marquee institutional owners. Foreign institutions hold ~37% and domestic institutions ~23% of the register. The ~₹7,500 Cr raise adds Warburg Pincus (~9.5%) and ADIA (~5.1%) as large, long-horizon holders — a strong endorsement and a stabilising influence.
  • Management & the key-man point: V. Vaidyanathan (MD & CEO) built ICICI's retail bank, founded Capital First, and has driven IDFC First's entire transformation. His vision and credibility are a genuine asset — and a genuine key-man risk: much of the story is tied to his leadership and strategy.
  • A distinct DNA: the bank's culture is unusually consumer-brand-led for an Indian bank — customer-friendly positioning, a strong app, aggressive early deposit-gathering — the Capital First / Vaidyanathan imprint rather than a legacy-bank mindset.
Why "no promoter" plus a strong CEO is a particular profile

Most Indian banks fall into two camps: promoter-driven (a family or group at the helm) or PSU (government-controlled). IDFC First is neither — it's professionally run and widely held, like HDFC Bank. But unlike a faceless professional bank, it has an unusually visible, founder-like CEO in Vaidyanathan, whose personal strategy is the company's strategy. That's a double-edged profile: you get the clean governance of a no-promoter bank and the drive of a founder-operator — but you also carry the risk that the story leans heavily on one person. The arrival of Warburg and ADIA on the register adds institutional ballast that partly offsets that concentration.

The industry and the rivals

Where a high-margin, transforming retail bank sits in the Indian banking pecking order.

Indian banking is large, growing and structurally favourable to well-run private banks that keep taking share from state-owned lenders. IDFC First occupies a distinctive niche: a full-service private bank with a small-finance-bank-like margin (~6%) thanks to its retail tilt, but with a universal banking licence, a large deposit franchise, and a transformation-plus-recovery narrative that sets it apart from every peer.

The right mental slot

Don't file IDFC First next to the sleepy old private banks (it earns a far higher margin and is mid-transformation), nor next to the giants (it's smaller and its RoA is still depressed). And it's not an SFB like AU — it already holds a full universal banking licence. The accurate slot is: "a full-service private bank that lends like a small-finance bank (fat retail margin) and funds like a big private bank (deep CASA), rebuilt over six years from a failing infra lender — now working through a microfinance dent on the road to matching big-bank returns." The peer with the most similar shape (high NIM + MFI stress + granular retail) is AU; the peer it's chasing on returns is HDFC/ICICI.

The regulatory backdrop: banks operate under the RBI's tight prudential regime — Basel III capital rules, cash-reserve (CRR) and statutory-liquidity (SLR) requirements, priority-sector quotas, and evolving rules on unsecured retail and microfinance lending. The RBI's tightening on microfinance and unsecured credit directly shaped the FY25–26 stress, and its stance on retail lending will keep shaping IDFC First's fastest-growing segments. A well-capitalised, deposit-rich, no-promoter bank generally enjoys regulatory goodwill — an intangible asset for a bank still proving out its model.

Financials & valuation

A superb top line, a temporarily crushed bottom line — priced on the recovery.

IDFC First's financials read like a bank with excellent revenue quality and, for now, poor profitability — the gap between them is the microfinance dent. NII and NIM are strong and growing; deposits and loans compound ~17–20%; but RoA (~0.4%) and RoE (~4%) sit far below what the margin implies, held down by MFI credit costs and a one-off Q4 fraud hit. How to think about the valuation

Because IDFC First's earnings are at a trough, a P/E multiple is misleading (it looks sky-high at ~40x precisely because profit is temporarily crushed). The honest lens for any bank is price-to-book against normalised RoE. IDFC First trades around a ~₹69,400 Cr market cap, roughly ~1.5× book value (book value ~₹55/share) — a modest multiple. The bull argues that's cheap for a bank with a ~6% NIM, a ~50% CASA franchise and a credible path to a ~1.5%+ RoA and a low-to-mid-teens RoE: as returns normalise, both the earnings and the multiple can re-rate — a powerful double. The bear argues the ~1.5× book already prices in a recovery that isn't guaranteed, that MFI (or the next high-yield segment) could re-stress, and that a bank still proving its through-cycle returns doesn't yet deserve a big-private-bank multiple. The entire valuation debate is a single bet: does RoA climb back to ~1.5% — and how soon?

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

An excellent franchise with a temporarily broken bottom line — the debate is the recovery, not the model.

IDFC First Bank has built the two hardest things in banking — a high-margin retail lending engine and a large, low-cost deposit franchise — from the wreckage of a failing infra lender, in barely six years. What it hasn't yet demonstrated through a full cycle is that this machine can deliver a big-bank RoA consistently. The microfinance stress was the first real test, and it hurt. The investment debate is almost entirely about whether RoA normalises back toward ~1.5%+ from here — and whether the market re-rates the stock as it does.

The bull case

  • A ~6% NIM franchise — a genuinely high-margin retail book, structurally superior to a corporate-heavy bank's top line and largely intact through the stress.
  • A deposit franchise built from scratch — ~50% CASA, ~32% deposit CAGR since 2018 — cheap, sticky funding that underpins the whole margin.
  • The MFI drag is ending — MFI cut to ~2.7% of assets, credit cost falling quarter-by-quarter, NPAs healed; management guides the lending business toward ~1.5–1.6% RoA.
  • Marquee capital & endorsement — ~₹7,500 Cr from Warburg Pincus + ADIA lifts CRAR toward ~19–20% and signals sophisticated conviction in the recovery.
  • Trough earnings, modest multiple — ~1.5× book on depressed returns; if RoA/RoE normalise, both earnings and the multiple can re-rate — a compounding double.

The bear case

  • Returns still unproven through-cycle — RoA ~0.4% and RoE ~4% today; the ~1.5% target is a guide, not a delivered fact, and the model hasn't yet weathered a full cycle intact.
  • Reach-for-yield risk is structural — the same high-NIM instinct that built the book pushed it into microfinance; the next stress could come from unsecured personal loans or cards.
  • Key-man dependence — much of the story rests on V. Vaidyanathan's vision and execution; leadership risk is real.
  • Dilution & capital hunger — a ~20%-growth retail bank keeps consuming capital; the raise dilutes existing holders, and more may be needed over time.
  • The multiple may already price the recovery — at ~1.5× book, the market may have paid ahead for a normalisation that could disappoint or slip.
The bottom line

IDFC First is a rare thing: a bank whose revenue quality (a ~6% NIM, a ~50% CASA franchise) is excellent, but whose profitability is temporarily broken by a concentrated, now-shrinking microfinance problem. The business the transformation built is genuinely strong; the question is entirely one of normalisation realised — can credit costs keep falling and RoA climb back toward the ~1.5%+ the margin implies, and will the market re-rate the stock as it happens. Watch three things each quarter: credit cost and RoA (are they normalising?), the MFI and unsecured books (is any new stress building?), and CASA/deposit growth (is the funding advantage holding?). Get those right and a cheap bank with a superb franchise compounds and re-rates as profit finally catches up to the margin; get them wrong and it stays the high-potential, high-NIM bank whose bottom line never quite matches its promise. The margin says the machine works; the credit cost decides whether investors ever get paid for it.
