00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

ACC Limited makes cement — the grey powder that, mixed with sand, stone and water, sets into the concrete of every home, road, dam and flyover in India. Its red-and-white bags have been a fixture on Indian building sites since before Independence: the company was founded in 1936 as The Associated Cement Companies, itself the product of merging ten existing cement firms into one. For most of its life it was run by the Tata group and later the Swiss cement giant Holcim. Since 2022 it has belonged to the Adani group, which bought Holcim's India business and, overnight, became India's #2 cement player behind UltraTech.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • A heavy commodity that can't travel. Cement is cheap per tonne and expensive to move, so it is sold within a few hundred kilometres of the plant. That makes it a regional business measured in a handful of numbers — installed capacity (MTPA), how much of it is used, volumes sold, the price per tonne (realisation), and the metric everyone watches, EBITDA per tonne. ACC sold a record 43.9 million tonnes in FY26 off ~40 MTPA of capacity.
  • Now an Adani cost-and-growth engine. Inside the Adani/Ambuja fold, this old professionally-run blue chip is being pushed hard on cost — cheaper green power (solar and waste-heat recovery), a better fuel basket, shared procurement and logistics — as part of a group racing toward a combined 140+ MTPA. FY26 volume grew fast, but a spike in fuel costs squeezed margins: Q4 EBITDA/tonne fell to ~₹471 and quarterly profit dropped ~68%.
  • The company is being merged out of existence. In December 2025, Ambuja's board approved absorbing ACC (and Orient Cement) into itself to create one unified "One Cement" entity. If it clears the courts, ACC will be delisted — shareholders receiving 3.28 Ambuja shares for every ACC share. The nine-decade-old ticker 500410 is, in effect, living on borrowed time.
In plain terms

ACC is a grand old brand that no longer steers its own ship. The plants, mines and the famous name are all still there — 40-odd million tonnes of cement-making muscle spread across India — but the strategy, the capital and the cost targets now come from Adani, and ACC is being run as one cog in a much larger machine. For a shareholder there are really two different questions stacked on top of each other. The first is the ordinary cement question: can the group drive costs down and prices up enough to lift profit per tonne back toward its old highs? The second is unusual and time-limited: since ACC is set to disappear into Ambuja at a fixed 3.28-for-1 swap, owning ACC is increasingly a bet on Ambuja's shares wearing an ACC label. The curiosity that makes the stock worth a look at all: strip out its enormous cash pile and the market is valuing ACC's entire plant network at a startlingly small number.

01

What cement really is: the economics of a bag of grey powder

No chemistry needed — just the six numbers that run the whole industry.

Financially, a cement company is a factory that turns rock into a near-identical commodity and sells it by the tonne. The plant is fixed, the costs are mostly fixed, and the profit comes from volume multiplied by whatever thin margin the regional price allows. Because one maker's cement is chemically much like another's, ACC can't charge a big premium the way a soft-drink or a soap brand can — it is largely a price-taker. Six numbers tell you nearly the whole story.

The profit engine, in one line

Boil it down and: operating profit ≈ tonnes sold × (price per tonne − cost per tonne) = tonnes × EBITDA/tonne. A cement maker therefore has only three levers — sell more tonnes (needs both capacity and demand), fetch a higher price (mostly outside its control), and strip cost out of each tonne (cheaper fuel, greener power, shorter hauls). Because so much of the cost base is fixed, the maths is unforgiving in both directions: a small rise in price drops almost entirely into profit, and a small cost spike — like the fuel jump ACC hit in early 2026 — can gut EBITDA/tonne even while volumes are setting records. That asymmetry is the whole reason cement is a famously cyclical business, and why the per-tonne number matters more than the top line.

02

From limestone to a bag on a truck

Five steps that explain why location, clinker and blending decide who wins.

Cement is made in two physically separate stages, and the split governs everything about where plants sit and how the money works. First comes the heavy, mine-bound "clinker" stage — where limestone is baked in a giant kiln. Then a lighter "grinding" stage — where that clinker is milled with additives into finished cement, and which can be placed closer to the customer. Understand this two-step and you understand the geography of the whole industry.

Step 5 · Dispatch

The trick every big player uses: build the expensive, mine-tied kiln once, then rail the clinker out to cheaper grinding units dotted near cities — so the finished cement travels the shortest possible distance. Padding each tonne of clinker with waste materials (fly ash, slag) stretches it further, cutting both cost and carbon.

Three words that decide a cement company's fate

  • Clinker is the baked, rock-hard core of cement — and the bottleneck. Making it is where nearly all the fuel is burned and nearly all the CO₂ is released, and it needs a scarce limestone mining lease. So a company's clinker capacity, not just its cement capacity, is the real ceiling on how much it can produce.
  • Blending is how you win on cost and carbon at the same time. Every tonne of clinker you swap out for industrial leftovers — fly ash from power stations, slag from steel plants — is a tonne you never had to bake. A high blended-cement mix is cheaper and greener; ACC leans on it, and on premium branded grades, to lift the value of each bag.
  • Freight is the tax the whole industry pays for gravity. Since a tonne of cement sells for only a few thousand rupees, trucking it a few hundred kilometres eats a chunk of its value. That single fact is why cement is regional — you build where the limestone and the buyers both are, and you never ship far.
Why cement is a patchwork, not a national market

Put the mine problem and the freight problem together and you reach the defining truth of the industry: India is not one cement market but five regional ones — North, South, East, West and Central — each with its own plants, its own demand and its own prices. A plant in Karnataka can't plug a shortage in Punjab; the freight would erase the profit. So a producer's strength is judged region by region — how much capacity it holds where, how tightly its plants cluster, how short its average haul is. ACC's answer is unusually broad: unlike most rivals it has a genuinely pan-India plant network, which is exactly why the Adani group prized it.

03

Born from a merger in 1936 — and the owners who followed

A company older than the Republic, passed down a remarkable chain of custody.

ACC has one of the longest pedigrees in Indian industry, and it began the same way it now looks likely to end — with an amalgamation. In 1936, ten separate cement companies combined to form The Associated Cement Companies Limited (ACC), a landmark consolidation shepherded in part by the house of Tata. For decades it was the country's bellwether cement maker, closely associated with the Tata group. In the 2000s the Tatas sold out and the Swiss building-materials giant Holcim took control. Then, in 2022, ownership changed hands once more — this time to the Adani group. The through-line across ninety years: ACC has always been someone's flagship, rarely its own master.

Chain of custody

  • 1936
    The Associated Cement Companies (ACC) is formed by merging ten existing cement firms into one — a founding act of consolidation, with the Tatas closely involved.
  • 1930s–90s
    Grows into India's most recognised cement brand and an industry pace-setter, long managed under the Tata umbrella; pioneers plants, R&D and the iconic ACC bag.
  • 2000s
    The Tatas exit; Swiss major Holcim builds a controlling stake, running ACC alongside its other Indian arm, Ambuja Cements.
  • 2022
    Adani buys Holcim's India business — Ambuja + ACC — for ~US$6.4bn (≈US$10.5bn incl. open offers). Adani instantly becomes India's #2 cement group. ACC becomes a subsidiary of Ambuja, which itself is Adani-controlled.
  • FY23–FY25
    Adani installs its cost-and-growth playbook — green power, fuel-basket optimisation, shared procurement, logistics — and the wider group binges on acquisitions (Sanghi, Penna, Orient) to add capacity fast.
  • Dec 2025
    Ambuja's board approves the full amalgamation of ACC (and Orient Cement) into Ambuja — a "One Cement" entity. If sanctioned, ACC will be delisted and its shareholders swapped into Ambuja at 3.28:1.
  • FY26
    Record volume (43.9 Mt) and revenue (₹25,962 Cr), but a sharp fuel-cost spike compresses margins — a reminder of the commodity's cyclicality even mid-transformation.

Who controls it now

  • Promoter — the Adani group, via Ambuja. Promoter holding is ~56.7%, of which 50.05% sits with Ambuja Cements (which is itself Adani-controlled) and the balance directly with Adani entities. In practice, ACC is a grandchild of the Adani group.
  • Public float. The rest is held by mutual funds (~14%), foreign investors (~5%) and retail/other shareholders — the minority whose interests the pending merger swap is designed to settle.
  • The name is a survivor. "ACC" has outlived the Tatas and Holcim and remains one of India's most trusted cement brands — a genuine consumer asset the Adani group is careful to keep on the bag, even as the legal entity behind it is set to vanish.
  • Not a family founder story. Unlike most Indian majors (Birla's UltraTech, the Dalmia family, the Bangurs of Shree), ACC has no founding dynasty at the helm — it has been a professionally-managed, serially-owned institution for its entire life.
04

The Adani takeover: how ACC changed hands

The 2022 deal that reshaped Indian cement — and set everything since in motion.

For most of the last two decades, ACC and Ambuja were the Indian arms of Holcim, the world's largest cement company. When Holcim decided to pull out of India, it triggered the country's largest-ever acquisition in infrastructure and materials. In 2022 the Adani group bought Holcim's entire India business — its stakes in both Ambuja and ACC — and in a single move went from having no cement at all to being India's second-largest producer, trailing only Aditya Birla's UltraTech.

Why cement, and why buy rather than build

  • Cement is an infrastructure adjacency. The Adani group's centre of gravity is ports, power, roads and logistics — all cement-hungry. Owning the cement too let it capture more of the value chain and lean on its own coal, power and logistics muscle to cut costs.
  • Buying skips the queue. A greenfield cement plant needs a scarce limestone mining lease and years of building. Acquiring Ambuja + ACC handed Adani a ready-made, pan-India, ~70 MTPA network (at the time) in one stroke — a decade of build compressed into a single cheque.
  • Two listed vehicles, one prize. The structure Adani inherited was awkward — ACC sitting under Ambuja, both listed, with overlapping plants and inter-company supply agreements. Tidying that structure is precisely what the 2025 merger sets out to do (section 06).
The surprise most people miss: ACC didn't just get a new owner in 2022 — it acquired a whole new strategy. A conservative, cash-rich, Holcim-run blue chip that had spent years under-earning its potential was suddenly plugged into one of India's most aggressive conglomerates, with orders to cut cost per tonne every year and expand at pace. The very things that made ACC sleepy under Holcim — a big cash balance, an under-pushed plant network, a premium brand — became the raw material for an Adani turnaround.
05

Inside the Adani machine: the cost-and-synergy playbook

How a 90-year-old cement maker is being re-engineered tonne by tonne.

In a commodity where you can't control the selling price, the only reliable way to make more money is to make each tonne cheaper. That is the heart of the Adani approach at ACC, and the numbers show it working even as the reported profit wobbles on fuel prices. The group has set explicit, public cost targets and is attacking the two biggest variable costs — power and fuel, and freight — on every front at once.

The four levers Adani is pulling

  • Cheaper, cleaner power. Green-power share leapt from ~14% to 26.2% in FY26 (waste-heat recovery + solar), and ACC also taps its parent's renewable power under a supply agreement — together helping cut its power cost roughly 9%. The target is ~60% green power ahead of FY28.
  • A smarter fuel basket. By blending in low-cost imported petcoke, captive coal and group-linked fuel, ACC cut its kiln fuel cost ~10% (from ₹1.73 to ₹1.56 per '000 kCal). Fuel is the single biggest swing in a cement plant's cost — this is where the money is won or lost.
  • Logistics and freight. Riding the Adani group's ports, rail links and logistics scale to shorten hauls and cut the ~20% of cost that freight represents — a direct benefit of belonging to an infrastructure conglomerate rather than a standalone cement firm.
  • Premium and trade mix. Pushing higher-value branded and green cements — approved for highways, metros and major structures — to lift realisation per bag rather than chase raw tonnage alone.

Why the cost target matters more than the price

Here is the logic in one thought. ACC can't set the price of cement — that's decided region by region by supply and demand. But it can decide what it costs to make a tonne. So while realisation drifts up and down with the cycle, the cost programme is a steady, self-controlled tailwind: pull ₹350–400 out of the cost of a tonne and, at flat prices, that entire amount lands in EBITDA/tonne. The catch on show in FY26 was the reminder that this is a race, not a victory lap — a sudden fuel-price spike pushed costs up faster than the savings could offset, which is exactly why Q4 EBITDA/tonne sagged to ₹471 even as green power hit records. The programme is real; so is the volatility it's fighting against.

06

The merger endgame: ACC folds into Ambuja

The single most important thing to understand about ACC as an investment today.

Everything above happens in the shadow of one decision. On 22 December 2025, the board of Ambuja Cements approved a scheme to absorb ACC and Orient Cement into itself, collapsing the group's three listed cement companies into a single "One Cement" platform. For an ACC shareholder this is the whole game: if the scheme is sanctioned, ACC ceases to exist as a listed company, its shares cancelled and replaced with Ambuja shares at a fixed ratio.

What the merger actually fixes

  • It untangles the structure. Today ACC sits beneath Ambuja, both listed, with overlapping plants and a web of inter-company Master Supply Agreements (for shared power, fuel, procurement). One entity means one management, one supply chain, one brand strategy — and no more related-party plumbing between the two.
  • It unlocks synergy. The company estimates the consolidation is worth roughly ₹100 per tonne in extra savings — better capacity use, streamlined logistics, centralised buying — stacked on top of the standalone cost programme.
  • It pools the firepower. A single ~140 MTPA-and-growing balance sheet, cash included, is a cleaner vehicle for the group's race against UltraTech than three separate ones fighting over the same procurement and the same distressed-asset deals.
  • It cleans up governance optics — while raising new questions. Killing the MSAs removes a recurring related-party irritant. But minority shareholders' entire return now hinges on whether the 3.28:1 swap is fair — a valuation call made by the controlling shareholder on both sides of the table.
The governance shadow you can't ignore: this group has a history of moving cash and restructuring ownership in ways minority investors have contested — when Holcim took ~₹3,500 Cr of cash out of the Ambuja/ACC structure years ago, institutions objected loudly. ACC still carries a very large cash balance, and a merger sets the terms of who ends up owning it. The swap ratio is fixed and independently opined on, but for a minority holder the honest reality is that the exchange terms — not ACC's own results — now dominate the outcome.
07

The footprint and the product mix

Why a genuinely pan-India network was the crown jewel Adani wanted.

Because cement is regional, where ACC's plants sit is half the story. And here ACC is unusual: where many rivals are lopsided toward one or two regions, ACC has plants and grinding units spread across all of India — North, South, East, West and Central — the fruit of ninety years of building. It runs over twenty cement plants plus ready-mixed concrete operations, giving it a national reach few competitors match. That breadth is precisely why it fit the Adani ambition of a truly pan-India platform.

ACC's real edge is balance — a national footprint that de-risks it from any single region's price war, and that dovetails with sister-company Ambuja's own map to give the combined group dense, overlapping coverage.

What ACC actually sells

  • Grey cement — the core. The bulk of revenue: OPC, blended PPC and composite grades sold to builders, contractors and individual home-builders. The famous ACC bag is one of India's most recognised building-material brands.
  • Premium and green cements. Higher-strength, branded and low-carbon variants approved for demanding uses — highways, metros, major structures — carrying better realisation than plain grey cement.
  • Ready-mixed concrete (RMX). ACC is also a large player in ready-mix — concrete delivered wet to urban sites — which lifts blended per-tonne revenue and deepens the customer relationship in cities.
  • Bought-in tonnes. Running near full utilisation in FY26, ACC topped up its own output with traded cement and clinker to meet demand — a sign of how tight its capacity ran against a record sales year.
The quiet advantage in a national footprint

A regionally-concentrated cement maker lives and dies by one or two local markets — brilliant when that region is tight, brutal when a price war erupts there. ACC's national spread smooths that out: weakness in one region can be offset by firmness in another, and its trucks and rail rakes can, at the margin, chase the better-priced markets. For the Adani group, bolting ACC's map onto Ambuja's created something more valuable than either alone — overlapping, dense, coast-to-coast coverage that shortens hauls and hardens pricing power. Scale in cement isn't just about total tonnes; it's about having the right tonnes in the right places, and ACC brought exactly that.

08

The duopoly race: UltraTech versus Adani

The industry story ACC now sits inside — a two-horse contest for the top of Indian cement.

India is the world's second-largest cement market, and in barely three years its power structure has crystallised into a two-giant race. On one side, UltraTech (Aditya Birla), the long-standing leader nearing ~190 MTPA. On the other, Adani, which used the 2022 Holcim deal plus a rapid string of acquisitions to build the group ACC belongs to — Ambuja + ACC + Orient + Sanghi + Penna — into a clear #2 heading toward ~140–155 MTPA. Everyone else, from Shree to Dalmia, is now competing for the space the two giants leave behind.

The paradox at the heart of the race

Consolidation is supposed to be good news for the survivors: fewer, larger players ought to hold prices steadier and earn better margins over a full cycle. That is the theory the whole industry is racing to realise. The trouble is the journey there. As UltraTech and Adani both pour capacity into the ground and jostle for regional share, they can trigger the very price wars that consolidation is meant to end — India may add ~85–90 MTPA in a couple of years, faster than demand grows. So ACC and its parent are running a two-front campaign: expand fast enough to stay in the duopoly, while trying not to flood their own regions and crush the prices that make expansion worthwhile. Belonging to a giant is the protection; the giants' own building spree is the risk.

09

Demand, pricing and the cement cycle

The demand tailwind is real and long — the near-term ride is bumpy.

A cement plant only earns if there is building to soak up its tonnes. India's demand story is structurally excellent and cyclically choppy — a strong long-run current with sharp short-run waves. The direction is set by infrastructure, housing and government capex, and points up for years; the timing of any given year is set by regional pricing and input costs, which swing hard. FY26 was the perfect illustration: record volumes met a fuel-cost spike, and profitability fell even as tonnage rose.

Volume hit a record in FY26 — yet profit per tonne fell, and fell hardest in Q4. Almost all of that swing is cost and price, not tonnage. This is the essence of a cyclical, price-taking commodity: a great volume year and a poor profit year can be the same year.

The tailwinds under the cycle

  • Infrastructure & government capex. Roads, railways, ports, metros, irrigation — cement-intensive and policy-driven. Public spending is the single biggest demand lever, and it plays directly to the Adani group's home turf.
  • Housing. Rural self-built homes and welfare housing, plus urban affordable housing and redevelopment — the largest end-use of Indian cement by far.
  • Low per-capita use. India consumes far less cement per person than China or the developed world — a long runway as the country urbanises and builds out.
  • A lighter tax on the bag. The GST on cement was cut from 28% to 18%, lowering the shelf price to the end-buyer and supporting demand — a structural nudge for volumes.
The input-cost watch: even in a strong demand year, profit hangs on costs. Power and fuel (petcoke/coal) are the biggest volatile line, and freight another fifth — which is exactly why ACC's fuel-basket and green-power work matters, and why a global petcoke spike could still overwhelm those savings in any given quarter, as FY26 showed. Watch the gap between the cost-per-tonne target (falling toward ~₹3,600 by FY28) and the actual realisation the cycle delivers.
10

Financials & valuation

A record top line, a fortress balance sheet — and a valuation warped by two things: a cash mountain and a fixed merger swap.

FY26 gave ACC its highest-ever revenue (₹25,962 Cr) and volume (43.9 Mt), but a fuel-cost spike held operating profit roughly flat and knocked reported earnings — Q4 net profit fell about 68%. What makes ACC genuinely unusual, though, isn't the income statement. It's the balance sheet: the company is essentially debt-free and sits on a cash-and-investments pile of nearly ₹14,000 crore — well over half its entire market value.

The number that changes how you value ACC

Run the cement-specific lens and something jumps out. ACC's market cap is ~₹25,900 Cr, but it holds ~₹13.5k Cr of net cash — so the enterprise value of the actual business is only ~₹12,400 Cr. Against ~40 MTPA of capacity, that is roughly ₹3,050 per tonne (~$36/tonne) — when it costs three to four times as much to pour a single new plant, and rivals routinely change hands at $90–130/tonne. On EV/EBITDA the business trades near ~4×. On paper, the market is handing you 90 years of limestone leases, kilns and a pan-India network for a fraction of replacement cost, with a fat cash cushion thrown in. That is the bull's whole argument — and it is real.

But you can't buy just the plants. The reason ACC looks so cheap is inseparable from the reason it's risky: the same controlling owner that runs the plants has already voted to fold the company into Ambuja at 3.28:1 and pool that cash onto a shared balance sheet. So the cheap EV/tonne isn't a free lunch waiting to be claimed by a minority holder — it's a value that will be realised through the merger swap, on terms the promoter sets on both sides. For an ACC shareholder, the metric that ultimately settles the case is less ACC's own EBITDA/tonne than whether 3.28 Ambuja shares end up worth more than one ACC share — the arbitrage the merger creates.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, cash-rich, iconic cement network — being run for a group, and about to lose its own name.

ACC is one of the more unusual situations in Indian materials: a ninety-year-old blue chip with a national plant network, a fortress balance sheet and a beloved brand, that no longer sets its own course and is set to be merged out of listed existence. The investment debate is really two debates — one about the cement business, one about the merger terms — and they don't resolve on the same clock.

The bull case

  • Startlingly cheap on the cement lens — ~₹3,050 EV/tonne and ~4× EV/EBITDA once the ~₹13.5k Cr cash is netted off, a fraction of replacement cost.
  • Fortress balance sheet — essentially debt-free, AAA-rated, with cash worth over half the market value.
  • Real cost momentum — green power doubled to ~26%, kiln-fuel cost −10%, a credible march toward ~₹3,600/tonne cost by FY28.
  • Pan-India network + Adani muscle — a balanced national footprint fed by group ports, power and logistics; record 43.9 Mt volume shows the demand pull.
  • Merger upside — the 3.28:1 swap into Ambuja plus ~₹100/tonne synergy could crystallise value the standalone stock never rated.

The bear case

  • You're really buying Ambuja — once merged, ACC's fate is a fixed swap into its parent; the "cheap" cash and plants get pooled on the group's terms.
  • Promoter on both sides — the swap ratio and cash pooling are set by the controlling owner; minority holders wear whatever the scheme decides.
  • Price-taker in a build-out — the giants' collective capacity binge can crush regional realisations and EBITDA/tonne, as FY26's Q4 (₹471) showed.
  • Fuel & freight volatility — a petcoke/coal spike can outrun the cost savings in any quarter, regardless of demand.
  • Governance history — this group has a contested record of moving cash and restructuring ownership around minority investors.
The bottom line

ACC is a rare thing — a genuinely cheap slice of a great franchise — wrapped inside a corporate action that decides how, and to whom, that cheapness gets paid out. For the business, the number to track is EBITDA per tonne: does the cost programme (green power, fuel basket, the ₹3,600/tonne goal, the ₹100/tonne merger synergy) drag it back up toward ₹1,000 as fuel normalises, or does the industry's own capacity flood keep it pinned near the FY26 Q4 trough? For the shareholder, the number that matters even more is the swap: whether 3.28 Ambuja shares prove worth more than the ACC share you hold. The whole situation converges on one dated event — the day the NCLT sanctions the amalgamation and the 500410 ticker goes dark, ending a listing that began when the ten founding companies came together in 1936. A company born from a merger is about to be settled by one; everything for an ACC investor rides on the arithmetic of that exchange.
