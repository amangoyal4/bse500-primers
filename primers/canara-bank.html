00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Canara Bank is one of India's largest public-sector banks — a 119-year-old, government-controlled lender born in Mangalore that takes deposits from savers nationwide and lends to households, farmers, small businesses and corporates. After the 2020 amalgamation that folded Syndicate Bank into it, Canara became India's fourth-largest public-sector bank, with a ~₹28 lakh crore total business (deposits + advances) and a deep South-Indian franchise now spread pan-India.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It's the one PSU bank with a genuine "value-unlock" story. In October 2025 Canara did what no other PSU bank has: it listed two of its subsidiaries — Canara Robeco (mutual funds) and Canara HSBC Life (insurance) — on the stock market. Overnight, stakes it had carried at a few hundred crore of book value acquired billions in visible market value, and FY26 profit even captured a ~₹1,930 Cr one-off gain from trimming those holdings. That gives Canara a sum-of-the-parts kicker the other PSU banks simply don't have.
  • A textbook turnaround with a live margin squeeze. A decade ago Canara was mired in the PSU bad-loan crisis; FY26 is a different bank — ₹19,187 Cr net profit (+12.7%), gross NPAs of just 1.84%, net NPAs of 0.43%, a ~94% provision coverage and a ~1.1% return on assets. But the net interest margin slipped to 2.54% in Q4 as rate cuts and a deposit war bit — so strong 16% loan growth barely lifted interest income.
  • It is ~63% owned by the Government of India. That is the double-edge the whole valuation turns on: sovereign backing makes its deposits ultra-safe and cheap, but state ownership, social-lending mandates and a history of NPAs keep the stock at a P/E of ~6× and around book value (~1.0× P/B) — a steep discount to private peers like HDFC and ICICI.
In plain terms

Think of Canara Bank as a ₹15.7-lakh-crore deposit machine wired to a ₹12.2-lakh-crore loan book, run by the government — plus a drawer of side-businesses most people forget it owns. It pays savers a little, lends that money out for more, and pockets the gap. For years the machine was clogged with bad loans; it has since been cleaned up, and Canara now earns a steady ~₹19,000 crore a year. What makes it unusual is that two of those side-businesses — a mutual-fund house and a life insurer — are now separately listed companies whose value you can actually see on a screen. The open questions: can it defend its thin margin, and does the market ever stop stamping "government-owned" as a 40%-off sticker on the shares?

01

Bank economics 101: how a bank actually earns

No finance degree required — just the numbers that drive everything.

A bank is, at its core, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors) and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap between what it earns on loans and pays on deposits — scaled to its assets — is the engine. A handful of terms tell you almost the whole story, and they matter doubly for a bank like Canara whose investment case is about a thin margin being defended and a bad-loan book that has been scrubbed clean.

The only banking units you need
TermWhat it means (and Canara Bank's number)
NIINet Interest Income — interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings. The core revenue line. Canara: ~₹37,210 Cr in FY26 (roughly flat, +0.4% — the margin pinch in one number).
NIMNet Interest Margin — NII as a % of interest-earning assets. The "spread", standardised. Canara: ~2.54% in Q4 FY26, down from ~2.7% a year earlier — the pressure point of the year.
CASACurrent + Savings Account deposits — money in everyday accounts that pays little or no interest. The cheaper your funding, the fatter your margin. Canara: ~29.8% — modest, and drifting down.
GNPA / NNPAGross / Net Non-Performing Assets — the % of loans that have gone bad. The single best gauge of lending discipline. Canara: 1.84% / 0.43% — a world away from the crisis years.
PCRProvision Coverage Ratio — how much of the bad loans the bank has already set money aside against. Canara: ~94.2%, meaning almost all recognised bad loans are pre-funded.
CAR / CET-1Capital Adequacy — the equity cushion versus loans, the buffer against losses. Canara: CRAR 17.04%, CET-1 12.44% — comfortably above the regulatory floor.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. So a bank gets richer four ways — lend more (grow advances), fund cheaper (more CASA, lower deposit cost), lose less (low NPAs and low credit cost), and earn fees (cards, insurance, forex, mutual-fund distribution). Canara's turnaround was almost entirely about the third lever: for years, loan losses were so enormous they swamped everything else. Fix the bad loans and the same interest spread suddenly drops through to profit — which is exactly what happened. The fourth lever is unusually alive at Canara, because it owns the insurer and the AMC whose products its branches sell.

Why "loan losses" are the swing factor for a PSU bank

  • Provisioning is the hidden lever. When a loan turns bad, the bank must set aside money to cover the expected loss — a provision, charged straight against profit. In the crisis years Canara was provisioning tens of thousands of crores; as the book cleaned up, that charge collapsed and profit surged. This is why credit cost (provisions as a % of loans) is the number that made the turnaround.
  • Slippages are the leading indicator. A "slippage" is a loan freshly turning bad. Canara's slippages are low and stable — the early-warning gauge that the book is healthy, not just historically cleaned. Watch this each quarter: it is the vital sign of a clean book staying clean.
  • The rate cycle sets the margin. Because most of Canara's loans reprice quickly when the RBI cuts rates, but deposits reprice more slowly, a rate-cut cycle squeezes NIM first — loan yields fall before funding costs do. That is precisely the pinch showing up in FY26.
02

How the money flows: deposits → loans → margin

Read this once and the whole bank clicks into place.

A bank is a conveyor belt for money. Savers' deposits come in one end; loans go out the other; the spread between the two is harvested as net interest income. Two things govern how much profit drips out: how cheap the deposits are (the CASA mix) and how much more the loans yield than the deposits cost (the margin). For Canara right now, the belt is running fast — loans are growing 16% — but the spread being harvested is getting thinner.

When the RBI cuts rates, Stage-3 loan yields fall almost immediately while Stage-1/2 deposit costs lag — so the spread narrows first. Rebuilding CASA and holding down deposit costs is the only way to defend the margin.

Why the CASA mix is the whole game

A current account pays 0% and a savings account ~3%; a fixed deposit pays 6.5–7.5%. The more of your funding that sits in low-cost CASA, the cheaper your money — and the wider your margin on the same loan. Canara's CASA of ~29.8% is on the lower side for a large bank (private leaders run 40%+), and it has drifted down from ~31% a year earlier as savers chase higher fixed-deposit and mutual-fund returns. Every point of CASA lost has to be replaced with pricier term deposits, and that is a direct headwind to margin — a big reason Canara's NIM is thinner than a Bank of Baroda's.

Volume up, spread down — why both can be true at once

It sounds contradictory that a bank can grow loans 16% and see net interest income go nearly flat. Here's why: net interest income = volume × spread. In FY26 Canara pushed volume up hard (advances +16%), but the spread (NIM) shrank because loan yields fell with RBI rate cuts while deposit costs stayed sticky and CASA slipped. The two almost exactly cancelled, so NII grew just ~0.4%. That headline profit still rose ~13% is thanks to other levers — lower provisions, higher fee income, and the subsidiary-IPO gains — not the core spread. The whole near-term investment question is whether volume and those other levers can keep out-running the margin squeeze.

03

The company and who controls it

A 119-year-old Mangalore institution, majority-owned by the state.

Canara Bank was founded in 1906 in Mangalore by the philanthropist-lawyer Ammembal Subba Rao Pai, who started it as the "Canara Hindu Permanent Fund" to bring institutional banking to South India. It was nationalised in 1969 with India's other large banks. Today it is a professionally-run public-sector bank headquartered in Bengaluru, whose controlling shareholder is the Government of India (~62.9%), operating ~9,800+ domestic branches and a modest overseas network. Its deep roots in Karnataka and the South remain its franchise DNA.

Ownership & management

  • Government of India ~62.9%. The state is the promoter and controlling shareholder. This anchors the stock's identity as a "PSU bank" and drives both its funding advantage and its valuation discount.
  • Public & institutional float ~37%. Foreign investors hold ~14%, domestic institutions (LIC, mutual funds) ~11%, and retail the rest. The government's high stake means periodic dilution risk — an OFS or fresh equity raise — hangs over minority holders.
  • Board-driven, government-appointed leadership. Like all PSU banks, top management (MD & CEO, executive directors) is appointed via the government's selection process, with fixed tenures — a governance model that differs sharply from the founder- or professional-CEO continuity of private banks.
  • A financial-services group. Alongside the bank sit subsidiaries and JVs in asset management (Canara Robeco), life insurance (Canara HSBC Life), securities/broking (Canara Bank Securities), and factoring — the value-unlock story covered next in §04.
04

The subsidiary value-unlock — Canara's SOTP kicker

The single feature that sets Canara apart from every other PSU bank — it owns pieces of businesses it just floated on the market.

Here is what makes Canara Bank genuinely different from Bank of Baroda, PNB or Union Bank. Buried inside it are stakes in fast-growing, capital-light financial businesses — a mutual-fund house and a life insurer — and in October 2025 Canara did something no PSU bank had done: it took two of them public. That converts value that was hidden on the balance sheet at a few hundred crore of book cost into a market price you can see on a screen. Analysts call this a "sum-of-the-parts" (SOTP) re-rating: the bank is worth more than its lending business alone, because the pieces add up.

What "value-unlock" actually means

When a bank holds a stake in a subsidiary, it carries it on its books at (roughly) what it originally paid — its book value. But if that subsidiary is a thriving AMC or insurer, its true worth can be many multiples of book. As long as it stays private, the market largely ignores that gap. Listing the subsidiary crystallises the difference: suddenly there's a share price, a market capitalisation, and a clear number for what the parent's stake is worth. Nothing about the underlying business changed — but its value became visible and tradeable. That is the unlock.

Why this is the section that matters

Every PSU bank owns a few subsidiaries, but they usually sit as an afterthought — a footnote worth "optionality". Canara has turned that footnote into a headline. Because it has now listed two of them, an investor can do a real sum-of-the-parts sum: value the core lending bank on its earnings/book, then add the market value of Canara's stakes in the listed AMC and insurer on top. In a normal PSU bank you can't do that cleanly; here you can. That's a genuine differentiator — it gives Canara a re-rating lever the others lack, and it hands the government (still ~63% owner) a proven playbook for surfacing more value from what it owns. The caveat: the stakes, while valuable, are still small relative to a ₹1.1-lakh-crore bank, so don't over-weight them — treat the SOTP as a real kicker, not the whole thesis.

Watch the remaining stakes: Canara didn't sell out — it trimmed. It still holds meaningful minority positions in both listed entities (~38% of Canara Robeco, plus a reduced stake in Canara HSBC Life). Those stakes now rise and fall with two separately-quoted stocks, so a good chunk of Canara's "hidden" value is now market-marked and visible each day. Any further stake sales would crystallise more value — and more one-off gains.
05

The 2020 Syndicate Bank amalgamation

The merger that made Canara India's fourth-largest public-sector bank.

On 1 April 2020 — at the very start of the pandemic lockdown — the government folded Syndicate Bank, a ~95-year-old lender founded in Manipal, Karnataka, into Canara Bank. Because both were Karnataka-born South-Indian banks with heavily overlapping geographies, this was one of the most natural fits in the PSU-consolidation wave. Overnight, Canara jumped to ~₹15.2 lakh crore of total business and ~10,300 branches, becoming India's fourth-largest public-sector bank.

Why the government did it

  • PSU consolidation. India had too many small, sub-scale state banks. The 2019–20 mega-merger wave collapsed ten PSU banks into four strong anchors. Merging Syndicate into Canara — two culturally and geographically similar Karnataka banks — was meant to create a bigger, more competitive lender and strip out duplicated branches and costs.
  • Deepen the South-India stronghold. Both banks were rooted in coastal Karnataka with strong agri and retail franchises. The combination reinforced Canara's status as the pan-India bank with the deepest Southern deposit base — a genuine funding franchise.
  • Scale & reach. The combined entity gained a far larger branch network and a bigger, more diversified deposit franchise, with deposit and advance market shares of roughly 6–7%.
The merger's legacy in today's numbers

Unlike a private-bank merger, this was a government-engineered consolidation of two similar banks, which made the integration comparatively smooth — branches, systems and ~5 crore accounts were unified over the following couple of years. The lasting effect is scale plus a doubled-down Southern identity: today's Canara is a genuinely national bank whose home turf is Karnataka and the South, giving it a sticky, low-cost regional deposit base. When you look at Canara's post-FY20 figures, remember they step up sharply versus its pre-merger history — comparisons to earlier years can mislead, because you are really looking at two banks in one.

06

The turnaround and the re-rating story

How a lender goes from the bad-loan crisis to ₹19,000 Cr of profit — and why it's re-rating.

Rewind to the FY16–FY18 crisis. Along with every large PSU bank, Canara's gross NPAs spiked into double digits, provisions exploded, and profitability was gutted. It was part of the great Indian PSU bad-loan crisis — years of aggressive, poorly-underwritten corporate lending (infrastructure, steel, power) that turned sour all at once. Understanding how a bank climbs out of that hole is the key to the entire Canara investment case, because a bank's earnings are governed less by how much it lends than by how little of what it lent goes bad.

The mechanism: why cleaning bad loans creates profit

A bank's profit is roughly net interest income + fees − operating costs − loan-loss provisions − tax. In the crisis years, the provisions line was so vast — the bank had to set aside money against tens of thousands of crores of bad loans — that it wiped out the entire spread the bank earned. Why this drives the "re-rating"

A "re-rating" means the market pays a higher multiple for the same earnings because it trusts them more. For years, investors valued Canara cheaply because its profits were fragile — a bad credit cycle could vaporise them, as FY16–18 showed. Now that GNPA is under 2%, net NPA is a negligible 0.43%, provision coverage is ~94% and returns on assets are back to ~1.1%, those earnings look durable. That is exactly why Canara's price-to-book has climbed back up to ~1.0× (from deep-discount territory) and its ROE now runs ~18% — genuinely competitive numbers. The bull thesis: a clean, ~1%-ROA, ~18%-ROE bank plus a subsidiary SOTP kicker should re-rate further. The bear counter: PSU banks always look cheap for structural reasons (§09) that never fully go away.

Why this is the section that matters: almost every good number in Canara's FY26 results — ₹19,187 Cr profit, ~1.1% ROA, ~18% ROE — exists because credit cost fell from crisis-era levels. The franchise didn't suddenly get better at lending; it stopped losing money on old loans. Watch slippages and credit cost every quarter: they are the vital signs of whether the clean book stays clean.
07

Deposits, NIM and the margin squeeze

The live pressure point — and why Canara's margin is thinner than its peers'.

With the bad-loan problem solved, the swing factor for Canara's earnings has shifted to the margin. And Canara's margin is being squeezed from both sides and starts from a thinner base than most peers: its NIM slid to 2.54% in Q4 FY26 from ~2.7% a year earlier. The RBI's rate-cut cycle drags loan yields down quickly, while a deposit war — banks competing hard for a shrinking pool of household savings — keeps funding costs stubbornly high. With NII up just ~0.4% for the full year, the squeeze is the reason strong loan growth barely moved core revenue.

Why margins are hard to defend right now

  • Loans reprice faster than deposits. A large share of Canara's loans are linked to external benchmarks (the RBI repo rate). When the RBI cuts, those loan rates drop almost immediately — but the bank's existing fixed deposits keep paying their old, higher rates until they mature. So in a rate-cut cycle, income falls before costs do, and NIM compresses first.
  • The deposit war. Indian households are increasingly moving savings into mutual funds, equities and higher-yield products. That shrinks the pool of cheap bank deposits, so every bank must offer higher fixed-deposit rates to attract funds. Higher deposit costs eat directly into the spread.
  • A structurally low CASA. Canara's ~29.8% CASA is lower than several large peers, and it has slipped from ~31%. Because CASA is the cheapest funding, a low and falling CASA means Canara relies more on pricey term deposits — a permanent drag that keeps its NIM below banks with fatter savings-account franchises.

Why the deposit franchise is the ultimate moat

Anyone can lend money; the hard, valuable part of banking is gathering deposits cheaply and reliably. A bank with a sticky, low-cost deposit base can lend profitably through any cycle; one that has to "buy" deposits at high rates is always squeezed. Canara's advantage here is its sovereign backing and deep Southern branch network: as a government-majority bank rooted in Karnataka, its deposits are perceived as ultra-safe, so savers park money with it even at slightly lower rates. But its relatively low CASA means that edge is weaker than a top private bank's — which is precisely why its margin sits at the lower end of the PSU pack. Watch the cost of deposits and CASA ratio: management guides NIM to stabilise around 2.5–2.6%, so if CASA firms up while the rate-cut cycle ends, the margin can hold; if CASA keeps bleeding, it stays pinched.

08

The RAM shift and the Canara ai1 super-app

Tilting the loan book toward granular, higher-yield lending — distributed through a 3-million-user app.

The other half of Canara's post-crisis strategy is a deliberate shift in the loan mix toward the "RAM" book — Retail, Agriculture and MSME. The bad-loan crisis was a corporate-lending disaster — a handful of large infrastructure and industrial borrowers defaulted and blew a hole in the book. The lesson every PSU bank learned was the same: grow the granular, secured, diversified RAM book, where losses are small and spread across millions of borrowers, and be far more selective with big corporate loans. That mix shift is both safer and higher-yielding — retail and MSME loans typically earn more than razor-thin wholesale corporate loans. It plays perfectly to Canara's agri-and-retail heritage.

Within retail, home and vehicle loans dominate, with personal loans a smaller, more closely-watched slice.

Canara ai1 — the digital distribution layer

Canara ai1 is the bank's unified mobile-banking "super-app", built on a "One Bank, One App" idea. It bundles 250–300+ features — UPI, NEFT/RTGS/IMPS transfers, deposits, bill payments, insurance, loan servicing — into a single application with 3 million-plus users. It matters for three reasons:

  • Cheaper distribution. Onboarding customers, opening accounts and disbursing small retail loans through an app costs a fraction of doing it through a branch. For a bank with ~9,800 branches and a huge rural/agri base, digital is the way to grow the customer count without proportionally growing cost.
  • Deposit and cross-sell reach. A good app helps gather low-cost CASA deposits and cross-sell cards, insurance (via Canara HSBC Life) and mutual funds (via Canara Robeco) — feeding both the funding side (cheaper deposits) and the fee side (non-interest income). Owning the products it distributes is a quiet advantage here.
  • Data-driven, safer lending. Digital footprints and account data let the bank underwrite small retail and MSME loans faster and more accurately — supporting the granular-book strategy that keeps NPAs low.
Why the RAM shift is a quiet margin defence

Here's the elegant part: the same strategy that made Canara safer also makes it more profitable per rupee lent. Retail and MSME loans (home, auto, personal, small-business) typically yield more than large corporate loans, which are priced razor-thin because big companies can shop around. So by growing the RAM book ~20% and retail ~33% — well ahead of the total book — Canara is quietly lifting its average loan yield, which partly offsets the deposit-cost pressure squeezing its margin. The Canara ai1 app makes that shift cheaper to execute at scale, and its ownership of the insurer and AMC means every cross-sold policy or SIP earns the group a fee. The risk to watch is fast-growing unsecured personal lending, where a downturn can bite.

09

Government ownership and the PSU-discount debate

Why a clean, ~18%-ROE bank still trades at ~6× earnings — and whether that changes.

Here is the puzzle at the heart of the Canara stock: it earns ~1.1% on assets, ~18% on equity, has fortress ~94% provision coverage — yet the market prices it at a P/E of ~6× and ~1.0× book, while private peers HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank trade at ~18–22× earnings and 2–3× book. That gap — the "PSU discount" — is not an accident. It reflects real structural concerns about government-owned banks, and whether it narrows is a major driver of the stock's return. Canara does, however, have one extra lever the others lack: the subsidiary SOTP (§04).

Why PSU banks trade at a discount

  • Governance & non-commercial mandates. The government is both owner and policymaker. PSU banks can be asked to support social schemes, farm-loan waivers, priority-sector lending and national-priority projects — lending that may not maximise shareholder returns. Management is government-appointed on fixed tenures, so continuity and incentives differ from a founder-led private bank.
  • The NPA scar tissue. Institutional investors remember the FY16–FY18 crisis, when PSU banks' hidden bad loans destroyed years of profit. Even with clean books today, that credibility gap lingers in the multiple.
  • Dilution risk. With the government at ~63%, there is persistent overhang from potential equity raises or government share sales (OFS) that could dilute minority holders — often at low valuations.
  • Slower structural growth. Private banks have taken market share from PSU banks for two decades on service, technology and underwriting. The market extrapolates that trend.
The re-rating case vs the value-trap case

The bull ("closing the gap"): Canara's fundamentals now rival a private bank — ~1.1% ROA, ~18% ROE, sub-2% GNPA, strong capital — and it carries a subsidiary SOTP kicker (listed AMC + insurer stakes) that adds value on top of the core bank. If it sustains this, the market should re-rate it above book value, and the sum-of-the-parts gives that re-rating an extra leg the other PSU banks don't have.

The bear ("value trap"): PSU banks have looked "cheap" for a decade and the discount has never fully closed, because the structural reasons — government control, mandate risk, dilution, slower growth, thin margins — never go away. A clean book and a couple of listed subsidiaries are nice, but without a change in ownership structure or governance, the discount is largely permanent, and the low multiple is the market correctly pricing lower-quality, government-directed earnings.

How to hold both ideas: Canara can be a genuinely good, cheap bank and a stock whose discount only partly closes. The realistic middle case is a modest re-rating (holding around/above 1× book) plus a healthy ~3% dividend yield and the optional SOTP upside from further subsidiary monetisation — a decent return — rather than full convergence to private-bank multiples. The swing factor is credibility: every quarter of clean results and every stake sale chips at the discount; any asset-quality wobble or dilutive raise widens it again.

How to think about the valuation

Canara trades around a ~₹1.15 lakh crore market cap (~₹127/share) on FY26 earnings — roughly 6× earnings, ~1.0× book value, with a ~3.3% dividend yield. For a bank, the multiple that matters most is price-to-book against ROE: a bank earning ~18% ROE would, in a "normal" world, trade well above 1× book. Canara's ~1.0× valuation is the market applying a large "PSU discount" — pricing in government control, mandate/dilution risk and lingering NPA scepticism (§09) — even though the fundamentals now justify more. The Canara-specific twist is the SOTP: a proper valuation adds the market value of its stakes in listed Canara Robeco and Canara HSBC Life on top of the core bank, a kicker peers lack. The bull case is a re-rating above book plus subsidiary upside; the bear case is that the discount is structural and the low multiple correctly prices government-directed earnings. Unlike a private bank, the debate here is more about the multiple than the earnings.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A healed, cheap PSU bank with a rare value-unlock angle — the debate is margins and the discount.

Canara Bank has done the hard part: it has climbed out of a near-existential bad-loan crisis to a clean book, steady ~₹19,000 Cr profits and ~1.1% returns on assets, while pulling off a subsidiary value-unlock no other PSU bank has managed. The investment debate now is almost entirely about whether those returns are durable (can it defend a thin margin in a tough rate-and-deposit cycle?) and whether the market ever pays up for a government-owned bank.

The bull case

  • Subsidiary value-unlock — listed Canara Robeco + Canara HSBC Life stakes give a real SOTP kicker (and one-off gains) no PSU peer offers.
  • Clean, de-risked book — GNPA 1.84%, NNPA 0.43%, PCR ~94%; crisis-era risk provided-for and behind it.
  • Strong, granular loan growth — advances +16.3%, led by RAM +20% and retail +33%; higher-yielding and safer.
  • Peer-beating returns, cheap price — ~18% ROE, ~1.1% ROA at ~6× P/E and ~1.0× book, plus a ~3.3% dividend yield.
  • Sovereign-backed Southern deposit franchise — ultra-safe funding, deepened by the Syndicate merger.

The bear case

  • Thin, squeezed margin — NIM at 2.54% is low for a big bank; rate cuts and the deposit war kept NII flat (+0.4%) in FY26.
  • Low CASA — ~29.8% and drifting; a structural funding disadvantage versus higher-CASA peers.
  • The PSU discount may be permanent — government control, social mandates and dilution risk have capped the multiple for a decade.
  • One-off flattered FY26 — the ~₹1,930 Cr subsidiary gain won't recur; core growth was more modest; Q4 profit fell ~10% on bond losses.
  • Cyclical & governance risk — asset quality can turn in a downturn; government-appointed management and mandate risk cap the quality premium.
The bottom line

Canara Bank is a genuinely healed public-sector bank trading around book value — with a value-unlock story none of its PSU peers can tell. The business risk is far lower than a decade ago: the book is clean, capital is strong, returns are actually peer-beating, and it owns stakes in two now-listed financial businesses. The two live questions are: can it defend its thin margin through the rate-and-deposit cycle so that strong loan growth finally translates into core-profit growth; and will a durable clean record plus a visible SOTP finally narrow the PSU discount. The story over the next few years will be written in a handful of numbers each quarter — NIM and cost of deposits, CASA, slippages and credit cost — plus any further monetisation of its subsidiaries. Get those right and a cheap, clean bank with a hidden kicker can re-rate and pay you a healthy dividend while you wait; let margins or asset quality slip, and it stays exactly what the market fears — a permanent value trap.
