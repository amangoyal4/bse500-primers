00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Solar Industries makes things that explode. For its first 25 years that meant industrial explosives — the emulsions, bulk explosives and detonators that mining and infrastructure companies use to blast rock apart to get at coal, iron ore and limestone, or to carve out roads, tunnels and dams. Today it is the largest explosives company in India (~25–30% market share) and runs the world's largest single-site packaged-explosives plant, at Nagpur. But the reason its share price has gone vertical is a second business: defence — ammunition, propellants, warheads, Pinaka artillery rockets and Nagastra loitering-munition drones for the Indian armed forces and for export.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A high-margin, high-return industrial compounder. FY26 revenue was ₹9,838 Cr (+30%), EBITDA ₹2,750 Cr at a ~28% margin, and net profit ₹1,737 Cr (+35%). It earns a ~37% return on capital and a ~31% return on equity — rare for a "chemicals" business — because it is deeply integrated and dominant in its niche.
  • Defence is the re-rating engine. Defence revenue nearly doubled to ₹2,634 Cr (27% of sales) in FY26, and the order book is ₹21,300 Cr — around ₹18,000 Cr of it defence, led by a ₹10,200 Cr Pinaka rocket programme. Its weapons were battle-tested in Operation Sindoor in 2025. Management guides FY27 revenue to ~₹14,000 Cr, with defence crossing ₹4,500 Cr.
  • The valuation is the whole debate. At ~₹1.70 lakh Cr market cap on ₹1,737 Cr of profit, the stock trades near 100× earnings — roughly double even its own historical multiple, and far above any explosives peer. The market is pricing Solar not as a chemicals maker but as a multi-year defence growth story. Get the ramp, and it grows into the price; stumble, and a 100× stock has a long way to fall.
In plain terms

Think of Solar as a company that started by selling controlled explosions to coal mines — a steady, unglamorous, cash-rich business where it quietly became the biggest player in the country. Then it took the same core skill — chemistry that makes things detonate on command — and pointed it at a far bigger, far higher-value customer: the military. The same factories that make mining explosives now also make the propellant that launches a rocket, the high-explosive inside a warhead, and the kamikaze drones India flew in a real war. That shift, from "commodity for miners" to "weapons for the army", is why a once-boring stock now trades like a hot growth company — and why the argument about it is almost entirely about whether that price is sane.

01

Explosives 101: how you actually earn money blowing things up

No chemistry degree required — just the four ideas that drive the whole business.

An explosive is simply a substance that, when triggered, converts into gas extremely fast — releasing enormous energy in a fraction of a second. In mining and construction, that energy does one job: it fractures rock so machines can dig out the coal, ore or minerals underneath, or so a hillside can be cleared for a highway. Commercially, the business rests on four ideas.

The only concepts you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for Solar)
Bulk vs packaged explosivesBulk is pumped straight into blast-holes at big open-cast mines (cheap per tonne, high volume). Packaged comes in cartridges for smaller or underground work (higher value per tonne). Solar leads in both.
Initiating systems (detonators)The small, precise devices — electronic, electric and non-electric detonators, detonating cord, boosters — that set the main charge off at exactly the right instant. Higher-margin, technology-led, and a key part of Solar's edge.
Ammonium nitrate (AN)The core raw material — the same chemical that is ~80% of most explosives. Its price swings a lot, so controlling AN is the single biggest lever on cost and margin.
Volume × realisationRevenue ≈ tonnes sold × price per tonne. Solar shipped rising volumes (~17% growth in a recent quarter) and pushed realisation up (to ~₹72,000/tonne), so both levers pulled together.
Why explosives is a surprisingly good business

On the surface it sounds like a grim commodity. But it has three quietly attractive features. (1) It's essential and recurring — a mine has to keep blasting every single day to keep producing coal, so demand is steady and consumable, not one-off. (2) It's heavily regulated — you need explosives licences, safety approvals and secure logistics to even operate, which keeps casual competition out. And (3) it's local — explosives are dangerous and expensive to transport far, so whoever has plants near the big mining belts wins the contracts. Solar has spent 30 years building exactly that: licences, plants next to the mines, and the scale to make the raw materials itself. The result is a "boring" business that throws off a lot of cash at high returns — the foundation everything else is built on.

02

The core moat: scale, integration and a licence to blast

Why Solar is hard to attack — read this once and the whole business clicks.

Solar's dominance rests on three reinforcing advantages: sheer scale, deep vertical integration (it makes its own inputs and its own detonators), and a regulatory / distribution lock that keeps rivals out. Together they let it earn fat margins on a product most people assume is a low-margin commodity.

The value chain — from a chemical to a controlled blast

Solar doesn't just mix and sell explosives; it owns the chain from raw material to the finished detonation system — and, increasingly, beyond it into weapons.

The three pillars of the moat

  • Scale that nobody matches. Solar has ~330,000 tonnes/year of explosives capacity and was the first Indian firm to cross 300,000 tonnes; its Nagpur complex is the world's largest packaged-explosives site at a single location. Scale means lower unit cost, better bargaining power on raw materials, and the ability to serve a nationwide customer base from 30+ Indian plants.
  • Vertical integration. It secures/produces its own ammonium nitrate and makes its own detonators and initiating systems in-house. This protects margins when AN prices spike, and captures the higher-value "trigger" part of the sale that many rivals buy in.
  • Regulatory & logistics lock-in. Making, storing and moving explosives requires hard-to-get licences and safety clearances and secure, local logistics. That is a genuine barrier — a new entrant can't simply undercut on price, and Solar's plants sit right next to the mining belts where demand is.
Why "high-energy materials" is the real franchise

The clean way to understand Solar is this: it is not really an "explosives company" or a "defence company" — it is a high-energy-materials company. The underlying skill is designing and manufacturing substances that store a huge amount of energy and release it precisely on command, safely and reliably, at industrial scale. A mining explosive and a rocket propellant are, at their core, the same kind of problem — controlled, high-speed energy release. Once you have mastered that chemistry, the manufacturing plants, the safety systems, the licences and the R&D, you can point it at a coal seam or at an enemy tank. That is why Solar could move from mining into weapons without reinventing itself — and why the moat travels with it.

03

The company and who controls it

A Class-10 dropout, a rented explosives depot, and a Padma Shri.

Solar is a promoter-founded, family-controlled business with an origin story that reads like fiction. Satyanarayan Nuwal — a first-generation entrepreneur from Bhilwara, Rajasthan, who left school after class 10 for want of money and once slept at railway stations — built it from a rented explosives depot into a ~₹1.7 lakh crore defence-and-explosives group. In January 2026 the government awarded him the Padma Shri for industry.

Timeline

  • 1980s
    Satyanarayan Nuwal starts as an explosives dealer to coal mines in Maharashtra — renting a depot, building relationships with mining customers.
  • 1995–96
    Founds Solar Industries in Nagpur (with a bank loan) and moves from trading explosives to manufacturing them.
  • 2006
    Lists on NSE & BSE. At IPO it was a ~₹78 Cr revenue, ~₹11 Cr profit explosives maker — a fraction of today's size.
  • 2010
    Becomes the first private Indian company licensed for military-grade explosives — the seed of the defence business.
  • 2016–17
    After the Uri/Pathankot attacks, wins its first big ammunition order — multi-mode hand grenades — with DRDO; the first private firm to get such a contract.
  • 2024
    Delivers the first Nagastra-1 loitering-munition drones to the Indian Army; scales propellants, warheads and munitions.
  • 2025
    Solar's weapons — Nagastra drones, munitions — are used in Operation Sindoor; the defence order book explodes.
  • FY26
    Record ₹9,838 Cr revenue; defence nearly doubles to ₹2,634 Cr; ₹10,200 Cr Pinaka rocket contract; founder awarded the Padma Shri.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter family: the Nuwal family holds ~73% of the company — an unusually high, tightly-held stake that means the founders' interests are firmly aligned with minority shareholders, and there's no overhang of promoter selling.
  • Leadership: Satyanarayan Nuwal is founder & Executive Chairman; his son Manish Nuwal is Managing Director & CEO and is the driving force behind the defence pivot. A hands-on, founder-led group where the people who built the explosives business are personally steering the move into weapons.
  • Balance sheet: modest leverage — net debt ~₹867 Cr at FY26 (up from a small net-cash position a year earlier, as capex and working capital rose), but net debt/EBITDA of only ~0.3×. This is a business that funds a heavy growth capex programme largely from its own cash flows.
Why the ownership matters: defence is a business of long relationships, security clearances and multi-year execution. A stable, credible, ~73%-owned promoter family with a decade-plus track record of delivering for the MoD is itself an asset — it's why Solar keeps winning "first private company to…" contracts. The flip side is concentration: the story is heavily tied to one family's continued execution and judgement on where to deploy capital.
04

The defence transformation — why the multiple exploded

The single most important thing to understand about this stock.

For most of its life Solar was valued as what it was: a very good explosives company. Explosives companies, however excellent, don't get 100× multiples. What changed is that Solar turned itself into a defence manufacturer — and defence, in an India racing to build indigenous weapons, is a much larger, longer, higher-margin and more strategically prized market. This section is the heart of the whole primer.

From ~5% of sales to a quarter of the company

Defence was a rounding error a few years ago. Management has said it was a "very small share of revenue" up to 2020. Watch the trajectory:

What Solar actually makes for the military

This is not a company that assembles someone else's design. It makes the energetic core of modern weapons across a strikingly broad range:

  • Propellants & high-energy materials — the fuel that launches rockets and missiles. Solar supplies composite propellants used in programmes like Pinaka, Akash and BrahMos, plus its own high-explosive formulations (e.g. SE-BEX). This is the crown jewel: propellant chemistry is exactly Solar's explosives DNA, and very few can do it.
  • Artillery rockets & munitions — Pinaka multi-barrel rocket-launcher rockets (area-denial and high-explosive variants), plus warheads, artillery fuses, 30 mm and other ammunition, mines, and explosive-filling of shells.
  • Loitering munitions / drones — the Nagastra-1 "suicide drone" that flies to a target and detonates on impact, and the Bhargavastra counter-drone micro-missile system. This is the modern, headline-grabbing end that captures the market's imagination.
  • Grenades, fuses, igniters, chaff, counter-measures — the multi-mode hand grenade that first opened the door, plus a long tail of components and sub-systems.
Why defence deserves a higher multiple than explosives

Four reasons the market treats a rupee of defence revenue as worth far more than a rupee of mining-explosives revenue. (1) The runway is enormous and structural — India is deliberately shifting from importing weapons to making them at home ("indigenisation"), and Solar is a rare private company trusted with real ordnance. (2) The barriers are brutal — a new defence product can take 5–10 years of approvals, trials and clearances before the first order; once you're in, you're very hard to dislodge. (3) The contracts are big and multi-year — a single Pinaka programme is worth thousands of crores over years, giving revenue visibility an explosives contract never could. (4) It's proven in combat — Solar's weapons were used in Operation Sindoor, which is the ultimate validation a defence supplier can get. Stack those together and you get a business the market is willing to pay up for — sometimes a lot up.

The Maharashtra mega-project: Solar has signed an MoU with the Maharashtra government for an anchor defence & aerospace project near Nagpur worth ~₹12,700 Cr of investment over ~10 years, with management flagging revenue potential many times that. It's a signal of how large the defence ambition is — and a reminder that a lot of the value is still in the future, not the current P&L.
05

The order book — visibility, and its execution risk

₹21,300 crore of signed work is the bull case in a single number — with a catch.

In a project-and-contract business, the order book — the value of work already won but not yet delivered — is the clearest window into future revenue. Solar's stood at a record ₹21,300 Cr at FY26, of which ~₹18,000 Cr is defence and ~₹3,000 Cr non-defence. Against ₹9,838 Cr of annual sales, that's more than two years of revenue already locked in — extraordinary visibility for a company this size.

The single biggest chunk is the ~₹10,200 Cr Pinaka rocket contract (Feb 2026, split with state-owned Munitions India), which alone is bigger than a full year of the company's defence sales. Add defence export orders (e.g. ~₹2,000 Cr multi-year) and the picture is of years of visible defence work.

The order book cuts both ways

  • The bull reading: two-plus years of revenue is booked, the defence share means high margins and long duration, and the pipeline keeps growing (fresh MoD, Army and export orders land regularly). That visibility is exactly what justifies paying up today for tomorrow's profits.
  • The bear reading: an order book is a promise, not cash. Defence execution is famously lumpy — deliveries depend on trials, inspections, government payment cycles and the customer's own schedule. Revenue can slip a quarter or a year for reasons outside Solar's control, and a delayed Pinaka delivery would dent numbers the market is pricing as near-certain.
In plain terms

Imagine a contractor who has signed deals worth two years of work — you can be fairly confident about their revenue, which is comforting. But "signed" isn't "delivered and paid". Defence customers (armies, ministries) move on their own timetable: a batch of rockets has to pass firing trials, a drone has to clear inspection, a payment has to work its way through a government finance department. So the order book tells you the demand is real and large — a genuine strength — but the timing of when it turns into profit is the risk. When a stock is priced at 100× earnings, even a normal, blameless delivery delay can hurt the share price, because the market has left almost no room for the numbers to arrive later than hoped.

06

The export & overseas engine

The quiet, second growth story — and now the biggest single slice of sales.

Lost in the defence excitement is that Solar's largest revenue slice is actually international: exports plus overseas operations were ₹3,815 Cr in FY26 — ~39% of sales, growing ~32%. Solar isn't just an Indian explosives champion; it runs manufacturing plants in around ten countries and sells into 80+ countries, and it is increasingly exporting defence products too.

The overseas footprint

  • Africa is the big overseas base — plants in Zambia, Ghana, Nigeria, Tanzania and South Africa serve the continent's large mining industry (copper, gold, coal). Mining explosives are local by nature, so owning plants near African mines wins African contracts.
  • Plus Turkey, Indonesia, Thailand and Australia — a spread that reduces reliance on any single economy or mining cycle and taps some of the world's biggest resource markets.
  • Defence exports are the new layer — Solar has won multi-year defence export orders (e.g. ~₹2,000 Cr class) for ordnance to international clients, riding global demand for artillery, munitions and drones amid worldwide shortages.
Why the overseas engine is underrated

Two things make the international business more valuable than it looks. First, it's a natural hedge: when Indian mining or infrastructure demand softens, African copper or global gold mining may be booming — so the geographic spread smooths the cycle. Second, and more importantly, it's the on-ramp for defence exports. Solar already has the plants, logistics, relationships and regulatory experience to operate across 80+ countries as an explosives maker — which is exactly the infrastructure you need to also sell munitions and drones abroad. As global militaries scramble for artillery shells, propellants and loitering munitions, a company that can already manufacture and ship high-energy materials worldwide is unusually well placed. The export engine, in other words, is both today's largest business and tomorrow's second defence growth lever.

07

Coal India, mining & the infrastructure link

The demand behind the core explosives business — and why less coal-dependence is a good thing.

The explosives business ultimately runs on one thing: how much rock India (and the world) needs to break. That means coal mining (still India's main power source), metal and mineral mining, and infrastructure — roads, tunnels, dams, ports. Solar's oldest and most important single customer is Coal India (CIL), the state coal monopoly — but its dependence on CIL has fallen sharply, and that is healthy.

Solar began life almost entirely dependent on selling explosives to coal mines. As defence, exports and other domestic customers have grown, CIL's share has fallen to under a tenth — a big reduction in single-customer risk, even though the CIL relationship remains valuable.

The demand drivers to watch

  • Coal & power — India keeps expanding coal output to feed electricity demand; more coal dug means more explosives consumed. CIL and Singareni Collieries are anchor buyers.
  • Metals & minerals — iron ore, limestone, bauxite and other mining for steel, cement and industry — Solar supplies majors like Vedanta, Tata, Jindal, SAIL and others.
  • Infrastructure & construction — the government's road, tunnel, dam and rail push, plus a broad housing and infrastructure cycle, drives non-mining explosives demand. Solar's realisations and volumes both rose on this backdrop.
Why diversifying away from Coal India matters

For years the knock on Solar was "it's a proxy for Coal India" — if CIL's output or tendering wobbled, so did Solar. Depending on one giant, price-sensitive government buyer is a weakness: it caps your pricing power and concentrates your risk. By growing exports, other domestic mining customers, infrastructure demand and — above all — defence, Solar has turned a one-customer explosives company into a four-engine business where Coal India is now under a tenth of sales. The demand base is broader, the pricing power is better, and a bad year at CIL no longer defines the company. It's a quiet but real de-risking that gets less attention than the flashy drone headlines.

08

The competitive set

Dominant in explosives; a rare private trailblazer in defence.

Solar competes in two very different arenas. In explosives it is the clear domestic leader in a small field. In defence it competes with government-owned giants and a new wave of private defence firms — but it occupies an unusual, defensible position as the private company trusted with the actual energetics of weapons.

The competitive tension to watch

Two dynamics matter. In explosives, Solar's lead is durable but the market grows slowly — you don't get a re-rating from a mature, ~25%-share domestic commodity, however well run. In defence, the opportunity is huge but so is the competition and the customer's power: the buyer is mostly the government, which can favour its own PSUs, split orders (as it did on Pinaka), squeeze prices, or slow-walk approvals. Solar's edge is that the hardest, most specialised part of a weapon — the propellant, the high explosive, the warhead fill — is precisely its home turf, and that expertise took decades and rare licences to build. Many "defence stocks" are really assemblers or component makers; Solar owns the energetic core. Whether it can keep converting that edge into a rising share of India's fast-growing indigenous-weapons spend — against both PSUs and well-funded private entrants — is the competitive question that underpins the whole valuation.

10

The valuation debate — is ~100× sane?

Everything about this stock comes down to this one argument.

Solar trades at around 100× trailing earnings — a market cap near ₹1.70 lakh crore on ₹1,737 Cr of FY26 profit, at a share price around ₹18,700. That is roughly double even its own long-run average multiple, and multiples of any explosives peer (which typically trade in the teens to twenties). The entire investment case hinges on whether that price is justified by the growth ahead or is simply "defence euphoria".

How to think about the ~100× multiple

A 100× P/E means, crudely, that at today's profit it would take a century of earnings to pay back the price — so the buyer is not buying today's earnings; they are buying a bet that earnings grow enormously from here. The bull maths goes like this: if defence keeps compounding (₹2,634 Cr → >₹4,500 Cr FY27, and revenue targeted at ~₹14,000 Cr), and if that ₹21,300 Cr order book converts, profit could roughly double over two-to-three years — at which point the forward multiple on those future earnings is far less scary, maybe 40–50×. Bulls argue you're paying a scarcity premium for the best-in-class, promoter-aligned, combat-proven private defence-energetics play in a country structurally rearming for a decade. Bears argue the opposite: at 100× there is zero margin for error — a delayed Pinaka delivery, an ammonium-nitrate spike that squeezes margins, an order that slips, a bad quarter — and a stock this richly priced can de-rate 30–50% even if the business is basically fine. Both are internally consistent. The tie-breaker is execution: does the defence ramp actually show up in the P&L, on schedule, quarter after quarter?

Solar is an exceptional business at a demanding price — the classic case where long-term conviction has to be weighed against near-term price risk.
The honest tension: the market has already priced in years of flawless defence execution. That means the business risk here is genuinely low — this is a great company — but the valuation risk is high. The two numbers to track every quarter are defence revenue growth (is the ramp real and on time?) and EBITDA margin (is the richer mix and pricing holding up against ammonium-nitrate costs?). If both keep delivering, the company grows into its price; if either stumbles, a 100× multiple has a long way to compress.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely great business — priced as if that greatness is guaranteed.

Solar Industries is one of the most impressive industrial stories in India: a dominant, integrated, cash-generative explosives leader that has successfully bootstrapped itself into a fast-growing, combat-proven defence manufacturer, run by an aligned founding family with a decade-plus track record. The investment debate is almost entirely about execution and price, not business quality.

The bull case

  • Structural defence tailwind — India is rearming and indigenising for a decade; Solar is a rare, trusted private energetics maker riding it.
  • Order-book visibility — ₹21,300 Cr (~₹18,000 Cr defence) is 2+ years of revenue, anchored by the ₹10,200 Cr Pinaka programme.
  • Combat-proven — weapons used in Operation Sindoor; the ultimate validation and a marketing edge for exports.
  • High-quality core — #1 explosives, ~28% margins, ~37% ROCE, four diversified engines, only light debt.
  • Aligned promoters — ~73% family-owned, founder-led, hands-on in the defence pivot.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — ~100× earnings leaves no room for error; can de-rate hard even on decent results.
  • Execution / lumpiness — defence deliveries hinge on trials, inspections and government payment cycles; timing can slip.
  • Government-buyer power — the MoD can split orders (as on Pinaka), favour PSUs, or squeeze prices.
  • Ammonium-nitrate volatility — a sharp AN price move can whipsaw revenue and pressure margins.
  • Concentration & expectations — a lot of value sits in future projects (Maharashtra mega-project); the story is priced for near-perfection.
The bottom line

Solar Industries is a structurally advantaged, high-return, founder-led business executing one of the most credible commodity-to-defence transformations in Indian industry — from blasting rock for coal mines to making the propellants, warheads and drones a modern military runs on. The business quality is not really in doubt. What's in doubt is price and timing: the stock already reflects years of flawless defence execution at ~100× earnings, so any delivery slip, margin wobble or ammonium-nitrate shock can hurt the share price even if the company keeps performing well. The next few years will be written in two numbers each quarter — defence revenue growth (is the order book converting on schedule?) and EBITDA margin (is the premium mix holding against input costs?). Deliver on both and Solar grows into its rich valuation; miss on either, and there's a long way for a 100× multiple to fall.
