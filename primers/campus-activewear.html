Campus Activewear, India's mass-premium sneaker

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Campus Activewear Limited makes the sports shoes on ordinary Indian feet — running shoes, walking shoes, casual sneakers and slip-ons for men, women and children, sold under the single well-known Campus brand at prices that undercut the global names by a wide margin. It is, by the company's own claim and by industry data, India's largest sports-and-athleisure footwear brand by volume, with roughly a 17% share of the organised "scaled" sports footwear market. It is a Delhi-headquartered family business that designs its own styles, assembles most of its shoes in its own plants across North India, and sells them through a huge trade-distribution network and a fast-growing online store-front.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It sells the "masstige" sports shoe — mass volume, aspirational look. Campus's sweet spot is the ₹500–₹2,000 sports shoe: cheap enough for a first-time branded buyer trading up from an unbranded local pair, stylish enough to feel like the global brands it visually echoes. That position — affordable, fashion-forward, made in India — is the entire identity. It is not a formal-leather brand like Bata's dress shoes, and it is not a premium global name like Nike or Adidas; it is the value-to-mid sports specialist in between.
  • In FY26, price did the heavy lifting — not pairs. Revenue grew ~11.4% to ~₹1,774 Cr, but volume rose only 4.2% to ~26 million pairs. The gap is average selling price (ASP), which jumped ~6.9% from ₹639 to ₹683 as buyers shifted into pricier sneakers. This is the crux of the current story: Campus is growing by selling dearer shoes, not just more shoes.
  • It is highly profitable and carries almost no debt — and the market prices it richly. FY26 EBITDA margin expanded to ~17.5% and net profit rose ~24% to ~₹150 Cr, on a largely debt-free balance sheet with a high return on capital. Yet the stock trades on a rich multiple — around 50× earnings — because investors are paying for a consumer-brand compounding story, not a cheap footwear cyclical.
In plain terms

Campus took the one thing global sportswear does — turn a functional running shoe into a fashion object young people want — and did it at an Indian price. A shopper who would never spend ₹8,000 on Nike will happily spend ₹1,200 on a Campus sneaker that looks the part, and until recently might have bought an unbranded ₹400 pair instead. The company's whole job is to keep pulling that shopper up the ladder: from no-brand to Campus, and then from a ₹700 Campus shoe to a ₹1,500 Campus sneaker. FY26 shows the machine working — the shoe got dearer faster than the customer count grew. The question the price of the stock asks is whether that upward drift in the price tag can continue for years, because at ~50× earnings the market has already bought a lot of tomorrow's premiumisation.

01

The sports-shoe opportunity: from no-brand to branded

Why a country that barely bought branded sports shoes is suddenly a growth market.

To understand Campus you first have to understand how under-shod, in branded terms, India still is. Footwear is one of the last big consumer categories where the unorganised, unbranded market — local cobblers, regional makers, no-name imports — still commands the majority of pairs sold. Inside that, sports and athleisure footwear is the fastest-shifting slice, because a running-style shoe has quietly become the default everyday shoe for a young, urbanising population. Campus lives precisely at that seam: unbranded shoppers trading up, and casual shoppers trading toward a sportier look.

Four forces push in the same direction, and together they are the reason a footwear company can be a growth story at all:

  • Unorganised → branded. A large part of India's footwear is still bought unbranded. Every year some of those buyers cross over to an affordable brand — and the affordable sports-shoe brand they most often meet at that price is Campus. This "formalisation" of a huge category is a structural tailwind independent of the economic cycle.
  • Sneakerisation of the wardrobe. The sports shoe is no longer only for sport. For a young Indian it is the shoe worn to college, to work, to the mall and to the gym — the same casualisation of dress that reshaped Western wardrobes. That makes the addressable occasion far larger than "athletes."
  • Rising discretionary spend and a young country. A median age in the twenties, growing urban incomes and deeper e-commerce reach expand both the number of buyers and how much each will pay for a shoe that signals style.
  • Import substitution by policy. A quality-control regime (covered in section 06) is squeezing the cheap imported shoes that used to fill the value end, handing domestic branded makers a structural opening. This is a genuinely new force, and it favours a made-in-India brand with its own factories.

Industry estimates size India's sneaker market at roughly US$3.2 bn in 2024, rising toward US$4.5 bn by 2030 — a mid-single-digit (~6%) compounding market. That headline growth is modest on its own; the real prize is that the branded, organised slice inside it grows far faster as shoppers move off unbranded product. Campus's reporting is in rupees, but the direction of that dollar-sized market is the current under it.

Why the "cheap" positioning is the strategic advantage, not a limitation

It is tempting to see Campus's low price point as a weakness against glamorous global brands. It is the opposite. The most crowded, most premium end of the market — the ₹5,000-plus performance sneaker — is where Nike, Adidas and Puma spend billions and where an Indian brand has little to offer. The largest and least-served pool of new buyers sits at the bottom, where an unbranded shopper makes a first branded purchase. Owning the price band where that crossover happens — roughly ₹500 to ₹2,000 — is a far bigger prize than fighting for the thin premium tier. Campus's job is to be the brand that shopper meets on the way up, and then to gently raise the shelf they reach for. The opportunity is the width of the bottom of the pyramid, not the height of its top.

02

What Campus actually sells: the masstige sneaker

One brand, a wide style range, and a deliberate climb up the price ladder.

Campus is a single-brand, multi-style business. Everything it makes carries the Campus name; the variety comes from styles and price bands aimed at different buyers — running shoes and walking shoes at the value end, chunky fashion sneakers and lifestyle styles at the upper end, plus sandals, floaters and slip-ons that round out the basket. The commercial engine is the movement of the range's centre of gravity upward: from plain sports shoes toward sneakers, and from opening prices toward the ₹1,500-plus band.

In a single year, nearly six points of the sales mix moved into the higher-priced sneaker band — the clearest signal that Campus's buyers are trading up inside the brand, which is what lifts both ASP and margin.

That mix shift shows up directly in the blended price: ASP rose from ₹639 (FY25) to ₹683 (FY26), a ₹44 uplift that looks small per pair but, across 26 million pairs, is worth more incremental revenue than all the extra pairs shipped combined — the arithmetic section 07 pulls fully apart.

Campus supports the range the way a consumer brand does: visible advertising, celebrity and youth-culture associations, colour and design refreshes each season, and a widening presence in women's and kids' styles. New launches — such as the Elan line introduced in early FY27 — are aimed squarely at keeping the sneaker end fresh and pushing the average ticket higher. The design-and-marketing muscle is what lets a shoe that costs modestly more to make command a materially higher shelf price.

Why the sneaker mix is the smartest dial management actually controls

Most of what drives a footwear year — festive demand, monsoon timing, competitor discounting, input costs — is outside any single company's hands. Product mix is the exception. Whether a buyer walks out with a ₹700 running shoe or a ₹1,600 fashion sneaker is something Campus can steer with design, marketing and shelf placement, and every rupee of that shift lands almost straight in ASP and gross margin. That is why "sneakerisation" is not marketing fluff here; it is the one lever the company owns that reliably improves the quality of each pair sold. The premiumisation push is, in plain accounting terms, a self-directed margin programme.

03

Two ways to the buyer: the trade shelf and the phone screen

A distribution-built brand is turning itself into a near-half-direct one.

Campus reaches its buyer through two very different roads, and the balance between them is shifting fast. The first is trade distribution — the traditional wholesale route of distributors supplying thousands of multi-brand footwear retailers across the country, the network on which Campus was built. The second is direct-to-consumer (D2C) — Campus's own website, its listings on the big e-commerce marketplaces, and its own branded stores. What was overwhelmingly a trade business a few years ago is now approaching a 50/50 split, and the mix change is one of the most important things happening to the company's economics.

And the mix is moving fast. In just twelve months, trade's share of Q4 revenue slipped from ~55% to ~51% while D2C climbed from ~44.8% to ~48.3% — roughly four points shifting from wholesale to direct in a single year. The direction is the point: Campus is steadily disintermediating the very channel it grew up on.

The two channels are not just different logistics — they have different economics and different information. Trade is capital-light for reach: distributors carry inventory and credit, so Campus can be present in over 800 districts without owning that shelf. But the trade route hands margin to the middle and blinds the brand to who is actually buying. D2C — especially online — flips that: Campus captures the full retail margin, sees the buyer directly, and can push new styles and higher price points without waiting for a distributor to restock. The trade-off is that D2C carries its own costs (marketplace commissions, returns, delivery, advertising to drive traffic) and demands the brand fund and manage inventory it once pushed downstream.

The quiet consequence of going direct: mix and margin move together

The channel shift and the premiumisation story are the same story seen from two angles. Online is where a fashion sneaker sells best — a shopper browsing a phone screen is far more likely to pay up for a ₹1,600 style than one grabbing a shoe off a small-town shelf. So as D2C online climbs, it pulls ASP and the sneaker mix up with it, and Campus keeps the retail margin it used to share with the trade. That is a large part of why margins expanded in FY26 even though volume growth was modest. The risk sits in the same place: online growth depends on advertising spend and marketplace terms the company only partly controls, and a direct model ties up working capital and exposes the brand to returns and discount-driven price wars that the trade channel used to absorb.

The physical footprint

  • ~300 exclusive brand outlets (EBOs) — Campus's own-name stores, the D2C-offline arm; a controlled shop-window for the full range and the premium styles.
  • Presence across 800-plus districts via the trade network — the reach that a purely online brand cannot cheaply replicate, and a genuine barrier to entry for a new challenger.
  • Marketplaces and own website — the D2C-online engine, where the fastest growth and the richest mix are being captured.
04

The family, the float, and who runs it

A promoter-family footwear house that went public in 2022.

Campus is a family-founded, family-run business that listed in 2022 after decades in Indian footwear. Understanding the ownership matters because the founding family still holds the controlling stake and sets strategy, and because the company's 2022 IPO was a route for early backers to sell rather than a fundraise — the money went to selling shareholders, not into the business.

Ownership & leadership

  • Promoter family control: the founding Agarwal family holds the controlling stake — the founder-chairman with over three decades in footwear as Chairman & Managing Director, and his son as Whole-Time Director & CEO running day-to-day operations. Investors should confirm the exact current promoter percentage in the latest shareholding filing, as promoters have trimmed stakes via block deals post-listing.
  • Public & institutional float: the balance is held by the public, mutual funds and foreign investors — a genuine free float created and widened by the IPO and subsequent promoter sell-downs.
  • Founder-led, professionally staffed: a family sets the direction while a professional management layer (a recently appointed CFO among them) runs finance, supply chain and the D2C build-out — the classic shape of a founder business maturing into a listed consumer company.
Why the ownership shape cuts both ways: a committed founder-family with deep category knowledge and skin in the game is a real asset for a brand that lives on merchandising instinct and dealer relationships. But post-IPO promoter share sales, related decision-making concentrated in one family, and the fact that the listing itself raised no growth capital are all things minority investors should keep an eye on — governance and capital allocation matter more, not less, once a founder business is public and richly valued.
05

Making the shoe: how "vertically integrated" really works

More in-house than most rivals — but less than the label suggests, which is the interesting part.

Campus's pitch to investors leans heavily on being vertically integrated — designing and manufacturing its own shoes rather than simply importing and branding them. That in-house base is real and it matters: it gives control over cost, quality, design turnaround and response speed, and it is a large reason Campus can hit its price points while still earning a strong margin. But the honest picture is more textured than the label implies, and the texture is where the operating story lives.

A sports shoe is really three things joined together: an upper (the fabric/synthetic top), a sole (the moulded bottom) and the assembly that unites them. Campus makes different shares of each in-house:

So the model is best described as integrated assembly with partial component-making: Campus controls the step that most affects quality and design — putting the shoe together — and it makes a meaningful chunk of soles and uppers itself, while topping up from a network of ancillary suppliers for the rest. That hybrid is deliberate. Owning assembly and design gives control where it counts; sourcing part of the components keeps the model asset-light enough to flex capacity up and down with demand without drowning in fixed costs.

That flexibility shows in utilisation. Against installed annual assembly capacity of roughly 30.7 million pairs, Campus sold about 26 million in FY26 — running in the mid-80s percent. That leaves genuine headroom to grow volume without major new capex, but it also means the fixed-cost base is already fairly well absorbed: the next leg of margin has to come from mix (ASP), not simply from filling the plants.

The genuine surprise: India's "largest sports footwear brand" buys in most of its uppers

Read the fine print and a smart generalist is caught off guard. The brand that markets itself on being vertically integrated actually makes only about 37% of its uppers and about half its soles in-house — the majority of both components come from outside ancillary suppliers, with Campus's own factories concentrated on assembly. That is not a scandal; it is a sensible, flexible model. But it reframes the moat. Campus's edge is not that it owns the whole supply chain — it does not. Its edge is design plus distribution plus brand at a price nobody can easily match, wrapped around an assembly operation lean enough to scale. The integration is a supporting actor, not the star — and knowing that changes how you weigh the "vertical integration" bullet on any pitch you read.

The operating leverage works both ways. When demand softens, the same fixed-cost base and inventory can compress margins quickly, which is exactly what happened in the FY23–FY24 slowdown. A make-and-assemble footwear business is not immune to the cycle; it simply amplifies whichever way the cycle turns.

06

The import wall: how a quality rule became industrial policy

The structural tailwind that has little to do with fashion and everything to do with Delhi.

One of the most important forces acting on Campus is not a design trend or a marketing campaign — it is a Quality Control Order (QCO). Since 2023, the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) has been rolling out mandatory quality standards on footwear: broadly, no shoe, sandal or slipper may be manufactured, imported or sold in India without meeting BIS norms and carrying the standard mark. The stated aim is quality and safety. The practical effect is a barrier against cheap imported footwear — much of which came from China and Vietnam — because overseas sourcing factories have to be BIS-certified to keep supplying India, and many were not.

A quality rule, on paper, functions as an import tariff in practice — and Campus, which already manufactures in India with its own compliant capacity, is on the right side of it by design rather than by luck.

Why does this matter so much for Campus specifically? Because the company's entire battleground is the value-to-mid price band — precisely the tier that cheap imports used to contest hardest. A domestic brand that already runs its own Indian factories does not have to scramble to comply; it was compliant to begin with. So the QCO acts as a quiet, ongoing transfer of demand from unbranded and imported product toward branded domestic makers — a tailwind that requires Campus to do nothing except keep being what it already is.

The under-appreciated structural gift — and its catch

Investors spend a lot of time on Campus's advertising and its sneaker designs, and comparatively little on a policy that may matter more than either. The QCO is, in effect, industrial protection dressed as consumer safety: it raises the cost and friction of putting a cheap imported shoe on an Indian shelf, and it does so year after year, not once. For a made-in-India brand sitting in the exact price band under attack, that is a durable demand tailwind few consumer companies enjoy. The catch is that what policy gives, policy can dilute — certification of overseas factories can catch up over time, standards can be eased under trade pressure, and domestic capacity constraints could invite exemptions. It is a strong tailwind, but a political one, and political winds change direction.

The point to hold on to: when you read that Campus is a "branded consumer growth story," remember that a slice of that growth is being handed to it by an import barrier, not earned purely on the shop floor. That makes the near-term runway more secure than a pure brand story — and it makes any future softening of the footwear QCO a specific, watchable risk that most brand-led analysis overlooks.
07

The ASP engine: why price beat pairs

The single most important idea in the current investment case — and it is specific to Campus's FY26.

Most people track a footwear company by how many pairs it sells. For Campus in FY26, that would have led you badly astray. The company shipped only about 4.2% more pairs — yet revenue grew 11.4% and profit grew 24%. The whole difference is price per pair. FY26 was not a year of selling many more shoes; it was a year of selling dearer shoes to roughly the same number of feet. Understand that one sentence and you understand the engine — and the risk — of the current story.

More than half of FY26's revenue growth came from a higher price per pair, not from more pairs. In a value footwear company, that is an unusual and important signature.

Now watch why the price rose. It is not raw price hikes on the same shoe — that would just cost Campus volume against cheaper rivals. It is mix: the sneaker share above ₹1,500 climbed from 52.7% to 58.4%, sneaker volumes reportedly doubled, and D2C-online (where dearer styles sell best) grew toward half of revenue. Each of those pulls the blended realisation upward. The ASP is simply the visible sum of all of them — a single ₹683 number that packages the entire premiumisation strategy into one figure you can track quarter by quarter.

The device that fits only Campus's FY26: ₹44 a pair beat a million pairs

Here is the arithmetic that summarises the whole company right now. Between FY25 and FY26, Campus added roughly one million pairs of volume — real, but modest. Over the same year it added ₹44 to the price of every one of its ~26 million pairs (₹639 → ₹683). Multiply it out: the ₹44-per-pair uplift generated more incremental revenue than the extra million pairs did. A value footwear brand that grew mostly by charging more, not selling more, is a genuinely different animal from the "ship more cheap shoes" business the category is usually assumed to be. The bet embedded in the share price is that Campus can keep finding the next ₹44 — year after year — by moving its buyers up the ladder faster than the ladder runs out of rungs. That single number, the annual drift in ASP, is the pulse of this company.

The reason this framing matters for an investor is that it tells you exactly what to watch and what would break the case. If ASP keeps compounding — sneaker mix rising, online scaling, new premium lines like Elan landing — then modest volume growth is enough to keep double-digit revenue and expanding margins going, and the rich valuation has something to feed on. If ASP stalls — because the sneaker trade-up saturates, or discounting erupts, or buyers resist higher prices in a weak year — then Campus is thrown back on volume growth in a mid-single-digit market, which is a very different, far cheaper kind of company. The whole debate reduces to the durability of that ₹44.

08

The competition: crowded above, crowded below, contested in the middle

Campus owns a specific slice — but everyone wants a piece of it.

Campus does not compete against a single rival; it is squeezed and courted from several directions at once. Above it sit the global sportswear majors — Nike, Adidas, Puma, Skechers, Reebok — with vastly bigger marketing budgets and aspirational pull, but at price points several times higher. Below and beside it sit the domestic value and mass brands — Relaxo, Bata, Action, Liberty, Redtape — and, still, the vast unorganised market. Campus's defensible ground is the branded value-to-mid sports shoe, and its challenge is that both the premium names (reaching down with cheaper lines) and the mass names (reaching up into sneakers) are pushing into that exact band.

The shape of the fight to keep in mind

Two things define Campus's competitive position. First, its moat is not a single unbeatable factory or a patented sole — it is the combination of a recognised value sports brand, an 800-district distribution reach, in-house assembly and a scaling online channel, all delivered at a price the global majors can't touch and the mass players can't match on style. That bundle is hard to replicate quickly, but no one piece of it is impregnable. Second, the middle of the market is where everyone is converging: Bata premiumising, Relaxo sportifying, global brands occasionally reaching down, and D2C-native newcomers appearing online. Campus's answer has to be the same one that is driving its numbers — keep the ASP and the design fresh enough that its shoe feels a rung above the mass makers and a fraction of the price of the global ones. It is a defensible middle, but it must be actively defended.

09

Seasons, inventory and the cash the model ties up

A make-and-hold consumer business runs on inventory — and inventory is cash.

Two operational realities shape Campus's cash flow and its risk profile: demand is seasonal and fashion-led, and the model is inventory-heavy. A footwear brand has to design, make and stock shoes ahead of demand, guess the styles that will sell, and carry the ones that don't. That working-capital cycle is where a profitable brand can still get into trouble, and it is worth understanding before reading the profit line as if it were cash.

The seasonality

  • Festive and wedding season (roughly Sep–Dec) is typically the strongest demand window, when discretionary footwear buying peaks and new styles land.
  • Summer supports open footwear (sandals, floaters, slip-ons), smoothing the sports-shoe cycle.
  • Monsoon and weak-sentiment stretches can slow offtake and leave product sitting in the channel — the FY23–FY24 slowdown was partly a demand-and-destocking episode of exactly this kind.

The revenue tilt toward the festive-heavy second half (roughly October–March) is modest but real, and it carries a sting: a single weak festive quarter can dent a whole year, because so much of the discretionary buying is concentrated in that window.

The bigger structural point is working capital. To have the right shoe on the right shelf (or in the right warehouse for next-day online delivery) at the right moment, Campus must hold a lot of finished-goods inventory, and the shift to D2C makes this more its problem, not less: when it sold mostly through the trade, distributors carried much of that inventory and the credit behind it; as it goes direct, Campus increasingly funds and owns the stock itself. The pay-off is margin and control (section 03); the price is a heavier balance sheet and the risk of markdowns on styles that miss.

Where a healthy-looking footwear brand can quietly stumble

The danger in a fashion-inflected footwear business is rarely the income statement in a good year — it is the inventory in a bad one. Design a season the buyer doesn't want, or hit a soft festive quarter, and unsold stock piles up; clearing it means discounts that hit both margin and the premium positioning the whole ASP story depends on. That is why the two numbers to read alongside Campus's profit are inventory days and the operating cash flow: they tell you whether the reported growth is turning into cash or into unsold shoes in a warehouse. Campus's largely debt-free balance sheet is a real cushion here — it can absorb a bad season without a solvency scare — but the discipline of the make-and-hold cycle is a permanent feature of the model, not a phase it grows out of.

Financials & valuation

A high-margin, debt-light brand growing steadily — priced like a consumer compounder.

Campus's financial signature is attractive on its own terms: strong gross margins for footwear, an EBITDA margin pushing into the high teens, a high return on capital, and almost no debt. FY26 improved all of these — profit grew far faster than revenue as premiumisation and the D2C mix lifted margins. The tension in the case is not the quality of the business; it is the price the market puts on it. At roughly 50× earnings, Campus is valued as a durable consumer-brand compounder, and that valuation is doing a lot of the talking.

The one number that settles the thesis: ASP, read with the sneaker mix

At ~50× earnings, Campus is not priced to be a cheap footwear cyclical — it is priced to keep compounding like a consumer brand, which means the burden of proof falls on average selling price. ASP (the ₹683 of FY26), tracked quarter by quarter alongside the share of sales from ₹1,500-plus sneakers and the D2C-online mix, is the metric that proves or breaks the case. If ASP keeps drifting up a few percent a year as the sneaker mix and online channel expand, then even modest volume growth sustains double-digit revenue and rising margins, and the rich multiple is fed by compounding earnings rather than by hope. If ASP flattens — premiumisation saturating, discounting flaring, buyers resisting higher tickets in a soft year — then Campus is revealed as a mid-single-digit-market volume grower wearing a compounder's price tag, and the multiple has a long distance to travel downward. Pairs times an ever-rising price per pair, funded increasingly through its own channel — that is the scoreboard, and ASP is the number on it that matters most.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality brand with real tailwinds, asked to justify a demanding price.

Campus enters FY27 in good form: a record-ish year behind it, ASP rising, D2C approaching half of revenue, an import barrier working in its favour, and a strong start to the new year with the Elan line launched to push the premium end further. The debate is not whether it is a good business — the margins, returns and brand say it is — but whether it can compound fast enough, for long enough, to earn a multiple that already assumes years of successful premiumisation. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Premiumisation is working — ASP up 6.9% and the ₹1,500-plus sneaker mix rising to 58.4%; the margin engine is proven, not hoped for.
  • D2C-online scaling — nearly half of revenue is now direct, capturing full retail margin and richer mix, and the shift is still young.
  • The QCO import wall — a policy tailwind steadily transferring value-tier demand from cheap imports to a made-in-India brand that is already compliant.
  • Structural formalisation — a large unbranded market feeding branded sports footwear for years, with Campus the default first-brand at its price band.
  • High returns, low debt — a capital-efficient, largely debt-free brand able to fund growth and absorb a weak season without stress.

The bear case

  • The valuation is demanding — ~50× earnings assumes years of continued premiumisation; a stall in ASP re-rates the story sharply.
  • Volume growth is modest — just 4.2% pairs in FY26; if the trade-up saturates, the underlying market grows only mid-single digits.
  • Crowded middle — Bata premiumising, Relaxo sportifying, global majors and D2C newcomers all pushing into Campus's price band.
  • Fashion & inventory risk — a missed season or soft festive quarter means markdowns that hit both margin and the premium positioning; D2C puts that inventory on Campus's own books.
  • Policy & promoter watch-items — a softening of the footwear QCO would remove a tailwind, and post-IPO promoter sell-downs and family-concentrated control warrant governance vigilance.
The bottom line

Campus Activewear is a genuinely good business doing a genuinely clever thing — turning an affordable Indian running shoe into an aspirational sneaker and steadily raising its price while a policy wall keeps cheap imports at bay. The margins, the returns and the debt-light balance sheet are all what you would want, and FY26 showed the premiumisation machine converting a modest rise in pairs into a much larger rise in profit. What the share price asks is not whether the machine works — it does — but whether it can keep finding the next ₹44 on the price tag, year after year, as rivals crowd the same middle. The next concrete test is datable and specific: whether ASP and the ₹1,500-plus sneaker mix keep climbing through the FY27 festive season as the Elan line ramps and D2C-online pushes past half of revenue. Keep ASP compounding and the demanding multiple has real earnings to feed on; let it flatten, and Campus is a fine mid-single-digit volume grower that the market has priced as something rarer.
