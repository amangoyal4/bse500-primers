Zaggle, a spend-management platform that lives on someone else's licence

A complete walk through one of India's few listed SaaS-fintech companies in corporate spend management — the firm behind the "Save" expense app, the "Zoyer" payables engine and the "Propel" rewards platform — how a business that issues millions of prepaid cards without owning a banking licence actually earns its keep, why its headline revenue is flattered by a large, near-pass-through rewards line, and why the single fee that pays its bills is set by a regulator and shared with a bank.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Zaggle Prepaid Ocean Services Limited runs a corporate spend-management platform — software that lets a company control, track and automate how its employees, vendors and channel partners spend money. Its best-known products are Save (an expense-management and employee-benefits app tied to prepaid cards), Zoyer (an accounts-payable and vendor-payments engine), and Propel (a rewards, loyalty and incentives platform). It sits in a hybrid category that is part SaaS (software sold on subscription) and part fintech (it earns a slice of card-transaction fees). Listed only in September 2023, it is one of the few pure-play spend-management names on the Indian market, and it grows fast — revenue has been compounding in the 40-50% range.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It runs a card business without a banking licence. Zaggle does not issue cards itself — it cannot; issuing prepaid instruments in India requires a Reserve Bank of India (RBI) authorisation that Zaggle does not hold. Instead it partners with banks, which issue the cards on their licence, and Zaggle provides the software, onboarding and controls around them. Every rupee of its core card fee is therefore earned on infrastructure it rents from a regulated bank, and shared with that bank. This is the B2B2C model that defines the company.
  • Its headline revenue is flattered by a low-margin rewards line. Of ~₹1,908 Cr of FY26 revenue, well over half — about ₹1,107 Cr — is Propel, its rewards and gift-card business, which is booked on a gross basis (the full face value of reward points passes through the P&L) at a wafer-thin margin. Strip Propel out and the real profit engine is far smaller — the interchange-linked Program Fee (~₹752 Cr) and the high-margin SaaS Platform Fee (~₹48 Cr). Reading Zaggle by its top line badly overstates its size.
  • FY26 was a record year — but FY27 opened with a wobble. FY26 delivered revenue +46%, adjusted EBITDA +51% and PAT +52%, the first year the company showed clear operating leverage. Then Q1 FY27 (June 2026) grew revenue a slower ~27.5% and PAT actually fell ~33%, as costs from the Dice acquisition landed before the revenue did and margins compressed to 8.2%. The stock trades around 21-22× earnings — a growth-fintech rating that asks the fast growth to keep coming.
In plain terms

Picture a software company that helps big employers manage the money their staff spend — booking travel, paying vendors, handing out rewards — and does it partly through prepaid cards. But Zaggle can't issue those cards; a bank does, on the bank's RBI licence, and takes a cut of every swipe. Zaggle keeps the rest, plus a subscription fee for the software, plus a large but low-margin rewards business that inflates its revenue without adding much profit. So there are really two Zaggles: a small, high-quality SaaS-and-interchange engine that makes the money, wrapped inside a big, low-margin rewards volume that makes the revenue look bigger than it is. The company is growing fast and just posted its best-ever year — but it depends on banks it doesn't control, and on an interchange fee a regulator can change with one circular.

01

Spend management 101: the problem Zaggle sells against

No fintech jargon needed — just the everyday mess it cleans up, and who pays to have it cleaned.

Every company of any size has a nagging, unglamorous problem: controlling and accounting for the money its people spend. Employees book flights, buy fuel, entertain clients, claim reimbursements; the finance team pays hundreds of vendors; marketing hands out incentives to dealers and rewards to staff. Left to spreadsheets and paper bills, all of this is slow, leaky and hard to audit. Zaggle sells the software — and the plumbing — that replaces that mess with a controlled, digital, rule-based system. To follow the company you need five terms.

The only spend-management terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Spend managementSoftware that lets a company set rules on, track and reconcile every rupee its employees and vendors spend — before the spend happens, not after. Zaggle's core category. Sold to the company's finance/HR team.
Prepaid / corporate cardA card loaded with company money and handed to an employee, with limits and rules built in. Zaggle's software rides on these cards — but a bank issues them; Zaggle cannot.
InterchangeThe small fee (roughly 1.7-1.8% of the amount spent) that a merchant's system pays when a card is swiped. It is shared between the issuing bank and Zaggle. This is the heart of the "Program Fee" — Zaggle's most important earnings line.
SaaS / subscriptionSoftware-as-a-Service: software rented monthly rather than bought. Zaggle charges corporates a per-user-per-month fee for its platforms — the "Platform Fee." Small in rupees, but the highest-margin, stickiest revenue it has.
B2B2CBusiness-to-business-to-consumer: Zaggle sells to a company (B2B), which then puts the product in the hands of its employees or channel partners (the final C). Zaggle rarely touches the end user directly — it reaches them through the employer.
The one dependency that shapes the whole model: it needs a bank

India does not let just anyone issue a prepaid card. Loading money onto a card that people can spend is a regulated activity — a Prepaid Payment Instrument (PPI) — and it requires an RBI licence. Zaggle does not have one. So its entire card business is built on top of partner banks (and their card networks, Visa/Mastercard/RuPay) that hold the licence, take the deposits, bear the compliance and issue the plastic. Zaggle supplies the software, the corporate relationship, the onboarding and the spend controls, and in exchange keeps a share of the interchange. That single arrangement explains the company's biggest strength — it is asset-light and doesn't carry banking risk — and its biggest vulnerability: it does not own the rails it runs on, and the fee it lives on is set by a regulator and split with a partner.

Who buys, and how Zaggle reaches the spender

  • The corporate finance / HR team — the actual customer. It buys Zaggle to cut leakage, automate reimbursements, run employee benefits and pay vendors faster. Zaggle counts roughly 3,900 such corporate customers as of FY26.
  • The employee / channel partner — the end user who never signs the contract but holds the card, uses the app, or redeems the reward. Zaggle reports over 5 crore cards issued and around 22.7 lakh users across its platforms.
  • The bank & card network — not a customer, but the indispensable partner. The bank issues the card on its PPI/credit licence; the network (Visa, Mastercard, RuPay) carries the transaction. Zaggle's Program Fee is a slice of the interchange they generate. More on this in sections 02 and 05 — it is the crux of the model.
02

The three revenue lines: how Zaggle actually earns

The single most important thing to understand — three streams with utterly different economics, bundled under one revenue number.

Most people assume a "SaaS company" earns a subscription fee and that is that. Zaggle is not that company. It earns from three distinct streams, each with its own driver, its own margin, and its own risk. Get these three straight and the rest of the business — including why its revenue and its profit tell different stories — falls into place.

The same platform earns three different kinds of money. The middle one is the biggest in revenue and the smallest in profit — which is the whole optics problem of section 03.

Stream 1 — Program Fee: the interchange engine

When an employee spends on a Zaggle-powered prepaid or corporate card, the merchant's payment system pays an interchange fee — roughly 1.7-1.8% of the amount. That fee is collected by the issuing bank and shared with Zaggle, on something like an 80/20 split in Zaggle's favour on the relevant portion. This is the Program Fee, and it is the company's real workhorse: it scales directly with the spend flowing across Zaggle's cards, it carries a healthy margin, and it is reported on a net basis (only Zaggle's share hits revenue). In FY26 it was roughly ₹752 Cr — around 39% of revenue but a much larger share of gross profit.

Stream 2 — Propel: rewards, loyalty and gift cards

Propel is Zaggle's rewards platform: companies use it to hand out incentive points and gift cards to employees, dealers and channel partners, who then redeem them. Here is the twist that governs Zaggle's accounts — Propel revenue is recognised on redemption of reward points, on a gross basis. That means when a company buys ₹100 of gift-card value through Propel, close to the full ₹100 can pass through Zaggle's revenue line, even though Zaggle keeps only a sliver as its own margin. Propel is the largest revenue line — about ₹1,107 Cr, roughly 58% of FY26 revenue — and grew a rapid ~43% in the year. But its margin is thin, historically as low as ~3% in some quarters (it can spike when high-commission products or overriding commissions land). It adds a lot of revenue and comparatively little profit.

Stream 3 — Platform Fee: the SaaS subscription

The Platform Fee is what most people picture when they hear "SaaS": a subscription, typically per user per month, that corporates pay to use Zaggle's software — Save (expense management and benefits), Zoyer (an AI-led accounts-payable and spend engine), and the broader EMS/employee-benefits suite. In rupees it is the smallest line — about ₹48 Cr in FY26, ~2.5% of revenue — but it is the highest quality: recurring, high gross margin, and sticky once a finance team builds its workflows around it. Management is trying to grow this line fastest because it is the one that most improves earnings quality.

The mental model to lock in: rank by revenue, then rank by margin — they invert

Zaggle's three streams line up in almost the opposite order depending on whether you count revenue or profit. By revenue: Propel > Program Fee > Platform Fee. By margin: Platform Fee > Program Fee > Propel. The biggest revenue line is the least profitable; the smallest is the most profitable. That inversion is the single most useful thing to hold in your head about this company, because it means the headline growth rate and the profit growth rate are driven by different engines — and it is exactly why Zaggle's revenue can grow 46% while the quality of that growth depends entirely on which stream did the growing.

03

The Propel optics: why the top line overstates the business

The one accounting reality that trips up every first-time reader of Zaggle's numbers — treated straight, no spin.

If you value Zaggle on revenue, you will get it wrong — and not by a little. More than half of what Zaggle reports as "revenue" is Propel, a rewards line booked on a gross basis at a very thin margin. A large chunk of that reported revenue is, in economic substance, value passing through Zaggle to reward recipients, not value Zaggle keeps. Understanding this one point separates people who understand the company from people who have merely read its press release.

The mechanics are simple once stated. Under accounting rules, when a company acts as the principal in a transaction — taking on the value and risk — it books the full amount as revenue (gross). When it acts as an agent — merely facilitating — it books only its commission (net). Zaggle books Propel gross (the reward-point face value flows through) and Program Fee net (only its interchange share). So the same ₹100 of underlying activity shows up as ~₹100 of revenue in Propel but only ~₹1.75 of revenue in the Program Fee line. That is why Propel is 58% of revenue and a far smaller share of gross profit.

This is not an accounting trick and Zaggle is not hiding it — the gross treatment is legitimate and disclosed. But it does mean two practical things for anyone reading the company. First, revenue growth flatters the picture: a big Propel quarter can lift the top line impressively while barely moving profit. Second, the right way to size Zaggle is by gross profit, or by Program Fee + Platform Fee, not by revenue. A reader who benchmarks Zaggle's price-to-sales against a normal SaaS company — where nearly all revenue is high-margin subscription — is comparing two different kinds of "revenue" and will conclude Zaggle looks cheap when it may not be.

The clear-eyed way to read Zaggle's scale

Take the reported revenue and mentally split it in two. The Propel half is high-volume, low-margin rewards throughput — real business, but closer to a distribution/pass-through activity than to software. The other half — interchange-linked Program Fee plus the small SaaS Platform Fee — is where the durable economics live. When you hear "Zaggle grew revenue 46%," the useful follow-up question is always: which half grew, and what did it do to the margin? In FY26 the answer was flattering — profit outgrew revenue as operating leverage kicked in. In Q1 FY27 it was not — revenue grew ~27.5% but profit fell, because costs ran ahead of the higher-quality revenue. The top line alone will never tell you which of those two worlds you are in.

The company, the founders, and the ownership

A founder-led fintech, thirteen years old, only recently public.

Zaggle is a founder-built company that spent most of its life private and scaling before a 2023 listing put it on the public market. Understanding its short public history — and its very recent, acquisition-heavy expansion — matters, because the track record investors get to judge is genuinely brief.

Ownership & leadership

  • Founder & Executive Chairman: Raj Narayanam — the promoter who built the company from 2011 and remains its largest individual shareholder and strategic anchor.
  • MD & CEO: Avinash Godkhindi — runs the operating business and is the public face on earnings calls, guiding investors on growth and margin.
  • Public since 2023: the company has a broad institutional and retail float built up since the IPO. As a recent listing, its shareholding pattern and promoter stake move with each capital raise and acquisition-linked issuance — investors should check the latest filing for the exact promoter percentage rather than assume it.
  • Young public track record: unlike a decades-listed company, Zaggle offers only a few years of audited public numbers. Its rapid growth is real, but it has not yet been tested by a full down-cycle in interchange, corporate spending, or fintech regulation as a listed entity — a point the bear case rightly presses.
Why the short history cuts both ways: a founder still at the helm of a fast-growing category leader is a genuine positive — aligned, motivated, and building a real platform. But a brief public record, an aggressive acquisition cadence, and gross-booked revenue optics mean investors are underwriting a story that is still being proven quarter by quarter. There is less history here to lean on than the headline growth suggests, and that is a fact to price, not to wish away.
05

The bank & PPI dependency: the fee a regulator controls

The structural exposure that management itself has called the biggest risk — read it straight.

Here is the fact that should sit at the centre of any Zaggle assessment: the company's most profitable revenue line, the Program Fee, is a share of interchange — and interchange is set by regulation and card networks, collected by a partner bank, and only then shared with Zaggle. Zaggle controls none of the three levers that set it. Management has, in past disclosures, described interchange exposure in stark terms — as effectively the biggest risk the company carries.

The dependency runs two ways at once. On the bank side, Zaggle needs an RBI-licensed partner to issue the cards; if a partner bank exits the business, tightens terms, or is itself restricted by the RBI (as has happened to fintech-partner banks in India), Zaggle's programs on that bank are disrupted. On the fee side, interchange rates are shaped by RBI policy and network rules. The RBI has already capped interchange on debit-card transactions; extending similar caps to prepaid instruments would hit the Program Fee directly. Because a meaningful share of Zaggle's high-margin revenue is interchange-linked, a regulatory move on that fee is not a minor input — it is a strike at the profit engine.

The dependency to take seriously, not to explain away

Being asset-light is genuinely attractive — Zaggle carries no banking licence, no deposit risk, no card-issuance capital, and can scale programs across many banks. But the flip side is real and should not be softened: the fee that funds its best margins is set above its head and split with a partner it does not control. A prepaid-interchange cap, a partner bank losing RBI favour, or a tightening of PPI rules would each land on the profit line quickly, and Zaggle's defence against any of them is limited to diversifying banks and pushing harder into subscription SaaS that does not depend on interchange at all. That pivot toward the Platform Fee is not merely a growth strategy — it is, in part, insurance against the day the interchange rules change.

Concentration to watch: because Zaggle's programs run through a limited set of partner banks, both bank-partner concentration and interchange regulation are single points of failure that a normal software company never faces. Track how many banking partners Zaggle runs live programs with, and any RBI signalling on prepaid interchange — those two items move the high-margin part of the business more than any sales number.
06

Unit economics & margin: thin blend, real leverage

Why the margin looks low, why it is quietly improving, and why one quarter just went the other way.

Zaggle's blended margin — around 10% adjusted EBITDA — looks thin for something called a SaaS company, and section 03 already told you why: the gross-booked Propel line drags the average down even though the SaaS and interchange lines are far more profitable underneath. The interesting question is not the level but the direction: is the margin structurally rising as the mix improves, and how fragile is that trend?

FY26 answered the first part encouragingly. For the first time the company showed clear operating leverage: adjusted EBITDA grew ~51% and PAT ~52% against revenue growth of ~46% — profit outpacing revenue is the signature of a business whose costs grow slower than its sales. Management guided FY26 to a 35-40% topline and a 10-11% EBITDA-margin band, and broadly delivered. The lever is mix: as higher-margin Program Fee and Platform Fee grow, and as scale spreads fixed costs, the blended margin should drift up even with Propel in the mix.

Then Q1 FY27 (June 2026) showed the fragility. Revenue still grew a solid ~27.5% to ~₹423 Cr, but that was a deceleration from the 40-50% investors had grown used to — and profit went the wrong way: PAT fell ~33% and the adjusted EBITDA margin compressed to 8.2% from 10.1% a year earlier. The cause was specific and mostly self-inflicted timing: costs from the Dice acquisition (and other investments and employee increments) landed in the quarter before the corresponding revenue showed up. It is a reminder that a fast-growing, acquisition-hungry company does not deliver a smooth margin line — integration costs arrive first, benefits later.

How to read Zaggle's margin without being fooled either way

Two truths sit side by side. Structurally, the mix shift toward Program Fee and SaaS should lift the blended margin over time, and FY26's operating leverage is evidence it can. But quarter to quarter, the margin will be lumpy — acquisitions front-load costs, Propel's mix weight swings the blend, and Q1 is seasonally the weakest quarter. So neither a great quarter nor a poor one should be extrapolated. The fair read is a business with a genuinely improving trend in earnings quality, delivered through a noisy quarterly line. Judge it over a full year, against the 10-11% margin band management has set for itself, not on any single print.

07

The moat question: is Zaggle actually defensible?

Where the durable advantage is real, where it is thin, and who Zaggle is up against.

Zaggle sells into a crowded, contested space — corporate spend and expense management is a category global and Indian players all want. The fair question is whether Zaggle has any lasting edge, or whether it is a fast-growing distributor of other people's rails that a better-funded rival could displace. The straight answer is: some genuine stickiness, wrapped around dependencies it does not own.

Where is the moat real? In three places. Switching cost — once a finance team wires its reimbursement, approval and reconciliation workflows into Save and Zoyer, ripping them out is painful, which is why the SaaS Platform Fee is the stickiest line. Breadth — few rivals cover expense management, corporate cards, vendor payments and rewards in one suite, and that bundle raises the cost of replacing Zaggle piecemeal. And the installed base and integrations — ~3,900 corporates, deep links into ERPs/HRMS, and India-specific tax and compliance plumbing acquired through deals like TaxSpanner, which a new entrant must rebuild.

The defensibility to hold in mind — a moat around a rented castle

Zaggle's stickiness is genuine at the software layer: workflow lock-in, a broad bundle, and an installed corporate base are real defences that compound as the SaaS mix grows. But two caveats keep it from being a fortress. The revenue that lock-in best protects — the Platform Fee — is still the smallest line; the big lines (Propel, Program Fee) are more contestable and, in the interchange case, dependent on partners and a regulator. So the moat is real but narrow, and it is deepest exactly where the revenue is thinnest today. The investment question is whether Zaggle can keep widening the sticky SaaS layer faster than the contestable, dependency-laden lines can be disrupted.

08

The acquisition engine: buying the stack

How Zaggle is assembling a "spend-as-a-service" platform through deals — and the risk that carries.

Much of Zaggle's recent growth and much of its recent margin noise comes from the same source: a fast run of acquisitions. Rather than build every capability, Zaggle has been buying adjacent products and folding them into its platform — a strategy that widens the offering quickly but front-loads integration cost and stretches management. It is central to both the growth story and the near-term margin wobble.

The acquisition strategy, judged fairly

Buying the stack is a legitimate way to build a platform fast, and each deal here has a clear rationale. But an acquisition-led model carries costs that a first-time reader should weigh honestly: integration expense lands before the revenue (exactly what dented Q1 FY27), goodwill and intangibles build on the balance sheet, reported profit is flattered by adding "cash PAT" back for acquisition amortisation, and management bandwidth is spread across many moving parts. None of this is disqualifying — it is the normal price of inorganic growth — but it means Zaggle's clean-looking growth rate is partly bought, and the quality of that growth turns on execution investors cannot yet fully verify. Watch whether acquired revenue converts to margin on the timeline management promises.

09

Financials & valuation

A fast grower on a full multiple — the question is durability of both growth and margin.

FY26 was, by the company's own account, its strongest year: revenue up ~46%, adjusted EBITDA up ~51%, PAT up ~52%, with operating leverage finally visible. Unlike a cheap cyclical, Zaggle is not priced for disappointment — it trades on a growth-fintech multiple of roughly 21-22× earnings, which requires the fast growth to persist. The debate is whether FY26's momentum and improving mix are the new normal, or whether the Q1 FY27 margin dip is a preview of a bumpier ride than the multiple assumes.

The one lens that settles the thesis: gross-profit growth, not revenue growth

At ~21-22× earnings, Zaggle needs to keep compounding to justify its price — a re-rating is not the base case; delivery is. So the metric that proves or breaks the story is growth in the higher-quality revenue — Program Fee plus Platform Fee — and the blended margin trend that comes with it, read together. If the sticky SaaS and interchange lines keep growing faster than Propel and the margin drifts toward the top of the 10-11% band, the multiple is earned and earnings compound. If growth keeps decelerating (as Q1 FY27 hinted) while acquisition costs and interchange pressure weigh on margin, a growth multiple on a Propel-inflated top line will look expensive quickly. Revenue tells you the volume; gross profit and margin tell you whether the volume is worth owning.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A fast-growing platform still proving its margin and its independence from the rails it rents.

Zaggle enters FY27 as a category leader in Indian corporate spend management, with a broadening product suite, a growing corporate base, and management guidance for continued 35-40% growth at a 10-11% margin. What it must still prove is that the growth stays fast, the margin trend keeps rising through the acquisition noise, and the interchange rug is not pulled by a regulator. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Fast, category-leading growth — a rare listed pure-play in a large, under-penetrated spend-management market, compounding revenue in the 40-50% range with a stated 35-40% runway.
  • Operating leverage is showing — FY26 profit outgrew revenue; as Program Fee and SaaS mix rise, the blended margin should structurally improve.
  • Sticky, broadening platform — workflow lock-in in Save/Zoyer plus a widening suite (tax, payments, rewards) raise switching costs and revenue per customer.
  • Asset-light and scalable — no banking licence, no balance-sheet card risk; programs scale across many banks and corporates with little capital.
  • Cross-sell optionality — ~3,900 corporates and 22.7 lakh users are a base the acquisitions are built to monetise further.

The bear case

  • Interchange & PPI regulation — the highest-margin revenue depends on a fee the RBI can cap (it already capped debit interchange); a prepaid cap would hit profit directly.
  • Bank-partner concentration — programs ride on a limited set of partner banks; a partner exit or RBI restriction disrupts revenue Zaggle cannot replace overnight.
  • Propel optics — over half of revenue is low-margin, gross-booked rewards; the top line overstates the real business and can flatter growth.
  • Margin lumpiness & acquisition risk — Q1 FY27 showed profit falling as Dice costs landed early; inorganic growth front-loads cost and builds intangibles.
  • Full multiple, short record — ~21-22× earnings on a company public only since 2023, not yet tested through a down-cycle in spending or fintech regulation.
The bottom line

Zaggle is a fast-growing, founder-led platform sitting in a real and expanding category, with a genuinely sticky software core, an asset-light model, and its best-ever year just behind it. The complications are equally real and deserve to be stated plainly: its headline revenue is inflated by a low-margin rewards line, its most profitable fee is set by a regulator and shared with a bank it does not control, its margin is lumpy because it grows partly by acquisition, and it carries a full multiple on a short public record. The next test is concrete and datable: whether the higher-quality Program Fee and Platform Fee keep growing faster than Propel through FY27, and whether the blended margin climbs back toward the 10-11% band once the Dice integration costs wash through. Deliver that, and the growth multiple is defensible; miss it, and a Propel-inflated top line at 21-22× earnings has room to disappoint.
