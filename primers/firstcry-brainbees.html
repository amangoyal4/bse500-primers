FirstCry, the internet company that built 1,000 shops

A complete walk through Brainbees Solutions — the Pune company behind FirstCry, India's dominant kids-and-baby retailer. How a website for nervous new parents grew into an omnichannel platform with its own factories' worth of house brands and a franchise store estate across small-town India, why its profit comes from making nappies rather than shipping other people's, and what has to happen for a business that still posts a loss to finally keep the money it makes.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Brainbees Solutions trades under the name of its famous consumer brand, FirstCry. It sells everything a baby or a small child needs — nappies, formula and feeding kit, clothes, shoes, prams and car seats, toys, school supplies, skincare — and it sells them through three doors at once: a specialist website and app, a network of well over a thousand physical FirstCry stores spread across Indian towns and cities, and a growing shelf of its own manufactured brands (BabyHug, Pine Kids, CuteWalk, Babyoye) that sit inside both of those channels. Around that core sit two more businesses: GlobalBees, a portfolio of acquired direct-to-consumer brands, and an international arm concentrated in the Middle East, plus a small preschool chain.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is an internet company that chose bricks. FirstCry began in 2010 as an online store. It now runs ~1,100-plus modern FirstCry stores (approx.), most of them franchisee-owned, in cities where a young mother would rather feel the fabric of a romper than trust a thumbnail. Very few Indian e-commerce natives went this way; FirstCry went early and went hard, and the store estate is now inseparable from the business.
  • Its profit engine is manufacturing, not retailing. House brands — products FirstCry designs, sources and owns outright — have climbed from about 37% of India multi-channel GMV in FY20 to over 58% in FY26. Those goods earn a full product margin instead of a thin marketplace commission. That single mix shift, more than any GMV headline, is what converts scale into money here.
  • It grows, it generates cash, and it still reports a loss. FY26 revenue rose ~12% to ₹8,548 Cr; adjusted EBITDA rose ~24% to ₹486 Cr; the net loss narrowed ~23% to about ₹204 Cr, with cash profit after tax of ~₹312 Cr (+49%). India multi-channel is profitable on its own; the loss lives in what is being built around it. At a market value near ₹11,100 Cr — roughly 1.3× sales — the market is paying for a platform that has not yet proved it can report a profit.
In plain terms

A parent acquires FirstCry at almost exactly the same moment FirstCry acquires them — somewhere between a positive pregnancy test and a first-birthday party. From there the relationship runs for years, through nappy sizes, shoe sizes, school bags and birthday-gift emergencies. FirstCry's entire economic design is about owning that window: get the parent early, be present both on the phone and in a shop two kilometres away, and then quietly sell them your own vest, your own nappy, your own pram — because each of those carries several times the margin of listing someone else's. The business is not really "an e-commerce company". It is a life-stage franchise with a manufacturing habit.

01

The kids-and-baby category: an odd, unusually good market

Why this corner of retail behaves differently from every other — and why a specialist can survive next to Amazon.

Most retail categories are won on price and delivery speed. Baby and kids' products are won on something messier: trust, sizing chaos and repeat frequency. Understanding the four quirks below is most of what you need to understand why a dedicated kids' retailer exists at all in a country where Amazon and Flipkart sell the same nappies.

Four things that make kids' retail its own animal
QuirkWhat it means (and why it matters here)
The customer keeps outgrowing the productA child changes clothing size several times a year, moves through nappy sizes in months, and cycles through toys by developmental stage. Nothing lasts. That forces a purchase cadence far above normal apparel — a customer who buys shoes once a year buys children's shoes three or four times.
Anxiety is the buying emotionParents buying for an infant are risk-averse in a way they never are for themselves. Counterfeit or low-quality goods are a genuine fear in categories touching skin, mouths and safety. A specialist that curates and stands behind assortment earns trust as a moat, not just convenience.
One life event starts the whole relationshipPregnancy and birth are among the few moments when a household's shopping pattern changes overnight, permanently, and predictably. The retailer that is present at that moment inherits years of demand, not one transaction.
Touch matters more than usualFabric softness, pram sturdiness, car-seat fit, shoe width for a toddler who cannot tell you it pinches — these are physical decisions. It is why a category born online kept running back to the high street.

Layer on India's specifics. The kids' and baby market here is large but still overwhelmingly unorganised — small local shops, unbranded clothing, regional distributors. Formal, branded, organised retail penetration remains low, which means an organised player's growth comes less from stealing share off Amazon and more from converting the neighbourhood shop's customer. Meanwhile Indian parents spend a disproportionate share of income on children relative to their own consumption, and the gifting culture around births, first birthdays and festivals adds an extra layer of demand that is not purely need-driven.

The demand curve that a general marketplace cannot copy

Amazon can sell you a nappy. What it struggles to do is know that your child turned fourteen months old last week, that the last pack you bought was size M, that you are therefore about to need size L, and that in six weeks you will be shopping for a first-birthday outfit. FirstCry's entire dataset is organised around the child's age, not the shopper's browsing history — the platform effectively runs a calendar for every customer's child. In a category where the correct product changes every few months, knowing the child's date of birth is more commercially useful than knowing what the parent clicked on yesterday.

The uncomfortable backdrop: fewer babies

One structural fact sits underneath every optimistic slide about this category. India's total fertility rate has fallen to roughly 2.0 or a little below (approx.) — at or under the replacement level of 2.1 — and annual births have been drifting down for years. The number of new customers entering the funnel each year is, on current trends, a slowly shrinking pool. This does not sink the business: spending per child is rising far faster than birth counts are falling, as smaller families spend more on each child and as unorganised spend shifts to branded retail. But it does mean the tailwind here is premiumisation and formalisation, not demographics. Any model that assumes an ever-growing cohort of newborns is building on sand.

02

Engine 1 — the online marketplace and the app

Where FirstCry started, and still the front door for most customers.

FirstCry.com launched in 2010 as a specialist destination: one place carrying essentially everything for a child from birth to about twelve, instead of a baby aisle bolted onto a general store. That range remains the core proposition — a very deep catalogue spanning many thousands of products across hundreds of third-party brands, plus its own labels, organised by the child's age and stage rather than by department.

Commercially, the online business runs on two different models sitting side by side, and the difference matters enormously for the P&L:

  • Inventory-led retail — FirstCry buys the goods, holds them in its warehouses, and sells them. It books the full sale price as revenue and earns the retail spread. This is how most of the business, and essentially all of the house-brand business, works.
  • Marketplace — third-party sellers list on the platform and FirstCry takes a commission plus fulfilment fees. Revenue booked is only the commission, but the capital intensity is near zero and the range expands without tying up cash in stock.

The gap between "GMV" and "revenue" in this company is largely that distinction, and it is the single most common source of confusion for a first-time reader of the results. GMV (gross merchandise value) is the total value of goods sold across the platform — India multi-channel GMV crossed ~₹9,783 Cr (over $1 bn) in FY26. Revenue for the same India multi-channel business was ₹5,753 Cr. The difference is not a mistake; it is marketplace sales where only the commission flows through the income statement, plus taxes and discounts.

GMV vs revenue, once and for all

If a third-party seller sells a ₹1,000 pram on FirstCry, GMV goes up by ₹1,000 but revenue might rise by ₹150. If FirstCry sells a ₹1,000 BabyHug pram it bought for ₹550, GMV goes up ₹1,000 and revenue goes up ₹1,000, with ₹450 of gross profit. Both transactions look identical to the shopper. To the shareholder they are entirely different animals — which is exactly why management talks about house-brand share of GMV as the headline operating metric rather than GMV growth alone. Watching GMV without watching the mix inside it tells you almost nothing about whether this company is getting better.

The delivery-speed pivot

Through FY26 FirstCry made a visible strategic turn toward faster fulfilment and better margins rather than raw GMV expansion — pushing same-day and next-day delivery in major cities, tightening the assortment toward higher-margin lines, and leaning on the store network as forward stocking points. The trigger was not ambition; it was defence. Quick-commerce apps had begun taking exactly the purchases that are most valuable to a baby retailer: the emergency 10 pm nappy pack, the run-out-of-formula moment, the wipes. Those are high-frequency, habit-forming, and — critically — the transactions that keep a parent opening the FirstCry app at all. Section 10 returns to this fight.

03

Engine 2 — the store estate nobody expected

Over a thousand physical shops, mostly other people's money, run by an internet company.

Here is where FirstCry stops resembling any of its peers. Starting barely a couple of years after launch, it began opening physical FirstCry stores — typically a few thousand square feet, stocked across the full age range, sitting in high streets and malls in cities large and small. Today the estate runs to roughly 1,100-plus modern stores (approx.) across a large majority of Indian states and union territories, supplemented by distribution into thousands of general-trade outlets.

The reflex reaction from anyone raised on e-commerce orthodoxy is that this must be a drag: stores mean leases, staff, inventory sitting still, and fixed costs that do not scale the way servers do. That reaction misses four things about this specific category.

The franchise arithmetic

A franchised store is a curious hybrid for a listed company. FirstCry books revenue when it ships goods to the franchisee, so store growth shows up as wholesale-like volume; the franchisee then carries the retail risk and takes the retail spread. That means the estate can expand without FirstCry writing large cheques for leases and fit-outs — but it also means FirstCry's reported revenue per store is not a shop's till receipts, and that a struggling franchisee's unsold stock is a problem that eventually travels back up the chain. The metric worth tracking is not store count but store productivity — like-for-like throughput per shop — because a chain that keeps adding outlets while each one sells less is manufacturing growth out of geography rather than demand.

A genuine surprise: the standard playbook for an internet retailer facing a physical incumbent is to undercut it and wait for the leases to kill it. FirstCry did the reverse — it went and signed the leases itself, via franchisees, while it was still a venture-funded startup. In a category where the buyer is a first-time parent who wants to hold the thing before buying it, the shop is not legacy baggage; it is a customer-acquisition channel that also happens to sell things. The company that "should" have been disrupted by e-commerce turned out to be the one FirstCry had to become.

Engine 3 — the house brands, where the money actually is

The mix shift from 37% to 58% of India GMV is the most important number in this primer.

Strip away the app, the shops and the logistics, and FirstCry's economics reduce to one question: whose product is in the bag? If it is Pampers or Johnson's or a third-party seller's dress, FirstCry earns a retail spread or a commission — real money, but thin, and permanently exposed to whoever will sell it cheaper. If it is BabyHug or Pine Kids or CuteWalk or Babyoye — brands FirstCry owns, designs and has manufactured to its specification — it captures the entire gap between factory cost and shelf price.

More than half of everything sold through FirstCry's India channels is now FirstCry's own product. Six years ago it was barely a third. Each point of shift adds gross margin without needing a single extra customer.

The house-brand portfolio is not one label but a shelf of them, deliberately segmented so they do not cannibalise each other:

  • BabyHug — the flagship, spanning infant and toddler clothing, nursery and hard goods (cots, prams, walkers, feeding). The broadest and best-known of the house labels.
  • Pine Kids — everyday kidswear for the older end of the age range, where volume and repeat purchase are highest.
  • CuteWalk — footwear, a category where fit anxiety makes the physical store especially valuable.
  • Babyoye — a brand acquired with the Mahindra baby-retail business in 2016 and folded into the house stable.
  • Plus adjacent own-label lines in nappies, wipes, skincare, toys and school supplies — the consumable, high-frequency end where a house brand quietly displaces a multinational's product in the basket.
Why a house brand is worth several times a marketplace commission

Take a ₹500 baby vest. Sold as a third-party listing, FirstCry might keep a commission plus a fulfilment fee — call it ₹60–₹90, and it never controls the price. Sold as a BabyHug vest sourced for perhaps ₹250, FirstCry keeps the whole ₹250 spread before its own costs. Same shelf, same shopper, same delivery van; roughly three times the gross profit. Multiply that across a basket a parent refills every few months for six years, and you can see why management treats the house-brand share of GMV as the number that defines the company. It is also why the group can post a 36% gross margin — a level a pure marketplace never reaches and a pure reseller rarely does.

There is a second, less obvious benefit. In a fragmented supply base full of small manufacturers and inconsistent quality, owning the brand means owning the specification — fabric, dyes, safety standards, sizing consistency. For a customer whose central fear is that something touching her infant is unsafe, that control is the product. The margin and the trust come from the same decision.

The pressure point: FY26 gross margin was ~36.2%, slightly below FY25's ~37.4%. Rising house-brand share should push gross margin up; it went mildly down. That gap is competitive intensity — discounting to hold customers against quick commerce and general marketplaces — and it is the first place to look each quarter for evidence that the mix story is or isn't translating into money.

Engine 4 — GlobalBees, the brand roll-up

A separate business bolted on: buying digital consumer brands and scaling them.

In 2021 FirstCry launched GlobalBees, a venture with an entirely different logic from the rest of the group. Rather than build brands, GlobalBees buys them — acquiring majority stakes in small, fast-growing direct-to-consumer companies across personal care, nutrition, home and lifestyle, then plugging them into shared infrastructure: sourcing, marketing muscle, marketplace management, logistics and capital. The model was fashionable globally at the time (the American original, Thrasio, was the template) and GlobalBees assembled a portfolio running to twenty-plus brands (approx.).

In FY26 GlobalBees delivered the fastest growth in the group: revenue up ~20% to ₹1,894 Cr, with the company flagging ~28% growth in its core categories and improved profitability. On the numbers, it is now roughly a fifth of consolidated revenue — no longer a side project.

What a roll-up promises, and what it risks

The promise is arithmetic: a founder-run D2C brand doing ₹40 Cr of sales is spending far too much per rupee on warehousing, ad buying and marketplace management. Put twenty of those under one roof and the shared costs collapse, the buying power rises, and each brand's margin should improve without changing a single product. The risk is that this arithmetic only works if the acquirer can genuinely operate twenty different consumer businesses at once — different customers, different seasons, different supply chains — and if the prices paid during the 2021 funding boom bear some relation to what the brands are worth now. Roll-ups fail in a specific and visible way: the acquired brands stall, and the goodwill on the balance sheet has to be written down. Every quarter GlobalBees grows profitably is a quarter that question recedes; a stall would bring it straight back.

It is worth being clear that GlobalBees is not a kids-and-baby business. It sits inside a group whose entire competitive advantage is life-stage specialism, selling ayurvedic personal care and nutrition to adults. The strategic rationale is shared infrastructure and a second growth engine; the strategic cost is that management attention and capital are being spent outside the moat. Whether that was the right trade is one of the genuinely unresolved questions in this story, and the market's scepticism about it is visible in the valuation.

06

Engine 5 — the Gulf, and a chain of preschools

Two smaller bets, one of which is quietly strategic.

FirstCry runs a genuine international business, concentrated in the UAE and the wider Middle East, combining online retail with physical stores. FY26 revenue there rose ~10% to ₹947 Cr — around 11% of the group — and the more meaningful development was that international adjusted EBITDA losses narrowed by roughly 35% over the year as the business shifted from land-grab to discipline.

The logic of the Gulf is not random geographic ambition. It is a market with a very large South Asian expatriate population already familiar with the FirstCry brand, high per-child spending, a young demographic profile, and a retail structure where a specialist kids' chain can win shelf space. It is, in effect, the India model transplanted to a wealthier customer — and because the house brands travel with it, the margin structure travels too.

Preschools and education

The group also runs FirstCry Intellitots preschools and daycare, built on the 2019 acquisition of the Intellitots chain, operating a network of centres largely on a franchise model. In revenue terms it is small enough to be a rounding error. In strategic terms it is more interesting than its size suggests: a preschool is where a parent physically arrives every single weekday for two or three years, at exactly the age band where FirstCry wants to keep selling. It is a retention asset dressed as an education business.

Five businesses, one customer

Read separately, these look like a conglomerate assembled by an acquisitive founder: a website, a store chain, a garment label, a brand roll-up, a Gulf retailer, a preschool network. Read as a whole, four of the five point at the same person — a parent of a young child — and try to be present at more of the moments in that parent's week. Only GlobalBees genuinely steps outside. That is a useful lens for judging any future capital allocation the company announces: does it deepen the hold on the parent, or does it merely add revenue?

07

The cohort ledger: how one parent pays for everything

The single most important idea in this primer, and it does not transfer to any other company in the library.

Retailers are usually judged on same-store sales or GMV growth. Neither captures what FirstCry actually is. This business acquires a customer at a fixed, predictable, once-in-a-lifetime moment — the arrival of a child — and then has a closing window of roughly eight to twelve years in which to extract value before that customer ages out of the category permanently and never comes back. No amount of marketing brings them back; the child simply grows up.

So the correct unit of analysis is not a quarter. It is a birth cohort: every parent acquired in a given year, and the rupees of gross profit FirstCry earns from them across the whole window. Set out that way, the business has exactly three levers, and only three.

The three levers, then:

  • How many parents enter the cohort — new-customer additions, which depend on brand reach, store openings into new towns, and (slowly working against it) the national birth count.
  • How much of the window FirstCry holds — retention through the 0–10 journey, which is what the app's age-based personalisation, the physical store two kilometres away, and even the preschool exist to defend.
  • How much of each rupee FirstCry keeps — the house-brand share, now 58% of India GMV, which converts held spending into actual gross profit.
The metric this framing produces: own-brand gross profit per acquired parent

Multiply the three levers and you get the only number that describes FirstCry's economics without flattery — the gross profit, from its own products, that a single acquired parent generates across the eight-to-twelve-year window. GMV growth can be bought with discounts. Store count can be bought with franchise agreements. Revenue can be bought by acquiring D2C brands. But own-brand gross profit per parent can only rise if the customer genuinely stays and genuinely buys FirstCry-made goods — and it is the number that has to clear the fixed cost of a thousand shops, a warehouse network and a national ad budget. Every strategic move the company makes is legible through this lens: the delivery-speed pivot defends lever two, the house-brand push is lever three, the Gulf and the preschools are attempts to widen lever one.

A supermarket can lose a customer and win them back next week. FirstCry loses a customer when their child turns eleven — and there is no winning that one back.

This is also why the quick-commerce threat (section 10) is more serious than its share of spending suggests. Nappies and wipes are low-margin items; losing them to a 10-minute delivery app looks survivable on a gross-profit line. But that repeat purchase is the heartbeat of lever two — it is what keeps a parent opening the app in the 0–2 years, right before the high-margin apparel and footwear years arrive. Lose the nappy and you may not be there for the shoes.

08

The company, the history, and who owns it

A Pune startup, a carmaker on the register, and a listed company with no promoter.

Brainbees Solutions was founded in Pune in 2010 by Supam Maheshwari and Amitava Saha, two entrepreneurs who had previously built and sold a corporate-training business and who started FirstCry after Maheshwari's own frustration sourcing baby products. Maheshwari remains Managing Director and CEO and is the public face of the company. It listed on the NSE and BSE in August 2024.

The share register

  • No promoter. Brainbees is classified as a professionally managed company with no identifiable promoter or promoter group — an unusual status for an Indian listed company and a direct legacy of its venture-capital funding history, where no single family or founder retained control. Governance therefore rests on the board and the institutional register rather than on a controlling shareholder.
  • SoftBank (through its investment vehicle) is the largest shareholder, holding roughly 19–21% (approx.) after the IPO and subsequent sell-downs, reduced from about 25.5% pre-listing.
  • Mahindra & Mahindra holds roughly 10–11% (approx.) — the carmaker sits on a baby retailer's register because it sold BabyOye into FirstCry for stock in 2016 rather than for cash.
  • Premji Invest holds approximately 10% (approx.), alongside other institutional and financial investors.
  • Supam Maheshwari, CEO, holds roughly 5.9% (approx.) post-IPO — meaningful founder skin in the game, but nothing resembling control.
Two structural quirks worth holding on to: first, a company with no promoter behaves differently — there is no controlling family to block a strategic change, and equally no anchor holder whose wealth is tied to the long term. Second, a large slice of the register is early venture money that has already been invested for well over a decade. Pre-IPO investors selling down is a mechanical, ongoing feature of a register like this, and it is worth separating that supply from any judgement about the business itself. Why a growing, cash-generating business still reports a loss

The gap between ₹486 Cr of adjusted EBITDA and a ₹204 Cr net loss, decoded line by line.

The most common question a first-time reader asks about FirstCry is the simplest: it sells ₹8,548 Cr of goods at a 36% gross margin and it still loses money — where does it all go? The answer is not mysterious, and it is worth walking down the income statement slowly, because the shape of the answer determines whether profitability is a matter of time or a matter of doubt.

₹3,096 Cr of gross profit becomes ₹486 Cr of adjusted EBITDA after fulfilment, marketing, store support and corporate overhead — then employee stock charges, depreciation and lease interest take it below zero.

Where the ₹2,600 Cr between gross profit and adjusted EBITDA goes

  • Fulfilment and logistics — warehouses, last-mile delivery, returns, and the cost of stocking a nationwide store network. Bulky, low-value baby goods are expensive to move; a pram is a shipping nightmare relative to its price.
  • Marketing and customer acquisition — a category where you must re-acquire the entire customer base every few years, because your old customers keep ageing out. This is a permanent, structural cost, not a growth-phase indulgence.
  • Store and franchise support — the systems, supply chain, training and brand spend behind ~1,100-plus outlets, plus company-owned store costs where they exist.
  • Corporate and technology — platform engineering, data, and the cost of running five businesses in two geographies.

And then the three items that take adjusted EBITDA to a loss

The word adjusted is doing real work in "adjusted EBITDA ₹486 Cr". Three charges sit below it:

  • Employee stock option (ESOP) charges — non-cash, but a real transfer of value from shareholders to employees. A recently listed, venture-built company carries a heavy ESOP charge for several years after IPO, and it is the largest single reason adjusted and reported profitability diverge.
  • Depreciation and amortisation — including amortisation of intangibles created by acquisitions (GlobalBees brands, Intellitots) and depreciation of store and warehouse assets.
  • Lease accounting (Ind AS 116) — rents on stores and warehouses split into depreciation on a "right-of-use asset" plus a finance cost, which sits below EBITDA. A store-heavy retailer carries a large charge here that a pure online player does not.
Loss-making, but not cash-burning — and that distinction matters

FY26's cash profit after tax of ~₹312 Cr, up ~49%, alongside a reported loss of ~₹204 Cr, tells you most of the gap is non-cash: stock compensation, amortisation and lease depreciation. The India multi-channel business — the core, the shops, the house brands — was PAT-positive and free-cash-flow positive in FY26 on the company's own reporting. What drags the group below the line is the cost of the things being built around that core: international, which is still loss-making though ~35% less so; GlobalBees integration; and the accounting weight of a decade of equity compensation. This is a very different condition from a company burning investor cash to buy revenue. It does not, however, make the losses imaginary — ESOP charges dilute existing owners just as surely as cash leaves a bank account.

The honest framing of the profitability question: the core India business already makes money, so profitability is not a question of whether the model works — it is a question of whether the group stops spending the core's profits on everything else, and how quickly the post-IPO ESOP charge rolls off. Those are timing and capital-allocation questions with observable answers each quarter, not existential ones. But "adjusted EBITDA" is not profit, and a reader should always look at the reported bottom line first.

The competitive ring

Giants above, ten-minute apps below, and a long tail of specialists in between.

FirstCry is attacked from four directions at once, and they threaten different parts of the cohort ledger. Sorting them by which lever they attack is more useful than ranking them by size.

Why quick commerce is the fight that matters

Rank these threats by revenue at risk and Amazon looks biggest. Rank them by damage to the cohort ledger and the ten-minute apps win. A parent of an eight-month-old buys nappies perhaps twice a month; that repetition is what keeps FirstCry present in her week, and presence is what converts into the far more profitable apparel, footwear and gifting purchases in years two to five. If Blinkit owns the nappy run, FirstCry's app becomes an occasional destination rather than a habit — and an occasional destination has to re-win every high-margin sale with advertising. That is the reasoning behind the FY26 pivot to same-day and next-day delivery, and behind using the store network as forward stock. FirstCry cannot win a ten-minute race nationally. It is instead trying to make "fast enough, from a shop nearby, with the full range" a good enough answer.

One asymmetry works in FirstCry's favour and is easy to miss. Quick commerce carries a few hundred of the fastest-moving baby items; a general marketplace carries thousands of listings with no curation. Neither carries a coherent, age-graded, quality-controlled wardrobe. When a parent needs six specific things for a two-year-old before a family trip, the specialist with 1,000-plus stores and a house-brand range is still the path of least resistance. The question is whether that convenience is worth enough to defend a premium — and FY26's slightly softer gross margin is the first real evidence that it is being tested.

The number that settles this thesis

Not GMV. Not store count. Not adjusted EBITDA. The case for FirstCry is proved or broken by India multi-channel revenue growth re-accelerating above its FY26 9% while the house-brand share of India GMV keeps climbing past 58% — and the group's reported bottom line, not the adjusted one, crossing into profit. Those three move together or the story does not work: growth without mix is unprofitable volume; mix without growth is a shrinking business optimising itself; and both without a reported profit means the money is simply being spent elsewhere. A watcher can check all three from the quarterly release in about ninety seconds. The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A dominant specialist with a real moat, asked to prove it can convert dominance into declared profit.

FirstCry enters FY27 holding a genuinely strong position: the leading organised kids-and-baby platform in India, a store estate no rival can replicate quickly, house brands at 58% of India GMV, a profitable and cash-generating core, and net cash on the balance sheet. What it does not hold is a reported profit, an accelerating growth rate, or the market's confidence about where the next rupee of capital will go.

The bull case

  • A moat built from bricks and brands — ~1,100-plus stores plus 58% house-brand GMV is a combination neither a marketplace nor a D2C brand can assemble quickly, in a category where touch and trust decide purchases.
  • The core already works — India multi-channel is PAT- and free-cash-flow-positive; the group loss is the cost of the periphery, not a broken model.
  • Mix has years left to run — house-brand share went 37% → 58% in six years; every further point is gross margin that requires no new customer.
  • Formalisation, not demographics — most Indian kids' spending is still unorganised; converting it is a longer runway than birth counts imply.
  • Cheap relative to its own history — ~1.3× sales, net cash, and improving adjusted profitability at a valuation far below its listing levels.

The bear case

  • Still no reported profit — FY26's ~₹204 Cr loss narrowed but did not disappear; "adjusted EBITDA" flatters a picture that ESOPs, amortisation and lease costs undo.
  • Quick commerce takes the habit — Blinkit, Zepto and Instamart are eating the high-frequency nappy-and-wipes purchases that keep parents in the funnel; FY26's gross-margin dip is the early evidence.
  • GlobalBees is an integration and impairment risk — twenty-plus acquired brands, bought in a very different funding climate, sitting outside the group's core competence and carrying goodwill.
  • A shrinking pool of newborns — India's fertility rate at or below replacement is a slow, permanent headwind for a business whose customers age out and are never replaced by the same household.
  • Discretionary, inventory-heavy, competitive — kidswear and toys are discretionary; own-brand manufacturing means fashion and inventory risk sits on FirstCry's own balance sheet, and Amazon and Flipkart can discount indefinitely.
  • No promoter, patient sellers — no controlling shareholder to anchor strategy, and a register full of long-held venture stakes.

What to watch over the next four quarters

  • India multi-channel revenue growth — does 9% re-accelerate, or does quick commerce keep it there?
  • House-brand share of India GMV — the march past 58% is the margin engine; a stall is the loudest possible warning.
  • Gross margin — recovery toward 37%+ would say pricing power held; further slippage says it did not.
  • Reported profit, not adjusted EBITDA — the quarter the bottom line prints positive is the quarter this becomes a different kind of investment case.
  • Store additions and store productivity — count is easy; throughput per store is the test.
  • GlobalBees and international — continued profitable growth at GlobalBees, and the international loss continuing to shrink from its ~35% FY26 reduction.
The bottom line

FirstCry has already won the hard part. It is the default place a young Indian parent goes for their child's things, it has a physical presence in towns no online-only rival will reach for years, and more than half of what it sells is now something it made itself. The core of that business makes money and generates cash. What remains unresolved is a capital-allocation question, not an existential one: whether the profits of a working India business keep being routed into a Gulf expansion, a twenty-brand roll-up and a decade's worth of stock compensation, or whether they are finally allowed to land on the reported bottom line. The clock on that is not abstract — every birth cohort FirstCry acquires has an eight-to-twelve-year window before it walks out of the category forever. The first quarter this company prints a profit without the word "adjusted" in front of it is the milestone that changes the argument, and the house-brand share of India GMV is the dial that gets it there.
