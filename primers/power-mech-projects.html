00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Power Mech Projects is a Hyderabad-based engineering, construction and services company built around power plants. Its original craft — and still its calling card — is erecting the heavy machinery at the heart of a thermal power station: the boiler, the turbine and the generator (together, "BTG"), plus all the pipes, pumps, ducts and structures around them ("balance of plant"). Over 25-plus years it has erected tens of thousands of megawatts of India's coal and gas fleet. But it does not just build plants — it also runs and maintains them under long-term operations-and-maintenance (O&M) contracts, and it has fanned out into civil construction, pollution-control retrofits, railways, water, roads and, most surprisingly, coal mining.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is two businesses wearing one uniform. One half is lumpy, project-based construction — win a contract, build the thing, book a modest ~12% margin, move on. The other half is a recurring service annuity — once a plant is running, Power Mech is paid year after year to keep it running. The construction book is the growth engine; the O&M book is the steadier ballast. Almost everything about the company makes more sense once you separate the two.
  • The ₹55,151 Cr order book is not what it looks like. That eye-catching backlog is dominated by a single ~₹39,576 Cr mine-operating contract that unwinds over 25 years — a slow annuity, not near-term construction revenue. Strip it out and the genuinely executable engineering-and-services book is closer to ~₹15,900 Cr. Reading the headline as "nine years of revenue booked" is the single most common mistake made about this company.
  • Growth has been about volume, not pricing. Net profit has tripled from ~₹138 Cr (FY22) to ~₹412 Cr (FY26) while the operating margin barely budged, sitting stubbornly around 11–12% the whole way. Power Mech is a throughput machine that takes a thin, fixed slice of an ever-larger pile of work — and the stock, at ~₹8,270 Cr and ~21× earnings, is priced for that pile to keep growing.
In plain terms

Power Mech is the contractor India calls to assemble, and then babysit, a power plant. It bolts together the giant boiler-turbine-generator, wires up everything around it, and hands over a working station — then signs a contract to operate and maintain that station for years. That "build it, then run it" pairing is the spine of the business. What has changed lately is how far it has stretched the idea: it now also retrofits pollution-control kit onto old coal plants, lays railway lines, builds water systems, and — the real curveball — has taken on 25-year contracts to develop and operate entire coal mines. The investment question is whether a firm that earns a modest, fixed margin on execution can keep scaling that work fast enough to justify a growth-stock price.

01

Power-plant services 101: the erect-and-maintain trade

No engineering degree needed — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

A coal or gas power station is one of the most complex machines humans build, and it does not assemble itself. Someone has to take thousands of tonnes of steel components — delivered by the equipment makers — and physically erect them on site into a working plant: raise the boiler, seat the turbine, connect the generator, run the pipework, and commission the whole thing until it feeds power to the grid. Then, for the plant's 25-to-40-year life, someone has to keep it running and repair it. Power Mech lives on both ends of that timeline. To follow the business, you need six terms.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
BTG"Boiler–Turbine–Generator" — the three big machines at a power plant's core. The equipment makers (BHEL, GE, Toshiba, Mitsubishi) manufacture them; erectors like Power Mech assemble and install them on site. Erecting BTG is Power Mech's founding skill.
ETC"Erection, Testing & Commissioning" — the on-site work of putting the machinery together, testing it, and switching it on. A specialised, labour-and-crane-heavy discipline where a good erector saves the plant owner months.
BoP"Balance of Plant" — everything other than the core BTG: pumps, fans, pipes, ducting, cooling towers, coal-handling, ash systems, electricals. Often a bigger scope than the BTG itself, and a natural extension for an erector.
EPC"Engineering, Procurement & Construction" — taking full turnkey responsibility for designing, sourcing and building a project. Higher value and higher risk than pure erection; Power Mech is climbing up into this tier.
O&M"Operations & Maintenance" — the long-term contract to run and service a plant after it is built: keep it available, overhaul it on schedule, fix what breaks. A recurring, annuity-like fee that lasts for years.
FGD"Flue-Gas Desulphurisation" — pollution-control equipment bolted onto a coal plant's exhaust to strip out sulphur dioxide. Newly mandated across India's coal fleet, creating a large retrofit market that an erector is well placed to win.
The economic difference that decides everything: a project vs an annuity

The single most useful distinction in this whole primer is between a project and an annuity. A project — erect a boiler, retrofit an FGD, build a road — pays you once. You mobilise labour and cranes, take execution risk (weather, delays, cost overruns), earn a margin of roughly 11–13%, and when it is done, the revenue stops. An annuity — an O&M contract, a mine-operating contract — pays you a fee every year for the life of the deal, with far less lumpiness. Power Mech runs on both, and the quality of its earnings depends on the mix. A rupee of annuity revenue is worth more than a rupee of project revenue because it recurs, so as the O&M and mining books grow, the character of the company quietly improves even if the headline margin does not.

Who hires a company like Power Mech

  • Equipment makers (OEMs) — BHEL, GE, Toshiba and others sell the boiler-turbine-generator but sub-contract the physical erection. Power Mech has long been their trusted pair of hands on site.
  • Power utilities & PSUs — NTPC, state generation companies, Singareni, Damodar Valley and their peers, who need plants built, retrofitted and maintained.
  • Industrial & private developers — steel, cement, refining and captive-power owners (Adani, SAIL, JSW-type buyers) building or servicing their own generation and infrastructure.
  • Government infrastructure agencies — railways, water boards, road authorities and coal companies awarding the civil and mining work Power Mech has diversified into.
02

What Power Mech actually sells: one plant, many trades

The service stack — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Power Mech's edge is that it can follow a power plant across its entire life — from the day the first crane arrives to the day, decades later, it is still overhauling the turbine. It started at one point on that timeline (erection) and has steadily added the stages on either side. Today the same firm can build the plant, connect everything around it, run it, and clean up its emissions. That single, cradle-to-grave relationship is the product.

The core trades, decoded

  • Erection & ETC-BTG — the founding business. Power Mech has erected boilers, turbines and generators across a very large slice of India's installed thermal capacity — a track record of tens of thousands of megawatts. This is skilled, on-site work: cranes, welders, and precise assembly of machinery that must run at hundreds of degrees and thousands of RPM for decades. Reputation and manpower are the moat.
  • Balance of plant, civil & EPC — the scope expansion. Having proven it can erect the core, Power Mech took on the surrounding balance-of-plant, then heavy civil construction, and increasingly full turnkey EPC packages. Winning the balance-of-plant EPC for a new 800 MW thermal unit is exactly this climb — from sub-contractor to main contractor, where value per project is far higher (and so is the risk).
  • O&M services — the recurring tail. Power Mech operates and maintains power plants across a very large installed base — running the plant, doing scheduled overhauls, guaranteeing availability. Small per plant relative to a big construction contract, but it recurs for years, is far less cyclical, and grows every time the firm adds a plant to its serviced fleet. It also feeds back leads: the team maintaining a plant is first to know when it needs a retrofit or a rebuild.
  • FGD & new verticals — the diversification. Pollution-control retrofits, railways, water, roads and coal mining now sit alongside the power work, widening the addressable market well beyond thermal generation. More on each of these below.

Why "cradle-to-grave" is worth spelling out

An owner building a power plant faces a string of hand-offs — one firm erects the boiler, another does the civil work, a third operates it, a fourth retrofits the emissions kit years later. Every hand-off is a seam where things go wrong and accountability blurs. Power Mech's pitch is that it can hold several of those stages under one roof, so the owner deals with fewer contractors and one accountable partner across the plant's life. The flip side is real: a firm spread across erection, EPC, O&M, FGD, railways, water and mining is juggling a lot of different execution disciplines at once. Breadth wins work and smooths the cycle; it also stretches management and invites the question of whether the company is a specialist or a generalist. The answer Power Mech is betting on is that its power-plant DNA travels — that the project-management muscle built erecting boilers is what lets it run a mine or lay a railway.

A founder-built, promoter-controlled engineering house out of Hyderabad.

Power Mech Projects was founded in 1999 in Hyderabad by S. Kishore Babu, who remains chairman and managing director and the largest individual shareholder. It is very much a founder's company — built up over a quarter-century from power-plant erection contracts into a diversified engineering group, and still tightly held by the promoter family.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter control: the promoter group holds ~58.96% (as of Mar 2026), with founder S. Kishore Babu the largest individual holder (~24%). The company states there is no promoter encumbrance (no pledged shares) — worth confirming in each quarter's shareholding filing, but a healthy sign for a contractor, where pledged-promoter stress has burned investors elsewhere.

The absence of pledged promoter shares and the steady returns suggest disciplined growth so far; the thing to watch is whether the sprawl into new verticals keeps that discipline as the balance sheet gets asked to fund longer-dated, more capital-hungry mining work.
04

The ₹55,000-crore order book — three books in one number

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — and the one most people get wrong.

Every contractor's headline metric is its order book — the value of work already won but not yet done. Power Mech's is huge: about ₹55,151 Cr. Against ~₹6,000 Cr of annual revenue, a naïve reading is "nine years of revenue in the bag." That reading is wrong, and understanding why is the key to the entire company. The order book is not one homogeneous pile of construction work — it is three different books, on three different clocks, jammed into a single number.

Here is the same idea in words. The 25-year mining (MDO) piece — about ₹39,576 Cr — is real revenue, but it releases in thin slices over a quarter-century as coal is actually dug and delivered; it is an annuity, not a construction backlog. The executable engineering-and-services book — around ₹15,900 Cr of erection, BoP, civil, FGD and EPC work — is the part that converts into revenue over the next few years at the company's ~12% margin. And running underneath both is the O&M annuity, a recurring stream that is often not even captured in the "order book" the way a fixed-scope contract is. Blend all three and you get a spectacular headline; un-blend them and you get a clear-eyed view of the business.

The device that only fits Power Mech: three clocks in one order book

Most contractors you can read with a single order-book number and a rough "book-to-bill" ratio. Power Mech runs on three clocks at once, and the headline backlog is a chord of all three: a fast clock (erection, FGD, civil, EPC — one to three years per job), a steady clock (O&M — running for the plant's life), and an ultra-slow clock (the 25-year MDO mining concessions). Value the company by adding one ₹55,151 Cr number to a revenue line and you will badly mis-time it. Value it properly and you track three things separately: how fast the ~₹15,900 Cr executable book converts at ~12% margin, how much recurring O&M revenue compounds, and whether the slow mining annuity actually starts producing coal — and cash — rather than sitting as a paper backlog. The order book is a chord; you have to hear the individual notes.

05

The O&M annuity: the quiet, steadier half

The recurring business that grows every time another plant joins the fleet.

Underneath the lumpy construction contracts runs a calmer stream: operations & maintenance. Once a power plant is built, someone has to run it every day and overhaul it on schedule for decades — and Power Mech does exactly that across a very large installed base of generating capacity. It is the least glamorous part of the story and, arguably, the most valuable.

Here is the mechanism that makes O&M worth so much more than its size suggests. An erection contract is a one-time event: you win it, execute it, book the revenue, and it is gone. But an O&M contract on that same plant pays a fee every year, often for a decade or more, with predictable margins and none of the win-or-lose lumpiness of chasing new projects. So each year of strong construction activity doesn't just add a one-off sale — it can permanently thicken the recurring book, because a plant that Power Mech helped build is a natural plant for Power Mech to maintain. Over time, a large serviced fleet becomes a high-visibility earnings base that keeps paying through the cycle.

Why the annuity is the calmer, higher-quality value

Construction is feast-or-famine: it booms when capex flows and goes quiet when tenders dry up, and a contractor over-exposed to it swings violently with the cycle. O&M does not behave that way — a plant built in a boom still needs operating and servicing through the following bust. That is the ballast in Power Mech's earnings: a slice of revenue that shows up whether or not new orders are being won this quarter, at steady margins, from a fleet that only tends to grow. For an investor, the quiet compounding of the O&M book is what separates Power Mech from a pure boom-bust construction play — and it is precisely the part the headline order book under-states, because a rolling service contract doesn't sit in a backlog the way a fixed-scope build does.

The nuance: O&M is steadier, but it is also lower-ticket than a big EPC or mining award, so it will not drive the headline growth that excites the market. Its job in the story is different — it de-risks the earnings and improves their quality, quietly, in the background. When you weigh Power Mech, treat the O&M base as the reason the company is more resilient than a typical contractor, even though it is the construction and mining books that will move the revenue line.
06

FGD: the mandated retrofit market

A wave of pollution-control work forced onto India's coal fleet — and an erector is well placed to catch it.

One of the most important tailwinds behind Power Mech has nothing to do with building new plants — it is about cleaning up old ones. India's coal-fired power stations have been ordered to install flue-gas desulphurisation (FGD) systems: large chemical-scrubbing units bolted onto a plant's exhaust to strip out sulphur dioxide before it reaches the air. Because this is a mandate, not a discretionary upgrade, it created a captive, time-bound retrofit market across a big chunk of the national coal fleet.

Why is this Power Mech's kind of work? An FGD retrofit is heavy on-site engineering — ducting, structures, pumps, absorbers — grafted onto a live or recently operating power plant, often in tight, awkward spaces without the luxury of a green field. That is exactly the erection-and-BoP discipline the company has practised for 25 years, and it had an early-mover credential: it installed India's first operational FGD at Vindhyachal (500 MW) back in 2018, as an associate to GE. When the mandate broadened, Power Mech was already on the board — and in 2022 it won a landmark ~₹6,163 Cr FGD package from the Adani group covering 15 coal units.

The honest complication with FGD

FGD looks like a clean, mandated growth market — and it is — but the reality on the ground has been stop-start. Regulatory deadlines for FGD installation have been repeatedly extended, and some of Power Mech's own FGD orders (notably parts of the large Adani package) have moved slowly, to the point where analysts have set them aside when judging the near-term executable book. So FGD is best understood as a large, real, but back-ended opportunity: the work exists and the rules require it, but the timing is at the mercy of policy deadlines and utility budgets that keep slipping. It is a genuine structural driver — just not a metronome you can set your revenue clock to.

The strategic prize is bigger than the retrofit fees. A plant Power Mech retrofits with FGD is a plant it now knows intimately — a natural candidate for the O&M of that FGD unit afterwards, feeding the recurring services book. That is the through-line of the whole company: nearly every project it wins is also a foot in the door for the next, longer-lasting relationship with the same asset.

07

The mining pivot: from erecting boilers to operating mines

The biggest, strangest and longest-dated bet in the book — a 25-year coal annuity.

The most consequential recent development at Power Mech is also the least obvious for an erection contractor: it has become a Mine Developer & Operator (MDO). Under an MDO contract, a company takes near-total responsibility for a coal or mineral mine on behalf of the owner — developing it, running it, extracting and delivering the coal — for a very long tenure, typically 25 years or more, in exchange for a per-tonne fee. It is a completely different business from bolting a turbine together, and Power Mech has moved into it at scale.

The logic Power Mech offers is that mining is, at its heart, a large-scale project-management and heavy-equipment challenge — exactly the muscle it built erecting plants and hauling steel. Develop the site, deploy the excavators and haul trucks, move enormous volumes of overburden and coal on schedule, and get paid per tonne for decades. If that skill genuinely transfers, MDO gives Power Mech something its core business never offered: a very long, annuity-like revenue stream that keeps paying for a quarter-century, smoothing the lumpiness of construction.

Why the mining bet cuts both ways

The upside is straightforward: a 25-year coal annuity worth tens of thousands of crores transforms revenue visibility and, if executed well, throws off steady cash long after any single construction cycle has turned. The risk is just as real. Mining is capital-hungry — fleets of excavators and trucks, mine development, ramp-up — which asks a lot of a contractor's balance sheet up front, before the per-tonne fees flow. It carries execution risks Power Mech has never owned before (geology, land, environmental clearances, coal evacuation), and it ties the company to coal for decades at a time when the world is trying to burn less of it. The MDO book is the reason the headline order figure looks enormous; it is also the reason to watch, closely, whether those mines actually reach steady-state production on time and at the promised economics. Paper tonnes in a backlog are not the same as coal on a conveyor.

The metric that will settle the mining question: not the ₹39,576 Cr headline, but tonnes actually mined and despatched — the point at which the MDO stops being a signed contract and becomes a producing, cash-generating annuity. Until those mines ramp, the mining book is potential; once they do, it is the ballast that could re-rate the whole company.
08

Beyond thermal power: railways, water, roads and abroad

The deliberate widening from a power-plant specialist into a broad infrastructure contractor.

For a company born erecting boilers, an obvious strategic worry is over-dependence on thermal power — a sector that, however durable in India, carries long-term questions as the energy mix shifts. Power Mech's answer has been to carry its core skills — heavy civil work, project management, on-site execution — into adjacent infrastructure markets, so the company grows even in years when power-plant capex is soft.

The right way to read the diversification

Diversification can be a strength or a symptom, and it is worth being clear-eyed about which. At its best, moving into railways, water and roads lets Power Mech recycle one core competence — disciplined heavy-civil execution — across many end-markets, so a slowdown in any single one does not sink the company, and government infrastructure spending broadly keeps the order book fed. At its worst, spreading a contractor across too many unfamiliar verticals dilutes focus and invites execution slips in businesses management understands less well. The evidence so far — steady margins and returns as the mix has broadened — leans toward the benign reading. The test is forward-looking: can Power Mech keep its ~12% execution discipline as non-power work becomes a bigger share, or do the new verticals eventually cost it on margin or write-offs?

The insight that separates this cycle from a simple "coal comeback"

It would be easy to file Power Mech as a leveraged bet on coal, but that misreads the mechanics. The company gets paid to build, service and operate infrastructure regardless of who ultimately owns the megawatt or the tonne — its fee is for execution, not for the commodity. That is a subtler, more resilient position than a pure coal producer: when new plants are built, it erects them; when old plants must clean up, it retrofits them; when the running fleet needs upkeep, it maintains it; and when coal output must rise, it operates the mines. The same firm profits from coal being built, cleaned, run and dug — four different phases of the asset's life. That breadth across the life-cycle, more than any single tailwind, is what gives the order book its durability.

None of this makes the demand risk-free. Thermal capex is ultimately a policy choice; FGD deadlines have slipped repeatedly; infrastructure budgets ebb with the fiscal cycle; and the long-run energy transition is a genuine question mark over coal-linked work a decade out. But the near-to-medium-term picture — new units, mandated retrofits, an infrastructure build-out and a coal-security drive all at once — is about as supportive a backdrop as an execution contractor in these markets could ask for.

At ~21× earnings the stock is not priced as a cheap contractor; it is priced as a business that will keep converting a fat, growing book at a dependable margin. 11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A disciplined execution machine, asked to keep scaling without a margin cushion.

Power Mech enters FY27 in strong shape: a diversified order book that gives years of visibility, tailwinds in nearly every market it serves, healthy returns, and a founder who has compounded the business for a quarter-century. The debate is not whether the company works — it plainly does — but whether a firm earning a thin, fixed margin on execution can keep growing volumes fast enough, and cleanly enough, to justify a growth-stock valuation while absorbing the new risks of long-dated mining.

The bull case

  • Deep, diversified order book — even ex-MDO, ~₹15,900 Cr of executable work plus a 25-year mining annuity gives rare revenue visibility for a mid-cap contractor.
  • Broad tailwinds — new thermal build, mandated FGD retrofits, an infrastructure super-cycle and a coal-security push all feed the same firm at once.
  • Life-cycle model — it profits from building, cleaning, running and now mining the same assets; the O&M annuity de-risks the earnings.
  • Consistent returns — ~21–23% RoCE and steady ~12% margins through years of rapid growth, with no promoter-pledge stress.
  • Up the value chain — moving from erection sub-contractor into full EPC and MDO raises value per contract and the strategic moat.

The bear case

  • Thin, fixed margin — ~12% leaves little cushion; a few cost overruns or delayed projects can dent profit fast, and there's no pricing lever to fall back on.
  • Order-book optics — the ₹55,000 Cr headline flatters near-term reality; most of it is a slow mining annuity, and FGD orders have repeatedly slipped.
  • Working capital & mining capex — contractor cash gets tied up in projects; the MDO build-out demands heavy upfront investment before per-tonne fees flow.
  • Execution & diversification risk — spread across power, FGD, railways, water and mining, the firm owns unfamiliar risks (geology, clearances, coal evacuation).
  • Valuation & coal exposure — ~21× earnings prices in continued flawless scaling, while a chunk of the future rests on coal for 25 years amid an energy transition.
The bottom line

Power Mech has quietly become one of the more capable execution houses in Indian infrastructure — an erector that learned to run what it builds, retrofit what it ran, and now operate the mines that feed it. The business is real, the returns are respectable, and the tailwinds are genuine. What it is not is a story of expanding margins or a hidden re-rating; it is a volume machine running at a dependable ~12% take, which is precisely why the valuation matters. The next few years turn on two checkable things: whether the ~₹15,900 Cr executable book keeps converting into revenue at that steady margin with the working capital under control, and whether the Tasra and Central Coalfields mines move from signed contracts to coal actually on the conveyor. Watch the executable book, the order inflow, and — the moment it starts — the mining tonnage; those prints, not the ₹55,000-crore banner, will tell you whether the compounding continues.
