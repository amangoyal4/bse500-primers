00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Waaree Energies makes solar panels — the dark blue-black glass rectangles you see on rooftops and in solar farms. It is India's largest solar PV module manufacturer, the largest on India's approved-manufacturer list, and the largest non-Chinese module maker in the world. It sells to Indian developers and, heavily, to the United States, and is rapidly expanding into solar cells, batteries, green hydrogen, and project-building (EPC).

Three facts frame everything:

  • Explosive, capacity-driven growth. Revenue roughly doubled in FY26 (tracking ~₹26,500 Cr vs ₹14,846 Cr in FY25), profit is up several-fold since FY24, and it sits on a ~₹47,000 Cr / ~25 GW order book. A blockbuster October 2024 IPO listed it up 55% on day one.
  • It's downstream-heavy in a China-controlled chain. Waaree is huge in the last step (modules) and now big in the second-to-last (cells), but India still imports almost all of the polysilicon and wafers that go into them — mostly from China. Its strategic project is backward integration: building cells, then wafers, to stop depending on China.
  • The US is both its biggest opportunity and its biggest threat. America has been ~50–60% of its order book. But in 2026 the US erected a ~234%-plus anti-dumping + countervailing tariff wall on Indian solar — and, days ago, US Customs ruled that Waaree specifically evaded tariffs, with duties up to ~271% on certain products. This is the defining risk.
In plain terms

Picture Waaree as a factory business riding a once-in-a-generation tailwind, squeezed between two superpowers. The tailwind is the global shift to solar (India alone targets adding ~175–180 GW by 2030). The squeeze: China controls the raw materials it needs to buy, and the United States — its richest customer — is slamming the door with tariffs. Waaree's entire strategy is a response to that vice: build the upstream at home so it needs China less, and build a US factory so it can sell in America without crossing the tariff wall.

01

Solar 101: how a panel makes money

No physics degree required — just the units and the economics.

A solar module (or panel) is a sheet of solar cells sandwiched under glass in an aluminium frame. The cells turn sunlight into electricity. Waaree's core business is making these panels by the millions and selling them to whoever is building solar power — utilities, developers, rooftop installers, and increasingly itself (via its EPC arm).

The only units you need

Reading the solar industry's vocabulary
TermWhat it means
Watt (W) / Watt-peak (Wp)The power a panel produces in full sun. A single panel is ~400–600 W.
Megawatt (MW) / Gigawatt (GW)1 MW = 1,000 W; 1 GW = 1,000 MW. Capacity is measured in GW. Waaree makes ~22 GW of panels a year — millions of panels.
$/watt (or ₹/watt)The price of a panel per watt — the single most important number in the industry. As panels get cheaper, $/watt falls.
EfficiencyHow much sunlight a cell converts to electricity (~20–23%). Higher efficiency = more power per panel = premium pricing.
The brutal economics of "$/watt"

Solar is a commodity with a falling price. Panels have gotten relentlessly cheaper for two decades — Waaree's own IPO filing noted module prices hit ~$0.11/watt in early 2024, down 43% in a single year. That's wonderful for solar adoption (cheaper power) but dangerous for manufacturers: if your selling price keeps dropping, you survive only by being a low-cost producer at massive scale, or by making higher-value premium panels. This is why scale and cost control are everything in this business, and why margins can swing violently with panel prices.

Why demand is booming

Despite falling prices — actually because of them — solar demand is exploding. Cheap solar is now often the cheapest electricity on earth. The drivers for Waaree:

  • India's targets — the government aims for ~500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030, implying ~175–180 GW of new solar this decade and an estimated ~$38 billion manufacturing opportunity.
  • Global energy transition — worldwide decarbonisation is driving record solar installation, and buyers want non-Chinese supply for security reasons — directly favouring India's largest maker.
  • Allied demand — batteries (to store solar) and green hydrogen (made with solar power) open adjacent markets Waaree is entering.
02

The value chain: from sand to panel

Five steps — and the single most important strategic map in this whole report.

A solar panel isn't made in one step. It runs through a five-stage value chain, starting from refined sand and ending in a finished module. Where a company sits on this chain determines its costs, its margins, and — for India — its dependence on China. Read this once and the entire Waaree strategy makes sense.

What happens at each step (plain English)

  • Polysilicon — ordinary sand (silica) is purified into ultra-pure silicon. Energy-intensive, capital-heavy; China makes ~80%+ of the world's supply. India makes almost none.
  • Ingot & Wafer — the polysilicon is melted into a solid block (ingot), then sliced into paper-thin squares (wafers). China makes ~95% of wafers. This is the single biggest gap in India's chain.
  • Cell — the wafer is treated with chemical layers so it actually generates electricity from light. This is the high-tech heart of the panel. Waaree has 5.4 GW of cell capacity — among India's largest — and is expanding.
  • Module — dozens of cells are wired together, laid on glass, framed, and sealed into a weatherproof panel. This is assembly-heavy and the easiest step. Waaree dominates here (~22 GW).

Why this gap is the whole investment story

Look at the chart: Waaree makes 22 GW of panels but has zero wafers and zero polysilicon. Every panel it makes still contains Chinese silicon. That's a strategic vulnerability (China could restrict supply or prices) and a margin leak (the upstream is where a lot of the profit sits). Waaree's entire capex programme — the 5.4 GW cell plant, the planned 6 GW wafer-to-module hub in Odisha, the fully-integrated Indosol plant — is about climbing leftward up this chain. If it succeeds, it becomes a true end-to-end manufacturer with better margins and real supply security. That is the bull case in one picture.

A note on cell technology (the jargon decoded)

You'll see acronyms — they're just generations of cell design, each more efficient than the last:

  • Multicrystalline → Mono PERC — older technologies; PERC was the workhorse for years.
  • TOPCon (Tunnel Oxide Passivated Contact) — the current mainstream high-efficiency tech; Waaree's newer G12/G12R TOPCon modules use it.
  • HJT (Heterojunction) — the premium, highest-efficiency frontier; Waaree has G12 HJT modules too.
  • Bifacial / BIPV — bifacial panels capture light on both sides; BIPV (building-integrated PV) doubles as building material.

The takeaway: cell technology keeps improving, which means manufacturers must keep reinvesting to avoid being stuck with obsolete lines — a constant capex treadmill that favours well-funded scale players.

03

The company and who controls it

From a 1990 fluid-connector firm to India's solar champion.

Waaree's origins are humble and a little surprising: it was incorporated in Mumbai in December 1990 as "Anmol Fluid Connectors Private Limited" — nothing to do with solar. It pivoted into solar in 2007, renamed itself Waaree, and rode India's solar boom to the top. It's controlled by the Doshi family, who built the broader Waaree Group.

Timeline

  • 1990
    Incorporated as Anmol Fluid Connectors Pvt Ltd in Mumbai.
  • 2007
    Pivots to solar — renamed Waaree Solar / Waaree Energies; begins module manufacturing with ~30 MW.
  • 2013
    Becomes a public limited company; adopts the name Waaree Energies Limited.
  • 2022
    Begins building solar cell capacity (backward integration); acquires Indosolar (~96% stake) for additional module capacity and a PLI vehicle.
  • 2023–24
    Scales module capacity to ~12–13 GW; commissions a 1.6 GW US module line in Brookshire, Texas.
  • Oct 2024
    IPO — ₹4,321 Cr, subscribed 76×, listed +55% on day one; later peaked near ₹3,865.
  • FY25–26
    Commissions India's largest 5.4 GW cell plant; revenue doubles; module capacity reaches ~22 GW; advances the Odisha wafer-to-module hub, batteries, and green hydrogen.

Ownership & the group

  • Promoters (~64%): the Doshi family — Hitesh Chimanlal Doshi (Chairman & Managing Director), Viren Doshi, Pankaj Doshi, plus Waaree Sustainable Finance. A high promoter holding signals strong family control and aligned skin-in-the-game.
  • Management: Dr. Amit Paithankar — Whole-Time Director & CEO; Sunil Rathi — Executive Director.
  • The wider Waaree Group includes two other listed entities: Waaree Renewable Technologies (WRTL) — the solar EPC / project arm — and Waaree Technologies — batteries. Waaree Energies is the flagship manufacturer.
  • Balance sheet: IPO-funded growth, rising profits, and a credit-rating upgrade to CARE A+ — though the aggressive capex means capital intensity is high.

Why a Texas factory is worth so much

US trade rules and incentives reward panels physically made in America. By manufacturing cells and modules on US soil, Waaree can sell into its biggest market without triggering the cross-border anti-dumping and countervailing duties that now make Indian-made panels uncompetitive there. It's the same playbook used by Qcells and others. As the US tariff wall went up in 2026, the Texas plant flipped from "nice diversification" to "essential survival asset" for Waaree's American business.

The product portfolio, broken into parts

Modules are the core — but Waaree is becoming a full clean-energy platform.

Waaree's revenue is overwhelmingly solar modules today, but it has deliberately built a portfolio that spans the value chain and adjacent clean-energy markets. Here's each piece.

A. Solar modules — the core (~most of revenue)

B. Solar cells — the integration play (5.4 GW)

Waaree's 5.4 GW cell plant in Chikhli, Gujarat, is among India's largest. Strategically, management has said cell output is prioritised for two uses: feeding its US module business and serving the domestic DCR market (projects that legally require Indian-made cells). Cells are the first big step up the value chain away from China.

C–F. The adjacent clean-energy platform

Beyond panels and cells, Waaree is building out the rest of the clean-energy stack — turning a module maker into an energy-solutions company:

  • Inverters — the devices that convert solar DC power into usable AC.
  • Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) — a planned 3.7 GWh lithium-ion facility; storage is the natural partner to intermittent solar.
  • Green hydrogen — a planned 300 MW electrolyser capacity; hydrogen made using solar power is a long-dated growth bet.
  • EPC & projects — engineering, procurement & construction of large solar plants, largely through group company Waaree Renewable Technologies, plus a move to own power projects (IPP) directly.
The strategic logic of "everything clean"

Why not just make panels? Because (1) it captures more of the customer's spend — selling a developer the modules, the inverters, the batteries, and building the plant; (2) the EPC/IPP arms create captive demand for Waaree's own panels; and (3) batteries and hydrogen are the next growth waves after solar modules commoditise. The risk is doing too many capital-hungry things at once — execution and capital discipline become the watch-items.

The order book and customers

Huge visibility — but concentrated, and increasingly US-exposed.

Unlike a consumer business, Waaree sells large volumes to a relatively small number of big buyers, and books orders well in advance. Its order book — the value of contracted-but-not-yet-delivered panels — is the clearest gauge of demand visibility.

The order book has grown rapidly — from ~16.7 GW at the IPO to ~26.5 GW in early 2025 and ~25 GW worth ₹47,000 Cr into FY26 — giving strong revenue visibility. But two features matter for risk:

  • Customer concentration — the top 10 customers were ~57% of revenue at IPO; losing one large order (or a cancellation) moves the numbers.
  • The international half is heavily US — which is precisely the exposure now under threat from American tariffs (Part 09). A large slice of that order book sits in the market that's becoming hardest to serve.
The Indosolar acquisition (Waaree's one big M&A): unlike a single transformational merger, Waaree mostly grows organically — but its 2022 acquisition of a ~96% stake in Indosolar (a previously-bankrupt listed cell maker) was strategically important: it added module capacity and became the vehicle ("Indosol") through which Waaree bid for a 6 GW fully-integrated plant under the government's PLI scheme. So the deal was as much about winning policy incentives and an integration pathway as about buying capacity.

The industry and the rivals

A booming domestic market, a gold rush of new capacity, and intensifying competition.

India's solar manufacturing industry is in a once-in-a-generation expansion, pulled by enormous domestic demand and pushed by government incentives. The flip side: everyone is building capacity, which raises the risk of future oversupply and price wars.

The competitive tension to watch

Two things define the competition. First, integration depth: the winners of the next phase will be those who build the most of the value chain at home (cells, wafers, even polysilicon). Waaree leads on scale but is not yet the most integrated — Reliance and Indosol are chasing full integration including polysilicon. Second, the oversupply risk: with everyone adding GWs, India could shift from a module shortage to a glut, pressuring prices. Waaree's defences are scale (lowest cost), its order book, its US factory, and its move into batteries/hydrogen.

08

The rulebook: how policy built Waaree

India's protectionist stack is the wind in Waaree's sails — understand it and you understand the moat.

Waaree's rise is inseparable from government policy. India deliberately built a protective wall around domestic solar manufacturing to reduce dependence on cheap Chinese panels and create a home-grown industry. Four instruments do the heavy lifting.

India's solar manufacturing policy stack
PolicyWhat it doesWhy it helps Waaree
BCD — Basic Customs Duty40% duty on imported modules, 25% on imported cells (since 2022)Makes Chinese panels expensive in India, so buyers choose domestic makers like Waaree.
ALMM — Approved List of Models & ManufacturersAn MNRE list; only listed manufacturers' panels can be used in government / subsidised projectsEffectively shuts non-approved (largely Chinese) panels out of a huge chunk of the market. Waaree is the largest ALMM-approved maker. A cell-level ALMM is being enforced too.
DCR — Domestic Content RequirementCertain schemes mandate India-made cells AND modulesCreates guaranteed demand for fully-domestic product — exactly what Waaree's cell plant enables it to supply.
PLI — Production Linked IncentiveCash incentives for domestic manufacturing, tiered by how integrated the plant isSubsidises capacity build-out; Waaree won PLI (via Indosol) for an integrated plant — direct financial support for going up the value chain.
Why this is the real moat

Strip away the technology talk and Waaree's biggest competitive advantage is this: India has made it expensive and often illegal to use Chinese panels in much of the domestic market, and Waaree is the largest approved domestic supplier. BCD raises import costs, ALMM locks out unapproved makers, DCR mandates Indian content, and PLI pays you to build. That policy wall is what lets a domestic maker earn healthy margins despite globally falling panel prices. The risk, of course, is that policy can change — duties can be cut, ALMM timelines deferred, or incentives wound down — so the moat is partly political.

09

Caught between China and America

The two-sided geopolitical squeeze — and the tariff wall that just got personal.

Waaree's biggest risks aren't operational — they're geopolitical, and they come from both ends of its supply chain. It imports from China and exports to America, and both relationships turned hostile.

The China side — import dependence

As the value chain showed, Waaree still relies on China for upstream materials. Its own IPO filing disclosed that in FY24 it imported ~90% of its total purchases, with China making up ~54% of those imports. Without long-term supply contracts, a Chinese export restriction or price spike on polysilicon/wafers would hit costs directly. This is exactly what the Odisha wafer hub and the cell plant are meant to fix — but until they're fully built, the China dependence is real.

The America side — the tariff wall (the defining 2026 event)

The US has been ~50–60% of Waaree's order book, and a large share of its revenue. In 2026, that lucrative market slammed shut behind a tariff wall, in three escalating moves:

The June 23, 2026 Customs ruling — read this carefully: US Customs & Border Protection issued a final determination that Waaree evaded anti-dumping/countervailing orders by routing Chinese-origin solar cells through Vietnam/Malaysia and entering them with materially false/omitted information — and will require cash deposits / assess duties up to ~271.28% on the affected products imported 2021–2026. Importantly, Customs declined to apply "adverse inferences," so the ruling hits only the specific products in question, not all of Waaree's US imports. Liquidation is suspended pending the final bill or a Waaree appeal — and Waaree has publicly issued a clarification contesting the characterisation. A separate Commerce final AD/CVD determination is due ~July 2026, with the ITC's final injury ruling expected later in the year.

How Waaree is fighting back

  • The Texas plant — making panels in the US sidesteps import duties entirely (the key hedge).
  • Supply-chain reconfiguration — sourcing cells from genuinely low-tariff countries, leveraging the long-standing customs principle that a panel's "origin" follows its cell.
  • Market diversification — pivoting toward Europe, West Asia, and especially the booming domestic market, which can absorb a lot of redirected volume.
  • Backward integration — domestic cells/wafers reduce the Chinese-origin issue that triggered the evasion case in the first place.

How to think about the valuation

11

The future — roadmap, risks, and the bull/bear case

A structural growth story with a binary tariff overhang.

The strategic roadmap

  • Climb the value chain — complete the 6 GW Odisha wafer-to-module hub and the integrated Indosol plant to reduce China dependence and capture upstream margin.
  • Localise in the US — scale the Texas plant toward ~5 GW so the American business survives behind the tariff wall.
  • Diversify markets — lean harder into India's booming domestic demand and into Europe/West Asia.
  • Broaden the platform — ramp batteries (3.7 GWh) and green hydrogen (300 MW) as the next growth legs beyond modules.

Bull case

  • Structural tailwind — India's ~175–180 GW solar build-out and global non-China demand are enormous and durable.
  • Scale leadership — largest Indian / largest non-Chinese maker; lowest-cost position; ~₹47,000 Cr order book.
  • Policy moat — BCD, ALMM, DCR, and PLI protect and subsidise the domestic franchise.
  • Backward integration — cells done, wafers coming → better margins and supply security.
  • US hedge — the Texas plant can serve America behind the tariff wall.
  • Optionality — batteries and green hydrogen as future growth legs.

Bear case

  • US tariff wall — ~234%+ AD/CVD plus the ~271% Waaree-specific evasion finding could gut a major export market and revenue stream.
  • China dependence — ~90% imported inputs; exposed to polysilicon/wafer prices and supply restrictions until integration completes.
  • Commodity cyclicality — falling panel prices ($/watt) can crush margins; ~80% of costs are materials.
  • Oversupply risk — India's capacity racing to ~125 GW could create a glut and price war.
  • Policy dependence — the moat is partly political; duty/ALMM/PLI changes could erode it.
  • Heavy capex & concentration — capital-hungry expansion; top-10 customers ~57%.

What to watch

  • The US trade outcome — the ~July 2026 Commerce final AD/CVD determination, the CBP duty bill / Waaree appeal, and the ITC injury ruling. The single biggest swing factor.
  • Backward-integration milestones — Odisha wafer hub and Indosol commissioning.
  • Module prices & margins — the $/watt trend vs the EBITDA-margin band.
  • Order-book mix — how fast the US share is replaced by domestic/Europe demand.
  • Domestic policy — ALMM cell-list enforcement, BCD continuity, PLI disbursement.
  • Capex & balance sheet — funding discipline as multiple gigafactories build at once.
Bottom line: Waaree is the scale leader of an Indian solar boom, with a genuine policy moat and a credible plan to climb the value chain — growing explosively and priced for it. But it is a capital-intensive, commodity-cyclical manufacturer caught in a geopolitical vice: dependent on China for inputs and now largely walled out of its richest export market. Where Paradeep's ceiling was set by policy and eClerx's by technology, Waaree's near-term fate turns on geopolitics and trade policy — a genuinely binary variable that the next few months will start to resolve.
