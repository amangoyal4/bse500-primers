00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Waaree Energies makes solar panels — the flat blue-black rectangles you see on rooftops and in giant desert power plants. It is India's largest solar photovoltaic (PV) module manufacturer, and after a burst of expansion it now describes itself as the largest non-Chinese module manufacturer in the world, with roughly 26 GW of module capacity (a gigawatt, "GW", is a billion watts — one large module factory's worth of annual output).

Three facts frame everything:

  • It scaled from small to enormous, fast. Revenue went from ₹2,854 Cr (FY22) to ₹26,537 Cr in FY26 (+84% in a single year); profit (PAT) hit ₹3,884 Cr, up 101%. It listed only in October 2024 in one of the most oversubscribed IPOs India has seen (~76×), and is now controlled ~64% by the founding Doshi family.
  • Its signature story is China, tariffs and America. The United States has slapped heavy anti-dumping/anti-subsidy duties on Chinese solar (and on Chinese product routed through South-East Asia). That makes clean, non-Chinese, Indian-made panels from Waaree hugely valuable to US buyers — which is why ~65–70% of its ₹53,000 Cr order book is export and it has built its own factory in Texas. This tailwind is also its biggest single risk.
  • It is climbing the value chain to protect margins. A module maker that buys its key input (solar cells) from China is exposed and thin-margined. Waaree is integrating backwards — it now runs India's largest solar-cell plant (5.4 GW), and is building ingots and wafers (a 10 GW plant in Nagpur) to become self-reliant from silicon block to finished panel.
In plain terms

Think of Waaree as India's answer to "who makes solar panels if you don't want to buy Chinese?" China dominates world solar so completely that Western governments have thrown up tariff walls to keep Chinese panels out. That created a gap — a global scramble for panels made anywhere but China — and Waaree, sitting on India's cheap labour, a supportive government and years of manufacturing know-how, ran straight into it. Its job now is threefold: keep filling its giant factories, march up the supply chain so it depends on China for less and less, and not get whipsawed if American solar policy suddenly changes. Get those right and it is a structural winner of the energy transition; get the timing wrong and it is a capital-hungry commodity maker at the mercy of foreign politics.

01

Solar 101: the value chain, from sand to panel

No physics degree needed — just the five steps that turn silicon into electricity.

A solar panel isn't one thing made in one place — it's the final assembly of a long supply chain, and where a company sits on that chain decides its cost, its margin and how dependent it is on China. Understanding the chain is the single most useful thing for understanding Waaree, because its whole strategy is about owning more of it.

Why the chain matters so much

Imagine a bakery that buys ready-made dough and only bakes and sells the bread. It's at the mercy of the dough supplier's prices, and it keeps only a slice of the profit. Now imagine it starts milling its own flour and growing its own wheat — it captures more of every rupee, and it stops being hostage to one supplier. That is exactly Waaree's play: it began as a "baker" (assembling modules from imported cells) and is now moving upstream to make its own "flour" (cells) and even mill its own "wheat" (wafers/ingots). Each step left lifts margins and cuts China dependence — the two things that most define whether a solar manufacturer is a fragile trader or a durable industrial champion.

02

The business model: a capacity machine with three markets

How Waaree actually turns factories into cash.

At its heart Waaree is a manufacturing scale business: it builds enormous factories, fills them with orders, and profits on the spread between what a panel costs to make and what it sells for. Three things drive the economics — utilisation (are the factories running full?), the cost curve (how cheaply can it make a watt of panel?), and mix (higher-priced export/premium sales vs commodity domestic sales).

Three routes to market

  • Exports (the crown jewel) — chiefly to the United States, where tariff-protected demand lets Waaree earn premium prices. This is the highest-margin, most strategically important channel and dominates the order book.
  • Domestic utility & commercial — supplying India's giant solar-farm build-out (developers, power producers, government tenders). Large volumes, protected by Indian policy (ALMM/PLI, §06), but more price-competitive.
  • Retail / franchise & EPC — rooftop panels sold through a franchisee network (₹5,515 Cr of retail revenue in FY26) plus engineering-procurement-construction of solar plants (~10% of sales). This brings brand, distribution reach and a foothold across the whole solar "system", not just the panel.
Why utilisation is the number to watch

A module factory has huge fixed costs — the building, the machines, the debt to fund them. Whether it runs at 50% or 90% of capacity, most of those costs are the same. So the difference between a half-full and a nearly-full factory is enormous for profit: extra panels made on already-paid-for machines are close to pure margin. This is why Waaree's relentless order-book growth matters more than any single quarter's price — a fat, multi-year order book (§07) is what keeps ~26 GW of capacity humming and the fixed costs spread thin. The flip side: if demand ever softens and utilisation drops, the same fixed costs that magnify profits on the way up magnify losses on the way down.

03

The company and who controls it

From a Mumbai trader started with $60 to India's biggest solar name.

Waaree is a founder-controlled, family-run business. It was incorporated in 1990 and built by Hitesh Doshi, who began as a small trader and turned Waaree into India's leading solar-panel maker — a rise so steep that the October 2024 IPO propelled the Doshi siblings into the ranks of India's richest. The Doshi promoter group still owns ~64%, so incentives are heavily aligned with long-term shareholders, and the family drives the aggressive expansion strategy.

04

China, tariffs and the US export engine

The defining force in the entire Waaree story — read this section slowly.

To understand Waaree you have to understand one global fact: China makes almost all the world's solar — roughly 80% or more of every step from polysilicon to finished panel. For decades that made Chinese panels the cheapest on Earth. But the United States decided that dependence — and what it calls unfair Chinese subsidies and dumping — was a problem, and built a tariff wall. That wall is precisely what turned Waaree from an Indian manufacturer into a global export champion.

What the US actually did — in plain English

  • Anti-dumping & countervailing duties (AD/CVD). The US imposes extra import taxes on solar it judges to be sold below fair value ("dumping") or unfairly subsidised. Chinese cells/modules have faced these for years.
  • Closing the South-East Asia back door. Chinese firms rerouted panels through Cambodia, Malaysia, Thailand and Vietnam to dodge the duties. In April 2025 the US finalised steep AD/CVD duties on those four countries — anti-subsidy rates ran into the hundreds of percent for some suppliers — slamming that back door shut.
  • Forced-labour rules (UFLPA). US law also blocks goods linked to forced labour in Xinjiang, where much Chinese polysilicon originates — another barrier to Chinese-linked solar.
Why this is rocket fuel for Waaree

When the cheapest supplier (China, directly or via South-East Asia) is suddenly taxed out of the US market, American developers still need panels — a lot of them, especially now that AI data centres are driving a surge in US power demand. They turn to the next-best source: countries not caught in the tariff net. India is the standout, and Waaree is the biggest Indian name. Crucially, Indian panels face far lighter US duties than South-East Asian ones, so a Waaree panel can land in the US cheaper than a tariff-laden rival and at a premium price to Waaree. That is why roughly two-thirds of its ₹53,000 Cr order book is export (chiefly US), and why US sales carry richer margins than domestic ones. In effect, American trade policy is subsidising Indian solar manufacturing.

The Texas hedge — building inside the wall

Waaree didn't just export to America; it built in America. Its Brookshire, Texas module plant went from 1.6 GW to 3.2 GW in 2025, with plans to reach ~5 GW by 2027 — potentially one of the largest solar factories in the US. A domestic US plant is a powerful hedge: it lets Waaree qualify for US "domestic-content" incentives, sit inside any future import barrier, and serve American demand even if trade policy tightens further. US Customs has also verified that Waaree's exports contain no Chinese-origin cells, de-risking that pipeline under forced-labour rules.

The two-edged sword. The same policy that created Waaree's export boom can take it away. US solar trade policy is volatile: expiring Section 201 tariffs, possible new AD/CVD cases aimed at India, Indonesia and Laos, changes to Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) subsidies, and shifting domestic-content rules can all swing US demand and pricing quickly. A single adverse ruling — say, an AD/CVD case that puts India in the crosshairs, or a rollback of US solar incentives — could compress the premium that today makes exports so lucrative. Waaree's biggest strength (US-tariff-driven demand) is also its biggest single dependency, and it sits in Washington's hands, not Mumbai's.
05

Backward integration: climbing up the value chain

The margin-and-self-reliance moat Waaree is building right now.

A module maker that buys its cells (and the wafers behind them) from China has two problems: thin margins (it only adds value at the last, easiest step) and dependence (its costs and supply hinge on a rival country). Waaree's answer, the centrepiece of its "Waaree 2.0" strategy, is to march backwards up the value chain — making the cells, and then the wafers and ingots, itself.

Why integration lifts both margin and safety

Every step Waaree brings in-house does two things at once. (1) Margin: instead of paying a Chinese supplier's profit on cells, Waaree keeps that value itself — the cell is the highest-value part of a panel, so making your own cells is the single biggest margin lever a module maker has. (2) Self-reliance: owning cells (and eventually wafers) means Waaree's costs and supply no longer swing with China's export policy, currency or price games — critical when your customers (US, India) specifically want non-Chinese supply. There's even a regulatory kicker: India's rules (§06) increasingly reward or require locally made cells, and US forced-labour rules reward verifiably non-Chinese content. Vertical integration is how Waaree turns "we assemble panels" into "we control the whole chain" — a far more defensible position.

The one gap that remains: even a fully integrated Waaree still needs polysilicon — the raw purified silicon at the very top of the chain, ~80–90% of which is Chinese. Wafers/ingots take Waaree most of the way, but that final upstream dependence (and the capital, technology and execution risk of building cell/wafer plants at speed) is the honest limit on the self-reliance story.
06

The India demand wave — and the policy shield

A home market booming on its own, with the government tilting the field toward local makers.

While the US export story gets the headlines, Waaree also rides an enormous domestic tailwind: India is building solar at breakneck speed, and — crucially — the government has stacked the policy deck in favour of domestic manufacturers. The result is a home market that is both large and structurally protected.

The demand: India's solar build-out

  • National targets: India is targeting ~500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030, of which solar is the biggest slice — a multi-decade construction wave.
  • Annual additions: India is expected to install on the order of ~42–45 GW of new solar in 2026 alone — every gigawatt of which needs panels.
  • Rooftop & the consumer: schemes like PM Surya Ghar (subsidised rooftop solar for households) create a huge retail market — exactly where Waaree's franchisee/retail network plays.

The neat symmetry of Waaree's position

Notice how the two big forces point the same way. America wants non-Chinese panels because of tariffs and forced-labour law; India wants domestically-made panels because of ALMM/PLI/DCR. Both, independently, create protected demand for exactly what Waaree makes — and both increasingly reward being integrated (owning cells, not just assembling them). Waaree's strategy is essentially to be the largest, most integrated, verifiably-non-Chinese solar manufacturer, and then let two of the world's biggest markets compete for its output. The risk on the India side mirrors the US: policy can loosen (import duties cut, ALMM relaxed) and cheaper Chinese supply could flood back — but for now the policy shield is a genuine, durable tailwind.

Two numbers that tell you how much of the future is already booked.

Why a multi-year order book is so valuable

Most manufacturers live quarter to quarter, hoping demand shows up. Waaree can see much further: a ₹53,000 Cr book — roughly two years of sales — much of it long-dated US export orders to be delivered over the next three-to-four years. That does three things: it de-risks utilisation (the factories have work booked), it justifies the aggressive capex (you build 26 GW of capacity because you can see the demand to fill it), and it smooths pricing (long contracts lock in volumes). The caveat: order books can be renegotiated, delayed or cancelled if end-markets wobble — so watch whether the book keeps growing quarter after quarter (a sign demand is real and repeating) rather than just being drawn down.

08

Beyond panels: the new-energy bets

Batteries, green hydrogen and inverters — optionality, and a capital appetite.

Waaree's ambition is to be an "everything solar/clean-energy" company, not just a panel maker. Alongside the module business it is pouring capital into three adjacencies — batteries, green-hydrogen electrolysers and inverters — each a potentially large market, each also a fresh call on cash and execution.

  • Batteries / energy storage (BESS). Solar only generates by day; storing it makes it dispatchable — the single biggest complement to solar. Waaree is building a 3.5 GWh lithium-ion cell plant, with plans to scale toward 20 GWh (an ~₹8,000 Cr+ investment). As grids add more solar, storage demand explodes — this could become a second growth engine.
  • Green hydrogen electrolysers. Machines that split water into hydrogen using renewable power — central to India's green-hydrogen mission. Waaree is scaling electrolyser capacity from 300 MW toward 1,000 MW. Early-stage, but strategically adjacent.
  • Inverters. The device that converts a panel's DC electricity into usable AC. Waaree is adding a ~4 GW inverter line — natural bundling with its panels, capturing more of each solar system's value.
  • EPC & services. Engineering and building whole solar plants (~10% of sales) — deepening customer relationships and pulling through module demand.
How to read the diversification

These bets are real optionality — batteries especially could be huge as storage becomes essential to a solar-heavy grid — but they are also a reminder that Waaree is in a heavy-investment phase. Across modules, cells, wafers, batteries, electrolysers and inverters, the company has approved tens of thousands of crores of capex. That is why free cash flow is thin even as profits soar (FY26 operating cash flow fell as capex and working capital rose): the profit is being reinvested into the next several factories. Bulls see a compounding clean-energy platform being built; sceptics see a lot of simultaneous, capital-hungry projects that all have to execute on time and find their markets. Both views are watching the same thing — whether these new lines start earning returns before the capex bill strains the balance sheet.

The competitive set

The scale leader in a field that is racing to integrate.

Waaree is the largest Indian module maker and the largest non-Chinese one globally, but it is not alone — a cohort of Indian rivals is expanding fast, and the real long-run competitor is always Chinese scale lurking behind the tariff walls. The defining contest is who can build the most integrated (cell + module + wafer), bankable, verifiably-non-Chinese capacity fastest.

The oversupply question: because policy is so favourable, everyone in India is building capacity at once (seven-plus players targeting ~10 GW of integrated capacity each by FY28). If domestic capacity races ahead of domestic demand, panel prices and margins could compress — which is exactly why exports (the US premium) and integration (lower costs) are Waaree's two defences against a home-market glut.

How to think about the valuation

At a market cap of ~₹83,000 Cr (June 2026) on ~₹3,884 Cr of FY26 profit, Waaree trades around 21–23× trailing earnings — punchy for a manufacturer, but notably lower than many high-growth Indian industrials (and far below, say, Solar Industries' ~100×), because the market prices in real cyclicality and policy risk. The bull maths: if FY27 operating EBITDA hits the guided ₹7,000–7,700 Cr (+20–25%) and integration keeps lifting margins, earnings can grow into and past the multiple. The bear maths: solar manufacturing is cyclical and policy-dependent — management itself guides to a more normal ~19–20% sustained EBITDA margin long-term (below FY26's peak), a hint that today's fat margins partly reflect a tariff-driven sweet spot that may not last. So the multiple is reasonable if the growth and margins hold, and expensive if the cycle or US policy turns.

One-off / quality flags: FY26's ~22% operating margin sits well above the ~19–20% management calls sustainable — treat the current margin as a peak-ish level, not a run-rate. And note the cash-flow gap: operating cash flow fell (~₹1,626 Cr vs ~₹3,158 Cr) even as profit doubled, because working capital and a large capex programme consumed cash. Reported EBITDA (₹6,617 Cr) exceeds operating EBITDA (₹5,909 Cr) partly due to other income — use the operating figure for the core business.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine structural winner — riding forces it doesn't fully control.

Waaree is one of the clearest ways to own the global energy transition and the "de-China-ification" of solar supply. It is the scale leader, it is integrating fast, and it has two of the world's biggest markets tilting policy in its favour. The debate is about durability — of the US tariff premium, of margins, and of demand versus a wall of new capacity.

The bull case

  • Tariff-protected export engine — US duties on Chinese/SE-Asian solar hand Waaree a large, premium-priced order book (~65–70% export).
  • Scale + integration moat — largest non-Chinese module maker, now making its own cells (5.4 GW) and building wafers — lower cost, less China dependence.
  • Twin policy tailwinds — US (non-China demand) and India (ALMM/PLI/DCR) both engineer demand for exactly what it makes.
  • Visibility — ₹53,000 Cr order book + 100 GW+ pipeline underpins utilisation for years.
  • Optionality — batteries, hydrogen and inverters could become second and third growth engines.

The bear case

  • US policy dependence — the export premium sits in Washington's hands; new AD/CVD cases on India, IRA rollbacks or domestic-content changes could hit hard.
  • Cyclical, commodity economics — panels are ultimately a commodity; management guides margins down to ~19–20% long-term.
  • Domestic oversupply — everyone is building capacity; a home-market glut could crush prices/margins.
  • Capex & cash flow — huge simultaneous investments; free cash flow thin, execution risk high across many new lines.
  • Residual China dependence — still reliant on Chinese polysilicon at the top of the chain.
The bottom line

Waaree is a genuinely advantaged, structurally-tailwinded manufacturer — the biggest non-Chinese solar maker, integrating up the chain, with two continents' worth of protected demand and a fat order book. The quality of the business is not in doubt; the durability is. Its fortunes are unusually tied to US trade policy and to a solar-manufacturing cycle that is prone to over-building. The two numbers to track each quarter are the order book (is it still growing, and holding its export premium?) and the margin (is it holding above the ~19–20% "normal" level, or drifting toward it?). Hold the tariff premium and execute the integration, and Waaree compounds as a national industrial champion; lose the premium to a policy shift or an oversupply glut, and a cyclical manufacturer re-rates to cyclical multiples.
