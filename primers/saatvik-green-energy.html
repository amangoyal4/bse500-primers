00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Saatvik Green Energy makes solar panels — the flat, glass-fronted rectangles of blue-black silicon that sit on rooftops and in vast desert farms, turning sunlight into electricity. In the trade they are called solar PV modules, and Saatvik is one of India's larger listed module manufacturers, running roughly 4.86 GW of module capacity out of Ambala, Haryana. It does not stop at making the panel: it also builds solar projects end-to-end (EPC), services them for years (O&M), and has just started owning power plants itself (IPP). It listed on the NSE and BSE in September 2025, so its public track record is measured in months, not years.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It grew explosively — then listed straight into a fall. Revenue went from ~₹1,088 Cr in FY24 to ~₹2,158 Cr in FY25 to ~₹4,548 Cr in FY26, more than quadrupling in two years, with net profit reaching ~₹357 Cr. Yet the stock trades below its ₹465 IPO price — a company tripling its top line whose shares are already underwater. The market is not disputing the growth; it is questioning what that growth is worth once the industry's easy years end.
  • It assembles the panel but doesn't make the cell. Saatvik buys in the solar cell — the wafer of doped silicon that actually generates the current, and the single most expensive part of a module — largely from imported (predominantly Chinese) suppliers, then laminates it into a finished, warranted panel. Its entire strategic bet is a greenfield 2.4 GW-plus cell factory in Odisha to make that part itself. Until it does, Saatvik captures assembly margin on a panel whose most valuable component it does not control.
  • It lives inside a policy greenhouse. Indian module makers are shielded by an approved-manufacturer list (ALMM), an import duty on foreign panels (BCD), and government tenders that require domestically made content (DCR). That protection is why a young manufacturer can earn ~30%+ returns on capital today. The bear worry is the same protection: what happens to margins when the shelter is crowded with new capacity, or when the rules change.
In plain terms

A solar module is a sandwich: a sheet of solar cells, sealed between glass and a plastic film, wrapped in an aluminium frame. The cells do the work; everything else holds them in place and keeps the weather out. Saatvik is very good at building that sandwich — cheaply, at scale, in the newer high-efficiency format — and it sells it to project developers, government utilities and rooftop installers. But the cells inside come from someone else, usually China. The company's whole plan for the next three years is to stop buying that ingredient and start growing it in-house, in a new plant in Odisha. If it pulls that off, it keeps far more of every rupee a panel earns. If the plant slips or module prices collapse first, it is a fast-growing assembler competing in a brutally cyclical commodity.

01

Solar modules 101: cell, wafer, and the panel on the roof

No physics degree needed — just the five words that decide who makes money in solar.

A solar panel converts sunlight into direct-current electricity through the photovoltaic effect: photons knock electrons loose in a treated slice of silicon, and that flow of electrons is your current. Financially, solar is a business of rupees per watt — how cheaply you can manufacture a given amount of generating capacity — layered on top of a fierce fight over where in the supply chain the value sits. To follow Saatvik, you need five terms.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
WaferA paper-thin square sliced from a cylinder of ultra-pure silicon. The raw substrate of a cell. India makes almost none of these — nearly all wafers are imported, overwhelmingly from China. The very bottom of the value chain.
CellA wafer that has been chemically treated and printed so it actually generates electricity when light hits it. The heart of the panel and its most expensive single component. Saatvik buys cells in today; making them is the whole Odisha project.
Module (PV module)The finished panel — many cells wired together and sealed between glass, encapsulant film and a frame. Saatvik's core product. A modern one is rated up to ~580 watts-peak (Wp).
Wp / MW / GWWatts-peak is a panel's rated output in bright sun. A million of them is a megawatt (MW); a thousand MW is a gigawatt (GW). Saatvik's capacity (~4.86 GW) and its orders are all counted this way — it is the industry's unit of everything.
EPC / O&M"Engineering, Procurement & Construction" is building the actual solar farm (land, mounting, wiring, grid link). "Operations & Maintenance" is keeping it clean and running for years afterward. Saatvik does both, on top of selling the panels.
The value chain, and where the money hides

Solar manufacturing runs in a line: polysilicon → ingot → wafer → cell → module. Each step adds value, but the value is not spread evenly. The upstream steps (polysilicon, wafer) are capital-monster commodities dominated by a handful of Chinese giants. The cell is where a lot of the technology and margin live. The module — the assembly step — is the easiest to enter and therefore the most crowded and price-competitive. India's solar industry grew from the bottom up: hundreds of firms can bolt cells into panels, but very few can make the cells, and almost none can make the wafers. That single fact — India is long on module capacity and desperately short on cell capacity — is the backdrop to everything Saatvik is trying to do.

Who buys solar modules

  • IPPs (Independent Power Producers) — companies that build large solar farms and sell the electricity under long contracts. They buy modules by the hundreds of megawatts and are ferociously price-sensitive.
  • EPC contractors & government utilities — firms that build projects for others, plus state and central bodies (SECI, NTPC, state discoms) running tenders that increasingly demand Indian-made panels.
  • C&I and rooftop buyers — factories, commercial groups and distributors putting solar on their own roofs to cut power bills. A steadier, higher-margin slice than the giant utility orders, and a growing part of Saatvik's book.
02

What Saatvik actually sells: one panel, four ways to earn

A manufacturer that also builds, services and — now — owns solar. Read this once and the company clicks into place.

At its core Saatvik is a module maker — that is where most of the revenue comes from and where its factories are. But it has deliberately widened into a mixed manufacturer-plus-developer model, so that a customer can buy panels, or a finished project, or a serviced asset, or even power from a plant Saatvik owns. Those four activities look similar from outside and behave very differently underneath.

The further right you move, the less it looks like a factory and the more it looks like an infrastructure business — steadier cash, but far more capital tied up per rupee of profit.

The four revenue lines, decoded

  • Module manufacturing — the engine. Saatvik makes both older mono-PERC panels and the newer, higher-efficiency n-type TOPCon panels, including DCR (domestic-content) variants that qualify for protected government tenders. It has supplied over 5.5 GW of modules cumulatively and produced ~3,162 MW in FY26 alone at ~84% utilisation. This is where scale, cost and the technology race are fought.
  • EPC — building whole solar systems. Saatvik has taken utility EPC work (for example, contracts from Himachal Pradesh Power Corporation) and does rooftop and captive projects. EPC absorbs the company's own panels and deepens customer relationships, but it is lumpy, competitive project work rather than a factory annuity.
  • O&M — the long-tail service on installed plants. Small relative to manufacturing today, but it is the recurring, cycle-resistant thread: a plant Saatvik built or supplied keeps needing cleaning and upkeep for two decades.
  • IPP (new) — owning generation. Saatvik has signed a power-purchase agreement for a 41 MW solar project in Maharashtra and taken a 49% stake in Intelligent Hydel Solutions, its formal step into being a power producer, not just an equipment seller. This is the most capital-heavy, longest-payback line — and the biggest departure from being a pure manufacturer.
Why a manufacturer wants to own power plants

There is a logic to a panel maker drifting downstream into projects and generation: it creates a captive buyer for its own modules and smooths the feast-or-famine of merchant panel sales with contracted, long-dated cash flows. But it comes at a price. A gigawatt of module capacity is a factory you turn over several times a year; a gigawatt of owned solar farm is capital locked up for 25 years earning a regulated return. The further Saatvik walks down that flow diagram, the more it stops being a nimble, high-return manufacturer and starts becoming a capital-hungry infrastructure owner — a very different animal to value. For now the IPP and O&M lines are small; the question is how big management lets them grow relative to the factory that pays for it all.

Don't confuse the labels: Saatvik is primarily a module manufacturer — closer to a Waaree or a Vikram Solar than to a pure developer like Adani Green or a pure EPC contractor like Tata Power Solar. When you compare it, compare it against the panel makers; the EPC, O&M and IPP arms are extensions of the factory, not the main event.
03

The company, the family, and who controls it

A Haryana promoter family, a decade of quiet scaling, and a fresh listing.

Saatvik is a promoter-family business built up over the last decade by the Garg family, headquartered in Gurugram with its manufacturing rooted in Ambala, Haryana. It spent years as a mid-sized private module maker before riding India's solar-manufacturing boom into a September 2025 IPO. The promoters still own the clear majority of the company.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Garg family — with Neelesh Garg as Chairman & Managing Director and Manik Garg as Managing Director, alongside a professional CEO (Prashant Mathur) — hold roughly 76% of the equity after the IPO. That is a heavily promoter-controlled company; the public float is small, which magnifies share-price moves.
  • The operating footprint: module manufacturing in Ambala, Haryana (with an in-house EPE encapsulant line), a growing project (EPC/O&M) business, a new transformer unit via Melcon, and the planned Odisha cell-and-module complex at Ganjam/Gopalpur that is the centrepiece of the strategy.
  • Balance sheet: modest debt (~₹939 Cr) against a business earning ~30%+ returns on capital, freshly topped up with ~₹700 Cr of primary IPO cash. The capital question is not today's leverage — it is how much more the company will need to raise or borrow to fund cells, modules and IPP assets all at once.
The structure matters because the story is a build-out: almost everything interesting about Saatvik is capacity that does not exist yet — a cell plant, a bigger module plant, owned power assets. A tightly held company financing several large greenfield projects at once is asking shareholders to trust execution over a multi-year horizon. That is a very different proposition from a mature manufacturer harvesting an installed base.
04

The TOPCon shift: why the panel itself is changing

The technology upgrade quietly re-rating every module maker's product — and its risk.

For a decade the workhorse solar panel was mono-PERC. The industry is now switching, fast, to n-type TOPCon — a newer cell design that squeezes more electricity out of the same patch of roof. Saatvik makes both and is tilting hard toward TOPCon, which now anchors its highest-value products (including DCR TOPCon panels rated ~550–580 Wp). This is not a cosmetic upgrade; it changes the economics of the panel.

TOPCon panels convert a higher share of sunlight, degrade more slowly and perform better in heat and low light. More watts from the same land, mounting and wiring means a lower all-in cost of solar electricity — which is why developers now specify TOPCon and pay up for it.

The upgrade helps a maker like Saatvik on two fronts. Higher-efficiency panels command better pricing per watt and richer mix, and being early and credible on TOPCon keeps the company on developers' approved lists as the market moves on from PERC. The catch is the flip side of any technology transition: the equipment and cell recipes are newer and less proven at scale, and a manufacturer that over-invests in one generation just as the next arrives can be left with stranded lines — a real hazard in an industry where cell technology has turned over roughly every few years.

The tell in Saatvik's own factory

Saatvik has quietly integrated one step below the panel that most people never think about: it built its own EPE encapsulant line — the specialised polymer film that seals the cells inside the glass and protects them from moisture for 25 years. Encapsulant is only a few percent of a module's cost, so making it in-house is not about the film itself. It is a signal of intent: a company that bothers to backward-integrate the sticky film is telling you it plans to keep integrating upstream — and the far bigger prize one step further back is the cell. The encapsulant line is the small move that rehearses the big one.

05

The cell gap: the missing 60% of the panel

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to where Saatvik sits in the chain.

Here is the fact that governs Saatvik's entire strategy, and it hides in plain sight inside every panel the company ships. A solar module's cost is not evenly spread across its parts. The solar cell — the treated silicon that actually makes the electricity — is by far the largest slice, typically well over half of what the finished panel costs to build. And Saatvik, today, makes none of its own cells. It buys them in, largely imported, and laminates them into panels. Look at where the money in a module actually goes.

This is the whole company in one chart. Saatvik adds real value — laminating, wiring, framing, testing and warranting a panel is skilled, capital-intensive work — but on the most valuable ingredient it is a reseller. Every panel it ships sends the majority of its bill-of-materials value to a cell supplier, often in China. That is why the module business, for all its growth, is structurally a thin-margin assembly step: the deep margin pools sit upstream, in the cell and the wafer, where India barely competes.

Saatvik's cell capacity today is essentially zero. The Odisha complex at Ganjam/Gopalpur is designed to add 2.4 GW of cells (Phase I, with 4 GW of modules) and a further 3.6 GW (Phase II) — the deliberate move to make in-house the part it currently buys.
The metric that will settle Saatvik's thesis: cell self-sufficiency

Forget, for a moment, the order book and the revenue-growth headline. The one number that decides whether Saatvik becomes a durable, high-margin business or stays a fast-growing assembler is the share of its own module output backed by its own cells — call it cell self-sufficiency, and today it is roughly zero. Every point that number climbs as the Odisha cell lines come up does two things at once: it captures the fat cell margin that currently leaks to importers, and it hardens Saatvik against the day the ALMM shield is extended from modules to cells (which is the stated policy direction). A module maker with its own cells is a fundamentally sturdier company than one without. Watch that ratio — not the revenue line — quarter by quarter as Odisha ramps. It is the scoreboard.

A genuine surprise: the entire bull case for Saatvik rests on a factory that does not yet exist. The company's headline growth is real and already in the accounts — but the thing that would make that growth defensible, the cell plant, is a greenfield project in Odisha still in the building. You are, in effect, being asked to pay today for margins that only arrive if a not-yet-built plant comes up on time, on budget, and into a cell market that hasn't been flooded by everyone else building the same thing.
06

The policy greenhouse: ALMM, BCD, DCR and the export window

Why an Indian module maker earns 30%+ returns — and why that is both the moat and the risk.

Saatvik's economics are not the economics of a free-market panel maker. They are the economics of a protected one. A stack of government policies deliberately tilts the field toward Indian manufacturers, and understanding that stack explains both why returns are so high today and why the bear case is so focused on their durability.

The four policy pillars propping up Indian module makers
PolicyWhat it doesWhy it helps Saatvik
ALMMThe "Approved List of Models & Manufacturers" — government-backed projects can only use panels (and, increasingly, cells) from an approved, mostly domestic list.Effectively fences imported Chinese modules out of a large protected market. Being on the ALMM is a licence to sell into it — Saatvik is preparing for an ALMM cell inspection as it integrates.
BCDA "Basic Customs Duty" of ~40% on imported modules and ~25% on imported cells.Makes foreign panels structurally more expensive than domestic ones, handing Indian makers a price umbrella. Note the asymmetry: modules are protected harder than cells — which is exactly why cell-making is the next frontier.
DCR"Domestic Content Requirement" tenders — certain government schemes (rooftop, PM-KUSUM, government solar) mandate Indian-made cells and modules.Creates a premium sub-market for fully-domestic panels that command higher prices. Saatvik makes DCR TOPCon modules — but true DCR compliance ultimately needs domestic cells, another pull toward Odisha.
PLI & the export windowProduction-Linked Incentives reward integrated manufacturing; separately, US anti-dumping action against Chinese/South-East-Asian panels has opened an export door for Indian modules.PLI subsidises the very backward integration Saatvik is pursuing; the US window offers a large, high-priced export market for Indian-made panels that sidestep the China tariffs.
The insight that makes sense of the whole sector

India's solar-manufacturing boom is, at bottom, a policy-manufactured boom. Left to a free market, Chinese modules — made at colossal scale and often below cost — would undercut almost any Indian factory. BCD, ALMM and DCR exist precisely to prevent that, and the fat returns Indian makers earn are the rent from that protection. This is not a criticism; it is the operating reality. It means two things for Saatvik. First, its margins are a policy variable, not just an operating one — they live or die on the shelter staying in place. Second, the protection is being deliberately ratcheted up the value chain, from modules toward cells, which rewards exactly the company that integrates backward fastest. The policy that protects Saatvik is also the policy pushing it to build cells.

The export angle is the sweetener on top. Because the United States has slapped anti-dumping and countervailing duties on panels routed through China and South-East Asia, Indian modules have — for now — a privileged path into a large, high-margin American market. That window is a genuine opportunity for a cost-competitive maker, but it is also a trade-policy variable that could narrow as quickly as it opened; it is upside to underwrite cautiously, not a foundation to build on.

07

The China problem: overcapacity and the price crash

The cyclical danger that hangs over every solar manufacturer on earth.

If policy is the tailwind, global oversupply is the gale blowing the other way. The world — overwhelmingly China — has built solar-manufacturing capacity far faster than demand has grown, and the result has been a brutal, multi-year collapse in module prices. Panels that sold for a certain price a few years ago now sell for a fraction of it. For a manufacturer, a falling selling price on a commodity product is an ever-present threat to margins, and no Indian maker is fully insulated from it.

Two forces pulling in opposite directions
ForceWhat it meansEffect on Saatvik
Chinese overcapacityChina holds a large multiple of global module demand in installed capacity, much of it running below cost to hold share.Depresses world module prices continuously; the ever-present risk that the protective duty wall is overwhelmed or eroded.
Cheap imported inputsThe same glut makes imported cells and wafers cheap.A double-edge: cheap cells help a module assembler's costs today — but that same cheapness is the reason building your own cell plant is risky.
India's duty wallBCD + ALMM keep the Indian market priced above the global floor.The single reason Saatvik's domestic margins hold up while global prices fall — and thus the thing to watch most closely.
Domestic capacity raceEvery Indian maker — Waaree, Premier, Vikram, Adani, Emmvee and more — is expanding modules and cells at once.Risks recreating China's oversupply inside the Indian wall, competing away the very margins that justified the build-out.
The paradox Saatvik has to navigate

Cheap Chinese cells are simultaneously Saatvik's friend and its enemy. As a module assembler, the company benefits every time imported cells get cheaper — its biggest input cost falls. But its whole strategic plan is to spend heavily building its own cell capacity in Odisha, and a cell factory only pays off if cells stay expensive enough (or protected enough) to justify making them at home. So Saatvik is investing billions to escape a dependency that is, right now, doing it a favour. The bet is that the policy wall around cells will rise (BCD and ALMM extending to cells) faster than global cell prices fall — that domestic cells will be protected into profitability. If that timing is wrong, the Odisha cell lines could open into a market already awash in cheap silicon.

The cyclicality is the deep risk, not the day-to-day one: Saatvik's near-term order book gives real revenue visibility, but solar manufacturing has a long history of savage boom-bust cycles driven by capacity gluts. A young, expanding, protection-dependent maker is most exposed at exactly the moment a cycle turns and the industry's collective new capacity floods a market — verify current module and cell pricing and the state of the duty regime before extrapolating any recent margin.
08

The capacity ramp — and who pays for it

A near-doubling of the factory footprint, funded while three build-outs run at once.

Saatvik's plan is unusually front-loaded with capital spending. In roughly two years it intends to take module capacity from ~4.86 GW to ~8.8 GW (an ~83% jump), stand up its first-ever cell lines (2.4 GW rising to ~6 GW in Odisha), and seed an IPP portfolio — all more or less simultaneously. That is a lot of concrete and equipment for a company its size, and how it is financed matters as much as whether the plants get built.

The IPO helped: the ~₹700 Cr of fresh primary capital raised in September 2025 was earmarked largely for this expansion. But a cell fab is one of the more expensive things to build in the solar chain, and doing cells and extra modules and owned IPP assets together is a bigger cheque than one IPO covers. So the realistic expectation is more capital — additional debt, possibly further equity — over the build phase. That is the capex/leverage tension at the heart of the story: the company is highly profitable today, but it is choosing to plough that profitability (and more) into assets that only earn later.

Why the funding path is the quiet risk

A company running its factories at ~84% utilisation with a multi-gigawatt order book has an obvious reason to expand — demand is real and the lines are full. The danger is not the logic of the build-out; it is the simultaneity. Financing a cell fab, a module doubling and an IPP portfolio at the same time stretches both the balance sheet and management's attention. Every large greenfield project carries slippage risk on cost and timeline, and Saatvik has stacked several on top of each other with limited listed-company operating history to point to. The reward if it lands is a genuinely integrated, protected, high-return manufacturer. The risk is a young company that over-reached its capital and its bandwidth in one cycle.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, fast-consolidating field of Indian module makers racing to integrate.

Saatvik is one of many Indian solar manufacturers all chasing the same protected market and the same backward-integration prize. The listed peer set has expanded rapidly — several of these names IPO'd around the same time — and they differ mainly in scale and how far up the value chain each has integrated. That second axis is the one that increasingly separates them.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, this is a scale-and-integration race, not a branding one — panels are close to a commodity, so the winners are those who make more of the chain (cells, ideally wafers) at the lowest cost inside the policy wall. On that map, Saatvik is a credible mid-scale maker that is behind the most integrated peers (Premier, Waaree) and racing to catch up via Odisha. Second, everyone is expanding into cells at the same time, which raises the real possibility that India recreates a milder version of China's oversupply within its own protected market — turning today's fat margins into tomorrow's ordinary ones. Saatvik's job is to get its cell capacity up while the shelter still pays, and to stay cost-competitive when it doesn't.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-return young manufacturer, asked to prove its best years aren't behind the policy wall.

Saatvik enters its life as a listed company with a rare combination: rapid growth, high returns, a full order book, and a clear strategic plan to integrate backward into cells. What it has to prove is that these are the foundations of a durable manufacturer and not the peak of a protected, cyclical surge — and that it can build a great deal of new capacity, on time and on budget, before the industry's collective expansion competes the easy margins away.

The bull case

  • Structural solar demand — India's ~500 GW non-fossil target needs enormous domestic module and cell supply for years; Saatvik's lines run near-full at ~84%.
  • Policy shelter — ALMM, BCD and DCR keep Indian makers priced above the global floor, and the protection is ratcheting up the chain toward cells.
  • Backward integration — the Odisha cell plant, if delivered, captures the ~58% of a panel's value Saatvik currently buys in, structurally lifting margin.
  • Technology & mix — an early, credible tilt to high-efficiency n-TOPCon and DCR modules keeps it on developers' approved lists.
  • Export optionality — the US anti-dumping window on Chinese/SE-Asian panels offers a large, high-priced market for Indian modules.

The bear case

  • Cell execution risk — the entire margin upside hinges on a not-yet-built greenfield cell fab coming up on time, on budget, and into a healthy cell price.
  • Commodity cyclicality — Chinese overcapacity keeps global module prices falling; a glut inside India's own wall could compress margins hard.
  • Policy dependence — high returns are rent from ALMM/BCD/DCR; any dilution of that protection hits the P&L directly.
  • Capex & funding strain — cells, modules and IPP assets funded together will likely need more debt or equity; margin already slipped from ~15% to ~12%.
  • Young, tightly held, concentrated — short listed history, ~76% promoter holding, a small float, and reliance on large lumpy orders and key customers.
The bottom line

Saatvik Green Energy is a genuinely good manufacturer caught at an inflection: it has ridden India's policy-built solar boom to explosive growth and ~30%+ returns, but it sells a panel whose most valuable part it doesn't yet make, inside a shelter that everyone is racing to fill. Its answer — build the cell plant in Odisha, integrate the margin, and get there before the crowd — is the right one, and the right one is also the hard one. The next two years hinge on a single, checkable milestone: the Odisha cell lines coming online and lifting Saatvik's margin per watt as they replace imported cells. Watch that ramp and that margin together. If the cells arrive and the margin turns up, this is a young integrated manufacturer worth far more than today's below-IPO price. If Odisha slips while module prices fall, FY26's returns were the high-water mark of a protected cycle — and the market's scepticism was the right call all along.
