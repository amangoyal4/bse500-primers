Elecon Engineering, the gearbox at the heart of heavy industry

A complete walk through India's largest maker of industrial gears — the hidden power-transmission components that step torque up or down inside almost every heavy machine — plus a lumpier material-handling business bolted alongside. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Elecon Engineering makes industrial gears — specifically gearboxes, the sealed metal boxes of meshing gears that sit inside heavy machinery and convert the raw spinning of a motor into the exact speed and turning-force (torque) a machine needs. It is India's largest industrial-gear manufacturer, and gearing throws off roughly 72% of its revenue and the large majority of its profit. Alongside that it runs a second, very different business — material handling equipment (MHE): the giant conveyors, stackers, reclaimers and cranes that move bulk materials like coal, ore and cement around ports, power plants and mines. Its customers span cement, steel, power, mining, sugar, ports, marine, defence and wind — anywhere a big machine needs to turn.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Two businesses, one carries the quality. The gears (transmission) segment — about ₹1,699 Cr of FY26 revenue — is the crown jewel: high-margin, engineered-to-spec, market-leading, and blessed with a recurring aftermarket of spares, service and replacement. The material-handling segment — roughly ₹662 Cr — is a lumpy, competitive, project/EPC-style business with thinner and more volatile margins. Most of what makes Elecon a quality name lives in the gears.
  • It is a quietly global engineering house. Elecon is not just a domestic gear-maker. It owns two long-established European/US transmission brands — Benzlers (Sweden) and Radicon (UK/US) — bought in 2010, and exports gears into Europe, the Americas and beyond. Overseas is already ~29% of revenue, and management wants exports to reach ~50% of revenue by FY30.
  • Strong, near-debt-free, but FY26 profit dipped. Elecon sits on ~₹700 Cr of net cash and has run a high return on capital for years. But FY26 was a wobble: revenue rose ~6% to ~₹2,366 Cr while reported PAT fell to ~₹341 Cr (from ~₹415 Cr), dragged by a one-off ~₹102 Cr goodwill impairment on the overseas gear business and softer project execution. The order book, though, jumped ~36% to ~₹1,292 Cr. The stock trades near 33× earnings — a premium the market pays for the gear franchise's quality.
In plain terms

A gearbox is the unglamorous, invisible component that decides whether a giant machine works. A cement kiln, a steel rolling mill, a ship's propeller shaft, a port crane, a sugar mill — each has a motor spinning far too fast and far too weakly to do the actual job, so a gearbox sits in between, trading speed for force (or the reverse). Elecon is India's biggest maker of those boxes. That business is excellent: the gearbox is spec'd into the machine, it's mission-critical, it wears out and needs spares, and Elecon is the share leader, so it earns fat margins and a steady annuity of service work. Bolted onto it is a rougher, bulk-materials machinery business that wins big lumpy projects at thinner margins. The investment case is really: how much of Elecon stays the high-quality gear-and-aftermarket company, and how much drag the projects leg adds.

01

Gears 101: the component you never see but always need

No engineering degree needed — just why a spinning motor is useless without a gearbox.

Start with a problem. An electric motor spins fast and weakly — say a few hundred or thousand revolutions a minute, with modest turning force. But almost nothing in heavy industry actually wants that. A cement kiln must rotate slowly and with enormous force. A conveyor belt must crawl but pull tonnes. A ship's propeller must turn at its own particular speed. The motor's raw output has to be converted — and the machine that does the converting is a gearbox. It is, quite literally, the translator between what a motor produces and what a machine needs.

Inside a gearbox, gears of different sizes mesh together. A small gear driving a large one trades speed for force: the output turns slower but pushes far harder (this is reduction, and most industrial gearboxes are speed reducers). A large gear driving a small one does the reverse. Get the ratio right and you can take one standard motor and make it do a thousand different jobs. That is the entire value of the product — and the reason it must be engineered, not commoditised.

Why a gearbox is a good thing to sell

A gearbox has four features that a shrewd investor loves in a component. It is mission-critical — the machine physically does not run without it, so nobody buys the cheapest one and hopes. It is spec'd in — the machine is designed around a particular gearbox, so once you're in, you're hard to displace. It is engineered, not commoditised — a custom reducer for a steel mill is a design problem, not a price-per-kilo of steel. And it wears out — gears run under punishing loads for years, so the installed base is a permanent stream of spares and service. Put those together and you get exactly what Elecon has in its gears leg: pricing power for the market leader, high switching costs, and a recurring annuity. That is a fundamentally better business than bending steel into a conveyor.

Where Elecon's gearboxes actually go

  • Cement — kiln drives, mills, crushers. Slow, brutal, continuous duty — the classic heavy-gear application.
  • Steel & metals — rolling mills, furnaces, casters, where gearboxes take colossal loads and downtime is ruinously expensive.
  • Power — coal, hydro and increasingly renewables; pulverisers, cooling systems, and drives across the plant.
  • Ports, mining, sugar, rubber, plastics — the broad industrial base of conveyors, crushers, mixers and mills.
  • Marine & defence — propulsion and specialised gearing, a higher-spec, higher-barrier niche with strategic pull (indigenisation of naval and defence gearing).
  • Wind — gearboxes for wind turbines, a fast-growing renewable-energy application that rides the same clean-power wave as the rest of the market.
02

The two segments: one quality engine, one project machine

Read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Elecon reports in two segments, and they could hardly be more different in character. Transmission Equipment (gears) is a product business: catalogue and custom gearboxes, couplings and drives, sold to thousands of industrial customers with a rich aftermarket behind them. Material Handling Equipment (MHE) is a project business: large, bespoke bulk-handling systems won in competitive tenders and executed over months or years. Same company, two economic animals.

The two segments, decoded

  • Transmission Equipment (gears) — the core. Elecon designs and builds the full family of industrial gearboxes (helical, bevel-helical, planetary, worm, marine, wind) plus couplings, sold to a wide, diversified customer base. Because gearboxes are engineered and spec'd-in, the business earns strong margins and enjoys real pricing power as the domestic market leader. Behind the new-equipment sales sits the aftermarket — the spares and service on gearboxes already in the field — which is smaller in rupees but higher in margin and far more predictable.
  • Material Handling Equipment (MHE) — the project arm. Elecon designs, builds and installs large bulk-material-handling systems: conveyors, stackers, reclaimers, wagon tipplers, and cranes for ports, power plants, cement works and mines. This is an EPC-style business — big orders, competitive bidding, execution over long periods, exposure to commodity input costs and to the customer's own site delays. Order intake can be strong (as it was in FY26) but the revenue is lumpy and the margins are thinner and more volatile than gears.
The distinction that decides the multiple

Product businesses and project businesses are valued differently, and for good reason. A product like a gearbox recurs — customers keep coming back for more units and for spares, and the revenue is diversified across thousands of buyers, so it is predictable and highly-rated. A project business is lumpy — a few big orders drive the year, a delayed site or a mispriced tender can wreck a quarter, and the earnings are inherently harder to forecast. Elecon is both at once. The reason it trades at a premium industrial multiple is the gears; the reason that multiple isn't even higher is that the material-handling leg keeps some project-business volatility in the reported numbers. Watch the mix and you understand the valuation.

03

The gears franchise: why market leadership compounds

What makes Elecon's core business genuinely hard to replicate.

Being India's largest industrial-gear maker is not a trophy — it is a machine that feeds itself. In a spec'd-in, mission-critical component, leadership brings advantages that a smaller rival simply cannot match, and those advantages compound quietly year after year. Understanding why is the difference between seeing Elecon as "a capital-goods company" and seeing why it earns the margins it does.

The four compounding advantages of gear leadership

  • Reference base. A steel mill or cement plant buying a mission-critical gearbox wants proof it will run for decades. Elecon has thousands of installations across every heavy industry — a track record a new entrant cannot fabricate. In critical components, the incumbent with the longest reference list keeps winning.
  • Breadth of catalogue. Because Elecon makes every major gear family across a vast power range, a customer can standardise on one supplier for a whole plant. That breadth is expensive to build and creates one-stop convenience that fragments a rival's opportunity.
  • Engineering depth & IP. Custom gearing for a specific duty — a marine drive, a defence application, a large planetary reducer — is a design capability, protected by know-how and testing infrastructure. This is where the margin and the moat live, and where scale funds the R&D that keeps you ahead.
  • The installed base. Every gearbox sold today becomes tomorrow's aftermarket customer — spares, service, refurbishment, replacement. The leader with the largest installed base automatically owns the largest annuity. This is the single most under-appreciated feature of the business, and it gets its own section next.
A genuine surprise: the gearbox is one of the most ubiquitous machines in industry and almost nobody outside engineering has heard of the companies that make them. There is a gearbox inside the cement kiln that built your city, the steel mill that made your car, the crane that unloaded your imports, and the ship that carried them. Elecon is India's number one in that hidden layer — and, more surprising still, it doesn't just serve India: it owns European and American gear brands and sells transmissions back into the industrial heartlands that invented them. A Gujarat engineering firm quietly exporting gearboxes to Europe is not the picture most people hold of Indian capital goods.
Why the leader's margin is structural, not luck

In a commoditised product, the biggest player often competes hardest on price and earns the thinnest margins. Gears invert that. Because the product is spec'd-in and mission-critical, the customer's real fear is failure, not price — a gearbox that fails halts a whole plant, dwarfing any saving on the box itself. So the trusted leader can price for reliability and engineering, not for the lowest bid. Add the aftermarket, where the leader faces almost no competition on its own installed base, and the margin structure is durable rather than a cyclical fluke. That is why gears, not material handling, is the part of Elecon that deserves a premium multiple — and why the FY26 goodwill hit, which sat in an overseas gear subsidiary, is worth separating from the health of the core Indian franchise.

04

The aftermarket annuity: the installed base that keeps paying

The quiet, recurring engine that a first-time reader almost always misses.

Here is the mechanism that turns Elecon's gears leg from "a good product" into "a compounding franchise". When Elecon sells a gearbox, it books that revenue once. But the gearbox then runs for fifteen, twenty, thirty years under heavy load — and across those years it needs spare parts, inspection, servicing, refurbishment, and eventually replacement. Almost all of that work flows back to the original maker, because the maker knows the design, holds the drawings, and the customer will not risk a mission-critical drive on an unknown spare. So every unit sold is not just one sale — it is the start of a decades-long relationship.

Now compound that. Each year of gearbox sales adds a fresh cohort to the installed base, and no cohort ever leaves — a gearbox sold in 2010 is still generating service revenue today, on top of everything sold since. The installed base only grows, so the aftermarket annuity only thickens. Over time this becomes a large, high-margin, recurring stream that behaves nothing like the lumpy project world — it keeps paying whether or not new-equipment orders are booming.

The device that fits Elecon and almost no one else: the base that pays

Most industrial companies you can value on this year's order book. Elecon needs two lenses at once — the flow (new gearbox and MHE orders, which are cyclical) and the stock (the installed base of gearboxes already spinning in thousands of plants, which quietly pays out spares and service for decades). The flow rides the industrial-capex cycle up and down. The stock does not — it grows every single year and pays through the downturns. A cement slump can freeze new-kiln orders, but every kiln already running still needs its gearbox serviced. So the right way to track Elecon is not just "did orders grow this quarter?" but "did the aftermarket-and-export share of gears keep climbing, and did the gear margin hold while the base got bigger?" The installed base is the asset that never appears cleanly on the balance sheet and matters most.

Why this changes how you read a soft year: in FY26 Elecon's reported profit fell, but a chunk of that was a one-off goodwill write-down and some project-execution softness — not the gears annuity drying up. The aftermarket doesn't lurch the way a project quarter does. If you judge a gear franchise only by its lumpiest project numbers, you will misprice the steady half. Separate the recurring gear-and-service base from the lumpy project leg, and the quality of the underlying business is clearer than the headline PAT suggests.
05

The company, the family, and how it got here

A 74-year-old Gujarat engineering house controlled by the Patel family.

Elecon Engineering was founded in 1951 and is based in Vallabh Vidyanagar, Gujarat — a company older than most of India's stock market. It is controlled by the Patel family, with veteran industrialist Prayasvin B. Patel as Chairman & Managing Director. Over seven decades it grew from a local engineering workshop into the country's largest industrial-gear maker and a serious material-handling contractor, then went global by acquisition.

Timeline

  • 1951
    Elecon Engineering founded in Gujarat, entering industrial equipment and gearing — the start of a long build into Indian heavy engineering.
  • 1960s–1990s
    Grows into both industrial gears and material handling, becoming India's leading domestic gearbox maker and a major bulk-handling contractor for power, cement and ports.
  • 2010
    Goes global: acquires the Benzlers (Sweden) and Radicon (UK/US) gear brands from David Brown Gear Systems — an Indian firm buying 70-year-old European transmission houses and a manufacturing/sales footprint across Europe and the Americas.
  • 2010s
    Integrates the overseas brands, expands the export push, and modernises domestic gear manufacturing; balance sheet is progressively de-geared toward net cash.
  • FY25
    Revenue ~₹2,227 Cr, PAT ~₹415 Cr — a strong, high-return year with the gears leg leading and net cash on the books.
  • FY26
    Revenue ~₹2,366 Cr (+6%); reported PAT ~₹341 Cr, dented by a ~₹102 Cr goodwill impairment in the overseas gear business and softer MHE execution; order book jumps ~36% to ~₹1,292 Cr; sets an export target of ~50% of revenue by FY30.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Patel family holds roughly ~59% (approx.) of Elecon, a firm controlling stake led by Chairman Prayasvin B. Patel. As always, verify the exact promoter holding and any pledge in the latest shareholding filing.
  • The group map (worth keeping straight): Elecon Engineering (ELECON) is the gears + material-handling company in this primer. Benzlers and Radicon are its overseas gear brands/subsidiaries. Note the separately listed Eimco Elecon (India) — a related mining-equipment company in the broader Elecon orbit — is a different listed entity; don't conflate the two.
  • Balance sheet: net cash of ~₹700 Cr and a long record of high return on capital (ROCE historically around the ~30% mark, approx.). This is a company that funds its own growth and pays dividends rather than leaning on debt.
The structural quirk to hold in mind: Elecon's global footprint is a genuine strength and the source of FY26's headline blemish. The overseas gear brands give it export reach and European engineering pedigree — but they also carry goodwill on the books, and when European industrial demand softens, that goodwill can be written down, as ~₹102 Cr was in FY26. The domestic gear franchise and the overseas subsidiaries are the same "gears" story but with different risk: one is a share-leading home market, the other is exposure to the European industrial cycle.
06

The margin split: where the profit really comes from

The single most important lens on Elecon — and it is specific to this company.

If you remember one idea from this primer, make it this: Elecon's revenue and its profit are split very differently between its two segments. Gears are ~72% of revenue but an even larger share of profit, because gear margins are structurally higher and steadier. Material handling is ~28% of revenue but a smaller share of profit, because its margins are thinner and its execution lumpier. The business improves not when total revenue grows, but when the gear-and-aftermarket mix grows — and it wobbles when the project leg or an overseas write-down intrudes.

This is why a first-timer can be misled by the headline. When Elecon reports revenue up but profit down — as in FY26 — the instinct is to conclude the business weakened. But the profit dip came largely from a one-off overseas goodwill write-down and softer project execution in material handling, while the core Indian gears-and-aftermarket engine remained the high-quality part. Conversely, a year where MHE revenue surges can flatter the top line while doing little for the margin. Revenue growth and profit quality are not the same thing at Elecon, and separating them is the whole skill.

The non-transferable test: track the mix, not the total

Ask of any Elecon quarter: did gear-segment revenue grow, and did the gear-segment margin hold while it did? Did the aftermarket-and-export share of gears climb? Those questions survive no other company's name swapped in — they only make sense for a gear-leader with a project leg bolted on and overseas brands in the mix. A rising total driven by low-margin MHE projects is a weaker signal than a steady total with a fattening gear mix. The company gets structurally better when the box that does the converting — the high-margin, aftermarket-rich gearbox — becomes a bigger slice of the whole. That is the mix to watch, quarter after quarter.

07

Material handling: the lumpy leg you have to price in

Not the quality core — but too big to ignore, and it moves the reported numbers.

Elecon's material-handling equipment business is the part that behaves like a construction contractor rather than a component maker. It designs, builds and installs the big machinery that moves bulk materials — conveyors, stackers, reclaimers, wagon tipplers, crushers and cranes — for ports, power stations, cement plants, steel works and mines. When a power plant needs a coal-handling system or a port needs a bulk terminal, this is the kind of work Elecon bids for.

The economics are genuinely different from gears. These are large, one-off orders won in competitive tenders, executed over long periods, with revenue recognised as the project progresses. That means the segment is lumpy (a couple of big wins or slips swing the year), margin-thinner (competitive bidding compresses pricing), and execution-exposed (site delays, the customer's own readiness, and input-cost moves like steel all land on Elecon). In FY26, management flagged exactly this: strong order intake in MHE, but "temporary softness in project execution" and pricing pressure from competitors carrying lower-cost inventory.

Why keep a lower-margin leg at all?

Two honest reasons. First, MHE is a real business with real demand — India's build-out of ports, power and infrastructure needs bulk-handling systems, and Elecon has the engineering and reference base to win them. Second, order intake there can be strong and lift the group's book (FY26's closing order book jumped ~36%), giving revenue visibility even when it comes at a thinner margin. But make no mistake about its role in the investment case: material handling is the drag on quality, not the source of it. It adds cyclicality and execution risk to a company whose premium rating rests on the steadier gears. The best outcome for shareholders is usually MHE executing cleanly and staying the minority — letting the gears set the tone.

The MHE leg is where a bad quarter usually originates — a slipped project, a mispriced tender, or a steel-cost spike. When Elecon's stock reacts sharply to a result (as it did after Q1 FY27, when the shares fell ~6% on execution softness and pricing pressure), it is far more often the project leg than the gears that is the culprit. Read a weak print by asking which segment moved before concluding anything about the franchise.
08

Exports & global brands: the gear-maker that sells back to Europe

The overseas push that reframes Elecon from a domestic name into a global engineering play.

The most under-appreciated part of Elecon is how global its gears business already is. Through the 2010 acquisition of Benzlers (Sweden) and Radicon (UK/US) from David Brown Gear Systems, Elecon owns established transmission brands with decades of pedigree, manufacturing and sales channels across Europe and the Americas. Overseas is already around 29% of consolidated revenue, and management has set an explicit ambition: exports to reach ~50% of revenue by FY30.

This matters for three reasons. It diversifies Elecon beyond the Indian industrial cycle, spreading demand across multiple economies. It gives access to higher-value, higher-spec gearing markets where European brands command trust. And it turns a domestic leader into a genuine global gear exporter — a rare thing for an Indian capital-goods firm, and a structurally more valuable one than a purely home-market player.

The double edge of owning European brands

The overseas franchise is a real asset — but it is exposure, not just upside. When European industry hums, Benzlers and Radicon widen Elecon's market and lift the export mix toward that 50% goal. When Europe softens, the same subsidiaries feel the demand dip, currency swings cut both ways, and the goodwill on the books can be written down — which is precisely what produced FY26's ~₹102 Cr impairment. So exports are the growth engine and a source of cyclicality at once. The right way to hold it: the export ramp toward ~50% is the prize; the European industrial cycle and FX are the price of admission.

09

The capex cycle and the rivals

What drives demand — and who Elecon is fighting in each segment.

Elecon's new-equipment demand — both gears and material handling — rides the industrial and infrastructure capital-expenditure cycle. When cement, steel, power, mining and ports build new capacity, they order new gearboxes and new bulk-handling systems. That is the tailwind and the risk in one: a broad Indian capex up-cycle lifts Elecon's order book (as FY26's ~36% jump showed), while a capex slowdown hits new orders. Crucially, the aftermarket annuity keeps paying through the dips — but the growth is a capex story.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two takeaways. First, Elecon is not one competitive contest but two: in gears it is the domestic leader defending a high-margin, spec'd-in franchise against global majors and a focused peer in Shanthi Gears — a fight it is well-placed to win at the critical end. In material handling it is one bidder among larger EPC players in a thin-margin tender market — a much harder place to earn a premium. Second, the deepest moat isn't visible in any tender: it is the installed base. A rival can undercut Elecon on a new gearbox bid; it cannot easily touch the decades of spares and service on the machines Elecon already put in the field. Competition sets the price of new orders; the installed base sets the floor under the profits.

Financials & valuation

A high-quality, cash-rich franchise — priced at a premium, after a stumble year.

FY26 was a study in the gap between headline and quality. Revenue grew modestly, the order book surged, the balance sheet stayed cash-rich — but reported profit fell on a one-off write-down and softer project execution. The market still affords Elecon a premium multiple for the gear franchise; the debate is whether the gears keep leading and the drags stay temporary.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality gear leader in a capex up-cycle — asked whether the mix keeps improving.

Elecon enters FY27 as what it has long been: India's leading industrial-gear maker, cash-rich, high-return, with a growing export franchise and a lumpier project leg alongside. The debate is not whether the gears business is good — it plainly is — but whether the mix keeps shifting toward the high-margin gear-and-aftermarket core fast enough to earn its premium, while the project drag and overseas cyclicality stay contained.

The bull case

  • Market-leading gear franchise — spec'd-in, mission-critical component with pricing power, high margins and a diversified customer base across every heavy industry.
  • Aftermarket annuity — a growing installed base of gearboxes throwing off recurring, high-margin spares and service that pays through the cycle.
  • Export ramp & global brands — Benzlers/Radicon plus new geographies, targeting ~50% of revenue by FY30 — a rare global Indian gear play.
  • Capex tailwind — India's industrial and infrastructure build-out drives new-gear and material-handling demand; order book already up ~36%.
  • Fortress balance sheet — ~₹700 Cr net cash and high ROCE; funds its own growth and pays dividends.

The bear case

  • Industrial-capex cyclicality — new-gear and MHE demand rides cement/steel/power/mining/infra capex; a slowdown hits new orders (the aftermarket cushions, but not the growth).
  • Material-handling drag — a lumpy, competitive, thin-margin project leg with execution and input-cost risk that repeatedly bruises reported quarters.
  • Overseas & FX exposure — Benzlers/Radicon tie earnings to the European industrial cycle; goodwill write-downs (like FY26's ~₹102 Cr) and currency swings cut both ways.
  • Stiff competition — global majors (SEW-Eurodrive, Flender, Bonfiglioli, David Brown) and a focused domestic peer (Shanthi Gears) in gears; L&T, TRF, thyssenkrupp in material handling.
  • Premium valuation — ~33× earnings prices in continued high-quality growth; a soft gear margin or stalled export ramp can de-rate it.
The bottom line

Elecon is, at its heart, one of the better businesses in Indian capital goods — the country's number-one maker of a hidden, mission-critical component, wrapped around a compounding aftermarket and a genuinely global brand footprint. What holds it back from being a pure quality name is the material-handling leg and the overseas cyclicality that periodically muddy an otherwise clean franchise, as FY26's revenue-up-profit-down print showed. The next few years answer one checkable question: does the export mix keep climbing toward the stated ~50%-of-revenue-by-FY30 mark while the gear margin holds and the project leg executes without more write-downs? Watch that export ramp and the gear-segment margin together each year — they, far more than the headline PAT, will tell you whether Elecon is widening its best business or merely defending it.
