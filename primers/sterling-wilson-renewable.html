00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Sterling and Wilson Renewable Energy is a solar EPC contractor — it engineers, procures and constructs large solar power plants for other people to own. When a developer or a state utility wants a 500-megawatt solar farm, they hand Sterling & Wilson a patch of land and a deadline; the company designs the plant, buys the panels, inverters, cabling and steel, builds the whole thing, connects it to the grid, and hands back a working power station. It is one of the largest pure-play solar EPC firms in the world — a cumulative portfolio of roughly 27 GWp of EPC across 28 countries — and it keeps earning after handover by running many of those plants under long-term operations-and-maintenance (O&M) contracts. Crucially, it is now a Reliance-anchored company: Reliance New Energy is its single largest shareholder and co-promoter.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It builds solar plants; it does not make panels or sell power. This is the distinction that trips people up. Sterling & Wilson is not a module manufacturer like Waaree or Vikram Solar (it buys panels), and it is not an independent power producer (IPP) that owns solar farms and sells electricity for decades. It is the builder-for-hire — paid a contract price to construct the asset, then a small annual fee to maintain it. Its economics are those of a large construction/engineering contractor, not a manufacturer or a utility.
  • The profits lurch — violently. FY26 was a record revenue year (₹7,548 Cr) that still ended in a net loss of ~₹296 Cr. That is not a typo: an EPC firm can grow the top line and lose money at the bottom in the same year, because margins are thin, projects are lumpy, and a single mis-priced contract or commodity spike can swallow a year's profit. The order book — a record ₹11,813 Cr unexecuted — is the gauge investors actually watch, because it is the promise of tomorrow's revenue.
  • It went to the brink, and Reliance pulled it back. After a 2019 IPO, the company nearly collapsed — its own founding promoters (the Shapoorji Pallonji group) failed to repay ₹2,000-crore-plus of loans owed to it, overseas projects soured, ratings fell to 'D' (default) in 2023. Then Reliance bought in, recapitalised it, and the company pivoted hard from a global order book back to India, riding the country's solar-capex supercycle. FY26's ~4.5 GW of commissioning — roughly 15% of India's utility-scale solar built that year — is the proof the turnaround is real.
In plain terms

Picture a specialist construction firm that only builds one kind of thing — enormous solar farms — anywhere in the world, on a fixed-price contract. It doesn't own the finished plant and it doesn't make the parts; it assembles them expertly and hands over the keys. That business is a volume game with a thin, fragile margin: win enough big contracts, execute them without a commodity shock or a customer who won't pay, and it prints cash; misjudge a few and a record year turns into a loss. Sterling & Wilson did exactly the latter after listing, was saved by Reliance's balance sheet, and has spent the last three years rebuilding on home turf while India installs solar faster than ever. The question now is whether it can convert a bulging order book into consistent profit — something it has managed only fitfully in its listed life.

01

Solar EPC 101: three completely different ways to "be in solar"

The one distinction that makes the whole company make sense.

"Solar company" is one of the most misleading labels on the market, because it hides three utterly different businesses with different economics, different risks and different valuations. Get this straight and Sterling & Wilson snaps into focus; miss it and you'll value the company completely wrong.

The three layers of the solar value chain — Sterling & Wilson is the middle one
LayerWhat they doHow they earnIndian example
Module makerManufactures the solar panels (and cells/wafers) in a factory.Sells panels by the watt; earns a manufacturing margin; rides capacity, technology and import policy.Waaree, Vikram Solar, Premier Energies
EPC contractorBuilds the power plant — designs it, buys the panels + all balance-of-system, constructs and commissions it. This is Sterling & Wilson.A fixed contract price per project; earns an execution margin; owns nothing afterward (except O&M contracts).Sterling & Wilson, Tata Power Solar, L&T
IPP / power producerOwns the finished solar farm and sells the electricity for 25 years.Long-term tariff income from a power-purchase agreement; a regulated-utility-like annuity.Adani Green, ReNew, NTPC Green

Read those three rows again. The module maker is a factory business — its fate is set by capacity utilisation, cell technology and tariffs on Chinese imports. The IPP is essentially a utility — it borrows heavily to build a plant, then collects a steady, contracted tariff for decades; investors value it on stable cash flows and asset base. Sterling & Wilson is neither. It is the contractor in the middle: it takes the risk of building the thing on time and on budget, pockets a slim margin if it does, and moves on to the next site. It carries no 25-year power tariff and no factory — just a pipeline of projects and the skill to execute them.

Why the EPC seat is the riskiest-looking but capital-lightest

An EPC firm owns almost no long-lived assets — no gigafactory, no fleet of power plants. That makes it capital-light in principle: it doesn't need billions tied up in generating stations. But it is working-capital-heavy and margin-thin, because it must buy panels and steel and pay workers months before the customer pays in full, and it competes for fixed-price contracts where the lowest credible bid usually wins. So the EPC lives or dies on two things a factory or a utility worries about far less: how tightly it prices and executes each project, and whether its customers pay on time. Both, as we'll see, are exactly where Sterling & Wilson has been burned before.

Who hires a solar EPC

  • Developers / IPPs — companies that will own and operate the plant but don't want to build it themselves; they outsource construction to an EPC. The traditional bulk customer.
  • Utilities & PSUs — state electricity boards and public-sector giants (NTPC, SJVN, state discoms) building solar to meet renewable mandates.
  • Corporates (C&I) — factories, data centres and industrial groups building captive solar to cut power bills and green their operations, increasingly including Reliance's own giga-scale renewable build-out.
02

What Sterling & Wilson actually builds — and the two revenue lines it runs

From an empty field to a running power station, then decades of upkeep.

The company's product is not a thing you can hold — it is a finished, grid-connected solar power plant, delivered turnkey. That single job breaks into a chain of stages, and then splits into two very different revenue streams: the big, lumpy EPC line, and the small, steady O&M line.

The two revenue lines, decoded

  • EPC (engineering, procurement & construction) — the overwhelming majority of revenue. The company signs a contract to deliver a plant for a fixed price, then makes its money on the gap between that price and what it costs to build. A huge slice of the cost is bought-in hardware — the panels alone can be half the project — which is why module prices and import duties feed straight into the margin. This is a scale business: the more gigawatts it builds, the more its fixed engineering and overheads spread out.
  • Balance of system (BoS) — worth naming because it is where an EPC's engineering skill actually shows. "Balance of system" is everything in a solar plant that isn't the panel: the mounting structures and trackers, inverters, transformers, cabling, the substation and the grid connection. A good EPC squeezes more energy and lower cost out of the same panels through smart BoS design — and that design edge, plus procurement scale, is Sterling & Wilson's real moat rather than any proprietary technology.
  • O&M (operations & maintenance) — the long tail. Once a plant is running, the company is often retained to operate and maintain it — cleaning panels (dust is a real enemy of output in India), monitoring performance, fixing faults, guaranteeing uptime. Small per megawatt, but it recurs year after year and compounds as the managed fleet grows. Sterling & Wilson's O&M book reached 13.5 GW in FY26.
Why "turnkey" is the whole pitch — and the whole exposure

A developer buying a solar plant has two fears: a build that runs late (every idle month is lost tariff income) and a build that comes in over budget (killing the project's returns). By taking on the entire job at a fixed price — design, procurement, construction, grid connection — Sterling & Wilson hands the customer a single point of accountability and absorbs those risks itself. That is attractive to a buyer who just wants a working plant. But it means the company owns every way the build can go wrong: a module supplier that hikes prices mid-project, a commodity spike in steel, a customer who delays payment, an overseas site with currency and legal risk. In a fixed-price contract, those surprises come straight out of Sterling & Wilson's margin, not the customer's. That asymmetry — capped upside, uncapped execution risk — is the structural nature of the EPC seat.

03

The economics of a solar EPC: why record revenue can still be a loss

The single most important idea in the investment case — and the reason this stock behaves so oddly.

Most companies grow revenue and grow profit together; a good top-line year is a good bottom-line year. Sterling & Wilson does not obey that rule, and understanding why is the whole game. Look at the last five years: revenue swings by multiples, and profit swings even harder — from deep losses to a small profit and back to a loss — with almost no stable relationship between the two.

How can the highest-revenue year in company history also lose money? Because an EPC's profit is not a function of how much it builds but of how well each contract was priced and executed. FY26's loss came despite record execution: it carried the drag of some older, thinner-margin projects, provisioning and legacy costs, and the reality that gross margins on solar EPC are wafer-thin — a few percent — so a modest cost overrun or a bad legacy contract can flip the whole year red. The encouraging counter-signal was that the fourth quarter of FY26 delivered a record ₹142 Cr profit, and operational EBITDA rose ~53% to ~₹444 Cr — a hint the newer, better-priced Indian book is more profitable than the mess it is working off.

The device that only fits an EPC like this one: revenue is vanity, the order book is the map, the margin is the whole verdict

For a factory you watch capacity utilisation; for a utility you watch the tariff and the asset base. For Sterling & Wilson you have to watch three numbers that don't move together. Order inflow tells you demand is landing (FY26: ₹10,062 Cr won, +43%). The unexecuted order book tells you how much revenue is loaded and coming (FY26: ₹11,813 Cr, a record). But only the execution margin tells you whether any of it becomes profit — and that is the number that has repeatedly betrayed shareholders. A bulging order book built at bad prices is a machine for turning revenue into losses; the same book built at good prices is a cash engine. The order book shows the size of the opportunity; the margin, quarter by quarter, is the only thing that reveals whether the company has actually learned to price and execute.

04

The near-death years: an IPO, a promoter's debt, and a slide to default

Told honestly — because the crisis is why the ownership and the strategy look the way they do today.

To understand today's Sterling & Wilson you have to understand how close it came to not surviving. This is not ancient history — the wounds are recent, and they explain both why Reliance is now in control and why the company retreated from the world to rebuild at home.

Timeline of the crisis and recovery

  • Aug 2019
    IPO at ₹780/share, raising ~₹3,125 Cr, spun out of the Shapoorji Pallonji (SP) group as a global solar-EPC pure-play. The listing was fully an offer-for-sale — the SP promoters cashed out, and were meant to use the proceeds to repay large loans they owed to the company.
  • Nov 2019
    The promoters failed to repay those inter-corporate loans (a balance of over ₹2,300 Cr) on schedule. The stock crashed ~42% in three days and fell ~63% below its issue price — a governance shock that branded the company in investors' minds.
  • FY21
    Module suppliers hiked prices sharply mid-project; steel and freight costs spiked to record highs. On fixed-price contracts, those overruns hit Sterling & Wilson's margin, not the customer's — squeezing profitability.
  • Oct 2021
    Reliance steps in. Reliance New Energy agrees to acquire a ~40% stake for ~₹1,100 Cr via a mix of primary investment, secondary purchase from SP, and an open offer — the beginning of the rescue.
  • FY22–FY23
    The bleedout. Overseas projects from prior years continued to sour; the company took on debt to fund working capital and losses, posting a combined net loss of over ₹2,000 Cr across the two years.
  • Sep 2023
    The nadir: India Ratings downgraded the company's bank facilities to 'D' (default) after delays in servicing debt. For a contractor whose entire business rests on counterparties trusting it to deliver, a default rating is close to an existential threat.
  • FY24–FY25
    Stabilisation. With Reliance capital and a deliberate pivot to domestic India orders, the balance sheet is repaired, ratings recover, and FY25 delivers a first modest profit (~₹86 Cr) in years.
  • FY26
    Record revenue ₹7,548 Cr and ~4.5 GW commissioned, though still a full-year loss — the top line has recovered faster than the bottom line.
Why this history is load-bearing, not trivia: the crisis was not primarily an operating failure of the solar business — the plants got built. It was a governance and balance-sheet failure: promoters who wouldn't pay what they owed, overseas exposure that turned toxic, and a working-capital squeeze that a thin-margin contractor could not absorb. That diagnosis is exactly why the fix was (a) new, deep-pocketed control and (b) a retreat to a market the company could manage. Judge the recovery on whether those two specific wounds have healed.
A genuine surprise

The event that nearly killed Sterling & Wilson had almost nothing to do with solar panels, project execution or the energy market. It was that its own founding owners — having sold shares to the public at ₹780 — did not repay more than ₹2,000 Cr of loans they owed the very company they had just listed. A world-class solar builder was brought to its knees not by a construction failure but by a related-party receivable it could not collect from the people who controlled it. That is why "who owns it, and can you trust them" is not a footnote in this primer — it is arguably the first thing to check, and it is precisely the thing Reliance's arrival was meant to settle.

The most important fact about Sterling & Wilson today is on its shareholder register. Reliance New Energy — the clean-energy arm of Reliance Industries — is the company's single largest shareholder and co-promoter, sitting alongside the residual Shapoorji Pallonji holding. That is not a passive financial stake; it is a strategic reset with three distinct effects.

The three things Reliance's control changes

  • Balance-sheet backing. A thin-margin, working-capital-heavy contractor lives or dies on funding and counterparty trust. Standing behind it with one of India's strongest corporate balance sheets restored both — it is far cheaper and easier for Sterling & Wilson to raise capital, secure bank guarantees and win large tenders when a Reliance entity is the promoter. This is the direct antidote to the working-capital squeeze that broke it.
  • A captive-scale demand pipeline. Reliance has declared giga-scale solar ambitions of its own — vast in-house renewable and green-hydrogen build-outs. A group solar-EPC arm is a natural builder for at least part of that, giving Sterling & Wilson a potential anchor customer of enormous size, on top of its third-party order book. The size of that internal opportunity is one of the genuine call-outs of the story.
  • Credibility and governance. The company's near-death was a governance failure. A promoter with a reputation to protect, professional oversight, and no history of failing to honour obligations to the listco is itself a form of de-risking — the reason a defaulted-and-rescued contractor can bid credibly for the country's biggest projects again.
The nuance to hold: Reliance backing is a powerful de-risker, but it is not a blank cheque, and the size and terms of any captive Reliance work are not guaranteed or disclosed as a fixed number — treat the "Reliance will feed it endless orders" thesis as an option, not a booked fact. What is concrete is the balance-sheet and credibility effect; the captive-demand upside is real but still has to show up as actual signed contracts.
06

The India pivot and the turnaround engine

From a scattered global order book to a domestic supercycle it can actually execute.

The strategic heart of the recovery is a deliberate reversal: the company that listed as a global solar EPC has swung its weight back to India, precisely as India entered the fastest solar-build phase in its history. The logic is simple — home projects are easier to fund, execute and collect on than far-flung overseas ones, and India's demand is now large enough that it doesn't need to chase risky international work to fill its plate.

The demand backdrop is genuinely large. India is targeting a vast expansion of non-fossil capacity — hundreds of gigawatts by 2030 — and utility-scale solar is the workhorse of that build. Every one of those gigawatts needs someone to construct it, and there are only a handful of firms with the scale, engineering depth and balance sheet to build 500-MW-plus plants reliably. Sterling & Wilson's FY26 execution — roughly 4.5 GW commissioned, about 15% of all of India's utility-scale solar built that year — is the concrete evidence that it is once again one of that handful.

Why "coming home" is a margin decision, not just a patriotic one

The overseas projects that helped sink Sterling & Wilson carried risks a domestic project doesn't: currency swings, unfamiliar legal systems, slow cross-border collection, and the cost of running teams across continents. When one goes wrong, it goes wrong expensively and far from head office. Concentrating on India shrinks that risk surface dramatically — shorter supply lines, rupee contracts, courts and customers it knows, and the ability to redeploy crews between nearby sites. The pivot isn't only about where the demand is; it's about building a book the company can actually execute at a profit, which is the exact thing it failed to do when it was chasing megawatts everywhere at once.

07

The O&M annuity: the quiet, steadier half

The recurring revenue that grows every time another plant is switched on.

Beneath the lumpy, boom-and-bust EPC business runs a calmer stream: operations & maintenance. Every plant Sterling & Wilson maintains pays it a modest annual fee — small next to a construction contract, but recurring, and the managed fleet only ever grows. In FY26 the O&M portfolio expanded ~50% to 13.5 GW, part of a cumulative global O&M footprint of roughly the same order.

Here is the mechanism that makes O&M valuable. A construction contract is a one-time event: build the plant, book the revenue, move on. But the O&M contract on that same plant pays a fee every year, for years — cleaning panels (dust and soiling can shave meaningful output off an Indian solar farm), monitoring inverters, replacing faulty parts, and guaranteeing a performance level. So a strong year of construction doesn't just add a one-off sale; it can permanently thicken the annuity underneath. Over time, a large fleet under management becomes a predictable, higher-margin, cycle-resistant earnings base that doesn't heave with the order-book cycle the way EPC does.

Why the annuity is the ballast against a lumpy business

EPC is feast-or-famine — it boomed to ₹5,199 Cr, collapsed to ₹2,015 Cr, then tripled again, all within five years. O&M does not behave that way: a plant commissioned in a boom still needs cleaning and servicing through the following bust, so the fee keeps arriving when construction revenue dries up. For a company whose EPC arm has whipsawed investors, this recurring layer is the ballast — the part of the business that keeps paying while the order book is between cycles. It won't ever be the biggest line, but it is the steadiest, and it quietly raises the quality of the whole earnings mix as the fleet compounds.

The realistic caveat: O&M is attractive but still a minority of revenue for a firm whose top line is dominated by construction. It softens the cyclicality; it does not remove it. Don't value Sterling & Wilson as if it were an annuity business — value it as an EPC contractor with a growing, useful annuity attached.
08

Beyond solar: wind EPC, storage, and the "renewable builder" ambition

Widening from a solar contractor into an all-of-the-above renewable-infrastructure builder.

Sterling & Wilson made its name in solar, but the market it builds for is changing shape — and the company is deliberately following it. The future grid isn't just fields of solar panels; it is solar plus storage plus wind, stitched into "firm" round-the-clock renewable supply. An EPC that can build all three, and the batteries that bind them, has a bigger and more durable addressable market than a solar-only contractor.

The reframe worth grasping

The bet here is that Sterling & Wilson stops being read as a "solar EPC" and starts being read as a renewable-infrastructure builder — a contractor that can construct whatever shape of clean-energy asset the tender demands. That matters because the single biggest risk to a one-technology contractor is that its one technology falls out of favour or gets commoditised. By adding storage and wind to its solar core, the company widens the set of projects it can chase and hedges against any single segment stalling. Storage in particular is the tell: every serious solar EPC now has to build batteries too, because a solar plant without storage is increasingly only half a bid.

09

The industry and the rivals

A concentrated contest of a few scaled builders — and the neighbours it is often confused with.

Large-scale solar EPC is not a fragmented cottage industry. Building 500-MW-plus plants reliably needs engineering depth, procurement scale, a strong balance sheet and a track record — so the field of firms that can credibly bid India's biggest projects is short. Sterling & Wilson's competition splits into direct EPC rivals and the adjacent businesses it is frequently — and wrongly — lumped together with.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two features define this contest. First, scale and balance sheet are the moat — not proprietary technology. Any competent firm can buy the same panels; what separates the leaders is the ability to fund huge working-capital swings, post big bank guarantees, procure at volume, and execute across many sites at once without slipping. That is exactly the ground on which Reliance backing helps Sterling & Wilson most. Second, the real strategic risk isn't losing share to another EPC — it's the industry-wide margin trap: fixed-price bidding on a commoditised build is a recipe for thin, fragile returns, which is why so many solar EPCs worldwide, Sterling & Wilson included, have had loss years. The winner isn't the one who builds the most gigawatts; it's the one who builds them at a margin that survives a commodity shock.

Financials & valuation

A revenue recovery that has outrun the profit recovery — priced on the promise of the gap closing.

The financial story of FY26 is a split-screen. The top line and the order book are at records; the bottom line is still red. The market's roughly ₹4,560 Cr valuation is not pricing a proven, steadily profitable contractor — it is pricing the expectation that the newer, domestic, better-priced book finally converts into consistent profit. Whether it does is the entire debate.

It is tempting to anchor on the record ₹11,813 Cr order book, but that number has misled Sterling & Wilson shareholders before — the company had plenty of orders in the years it lost over ₹2,000 Cr. The metric that actually decides the case is EPC execution margin, held for several consecutive quarters. If gross and EBITDA margins on the new domestic book stay at the improved FY26-exit levels — the ₹444 Cr operational EBITDA and the ₹142 Cr Q4 profit being the first data points — then a ₹7,500 Cr-plus revenue base throws off real, repeatable profit and the recovery is complete. If margins slip back toward the wafer-thin levels of the crisis years, the same order book simply converts revenue into more losses, and the valuation unwinds. Read the order book for scale; read the margin, quarter after quarter, for the verdict. The scoreboard is the margin line, not the backlog headline.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A rescued builder in a booming market — asked to prove it can build at a profit, not just at scale.

Sterling & Wilson enters FY27 in far better shape than at any point since its troubled listing: Reliance-anchored, a record domestic order book, ~4.5 GW of annual execution, and a widening scope into storage and wind. What remains unresolved is the one thing that has repeatedly undone it — turning a big, busy order book into consistent profit rather than record revenue with a red bottom line.

The bull case

  • India solar supercycle — hundreds of GW of solar (and storage) to be built this decade; a handful of scaled EPCs will construct it, and this is one of them.
  • Reliance anchor — balance-sheet backing, restored credibility, and a potential captive giga-scale pipeline the crisis-era company could only dream of.
  • Record, domestic-heavy order book — ₹11,813 Cr unexecuted, ~78% Indian, giving multi-year revenue visibility on lower-risk work.
  • Margin recovery signal — a record ₹142 Cr Q4 profit and +53% operational EBITDA suggest the newer book is more profitable than the legacy mess.
  • Widening scope + O&M ballast — storage, wind and hybrid open new addressable work, while a 13.5 GW O&M book adds recurring, steadier revenue.

The bear case

  • Margin fragility — solar EPC is a thin, fixed-price, commoditised build; FY26 lost money despite record revenue, and a single commodity spike or bad contract can flip a year red again.
  • Loss-making at the full-year level — the recovery is in revenue and orders, not yet in durable annual profit; the turnaround is unproven across a full cycle.
  • Execution & working-capital risk — the exact pressures (payment delays, cost overruns) that broke it once are inherent to the model and never fully gone.
  • Reliance-dependence & captive-order uncertainty — the group-pipeline upside is an option, not a signed number; heavy reliance on one promoter's plans cuts both ways.
  • Competition & commoditisation — Tata Power, L&T and global EPCs bid the same low-margin work; scale, not technology, is the only real moat.
The bottom line

Sterling & Wilson has already done the hard part twice over: it survived a governance-and-balance-sheet crisis that should have finished it, and it rebuilt a record, domestic order book under Reliance's wing while India's solar market ran hot. What it has not yet done is the deceptively simple thing an EPC exists to do — convert that order book into steady annual profit, rather than record revenue wrapped around a loss. The whole case now rides on a single, checkable line: whether the improved margin of the FY26 exit quarter holds across FY27. Watch the quarterly EPC margin and the movement of that ₹11,813 Cr book into commissioned, profitable revenue — the first full year in which Sterling & Wilson posts record revenue and a clean, sustained profit is the moment the turnaround stops being a promise and becomes a track record.
