00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Jubilant Pharmova is a diversified life-sciences company from the Jubilant Bhartia Group, run across five very different businesses. Its most valuable one is radiopharmaceuticals — radioactive drugs, made and rushed to hospitals for scanning and treating disease, sold in the United States through 45 nuclear pharmacies. Around that sit a contract sterile-injectables factory that fills medicines into vials for global pharma, a US generics arm, an allergy-immunotherapy business (allergy shots and venom extracts), and a drug-discovery / novel-drug research operation. One holding company, five machines, wildly different economics.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The moat is a distribution network, not a molecule. A radioactive imaging drug can lose half its usefulness in hours — sometimes in a single afternoon. You cannot manufacture it centrally and ship it; it decays en route. So Jubilant runs a web of 45 local radiopharmacies across the US that compound the dose that morning and hand it to a hospital the same day. That physical, licensed, hard-to-replicate network is the real asset — and the biggest source of group profit.
  • Every segment lives or dies on a US-FDA inspection. In FY26 a single facility — the Montreal SPECT plant — drew FDA observations and was partly shut for remediation, and that alone dented the whole company's most profitable segment. Regulatory status is not a footnote for this company; it is the swing factor that moves group EBITDA.
  • FY26 grew the top line but squeezed the margin. Revenue rose ~14% to ₹8,280 Cr, but EBITDA rose only ~8% to ₹1,326 Cr (a ~16% margin) and normalised PAT ~7% to ₹442 Cr — the gap is the radiopharma remediation cost, even as the sterile-injectables arm grew a blistering 38%. The stock trades near 39× earnings on a ~₹14,450 Cr market value — a diversified pharma re-rated on the radiopharma growth story.
In plain terms

Most of what Jubilant does is ordinary pharma manufacturing — fill a vial, make a generic pill, bottle an allergy shot. One part is not ordinary at all: it makes drugs that are radioactive by design, and because those drugs are dying from the second they're made, it has to own a same-day delivery network of 45 pharmacies dotted across America to get them into a patient before they're useless. That network is expensive to build, licensed to handle radioactive material, and slow for a rival to copy — which is exactly why it earns the fat margins that carry the group. The catch is the same one that runs through the whole company: an FDA inspector at a single plant can turn the best business into the biggest problem overnight.

01

Nuclear medicine 101: drugs you can see inside the body

No physics degree needed — just the handful of ideas that make radiopharma a business.

A radiopharmaceutical is a medicine with a tiny amount of radioactive material attached to it. Doctors give it to a patient, and a special camera detects the radiation coming back out — producing a picture of what is happening inside the body: a tumour lighting up, blood flow to the heart, bone that is diseased. Some of these drugs go a step further and carry a radioactive payload strong enough to destroy cancer cells rather than just show them. To follow the business, you need five terms.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
RadiopharmaceuticalA drug carrying a radioactive tag. Used either to image disease (diagnostics) or to treat it (therapy). Jubilant makes and distributes both, mostly in the US.
Half-lifeHow fast the radioactivity fades. Some common isotopes lose half their strength in hours; the workhorse Technetium-99m has a ~6-hour half-life. This single fact dictates the entire business model — you cannot stockpile or ship these drugs.
PET / SPECTThe two camera technologies. SPECT is the older, widespread workhorse (cardiac and bone scans); PET is the sharper, faster-growing one (oncology, cardiac perfusion). Each needs its own isotopes — Jubilant plays in both.
RadiopharmacyA licensed local lab that receives raw isotope, compounds the exact patient dose that morning, and dispatches it to nearby hospitals same-day. Jubilant runs 45 of these across the US. This is the network.
TheranosticsThe fast-growing frontier: a matched pair where one radioactive drug images the tumour and a sister drug treats it — "see it, then treat it." The reason nuclear medicine is suddenly a growth industry rather than a sleepy corner of radiology.
Why "it decays" is the whole business, not a detail

An ordinary pill can be made in one big factory in India, shipped across the world, and sit on a shelf for two years. A radioactive imaging drug cannot do any of that. By the time a same-day courier crossed a single US state, a six-hour-half-life dose would have lost a chunk of its signal; wait a day and it is close to useless. That physical constraint flips the normal logic of pharma manufacturing: instead of one giant low-cost plant, the winning model is many small local nodes, each close enough to hospitals to deliver a fresh dose before breakfast. The drug's own physics forces a distribution-heavy business — and distribution networks, unlike molecules, are slow and costly to copy.

Why nuclear medicine is growing now

  • Cancer imaging is booming. As oncology shifts toward precisely targeted therapy, doctors need to see exactly where a tumour is and whether it is responding — which is precisely what PET radiopharmaceuticals do.
  • Theranostics has arrived commercially. Blockbuster paired diagnostic-plus-therapy products (the field pioneered by names like Novartis's prostate-cancer therapy) have proven that "image then treat with radiation" is a real, large market — pulling investment and new products into the whole space.
  • An ageing population needs more cardiac and bone scans. The bread-and-butter SPECT diagnostics — heart perfusion, bone imaging — grow simply with demographics, giving the network a steady base beneath the exciting oncology growth.
02

The radiopharma engine: the crown jewel and its half-life moat

The single most important idea in the whole company — and it belongs to no other primer.

Strip Jubilant down to one asset and it is Jubilant Radiopharma — a largely US business that both makes radioactive drugs and, crucially, distributes them through its own network of 45 radiopharmacies. In FY26 the segment did ~₹1,178 Cr of revenue (+10%), and while it is not the group's biggest line by sales, it is structurally its richest by margin. The reason sits in a clock that starts ticking the instant the drug is made.

Every arrow is a race against the half-life. A central "one big factory" model is physically impossible here — which is exactly why the 45-node network is the asset.

Read that chain again and the moat becomes obvious. A rival can license the same molecule, buy the same isotope, even build the same reactor relationship. What it cannot quickly build is the physical, licensed, staffed web of local pharmacies close enough to America's hospitals to deliver a fresh dose the same morning — plus the radioactive-materials licences, the trained nuclear pharmacists, the transport permits and the hospital relationships that make each node work. That network took decades and enormous capital to assemble. It is the single largest US nuclear-pharmacy footprint in independent hands, and it is why Jubilant earns what it earns.

The device that fits only Jubilant: the product is decaying while the invoice is being written

In almost every other business, distance is a solved problem — you ship, you stock, you sell later. In radiopharma, distance is the enemy and time is the product. A dose made at 6am and undelivered by noon is not "late inventory"; it is worth less every hour and then worth nothing. That inverts the usual pharma moat: the defensible thing is not a patent or a low-cost plant, it is a distribution network dense enough to beat the decay clock. Jubilant's edge is 45 nodes of proximity. A competitor with a better molecule but no network still can't get it to the patient in time — and a competitor with a network can absorb almost any molecule. The moat is the map, not the medicine.

What the network actually sells

  • SPECT diagnostics — the volume base: cardiac perfusion agents, bone scans, the everyday nuclear-imaging workload run through the pharmacies. Steady, demographics-driven, but lower-growth.
  • PET diagnostics — the faster-growing, higher-value oncology and cardiac PET agents; the segment where new theranostic launches land.
  • Proprietary products & generators — Jubilant's own branded radiopharmaceuticals and its Ruby-Fill rubidium generator for cardiac PET, whose installed base grew strongly in FY26. A generator placed in a hospital is a razor-and-blade annuity: it keeps consuming Jubilant's isotope for years.
  • Distribution for others — the network also carries third-party radiopharmaceuticals, so when a big new theranostic launches, Jubilant's pharmacies can be the rails it rides on — earning off other people's molecules.
A genuine surprise: the standout diagnostic isotope, Technetium-99m, is not mined or grown — it is "milked" daily from a parent isotope (Molybdenum-99) that is itself made in a tiny handful of ageing nuclear research reactors worldwide. So a chunk of Jubilant's most profitable business ultimately depends on a global supply chain of a few reactors and generators — and a maintenance shutdown at one of them can ripple straight into a nuclear pharmacy's shelves in Ohio. The crown jewel's raw material is, quite literally, radioactive and perishable at the source.
03

The company, the group, and who controls it

A Bhartia-family company — and the group behind a household pizza brand.

Jubilant Pharmova belongs to the Jubilant Bhartia Group, founded and controlled by brothers Shyam S. Bhartia and Hari S. Bhartia. The company was formerly Jubilant Life Sciences, renamed Jubilant Pharmova in 2021 as the group sharpened it into a pure pharma-and-life-sciences vehicle. It carries a heritage that stretches back to the group's chemicals and organic-intermediates roots.

The surprise most investors miss: the same promoter family that runs a US network of nuclear pharmacies also controls Jubilant FoodWorks — the master franchisee of Domino's Pizza in India (and Popeyes, and until recently Dunkin'). The Bhartias sit behind both a same-day radioactive-drug delivery network in America and same-day pizza delivery across India. Two utterly different businesses, one logistics-obsessed family — and a useful reminder that "Jubilant" spans several separately listed companies that should never be confused with one another.

Timeline

  • 2007

  • 2015
    Jubilant Life Sciences deepens its CDMO / sterile-injectables and allergy-immunotherapy franchises in the US.
  • 2021
    Renamed Jubilant Pharmova; the group demerges its life-sciences-ingredients arm (Jubilant Ingrevia) into a separate listco, leaving Pharmova as the pure pharma play.
  • 2023–2025
    Steady build-out: third sterile-injectables line at Spokane, Ruby-Fill generator momentum, and consolidation of the API business into the drug-discovery arm to create an end-to-end CRDMO.
  • FY26
    Revenue ~₹8,280 Cr (+14%); CDMO sterile injectables +38%; but radiopharma EBITDA hit by the Montreal SPECT FDA remediation and temporary shutdown. Promoter stake trimmed to ~47.7%.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Bhartia family and group entities held roughly 47.7% as of March 2026, down from ~50.7% a year earlier after a ~3% reduction in the June-2025 quarter (worth tracking — a promoter sell-down is always a signal to read, though this took the stake off a majority rather than to a low level).
  • Don't confuse the Jubilant companies: Jubilant Pharmova (JUBLPHARMA) is this company — pharma and radiopharma. Jubilant Ingrevia (JUBLINGREA) is the separately listed specialty-chemicals / life-science-ingredients business. Jubilant FoodWorks (JUBLFOOD) is Domino's-India. Three different listed companies, one promoter group — and a frequent source of ticker mix-ups.
  • Balance sheet: moderately levered, with net-debt/EBITDA around 1.3× at March 2026 (up from ~1.1× a year earlier) as the group funds capacity expansion — implying net debt on the order of ~₹1,700 Cr (approx.). Manageable, but a real constraint on a company also investing in cash-hungry novel-drug research.
Why the group structure matters to a shareholder

Buying Jubilant Pharmova is buying five businesses in one wrapper, run by a family that has repeatedly reshaped the group — spinning out ingredients (Ingrevia), keeping food (FoodWorks) separate, folding API into discovery. For an investor that cuts both ways: the diversification means no single stumble sinks the company, but it also means the market is forever doing a sum-of-the-parts on segments with margins ranging from thin generics to fat radiopharma. The promoter's willingness to keep simplifying — housing the API and discovery businesses together as one CRDMO, for instance — is a tell that the structure is still being tuned toward clarity.

04

CDMO sterile injectables: the fastest-growing leg

Making other companies' injectable medicines — and FY26's real growth star.

The second pillar is contract development and manufacturing (CDMO) of sterile injectables — Jubilant makes injectable medicines for other pharma companies, filling drugs into vials and syringes under contract. In FY26 this was the standout, growing ~38% to ₹1,755 Cr, making it the single largest revenue line and the clearest evidence that the pharma-outsourcing wave is reaching Jubilant.

Why injectables specifically, and why "sterile"? An injectable drug goes straight into the bloodstream, so it must be made in an absolutely sterile environment — a single contaminating particle can harm a patient. That makes sterile fill-finish a high-barrier, quality-obsessed business: hard to get right, hard to get regulators comfortable with, and therefore commanding better economics than ordinary tablet manufacturing. Global pharma increasingly prefers to outsource this delicate step to specialists rather than build their own sterile lines.

What Jubilant's CDMO actually does
CapabilityWhat it means

LyophilisationFreeze-drying delicate biologic-type drugs so they survive storage and shipping — a specialised, higher-value capability.
OphthalmicsA full-service eye-medicine offering (liquids, ointments, creams) — a niche where sterility standards are just as demanding.
US + India footprintPlants including Spokane (US), whose third line ramped in FY26 and drove much of the segment's growth.
Why the CDMO is quietly a big deal

Two structural tailwinds meet here. First, big pharma is outsourcing more of its manufacturing to specialists to stay asset-light and focus on discovery. Second, US regulators and buyers increasingly want supply chains they can trust and inspect — which favours a proven sterile operator with US plants over the cheapest possible bidder. Jubilant sits in that sweet spot, and the 38% jump shows demand landing. The same trait, though, is the risk: sterile injectables is the most inspection-intensive corner of pharma, so the CDMO's fortunes rise and fall with how clean its plants stay in the eyes of the FDA — a theme that recurs across this entire company.

05

Generics: the thin-margin volume leg that just turned

Selling off-patent medicines into a brutally competitive US market.

Jubilant's generics business makes and sells off-patent medicines — mainly solid-dosage and specialty products for the US and other regulated markets. In FY26 it did ~₹774 Cr of revenue (+13%), but the eye-catching number was profit: segment EBITDA surged ~250% to ₹83 Cr, with margins improving more than seven percentage points. From a low base, that is a real inflection.

Generics is the least glamorous, most cyclical part of the portfolio. Once a drug loses patent protection, many manufacturers pile in and prices erode — sometimes viciously. The US generic market has spent years in a price-erosion grind that punished Indian exporters. So a business like Jubilant's swings with two things: the specific products it happens to sell (some niches erode slower than others) and, once again, the FDA status of the plants that make them, since a regulatory hold can knock a product out of the market entirely.

How to read a generics turn

A 250% EBITDA jump sounds spectacular, and off a small base it is easy to over-read. What actually happened is more mundane and more useful: a portfolio that had been barely profitable found better pricing and mix, and a little operating leverage did the rest. The lesson for the whole company is that generics is the swing leg — capable of adding real profit in a good year and evaporating in a bad one. It is not where Jubilant's durability comes from; it is where its quarterly surprises, up and down, tend to originate. Treat a good generics quarter as welcome but not structural.

06

Allergy immunotherapy: the small, sticky specialty

Allergy shots and venom extracts — a niche with unusually good economics.

One of the quieter jewels is allergy immunotherapy — Jubilant makes allergenic extracts (the substances used in allergy shots that gradually desensitise a patient) and venom products (for people dangerously allergic to insect stings). In FY26 the business did ~₹785 Cr of revenue (+12%) and, notably, ~₹278 Cr of EBITDA (+13%) — a roughly 35% margin, among the richest in the group.

Why does a niche allergy business earn such fat margins? Because it is a small, specialised, high-barrier market in the US with only a handful of licensed players. Making biological allergen extracts to regulatory standard is genuinely hard, the products are prescribed as long courses (patients come back for months or years), and there is no flood of generic competition the way there is in ordinary pills. The result is a stable, sticky, high-margin annuity that quietly does a lot of the group's profit-carrying alongside radiopharma.

Why this tiny segment matters more than its size

On revenue, allergy immunotherapy looks like a rounding error next to the CDMO. On profit, it punches far above its weight — a ~35% EBITDA margin means it contributes profit out of proportion to its sales. For a company whose flashiest segment (radiopharma) had a wobbly FY26, having a second high-margin, low-drama specialty business is exactly the ballast that keeps group earnings from swinging wildly. It is the sort of unglamorous, hard-to-enter niche that rarely makes headlines but reliably pays the bills.

07

Drug discovery & novel drugs: the science bet

Contract research for others — and a long-odds shot at owning a real medicine.

The fifth leg is the most speculative and the least understood. It has two parts. Jubilant Biosys is a contract research business — scientists-for-hire who help global pharma discover and develop new molecules, now merged with the API business to form an end-to-end CRDMO (contract research, development and manufacturing). Drug-discovery services grew steadily in FY26 (the discovery line ran at roughly a fifth-margin, ~₹160 Cr a quarter). Alongside it sits Jubilant Therapeutics, the group's attempt to discover its own proprietary novel drugs — a genuine, cash-burning research bet on owning a breakthrough medicine outright.

The two faces of the science leg
BusinessWhat it isEconomics
Jubilant Biosys (CRDMO)Contract research + API/development for global pharma — paid to help others find and make drugs.Fee-for-service; steady, decent margins; scales with pharma R&D outsourcing.
Jubilant TherapeuticsProprietary novel-drug discovery — trying to invent and own Jubilant's own new medicines.Cash-consuming, binary, multi-year. A lottery ticket with real science behind it — mostly a cost today.

The logic of housing the API business inside Biosys is to offer pharma clients a single door — "help us discover it, develop it, and make the active ingredient" — which improves both efficiency and brand recall as a full-service CRDMO. That is a sensible, incremental value-add. Jubilant Therapeutics is a different animal entirely: it is optionality, not earnings. It costs money every year and may never produce an approved drug — but if one of its programmes works, the payoff dwarfs anything else in the portfolio.

How to weigh a cash-burning research arm

A first-time reader should hold two thoughts at once. The services business (Biosys/CRDMO) is a real, sellable, margin-generating operation that rides the same pharma-outsourcing wave as the CDMO — value it like a business. The proprietary business (Therapeutics) is a bet — value it like a call on a low-probability, high-payoff event, and assume it consumes cash until proven otherwise. Lumping them together as "R&D" hides the point: one pays the rent, the other buys a chance at the jackpot, and only one of them belongs in your earnings model today.

Sum of the segments: where the profit actually sits

Five businesses, one number — and the mix is the whole point.

Because Jubilant is genuinely five companies in a trench coat, the only way to understand it is to lay the segments side by side. Revenue tells one story; margin tells the real one. The high-growth leg (CDMO) is not the high-margin leg; the high-margin legs (radiopharma, allergy) are what carry group profit. Get the mix right and the company makes sense.

The sum-of-the-parts trap

A diversified company is only worth adding up if you value each part on its own terms. Value all of Jubilant on one blended multiple and you either overpay for the generics and the research burn, or you underpay for the radiopharma network and the allergy annuity. The honest way to hold this business is as a portfolio: a high-margin radiopharma distribution moat, a sticky specialty (allergy), a fast-growing but inspection-exposed CDMO, a swing-factor generics book, a services CRDMO, and a lottery ticket (Therapeutics). The share price is the market's running attempt to weigh those six things — and it re-weights every time one of them surprises.

09

The two structural risks: FDA inspections and isotope supply

The dependencies that can swing this whole company — read this before the valuation.

Two risks sit above all the others because they are structural — baked into the kind of company Jubilant is, not just this year's weather. The first is the US-FDA inspection. The second is the radioactive supply chain. FY26 delivered a live example of the first, and the second is a permanent feature of the radiopharma business.

Risk one: a single FDA observation can move group EBITDA

Almost everything valuable Jubilant does — sterile injectables, generics, radiopharma manufacturing — is sold into the US and therefore lives under the FDA's inspection regime. When an inspector finds problems at a plant, the consequences run from a warning letter to a halt in production and shipments. In FY26, the Montreal SPECT facility drew FDA observations and was partly shut for remediation, cutting production of certain SPECT products and squeezing the margin of the group's most profitable segment. That is the entire reason FY26 EBITDA grew only ~8% while revenue grew ~14%: one plant's regulatory status leaked straight into the group's profit line.

This is not hypothetical for Jubilant. The company has a documented history of FDA scrutiny across its US-facing plants, and its share price has swung on inspection outcomes before. For a business this dependent on US regulated markets, "clean inspection status across the plant network" is not compliance box-ticking — it is a core financial variable that a shareholder must watch as closely as revenue.

Risk two: the isotope supply chain is thin and radioactive

The radiopharma engine depends on a raw material that is scarce, perishable and made in very few places. Diagnostic isotopes like Molybdenum-99 (which yields the workhorse Technetium-99m) come from a small number of ageing research reactors worldwide; a maintenance shutdown or outage at one can tighten global supply within days. Jubilant manages this with generator technology and supply relationships, but it cannot make the underlying scarcity disappear. When isotope supply is disrupted, even a perfect distribution network has nothing fresh to deliver.

Why these two risks are really the same risk

Both come from the same source: Jubilant makes hard, tightly-regulated, physically-demanding products, and that difficulty is exactly what gives it pricing power and moats. You cannot have the fat radiopharma margin without the isotope fragility; you cannot have the high-barrier sterile-injectable business without the ever-present FDA inspector. The barriers that keep competitors out are the same barriers that can trip the company itself. An investor is not choosing between the moat and the risk — they are the two faces of one coin, and the whole question is whether Jubilant executes cleanly enough to keep collecting the moat's rent without paying the risk's penalty.

The industry and the rivals

A different competitive set for each segment — with radiopharma the one that matters most.

Because Jubilant is five businesses, it has five competitive fields. The one that decides the investment case is radiopharma, where the rivals are large and specialised. Elsewhere it competes in the crowded CDMO and generics fields against very different sets of players.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

In radiopharma, the giants (Lantheus, Curium, GE HealthCare, Novartis's AAA) are formidable on molecules and scale — but the game Jubilant plays is partly logistics, and there its 45-node US network is a genuine edge that a product-focused rival cannot replicate overnight. In the CDMO and generics fields, by contrast, Jubilant is one competent player among many, winning on execution and inspection track record rather than any structural moat. The clean read: Jubilant has a real, defensible position in radiopharma and allergy, and a fair-fight position everywhere else. The stock's premium rests on the first pair, not the second.

Financials & valuation

So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the radiopharma segment's EBITDA margin returning to its structural high — which requires the Montreal plant remediated and the US plant network staying FDA-clean. Read it alongside three supports: the CDMO sterile-injectables ramp (is the 38% growth durable as more Spokane capacity fills?), the allergy margin holding near 35% (is the ballast intact?), and net-debt/EBITDA drifting back toward ~1× (is the balance sheet financing growth without straining?). If radiopharma margin recovers on clean inspections, the multiple is defensible. If FDA problems recur or isotope supply tightens, the same diversified pharma re-rates toward its more ordinary parts. Radiopharma's margin line is the scoreboard; the rest is commentary.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A real moat and a real recovery story — asked to prove it can keep the plants clean.

Jubilant enters FY27 with a genuine structural asset (the radiopharma network), a fast-growing CDMO, a sticky high-margin specialty (allergy), and a clear task: remediate Montreal, hold the plants FDA-clean, and let the profit engine recover. The debate is not whether the businesses are real — they plainly are — but whether execution and regulatory discipline can keep pace with a multiple that already assumes the recovery.

The bull case

  • Radiopharma distribution moat — 45 US nuclear pharmacies form a decades-in-the-making network that product-only rivals cannot quickly copy; it rides the theranostics boom.
  • CDMO ramp — sterile injectables +38%, Spokane capacity filling, and a structural pharma-outsourcing tailwind behind it.
  • High-margin ballast — allergy immunotherapy at ~35% EBITDA margin, a sticky niche with few competitors, steadies group earnings.
  • Self-help recovery — much of FY26's margin dent was one plant's remediation; normalisation from H2 FY27 lifts profit without needing new demand.
  • Diversification — five uncorrelated legs mean no single stumble sinks the company, and a novel-drug lottery ticket sits on top for free.

The bear case

  • FDA inspection risk — a single warning letter or plant halt can hit the most profitable segment, as Montreal did in FY26; a repeat anywhere is a direct earnings hit.
  • Isotope supply fragility — the radiopharma engine depends on a thin global chain of ageing reactors making perishable Moly-99.
  • Segment concentration in profit — radiopharma and allergy carry the margin; a stumble in either matters more than its revenue share suggests.
  • Generic price erosion & cash-burning research — the generics swing leg can reverse, and Jubilant Therapeutics consumes cash with a binary payoff.
  • Rich valuation & leverage — ~39× earnings prices in a clean recovery, while net-debt/EBITDA at ~1.3× limits the cushion if execution slips.
The bottom line

Jubilant Pharmova owns something rare in Indian pharma — a physical distribution moat in radiopharmaceuticals, built on the one constraint no competitor can wish away: a drug that decays before it can be shipped, forcing a same-day network of 45 pharmacies that took decades to assemble. Around it sit a fast-growing sterile-injectables CDMO, a fat-margin allergy niche, a swing-factor generics book, and a science bet. What FY26 proved is that this collection is only as good as its regulatory hygiene: one FDA-hit plant in Montreal was enough to hold group profit flat despite double-digit revenue growth. The next few quarters resolve it in a single, checkable line — whether the radiopharma segment's EBITDA margin climbs back toward its structural high as Montreal is remediated and the plants stay inspection-clean. Watch that margin print; it tells you whether the moat is compounding or the risk is collecting.
