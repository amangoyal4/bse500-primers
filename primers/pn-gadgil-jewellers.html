00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

P N Gadgil Jewellers Limited (PNGJL) sells gold, diamond, silver and platinum jewellery under the well-known "PNG" brand — a name that has been over jewellery counters in Maharashtra since 1832. It is one of the state's largest organised jewellers, headquartered in Pune, running roughly 78 showrooms (overwhelmingly across Maharashtra and Goa, plus a single store in the United States) through a mix of company-owned and franchise outlets, backed by a growing e-commerce arm. It came to the public market in a September 2024 IPO and is expanding stores at a rapid clip.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The gold is not really the product — trust and the making charge are. When you buy a gold necklace, the price is mostly the metal, which the jeweller passes through at close to cost. What PNGJL actually keeps is the making (labour) charge plus a richer margin on diamond/studded pieces. That is why a company selling ₹10,739 Cr of jewellery keeps only a thin slice — ~₹704 Cr of EBITDA (~6.5% margin) and ~₹410 Cr of net profit in FY26. The metal flows through; the margin is the workmanship and the mix.
  • The moat is a name and a network, not a formula. Buying jewellery is an act of trust — customers hand over lakhs for purity they cannot verify at the counter, often for a wedding. A 194-year heritage brand with dense presence in one home state is exactly the reassurance that lets an organised chain take share from the neighbourhood goldsmith. PNGJL's edge is that trust, compounded across more and more Maharashtra shop-fronts.
  • FY26 was a blockbuster year — but it rode a rising tide. Revenue jumped ~39.6%, EBITDA ~89.6%, and profit ~87.8% to ~₹410 Cr, on ~43% same-store sales growth and aggressive store additions. A rampant gold price and a strong wedding-and-festive season did much of the lifting. The stock trades at roughly 21× earnings — a growth rating that assumes the store roll-out and the margin-mix upgrade keep delivering.
In plain terms

Picture a jeweller as two businesses stacked in one shop. The first is a near-commodity: it buys gold, shapes it, and sells it back at roughly what the metal costs — enormous turnover, almost no margin. The second is where the money lives: the labour charged to make the piece, and the fatter markup on diamond-studded jewellery, both of which a customer will only pay to a name they trust. PNGJL's whole strategy is to grow the first (open more showrooms, sell more tonnes of gold through them) while enriching the second (nudge more customers toward studded pieces, keep raising the making charge a trusted brand can command). FY26 saw both fire at once, helped by a gold price that made every display case worth more. The question for every year after is whether the network keeps widening and the mix keeps richening — because on a plain bar of gold, this is a low-margin trade.

01

How a jeweller actually earns its money

No finance background needed — just where each rupee of a gold bill goes.

The single most important thing to understand about PNGJL — and every branded jeweller — is that the price on the tag is mostly not profit; it is mostly gold. A jewellery business is a low-margin, high-turnover trade dressed up as a luxury one. To follow the company you need to see how a single bill breaks apart.

On a plain-gold sale, the metal can be 85–90% of the bill and passes through at near-cost; the jeweller lives on the making charge. Diamond and studded jewellery carry a meaningfully higher margin — which is why the mix matters so much (section 06).

Because the metal dominates the ticket and is passed through, a jeweller's gross margin looks tiny next to a normal consumer company: PNGJL runs a single-digit gross margin and an EBITDA margin around 6–7%, versus the 20–50% a branded-goods maker might post. That is not weakness — it is the physics of selling gold. The trade-off is velocity: a jeweller turns over huge rupee volumes through each square foot of store, so even a thin margin, multiplied across a big and growing sales base, becomes real profit. The five terms below are all you need.

The one idea that reframes the whole business

Stop reading PNGJL as a "gold" company and start reading it as a trust-and-workmanship company that happens to move gold to do it. The metal is a low-margin pass-through it is exposed to but doesn't truly profit from; the earnings come from the making charge a customer will only pay a name they trust, and from the fatter markup on studded pieces. Everything the company does — opening showrooms, guarding the brand, pushing diamonds — is aimed at selling more workmanship and more studded jewellery through more trusted counters. The gold is the river; PNGJL charges for the crossing.

02

The structural tailwind: unorganised to branded

Why the whole sector is a slow-motion market-share transfer — and PNGJL is on the winning side.

India's jewellery market is enormous — on the order of ₹6 lakh crore a year — and for most of history it has been dominated by the local family goldsmith: the corner shop where you knew the owner and trusted (or hoped) the purity. The single biggest force in this sector is that this is changing. Organised, branded chains are steadily taking share from that unorganised base, and the shift is structural, not cyclical.

The organised share of the market is estimated to be moving from roughly 30% toward 40% by 2028. Several forces push in the same direction, and all of them favour a scaled, compliant brand like PNGJL over the neighbourhood goldsmith:

  • Mandatory hallmarking. The government now requires BIS hallmarking (a stamped purity guarantee) on gold jewellery. This is precisely the reassurance branded chains already offered — and it raises the compliance bar for small unorganised players, nudging buyers toward names they trust.
  • GST and formalisation. A formal tax regime and tighter documentation erode the cash-economy advantage the corner jeweller once enjoyed, levelling the field toward organised retailers who were always in the tax net.
  • The trust premium on big-ticket buys. A wedding purchase can run into lakhs. As incomes rise and buyers grow more careful about purity, certification and buy-back terms, the branded showroom — with its guarantees, transparent pricing and return policy — wins the anxious customer.
  • Aspiration and experience. Younger, urbanising buyers want lit displays, air-conditioned showrooms, design variety and financing schemes — the organised-retail experience a single-shop goldsmith struggles to match.
Why this tailwind matters more than any single good year

Here is the quietly powerful part. Even if the overall jewellery market grew barely at all, an organised chain could still grow briskly simply by taking share from the unorganised half of the market. That is a very different, far more durable growth source than betting on the gold price or the wedding count. It means a well-run branded jeweller has a structural runway that does not depend on the pie getting bigger — only on its own slice getting wider. PNGJL's rapid store roll-out is, at heart, a land-grab of that shifting share, one Maharashtra town at a time, before Titan's Tanishq and Kalyan plant their own flags there.

The catch — and it is the recurring tension in this primer — is that everyone can see this tailwind. Titan (Tanishq), Kalyan Jewellers, Senco and others are chasing exactly the same unorganised share, and several are pushing into Maharashtra, PNGJL's fortress. The structural opportunity is real and large; the fight to capture it is intense.

03

The 194-year name: why heritage is the moat

In a business built on trust, a two-century brand in its home state is a genuine asset — with one subtlety.

The "PNG" brand traces to a shop opened by Ganesh Gadgil in Sangli in 1832 — nearly two centuries of continuous association with jewellery in Maharashtra. In most industries an old name is nostalgia. In jewellery, where a buyer is trusting an invisible promise of purity with a large sum, an established, multi-generational name is one of the few durable competitive advantages there is. It is why the sector's strongest franchises are old ones.

What that heritage buys PNGJL, concretely:

  • Pricing power on the making charge. A trusted brand can charge a little more for the same workmanship, because the customer is also paying for confidence in purity, fair weighing, and honest buy-back. That premium goes almost straight to gross margin.
  • Regional density as reinforcement. PNGJL is not thinly spread nationwide; it is deep in Maharashtra and Goa, where the "PNG" name carries the most weight. Density compounds trust — the more visible the brand locally, the safer the next customer feels, and the cheaper it is to open the next store into an already-warm market.
  • Word-of-mouth in a referral purchase. Wedding jewellery is bought on family recommendation. A brand woven into a region's weddings for generations enjoys a referral engine no advertising budget can quickly buy.
The subtlety a careful reader should know: the Gadgil family legacy is shared across more than one business. The listed company, P N Gadgil Jewellers Ltd (the "PNG" brand, led by Saurabh Gadgil), is distinct from other family-linked jewellery names that also draw on the P N Gadgil heritage (for example, the separate "P N Gadgil & Sons" lineage). The names are close enough to confuse a first-time buyer of the stock. This primer is strictly about the listed PNGJL — and it is a wholly different company from the North-India jeweller PC Jeweller, with which it is sometimes muddled. When you research the shares, anchor on the ticker PNGJL / BSE 544256, not the brand words alone.
The surprise hiding in a "heritage" brand

You might assume a 194-year-old name is a slow, sleepy franchise coasting on the past. The opposite is true here: PNGJL is one of the fastest-expanding jewellers in the listed space, roughly doubling its store count in about two years around its IPO. The heritage isn't a museum — it is the launch-pad. Two centuries of trust are exactly what let a company open showroom after showroom into new Maharashtra towns and have customers walk in on day one. The old name is what makes the new-store maths work; without it, each opening would be a cold start.

04

The company, the IPO, and who runs it

A Pune family business that went public in September 2024 to fund its expansion.

PNGJL is a promoter-led family enterprise: the modern company is built around the "PNG" retail brand and led by the Gadgil family, with Saurabh Gadgil as its chairman and managing director. For most of its life it was a private, regional powerhouse. Its arrival on the stock market is recent, and the reason it listed tells you what kind of company it is.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter-controlled. The Gadgil family/promoter group retains the majority stake post-IPO, with the balance as public float. This is a classic founder-run retailer where the family's name is the brand — an alignment strength, but one that makes governance, succession and related-party discipline worth watching.   • Why it raised money. The fresh-issue proceeds were directed largely at funding new showrooms and reducing debt — i.e. bankrolling the expansion engine that is the entire equity story. This is growth capital for a land-grab, not a cash-out.
  • Multi-brand architecture. Under the PNG umbrella sit several sub-brands spanning gold, diamond and platinum jewellery, plus dedicated formats and an e-commerce channel — a deliberate attempt to address different price points and customer segments under one trusted parent name.
What the IPO really signalled

A company doesn't sell a quarter of itself to the public to stand still. PNGJL listed to accelerate — to turn a regional heritage brand into a much larger store network faster than internal cash could fund. That reframes how to judge it: the IPO handed management the capital to press its structural advantage while the unorganised-to-branded shift is live and before national rivals saturate Maharashtra. The scoreboard, then, is not this year's profit but whether that capital is being converted into productive, well-located stores that hold their same-store growth — the subject of the next two sections.

05

The store-expansion engine: COCO plus franchise

The growth model — and the deliberate trade-off between control and capital.

PNGJL's value is, quite literally, a multiplication: number of stores × revenue per store × the margin it keeps on that revenue. Everything management does bends toward growing the first two terms without wrecking the third. The chosen tool is an aggressive showroom roll-out, run through two different store models that trade off control against capital.

From ~33 stores at the IPO to ~78 by FY26 exit — the network roughly doubled in about eighteen months. Store additions are the most visible growth lever, and the one PNGJL most directly controls.

Two store models, one network

  • COCO — company-owned, company-operated. PNGJL owns the store and the inventory and runs it directly. It funds the gold and fit-out, but keeps the full retail margin and total control over the customer experience. This is the core of the network and the bulk of revenue.
  • Franchise (FOCO-style). A local partner funds the store and inventory; PNGJL lends its brand, supply and systems and takes a lighter cut. It is capital-lighter — the company can plant its flag in more towns without funding every rupee of gold — but it earns less per store and cedes some control over execution.

Franchise outlets have been a meaningful share of the store count (roughly a third at points) but only a small slice of revenue — because the big-format, high-throughput company-owned stores do the heavy selling. The shape of this split is the clearest read on how PNGJL trades capital against margin.

The economics of a new COCO store are simple to picture: sink capital into fit-out and gold inventory, lean on the PNG brand so customers arrive from day one, and let the store ramp toward the network's average revenue. In FY26 the average store did roughly ₹138 Cr of revenue and ~₹5 Cr of net profit — so each productive new showroom is a material addition. The franchise route lets PNGJL widen its map faster and cheaper, at the cost of a thinner take and less grip on standards. Getting that balance right — enough COCO to own the margin, enough franchise to cover the ground before rivals do — is one of the genuinely strategic decisions management makes.

The trade-off franchise-led growth quietly makes

Franchising looks like a free lunch — expansion without the capital bill — but it is not costless. Every franchise store is margin PNGJL doesn't fully keep and an experience it doesn't fully control, in a business where a single purity scandal or a shabby showroom can dent the very trust the whole model rests on. Lean too hard on franchise and you grow the map while diluting both the earnings and the brand discipline that make the map worth having. Lean entirely on COCO and you grow slower and heavier. PNGJL's answer so far — COCO for the big revenue-generating cities, franchise to extend reach — is sensible, but it is a dial to watch: the revenue-vs-store-count split above is where you can see the choice being made.

06

Making charges and the stud ratio: where the profit really moves

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a branded jeweller.

If the metal is a pass-through, then a jeweller's profitability is decided almost entirely by two things it can influence: how much it charges for making, and how much of its mix is higher-margin studded jewellery rather than plain gold. Watch these two, not the gold price, to understand where PNGJL's margin goes.

On plain gold, the making charge is the gross margin — a few percent of a bill dominated by metal. Studded jewellery is a different animal: a diamond-set piece carries a far richer margin because the value is in design, stones and craftsmanship rather than a daily-quoted metal rate. So the mix between the two is a direct dial on blended profitability. Here is the striking part: PNGJL's stud ratio is only around 9–11% of sales, and management has publicly targeted lifting it toward ~15%, with a stated ambition to push gross margin toward 10–12%.

Because studded jewellery is so much richer than plain gold, a move from ~11% to ~15% of mix lifts blended gross margin materially — the difference between a low-single-digit and a low-double-digit gross margin.
The device that only fits a branded jeweller: metal is rented, the margin is the making charge × the mix

Forget "the gold price went up, so PNGJL made more." The number that runs this company is (making charge on plain gold) + (the fatter margin on studded) − (store, staff and the cost of carrying gold), multiplied across a growing pile of tonnes sold through more counters. The metal itself is almost rented — bought (or leased) at cost and passed to the customer at cost. So PNGJL's profit is not the sticker price of a necklace; it is the workmanship it can charge for and the share of its sales that sparkles. That is why the two numbers to track are the same-store sales growth (are existing stores selling more?) and the stud ratio (is each rupee getting richer?). A gold bar and a diamond ring can leave the same shop; only the mix tells you which one paid PNGJL's rent.

This is also why headline revenue can flatter or mislead. When the gold price spikes, revenue balloons (each gram is billed higher) even if the volume of jewellery sold barely moved and the making margin didn't improve — a bigger top line that isn't necessarily a better business. FY26's revenue surge owed a lot to a soaring gold price doing exactly this. The truer reads are grams sold, same-store growth, and the stud ratio. Hold those three, and the gold price becomes background noise rather than the story.

07

Gold, inventory and the float: the balance-sheet reality

The working-capital truth a first-timer never sees coming — and why a "gold" company isn't fully long gold.

Here is the fact that surprises almost everyone looking at a jeweller's balance sheet for the first time: a jewellery retailer's biggest asset by far is its inventory — the gold and diamonds sitting in its display cases and vaults — and financing that hoard is the central working-capital challenge of the business. A jeweller is, in a real sense, a large pile of gold with a shop attached, and the art is carrying that pile without either running out of stock or drowning in the cost of holding it.

This creates a counter-intuitive dynamic. To grow, PNGJL must open stores, and every new store must be filled with inventory before it sells a single item. So faster growth consumes cash and tends to lift borrowings, even in a great profit year — the money is tied up in the gold on the shelves of stores that are still ramping. PNGJL carried around ₹1,700 Cr of borrowings at FY26 — not to build factories, but largely to fund the gold that makes a growing network of showrooms possible.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with: a gold company that isn't fully long gold

You'd assume a jeweller holding thousands of crore of gold is a giant bet on the gold price — that a fall in gold would gut it. Not quite. Branded jewellers use gold-on-lease (GML) and hedging to reduce their exposure: instead of buying gold outright and sitting on the price risk, they borrow physical gold from banks and effectively lock in the metal cost, so a swing in the gold price largely passes through to the customer rather than landing on the jeweller's P&L. The upshot is a genuine surprise — PNGJL profits from selling jewellery, not from owning gold as it appreciates. A rising gold price helps mostly by inflating the ticket size and stoking sentiment; it is not, by design, a leveraged bet on bullion. Manage the hedge badly, though, and that protection can turn into a loss — which is why gold-price and hedging risk still sits on the risk list even for a company that tries hard not to be long the metal.

Why this reframes the whole company: it means the health of PNGJL is never captured by the profit line alone. Every year you must ask a second question: is the growth being funded sensibly, or is inventory and debt ballooning faster than sales? A jeweller can post record profits while its cash conversion quietly deteriorates and borrowings climb. Read the inventory, the borrowings and the operating cash flow alongside the profit — together they tell you whether the store roll-out is creating value or just consuming gold.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, PNGJL's advantage is regional depth, not national scale — its "PNG" trust is concentrated where Titan and Kalyan are relatively thinner, and its store density and word-of-mouth in Maharashtra are hard for an outsider to replicate quickly. Second, that very fortress is exactly where the giants are now expanding: Tanishq and Kalyan opening stores in Maharashtra is the central competitive risk, because they arrive with deeper pockets, bigger ad budgets and, in Tanishq's case, a trust brand of national stature. The contest is not about who spots the organised-share opportunity — everyone has — but about who can plant productive, trusted stores in each town first and hold their same-store growth once a rival opens across the street.

10

Valuation: a growth rating on a thin-margin trade

Unlike a sleepy cyclical, PNGJL is priced for the expansion to keep delivering.

PNGJL is not cheap in the way a commodity converter is cheap. At roughly 21× trailing earnings and a market capitalisation around ₹9,100 Cr, the market is paying a growth multiple — it expects the store roll-out and the margin-mix upgrade to compound for years, not for FY26 to be a one-off. That is a reasonable stance for a fast-growing organised retailer riding a structural tailwind, but it sets a clear test: the growth has to show up.

So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the combination of same-store sales growth and stud ratio, tracked quarter by quarter, read alongside the pace of productive new-store additions and against two hazards: the gold price / hedging outcome and the inventory-and-borrowing trend (is expansion funded sensibly?). If PNGJL keeps opening stores that ramp to the ~₹138 Cr average, holds double-digit same-store growth, and edges the stud ratio toward 15% while Titan and Kalyan press into Maharashtra, the growth multiple is earned and profit compounds. If same-store growth normalises hard once the gold-price tailwind fades, or new stores cannibalise old ones as rivals crowd in, then FY26's ~88% profit jump will look like the peak of a gold-and-festive cycle rather than a durable run-rate. Stores added × sales-per-store × the mix — measured every quarter — is the scoreboard.

It is worth being blunt about what the FY26 numbers do and don't prove. They prove PNGJL can grow revenue and profit explosively when store additions, same-store demand and the gold price all cooperate. They do not yet prove the model's durability through a flat gold year, a weak wedding season, or a Maharashtra saturated by national rivals. A high-teens-to-twenties multiple is a bet on the second thing, extrapolated from the first. The rest of the case comes down to whether the next several quarters of same-store prints and stud-ratio progress back that bet up.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A trusted regional brand pressing a real advantage — asked whether it can keep expanding profitably as the giants arrive.

P N Gadgil Jewellers enters FY27 with genuine momentum: a record year behind it, a near-two-century brand, a rapidly widening store network, fresh IPO capital, and a structural shift from unorganised to branded jewellery running in its favour. What it cannot promise is that the store additions stay productive, the margin-mix upgrade lands, and the gold-price tailwind that flattered FY26 doesn't reverse — all while Titan and Kalyan march into its home state. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Structural share shift — organised jewellery moving from ~30% toward ~40% of a ₹6 lakh crore market gives a trusted chain a long runway independent of the gold price.
  • Heritage-plus-density moat — a 194-year "PNG" brand, deep in Maharashtra, is exactly the trust that lets new stores open warm and hold their footfall.
  • Store roll-out with real unit economics — ~₹138 Cr revenue and ~₹5 Cr profit per store, plus ~43% same-store growth, mean each productive opening compounds.
  • Margin-mix upside — lifting the stud ratio from ~11% toward ~15% is a concrete, self-controlled lever to raise blended gross margin from single into double digits.
  • Fresh capital, aligned promoter — IPO proceeds funding stores and debt reduction, run by a family whose name is the brand.

The bear case

  • National giants invading the fortress — Tanishq and Kalyan expanding into Maharashtra with deeper pockets is the central threat to same-store growth and new-store returns.
  • Priced for continued growth — ~21× earnings on a ~6.5%-margin trade leaves the stock reliant on the roll-out and mix upgrade actually delivering.
  • Gold-price & hedging risk — much of FY26's revenue surge rode a rising gold price; a reversal shrinks ticket sizes, and a hedging misstep can bite the P&L directly.
  • Working-capital hunger — growth means buying gold before it sells; inventory and borrowings scale with the network, and cash conversion can lag reported profit.
  • Execution & concentration — heavy Maharashtra concentration, franchise-quality control, stud-mix execution and wedding/discretionary cyclicality all have to go right at once.
The bottom line

P N Gadgil Jewellers is a trusted, nearly-two-century Maharashtra brand converting IPO capital into a fast-growing store network, at the precise moment when Indian buyers are migrating from the corner goldsmith to names they trust. The advantage is real and specific — heritage plus regional density is hard to copy — and the self-help levers, more productive stores and a richer studded mix, genuinely widen the thin margin the business lives on. But the same tailwind has drawn the giants onto its lawn, and FY26's spectacular numbers were flattered by a gold price and a festive-and-wedding season that will not repeat on command. The next test is concrete and datable: whether same-store sales growth holds up and the stud ratio climbs toward 15% across the FY27 Akshaya Tritiya and Diwali wedding seasons, even as Tanishq and Kalyan open stores across Maharashtra. Keep the stores productive and the mix richening through a normal gold year, and the growth multiple is earned; let same-store growth fade as rivals crowd in, and FY26 will read as the high-water mark of a very good cycle rather than the base of a longer climb.
