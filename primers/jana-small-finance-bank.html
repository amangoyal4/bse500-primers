00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Jana Small Finance Bank is a Bengaluru-based small finance bank (SFB) — a licensed, deposit-taking bank built for financial inclusion, lending mostly to households and small businesses that a big private bank often overlooks. Its distinguishing feature is its origin story: it was born from Janalakshmi Financial Services, which by 2017 was India's largest microfinance institution before demonetisation nearly destroyed it. Everything the bank does today is shaped by that near-death experience.

Three facts frame the whole business:

  • It is de-risking its book, on purpose. A microfinance lender makes tiny, collateral-free loans to groups of low-income women — high-yield, but brutally cyclical. Since becoming a bank, Jana has driven the secured share of its lending from ~60% to 73% (targeting ~80%), pushing into affordable home loans, loan-against-property, MSME, gold and vehicle finance. Unsecured microfinance, once the entire company, is now down to roughly a quarter of the book.
  • The microfinance cycle just bit — hard. FY25 and FY26 saw a sector-wide microfinance stress event (over-lending, borrower over-leverage, patchy repayment). Jana's still-sizeable unsecured book took the hit: FY26 net profit fell to ₹326 Cr from ₹670 Cr in FY24, even as the balance sheet grew ~23%. Gross bad loans peaked near ~2.9% mid-year before easing to 2.33%.
  • It is still a young, deposit-hungry bank. AUM is ~₹36,289 Cr and deposits ~₹35,784 Cr (both +23% in FY26), but its funding is expensive — a low CASA ratio (~18%) and a high cost-to-income (~60%) are the standard growing pains of an SFB still building a branch and deposit franchise. Capital is comfortable at a 19.4% adequacy ratio.
In plain terms

Jana is a bank running two engines at once. One is the old, high-octane microfinance engine — fat margins, but it stalls whenever the rural-credit cycle turns, as it did in FY25–26. The other is a newer, lower-revving secured engine — home loans, property-backed and small-business loans that yield less but rarely blow up. The entire investment case is about the bank rotating weight from the first engine to the second fast enough to smooth out the ride, while gathering enough cheap deposits to fund it. It survived being wiped out once; the whole strategy is an insurance policy against that ever happening again.

01

Small-finance banking 101: a different kind of bank

Why an SFB earns a wide margin, spends a lot to run, and lives or dies on funding cost.

A small finance bank is a category the RBI created in 2015 to pull financial inclusion into the formal banking net. The licence lets former microfinance lenders and niche NBFCs take retail deposits and operate as full banks — with a catch: at least 75% of their lending must go to priority sectors (small farmers, micro-enterprises, weaker sections), and half of all loans must be under ₹25 lakh. In exchange for serving harder-to-reach borrowers, an SFB earns a much wider margin than a large bank — but carries higher costs and higher risk. Five numbers tell the story.

The simple economics of an SFB

Roughly: profit ≈ (loan yield − funding cost) − operating cost − credit cost + fees. An SFB starts with a huge advantage on the first bracket — it lends at 14–24% while funding at 7–8%, so the raw spread is enormous. But it gives most of that back through two heavy costs a large bank barely feels: the expense of running thousands of small-ticket relationships (the high cost-to-income), and the losses when small, collateral-free borrowers stop paying (the credit cost). The art of building a durable SFB is to keep the fat margin while taming those two costs — which is exactly why Jana is racing toward secured, lower-loss lending and cheaper deposits at the same time.

Why "secured vs unsecured" is the fault line

  • Unsecured microfinance — a ₹40,000 group loan to a rural woman with no collateral. Yields ~22–24%, but if the local repayment culture breaks (a bad monsoon, over-lending, a political loan-waiver rumour), whole villages default together. The loss, when it comes, is the full loan.
  • Secured lending — a home loan or a loan against property or gold. Yields far less (10–14%), but the bank holds an asset it can recover against, so losses in a downturn are a fraction of the unsecured case.
  • The trade-off is the whole strategy. Moving toward secured lending sacrifices some margin today to buy a smoother, lower-loss book tomorrow. For a lender that was once nearly wiped out by an unsecured shock, that trade is not academic — it is existential.
02

From India's biggest MFI to a bank

A near-death experience that explains everything the bank now does.

Jana's strategy makes no sense without its history. The bank grew out of Janalakshmi Financial Services, founded in 2006 by Ramesh Ramanathan — a former Citibank banker — and his wife Swati Ramanathan, to bring credit to India's urban working poor. By January 2017 it had become the country's largest microfinance institution, with a loan portfolio of ~₹12,253 Cr across 284 districts and 19 states. Then, in a single policy stroke, it almost ceased to exist.

The demonetisation shock

In November 2016, the government abruptly voided ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes. Microfinance runs on cash — small weekly repayments collected in person — so the shock hit Janalakshmi at its core. Its collection efficiency, normally ~98%, collapsed to 77% within two months. Borrowers who had never missed a payment simply had no valid notes; some read the chaos as licence to stop paying entirely. By the time it converted into a bank, Janalakshmi had racked up accumulated losses of roughly ₹2,504 Cr — its capital was nearly erased, and it survived only through emergency equity injections. This is the memory that drives the secured pivot.

The scar that became the strategy: a lender that lost the equivalent of years of profit to a single unsecured shock does not forget it. Jana's relentless push into collateral-backed lending and retail deposits is, at its root, an institution building armour against the exact event that once nearly killed it. Read every later section through that lens.
03

The secured pivot — the thesis in one chart

The single most important thing happening at this bank: the book is changing shape.

If you understand only one thing about Jana, make it this: the bank is systematically converting itself from an unsecured microfinance lender into a majority-secured retail bank. When it became a bank in 2018, microfinance was essentially the whole book. Today, secured lending is 73% of it and management wants ~80% in the medium term. This is not a slogan — it is visible in the numbers every quarter, and it is the mechanism by which Jana is trying to earn a durable, re-rateable franchise instead of a boom-bust one.

Why the mix shift is the entire re-rating case

A bank that earns most of its money from unsecured microloans will always trade at a discount, because the market knows a downcycle can vaporise a year of profit — as FY25–26 just demonstrated. A bank that earns most of its money from home loans, property-backed and small-business credit is a fundamentally steadier animal, and steadier animals command higher multiples. So the secured pivot does two things at once: it lowers the real risk of the book, and it lets the market gradually re-rate Jana from "recovering microlender" toward "diversifying retail bank." The catch is patience — secured loans amortise slowly and yield less, so the mix moves in single-digit points a year, not overnight. The proof is in the direction, and the direction has been consistent.

Same institution, same Bengaluru roots — but the loan that once defined it has inverted. Microfinance was effectively 100% of Janalakshmi's book in 2016; it is 27% of Jana's today, and the bank wants it near 20%.
04

The microfinance cycle and the FY25–26 stress

The risk that is not hypothetical — it just played out, and it is the reason the stock is where it is.

The bull case for Jana is the secured pivot. The bear case is that the pivot is not finished — and while a quarter of the book is still unsecured microfinance, the bank remains exposed to a downturn in that market. FY25 and FY26 proved the point. A sector-wide microfinance stress event — years of over-lending had left many rural borrowers with loans from four, five, six lenders at once — turned into a wave of defaults across the whole industry. Jana's still-material microfinance book took the hit, and earnings buckled.

The non-transferable arithmetic

Hold this comparison in your head: in FY24 Jana earned ₹670 Cr; in FY26, on a balance sheet roughly 23% larger, it earned just ₹326 Cr. The bank did not shrink, mismanage its deposits, or lose its franchise — deposits and AUM both grew strongly throughout. The entire ~₹344 Cr swing is the microfinance credit cycle passing through a book that is still a quarter unsecured. That is precisely why the secured pivot matters: the smaller the unsecured slice, the smaller the next cycle's bite. FY26's profit is what the microfinance downcycle costs a bank at 73% secured. At 80%+ secured, the same shock would sting a good deal less.

Management's response has been threefold: provide aggressively against the stressed book (taking the pain up front rather than deferring it), tighten microfinance underwriting and stop lending to over-indebted borrowers, and accelerate the secured build so the unsecured share falls faster. The FY27 guidance — an ~80% rebound in profit — rests on the stress having peaked and provisions normalising. If the microfinance cycle has genuinely turned, FY27 is a snap-back year; if a fresh leg of stress emerges, that guidance is the first thing to slip.

The risk, stated plainly: Jana is not yet a fully de-risked bank. Roughly a quarter of its lending is still collateral-free microfinance, and Indian microfinance has a long history of periodic, region-specific blow-ups — bad monsoons, local over-lending, political loan-waiver announcements. Until the secured mix is comfortably in the 80s and the microfinance book is both smaller and cleaner, credit cost remains the number that can turn a good year into a poor one without warning.
05

Building a deposit franchise from scratch

The other half of the job — and the harder, slower one.

An NBFC-turned-bank does not just get to lend differently; it gets to fund itself differently — by taking deposits instead of borrowing wholesale. That is the great prize of a banking licence. But cheap, sticky deposits are the hardest thing in banking to build, and a nine-year-old bank with no legacy branch network and a value-segment customer base starts far behind. Jana has grown deposits impressively — up 23% to ~₹35,784 Cr in FY26 — but the quality of that funding is still a work in progress.

Why CASA is the weak spot

  • The mix is skewed to expensive money. Roughly 82% of deposits are fixed deposits paying 7–8%; only ~18% sits in cheap current and savings accounts. To win those FDs, Jana offers among the higher rates in the market — good for savers, costly for the bank.
  • CASA is inching up, not surging. It recovered toward ~20% during FY26 from ~18% — progress, but a mature bank runs 35%+. Every point of CASA gained is a direct, permanent reduction in funding cost.
  • Branches are the deposit machine. With ~770-plus branches and over 12 million customers, Jana is expanding its physical footprint precisely to gather stickier, lower-cost deposits and salary accounts — the slow, unglamorous work that eventually lowers the cost of funds.
Why the deposit build decides how good the margin can get

Jana's ~6.6% margin looks enormous next to a big bank's ~3.4%, but a large chunk of it is consumed by an expensive, FD-heavy deposit base. If the bank can steadily lift CASA and let its brand and branch network mature, its cost of funds falls — and because its loans reprice more slowly, that flows straight through to a wider, more durable spread. The uncomfortable truth is that this is a multi-year grind with no shortcut: deposit trust is earned deposit by deposit, branch by branch. A young SFB's margin is high but fragile; a maturing one's margin is a little lower but far more dependable. Jana is trying to travel from the first to the second.

06

The company and who owns it

A widely-held, professionally-run bank with no dominant promoter family.

Jana is not a promoter-controlled bank. Its equity is held by a spread of institutional and financial investors — a legacy of the many capital rounds that rebuilt Janalakshmi after demonetisation — with no single family in control. Governance runs through a professional board and management team, and the founders' original social-inclusion mission still frames the bank's identity.

Ownership and leadership

  • No promoter family. After multiple rescue and growth capital rounds, ownership is dispersed across institutional investors, private-equity and development-finance backers, and — since the February 2024 IPO — public shareholders. This makes Jana a professionally-managed, widely-held bank rather than a founder-driven one.
  • Founding mission, institutional spine. Ramesh and Swati Ramanathan built Janalakshmi around urban financial inclusion; that DNA persists in Jana's value-segment focus, even as the balance sheet professionalises and diversifies.
  • A recently-listed bank. The 2024 IPO raised ~₹570 Cr and brought public-market discipline — quarterly disclosure, an independent-heavy board, and market scrutiny of the very asset-quality and margin metrics this primer walks through.
  • Capital as the anchor of confidence. A 19.4% capital adequacy ratio — well above the regulatory floor — is the reason the bank could absorb the FY25–26 microfinance shock without stress to solvency. For a lender with this history, a fat capital buffer is not optional; it is the whole point.
What being widely-held changes

Most Indian banks and NBFCs are steered by a controlling promoter whose name is the brand. Jana is not — which cuts both ways. There is no single owner whose judgement or succession risk you are underwriting, and no promoter pledging or related-party worry to track. But there is also no deep-pocketed patron guaranteed to backstop the bank in a crisis, so the equity market and the bank's own capital buffer are the safety net. That places extra weight on the two things this bank most needs to demonstrate: disciplined underwriting and a fortress balance sheet. So far the capital ratio says it has kept its side of that bargain.

07

The small-finance-bank arena

A crowded field of ex-microlenders, all racing to look more like real banks.

Jana competes in one of India's most contested niches. A dozen SFBs — nearly all born from microfinance or niche NBFCs — are chasing the same value-segment borrowers and the same scarce deposits, while trying to climb the credit-quality ladder toward mainstream banking. Above them sit the affordable-housing financiers and big private banks moving down-market; below them, the pure MFIs and fintech lenders. Jana's differentiation is its diversification: it is neither a monoline microlender nor a niche housing financier, but a genuinely multi-product retail bank in the making.

The competitive point that matters most

The single most useful comparison in this whole sector is Jana versus AU. AU Small Finance Bank started life the same way — a niche vehicle-finance NBFC — and has since graduated into a full universal bank, with a secured-heavy book, a stronger deposit franchise, cheaper funding, and a premium valuation to match. It is living proof that the SFB model can mature into something durable and richly-valued. Jana is several years behind on that same road: less secured, more expensive funding, a lower multiple. The bet is that Jana keeps walking AU's path. The risk is that it stalls, still carrying a microfinance tail, while better-funded rivals pull ahead.

The regulatory backdrop: SFBs live inside a tight RBI framework — the 75% priority-sector lending floor, the sub-₹25-lakh loan-size rules, capital requirements, and the microfinance conduct norms (borrower-indebtedness caps, pricing transparency) that were tightened after the FY25 stress. Regulation both constrains the model and, by pushing responsible lending, protects it. And it holds out the ultimate prize: an SFB with a clean record and enough scale can apply to become a full universal bank — which is exactly what Jana is now trying to do.
08

The universal-bank prize

The optionality at the top of the ladder — and why it is not a done deal.

The RBI allows an SFB with a strong track record — five years of operation, minimum net worth, a listed status, a clean asset-quality and profitability record — to apply to convert into a full universal bank. That upgrade is transformational: it removes the priority-sector and loan-size straitjacket, lowers the cost of doing business, lifts the brand, and typically earns a re-rating, as AU's graduation showed. Jana has stated its ambition to travel that road and applied for a universal-bank licence.

Why it matters — and why it is optionality, not a certainty

  • The re-rating lever. Universal banks trade at richer multiples than SFBs because they are less constrained, better funded and more diversified. A successful conversion would be the clearest single catalyst for Jana's valuation.
  • The freedom lever. Escaping the 75% priority-sector requirement and the small-ticket caps would let Jana lend across a wider range of customers and products — a structurally more profitable and flexible bank.
  • But the RBI holds the gate. Conversion is discretionary and criteria-driven, not automatic. Tellingly, the regulator returned Jana's initial application — the bank is now reworking it and intends to resubmit "at an appropriate time." An SFB carrying elevated microfinance stress is not the ideal candidate; the licence and the asset-quality cleanup are linked.
How to weigh the licence in the case

Treat the universal-bank licence as upside you are not paying much for, not a foundation you are relying on. If it arrives, it is a genuine catalyst — cheaper operations, a wider mandate, a higher multiple. If it is delayed or refused, the investment case still has to stand on the secured pivot, the deposit build and the earnings recovery alone. The fact that the RBI sent the first application back is a useful reality check: the regulator effectively told Jana to finish cleaning up the microfinance book and prove its metrics first. In other words, the licence and the FY27 recovery are the same story — nail the asset-quality turn, and the licence becomes far more attainable.

How to think about the valuation

Jana trades around a ~₹6,150 Cr market cap — roughly 1.0× book value and a mid-teens multiple of cyclically-depressed FY26 earnings. For a bank, the number that settles the case is not the P/E but the return on assets and return on equity, normalised through the cycle. Today ROE sits near ~7.6% — well below where a maturing SFB should run — precisely because provisions crushed FY26 profit. The whole valuation debate reduces to one judgement: is Jana's through-cycle ROA/ROE structurally higher than this depressed print, and how fast does it get there? If the microfinance stress has peaked and the guided FY27 profit rebound lands, the bank re-rates on normalising returns. If credit costs stay elevated, a ~1× book multiple on a sub-8% ROE is simply the market pricing a bank that has not yet proven its returns are durable. The book value grows either way; the multiple hinges on the ROE recovery.

10

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A rebuilt bank mid-transformation — the debate is execution, not existence.

Jana is no longer the fragile microlender demonetisation nearly finished. It is a diversified, well-capitalised small finance bank with a clear, credible strategy: secure the book, gather deposits, mend asset quality, and — eventually — graduate to a universal bank. What is unresolved is pace and proof: how fast the secured mix crosses 80%, how quickly funding cost falls, and whether the microfinance cycle has truly turned.

The bull case

  • The secured pivot is working — 73% secured and climbing toward ~80%; each point lowers cyclicality and supports a re-rating.
  • Cheap valuation — ~1× book on cyclically-depressed earnings, with an ~80% profit rebound guided for FY27 as provisions normalise.
  • Fortress capital — 19.4% CAR absorbed the microfinance shock without solvency strain; it can fund years of growth.
  • Deposit and branch momentum — +23% deposit growth, 12M+ customers, a widening footprint that should slowly lift CASA and cut funding cost.
  • The AU precedent — a real, walked path from SFB to premium-valued universal bank; the universal-licence application is free optionality on top.

The bear case

  • Still an unsecured tail — ~27% microfinance means the next rural-credit downcycle bites again before the pivot is finished.
  • Earnings just halved — FY26 profit of ₹326 Cr, below FY24's ₹670 Cr, shows how exposed the model still is to credit cost.
  • Expensive funding — ~18% CASA and an FD-heavy base keep the cost of funds high; the fix is slow and structural.
  • Licence not guaranteed — the RBI returned the universal-bank application; the upgrade and the multiple that comes with it are deferred, not banked.
  • Crowded field — better-funded SFBs (AU, Ujjivan, Equitas) are further along the same road and competing for the same deposits and borrowers.
The bottom line

Jana is a survivor's bank — an institution that was nearly erased and rebuilt itself around one hard-won lesson: never again let an unsecured shock threaten the whole enterprise. Its strategy is coherent and its capital is strong, but it is caught mid-transformation, and FY26 showed exactly what the unfinished part costs. The next few numbers to watch are concrete and near-term: the secured mix crossing 80%, the microfinance GNPA falling for consecutive quarters, the CASA ratio grinding higher, and — the one that settles it — return on equity climbing back toward mid-teens as the guided FY27 profit rebound arrives. Hit those, and a cheaply-valued bank re-rates as the pivot completes and the universal-bank door reopens. Miss the asset-quality turn, and Jana stays what the market currently prices it as: a recovering microlender that has not yet earned the "bank" premium.
