Indus Towers, the landlord of India's mobile network

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're actually looking at — read this before anything else.

Indus Towers is India's largest telecom-tower company — a "passive infrastructure" business that owns roughly 2,64,500 mobile towers and rents space on them to telecom operators (Bharti Airtel, Vodafone Idea, Reliance Jio, BSNL) on long-term contracts. It doesn't sell mobile plans, spectrum or handsets; it is the landlord that owns the steel, land, power and back-up systems the operators bolt their antennas onto. That makes it a rare thing in telecom: a stable, annuity-like, high-margin, cash-generative business rather than a subscriber-churning, price-warring one.

Three facts frame everything:

  • This is an annuity, not a growth stock. Indus earns long-term rent per tenant per tower under multi-year contracts. FY26 revenue was ~₹32,493 crore with a reported EBITDA margin around 55% and a pre-tax return on capital of ~20%. The economics are governed by one master number — the tenancy ratio (tenants per tower, currently 1.62) — because each extra tenant on an existing tower is almost pure profit (§2).
  • Its owner is also its biggest customer. Bharti Airtel controls Indus (~48–50% stake) after Vodafone Group fully exited in 2024 — and Airtel (with Bharti Hexacom) is also Indus's largest tenant. That dual role of owner-and-customer is both a strength (a committed anchor) and a governance nuance (§4).
  • The Vodafone Idea saga is the defining risk. Indus's other big customer, Vodafone Idea (Vi), spent years in financial distress, ran up huge overdue rent, and forced Indus to book massive doubtful-debt provisions that crushed reported profits — then, as Vi got funding, Indus wrote those provisions back, inflating a later year's profit. FY26's optically ugly "−28% PAT" is mostly this base effect. Whether Vi survives and keeps paying is the single biggest swing factor in Indus's earnings (§5).
In plain terms

Think of Indus as the owner of tens of thousands of tall steel poles dotted across India, each on a patch of land with a power supply and a diesel/battery back-up. Mobile companies don't want to build and maintain their own poles everywhere — it's cheaper to rent space on Indus's. So Indus signs long leases and collects rent, month after month, like a commercial landlord. The magic is that a single pole can host several tenants at once: the pole costs roughly the same whether one operator or three hang their antennas on it, so every extra tenant is nearly free money. That is the whole business. The one thing that can go wrong: a big tenant that can't pay its rent. For years, that tenant was Vodafone Idea — and its troubles are the reason Indus's reported profits have swung so wildly.

01

How a tower company actually earns — the annuity model

Forget subscribers and ARPU. A tower company is a landlord, and it's judged like one.

To understand Indus you have to stop thinking about it as a "telecom" company and start thinking about it as commercial real estate for radio signals. A mobile operator needs somewhere high to put its antennas, with power and back-up so the signal never drops. Building and running that infrastructure everywhere is enormously expensive. So operators outsource it — they rent space on a shared tower company's sites and pay a monthly fee. Indus is that shared tower company, at national scale.

The tower-company dashboard — the terms that actually matter
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for Indus)
Tower (site)The physical structure — steel mast, land, power, diesel/battery back-up. Indus had ~2,64,500 towers at March 2026. Building one costs money once; it then earns rent for years.
Tenant / co-locationEach operator that mounts its equipment on a tower is a tenant (a "co-location"). One tower can hold several. Indus had ~4,28,000 co-locations — i.e. it rents its towers out ~4.28 lakh times over.
Tenancy ratioCo-locations ÷ towers = tenants per tower. At 1.62, the average Indus tower carries 1.62 operators. This is the single most important number in the business — decoded fully in §2.
Rental revenueThe recurring monthly rent each tenant pays under a long-term (typically ~15-year, MLA-based) contract, usually with inflation-linked escalations and exit penalties. This "core revenue" (~₹20,900 Cr in FY26) is the annuity.
Energy pass-throughIndus also recovers the cost of powering sites (diesel, grid, solar) from tenants. This inflates headline revenue but is largely a pass-through — which is why analysts watch core/rental revenue, not gross.

Because the contracts are long and the customers are the country's biggest operators, the revenue is remarkably predictable. Once an antenna is installed on a tower and connected into a network, moving it is expensive and disruptive — so tenants tend to stay. That stickiness, plus contractual escalations, is what makes tower revenue an annuity: a stream of payments you can forecast years out, far more stable than the operators' own subscriber revenue.

Why "annuity" and "operating leverage" are the two words to remember

An annuity is a steady, contracted stream of cash — the opposite of a boom-and-bust business. Indus has one because rent keeps coming whether the operator gains or loses subscribers that month. Operating leverage means most of Indus's costs are fixed — the tower, the land lease, the maintenance crew cost roughly the same regardless of how many tenants are on the mast. When you add fixed costs and rising revenue, profit grows faster than revenue. Together these two properties explain the whole appeal of a tower company: dependable cash today, and a profit engine that gets more efficient every time another tenant is added. The next section shows exactly how powerful that second effect is.

02

The tenancy-ratio engine — why one number rules everything

The master lever of tower economics. Read this section slowly — it explains the whole business.

If you learn one thing about Indus, learn this: the tenancy ratio is the master lever of its profitability. A tower costs almost the same to build and run whether it carries one tenant or three. But each tenant pays rent. So every additional tenant on an existing tower drops almost entirely to profit — the fixed cost is already paid. Raising the tenancy ratio, not just building more towers, is how a tower company gets dramatically more profitable without spending much more.

Why raising the ratio is so much better than building towers

  • Building a new tower requires fresh capital (land, steel, power, permits) and starts life at a tenancy of 1.0 — low-margin until a second tenant arrives. It grows the business but dilutes the average return until it fills up.
  • Adding a tenant to an existing tower requires almost no new capital. The second and third tenants pay rent against a cost base already funded by the first. That is why analysts obsess over net co-location additions and the tenancy ratio — they measure the high-return growth, not the capital-hungry kind.
  • The math of the annuity. Indus's pre-tax return on capital employed runs around 20% and its EBITDA margin around 55% precisely because a chunk of its tenants are "free-rider" second/third tenants riding on already-paid-for towers.
The tension in FY26: Indus is adding towers fast (~15,200 in FY26) but its tenancy ratio has been stuck around 1.62 and organic co-location additions actually softened. In plain terms, it's building lots of single-tenant towers (to grab Airtel/BSNL rollout and rural coverage) faster than it's filling them with extra tenants. That grows revenue but keeps the quality of growth — the high-margin second-tenant kind — from improving. A rising tenancy ratio is the bull case; a flat one is the bears' chief complaint (§11).
A tower company doesn't get rich by building towers. It gets rich by filling them. The tenancy ratio is the scoreboard.
03

The company and how it got here

Built by rivals to share infrastructure — then reshaped by a landmark merger.

Indus Towers was born in 2007 as a genuinely unusual idea: three fierce competitors — Bharti Airtel (via Bharti Infratel), Vodafone and Idea Cellular — pooled their towers into a shared, neutral infrastructure company that would rent capacity back to all of them, and to others, on non-discriminatory terms. Sharing towers is far cheaper than each operator building its own everywhere, so the model spread fast. In November 2020, Indus merged with Bharti Infratel (the listed tower arm of Airtel), creating one of the world's largest tower companies outside China and putting the combined entity on the stock exchange under the Indus Towers name.

Timeline — from shared-tower experiment to Airtel subsidiary

  • Nov 2020

  • 2024
    Vodafone Group exits. In stages through 2024, Vodafone Plc sells its entire Indus stake (partly to fund Vi and repay its own debt); Indus becomes a Bharti Airtel subsidiary. Indus also buys ~16,000 towers from Airtel/Hexacom and runs a ~₹2,640 Cr buyback.

The structural irony: Indus was created by three rivals to be a neutral host. Today it is controlled by one of them (Airtel), while another (Vodafone, via Vi) is a strained customer, and the market leader (Jio) is a modest and reluctant tenant. The "neutral shared-infrastructure" origin now sits somewhat awkwardly with an Airtel-controlled cap table — a nuance we unpack next.
04

Who owns Indus now — the owner that is also the customer

Bharti Airtel controls Indus and rents from it. That dual role is central to the story.

After a decade of shared ownership, Indus's cap table has consolidated around one controlling shareholder: Bharti Airtel, which holds roughly 48–50%. Crucially, Airtel is not just the owner — it is also Indus's largest tenant. Vodafone Group, the co-founder that once held ~28%, fully exited by December 2024, selling down partly to fund the ailing Vodafone Idea and repay its own debt. Understanding this owner-is-also-customer structure is essential to reading Indus correctly.

Why "owner is also customer" cuts both ways

  • The strength: Airtel is a committed anchor tenant. As both owner and biggest customer, Airtel has every incentive to keep loading tenancies onto Indus towers, to keep Indus well-run, and to route its own network expansion through Indus — a rock-solid demand base most tower companies would envy. Airtel has also sold towers into Indus (e.g. ~16,000 Airtel/Hexacom sites), growing the estate.
  • The nuance: when the landlord is controlled by its biggest tenant, related-party pricing matters. The rent Airtel pays Indus is, in effect, money moving within a group Airtel part-owns. Minority shareholders rely on the contracts (Master Lease Agreements) and independent directors to ensure terms are fair — Indus's neutral-host origins and regulatory oversight are meant to protect this, but it is a governance point to keep in view.
  • The Vodafone exit's meaning: Vodafone leaving wasn't a vote against Indus's business — it was Vodafone raising cash for its own and Vi's survival. But it removed a co-founding counterweight and cemented Airtel's control, tilting Indus further toward being "Airtel's tower arm" in market perception.
In plain terms

Imagine a shopping-mall landlord whose biggest tenant also owns the mall. That tenant will keep the mall full, well-maintained and prosperous — great for the other shopkeepers (minority shareholders). But you'd also want to be sure the big tenant isn't quietly paying itself a sweetheart rent at the others' expense. That's Indus. Airtel's control is mostly a positive — a deep-pocketed, aligned anchor that wants the towers busy — but it means the fairness of related-party rents and capital decisions is something a careful investor keeps an eye on. It also means Indus's fortunes are now closely hitched to Airtel's own network strategy.

05

The Vodafone Idea saga — the receivables story that defines the stock

The single biggest reason Indus's reported profits have swung so violently. Read this closely.

No force has shaped Indus's reported earnings more than the financial distress of one customer: Vodafone Idea (Vi). Because Vi is one of Indus's two largest tenants, when Vi couldn't pay its rent, Indus faced a brutal accounting reality — it had to assume some of that rent might never be collected, and set aside huge "doubtful-debt provisions" that gutted reported profit. Then, as Vi got funding and started paying, Indus reversed those provisions — a "write-back" that inflated a later year's profit. This provision-then-write-back rollercoaster is why Indus's headline PAT looks so erratic — and why FY26's "−28%" is misleading.

A "provision" is not a cash loss — it's a book estimate that money may not arrive. A "write-back" is reversing that estimate when the money does arrive. Both hit reported profit hard, in opposite directions — which is why you must look through them.

The numbers behind the saga

  • The provisions. As Vi's distress deepened, Indus booked repeated doubtful-debt charges — for example a ₹2,298 Cr provision in Q3 FY23 — and flagged that Vi's status as a "going concern" depended on it raising funds. At its peak, doubtful receivables tied to Vi ran into the thousands of crores (~₹5,386 Cr at March 2024, falling to ~₹3,548 Cr by Sept 2024 as Vi paid down).
  • The partial recovery. Vi resumed paying its monthly bills from January 2023 and cleared chunks of overdue dues (e.g. ₹2,328 Cr of arrears around Q2 FY25), helped by its ₹18,000 Cr FPO and the government converting Vi's dues into equity. That let Indus write back ~₹5,100 crore of earlier provisions in FY25.
  • The FY26 base effect. That FY25 write-back created an artificially high profit base. So when FY26 came in clean (no big write-back), reported EBITDA fell ~14% and PAT fell ~28% versus that inflated base. Strip the one-offs out and the underlying business actually grew EBITDA ~11% and PAT ~13%. The "decline" is an accounting illusion.
Why Vi's survival is existential for Indus: Vi is not just a debtor — it is a large, recurring tenant whose rent is baked into Indus's revenue run-rate. If Vi were to collapse and vacate its tenancies, Indus would lose a big slug of high-margin rental revenue that can't easily be replaced, and would have to re-provision uncollected dues. That is why the market treats every twist in Vi's rescue — government equity conversions, AGR relief, funding rounds — as directly relevant to Indus. Indus's fortunes are hostage to whether Vi keeps paying and keeps operating.
The one habit that keeps you from being fooled

When you read Indus's results, always ask: was there a big provision or write-back this period? A tower company's underlying business changes slowly (rent trickles in, tenancies inch up), so any violent move in reported profit is almost always the Vi receivables line, not the business. Judge Indus on its normalised EBITDA and PAT (ex-one-offs), its core/rental revenue, its net co-location additions and its tenancy ratio — and treat the Vi provision/write-back swings as balance-sheet noise layered on top. That single habit turns Indus's confusing headline numbers into a clear picture.

06

Customer concentration — two tenants carry almost the whole business

A short customer list is Indus's greatest strength and its greatest vulnerability.

India's telecom market consolidated into a handful of giant operators, and Indus's customer base consolidated with it. The result: Indus depends overwhelmingly on Bharti Airtel and Vodafone Idea, with Reliance Jio and BSNL as smaller tenants. This concentration is a double-edged sword — it delivers huge, dependable anchor demand, but it means the fortunes of just two companies drive most of Indus's revenue.

  • Airtel — the anchor. As owner and largest tenant, Airtel is a bedrock of demand. Its own 4G/5G expansion and rural rollout translate directly into new Indus tenancies and tower orders. This is the most reliable pillar of the business.
  • Vodafone Idea — the swing factor. Vi's tenancies are large and high-margin, but its ability to pay has been the central uncertainty (§5). Every improvement in Vi's finances de-risks Indus; every relapse re-introduces provisioning risk.
  • Jio — the structural gap. Reliance Jio built much of its own tower network and houses it in a separate infrastructure vehicle, so Jio is only a partial Indus tenant. Winning more Jio loading would diversify Indus away from the Airtel/Vi pair — a genuine upside lever, but not one Indus controls.
  • BSNL & new demand. The revival of state-owned BSNL's network and rural-coverage mandates offer incremental tenancy demand that is less tied to the big-two.
Why concentration is the flip side of the annuity

The same thing that makes Indus's revenue stable — a handful of huge, long-contracted operators — is what makes it concentrated. With only three-to-four possible customers in the entire country, losing or impairing even one is a material event. A diversified landlord with a thousand small tenants can shrug off a few defaults; Indus cannot, because two tenants are the business. That's why "customer concentration" and "the Vi receivables saga" are really the same risk viewed from two angles — and why the health of India's telecom operators is the health of Indus.

07

The 5G-densification & rural tailwind — where new tenancies come from

The structural reason a mature tower estate can still grow: more antennas per square kilometre.

A tower company in a "3-player, saturated-subscriber" market might look like it has nowhere to grow. But India's shift to 5G and deeper rural coverage is quietly a powerful tailwind — because 5G needs far more, and denser, radio sites than 4G, and rural India still needs coverage. Both create demand for exactly what Indus sells: places to hang antennas. This is the engine that can lift tenancies and, ideally, the tenancy ratio.

Why 5G means more towers and more tenants

  • Higher frequencies travel shorter distances. 5G often uses higher-frequency spectrum that covers a smaller area per cell, so operators must install more sites (densification) to blanket the same geography — more sites means more Indus tenancies.
  • Data explosion needs capacity. India's data consumption grew ~29% YoY in FY26 and 5G already accounts for ~40% of data traffic on a base of 500,000+ 5G base stations across networks. Handling that traffic requires continuous network loading — new co-locations and upgrades on existing towers.
  • Rural & 4G catch-up. Beyond 5G, Vi's 4G expansion, BSNL's revival and government rural-coverage goals all add tower and tenancy demand in areas that were previously under-served.

Indus is leaning into this with record tower additions, "leaner tower" designs for cheaper sites, and a big sustainability push (solar at ~42,400 sites, ~7% lower diesel use even as sites grew) that cuts the energy cost of each tower. It is also diversifying: fibre, small cells and in-building solutions extend the "landlord" model beyond macro towers into the denser, urban 5G layer.

The catch — additions vs. filling: the FY26 growth is real, but it is tower-led (more masts) more than tenant-led (more tenants per mast). Organic co-location additions actually softened and the tenancy ratio stayed at 1.62. The bull case needs 5G densification to eventually flow into a rising tenancy ratio — extra tenants on existing towers — which is where the high-margin growth lives. So far the tailwind is filling the order book more than it is lifting the ratio.

09

Cash returns & valuation — the dividend debate

A cash machine that stopped paying dividends — and has just started again.

A mature tower company generating steady free cash flow should, in theory, be a dividend machine. Indus was — until the Vi crisis forced it to hoard cash as a buffer against uncollected rent and to fund tower buys and buybacks. It paused dividends for three years (FY23–FY25). In FY26, with Vi largely paying and receivables normalised, Indus resumed dividends at ₹14/share, distributing essentially all of its ~₹3,760 Cr free cash flow. How it returns cash — dividend vs buyback — is now a live debate.

The valuation picture (mid-2026)

  • Market cap ~₹1.05 lakh crore, share price in the ~₹390–430 band, trailing P/E ~16× and forward ~15× — inexpensive for a business with 55% margins and 20% returns, reflecting the market's caution on Vi risk and flat tenancy growth.
  • EV/EBITDA ~7× — a classic infrastructure/utility-style multiple. Enterprise value is only modestly above market cap because Indus carries little net debt (it's net-cash), unlike most tower companies globally which are heavily levered.
  • The buyback vs dividend twist. India's 2026 Budget changed how buyback proceeds are taxed (now as capital gains in shareholders' hands), reshaping the math. Indus has used both — a ~₹2,640 Cr buyback in 2024 and now a resumed dividend — and management has signalled a "steady and progressive" distribution policy tied to free cash flow.
  • Analyst spread is wide. Targets have ranged from ~₹345 (bearish, worried about flat tenancy and Jio overhang) to ~₹580 (bullish on 5G loading and cash return) — a spread that itself tells you the debate is about growth quality and Vi, not the stability of the annuity.
Why the multiple is low for the quality

On paper, a 55%-margin, 20%-return, net-cash annuity should command a premium. Indus trades at a fairly ordinary ~16× earnings because the market prices in three discounts: (1) customer risk — the Vi overhang and reliance on just two big tenants; (2) flat tenancy growth — the concern that Indus is building towers faster than it's filling them, so per-tower economics aren't improving; and (3) the Airtel/Jio backdrop — a saturated, three-player market with limited new-operator demand. If Vi keeps stabilising and the tenancy ratio starts rising, those discounts can narrow — which is the essence of the bull case (§11).

10

Competition and new frontiers

Where Indus sits in the tower market — and where it's trying to grow next.

Indus is the clear #1 tower company in India by a wide margin, but it is not the only game. Its competitive position rests on scale and Airtel anchoring; its growth frontiers lie in new tenants, adjacent infrastructure, and — for the first time — overseas expansion into Africa.

The growth frontiers

  • Loading existing towers (the best growth). Winning more Jio/BSNL tenancies and 5G small-cell loading onto already-built towers — the high-margin, tenancy-ratio-lifting kind of growth.
  • Adjacent infrastructure. Fibre, small cells, in-building solutions and smart-city infrastructure extend the "neutral host" model into the dense urban 5G layer beyond macro towers.
  • Africa — the new bet. Indus is entering Zambia, Uganda and Nigeria, securing licences and lining up anchor customers — its first move outside India, chasing higher-growth, under-towered markets. Early-stage and small, but a strategic diversification away from the saturated, two-tenant Indian base.
  • Energy & sustainability as a cost moat. Aggressive solarisation (~42,400 solar sites) and diesel reduction cut the running cost of each tower — improving margins and ESG standing simultaneously.
11

Future, risks, and the bull/bear case

A high-quality annuity whose upside hinges on tenancy growth and Vi's health.

Indus is one of the most stable, high-margin, cash-generative businesses in the Indian large-cap universe — a genuine infrastructure annuity with net cash and ~20% returns. The debate is not about whether the business is good; it's about whether it can grow the high-margin way (rising tenancy ratio) and whether its customer risk (Vi) is truly behind it. Here is the case on both sides.

The bull case

  • Annuity quality. Long-term contracted rent, ~55% EBITDA margins, ~20% returns, net cash — an unusually defensive, predictable cash machine.
  • Vi de-risking. With Vi paying monthly, provisions written back and receivables normalised, the single biggest historical risk has materially eased.
  • 5G densification runway. Data +29%, dense 5G loading and rural/BSNL rollout can lift tenancies — and, crucially, the tenancy ratio — for years.
  • Cash returns resumed. ₹14/share dividend back after 3 years, with a progressive-distribution signal; a net-cash balance sheet supports steady returns.
  • Cheap for the quality. ~16× P/E and ~7× EV/EBITDA under-price a 55%-margin, 20%-return annuity if growth and Vi stay on track.

The bear case

  • Tenancy ratio stuck at 1.62. Growth is tower-led, not tenant-led; organic co-location adds softened — the high-margin engine isn't accelerating.
  • Vi is not fully cured. Vi still carries enormous government dues and going-concern uncertainty; any relapse re-opens the provisioning risk that gutted profits before.
  • Two-tenant concentration. Airtel + Vi are the business; there is no diversified tenant base to cushion the loss or impairment of either.
  • Jio overhang & saturation. A saturated three-player market with Jio largely self-hosting caps new-tenant demand and long-run growth (analysts model just ~3–8% earnings CAGR).
  • Capex & related-party questions. Rising capex on single-tenant towers, plus Airtel-controlled related-party pricing, are governance/return-quality watch-items.
The bottom line

Indus Towers is the landlord of India's mobile network — a stable, high-margin, annuity-like infrastructure business whose economics are governed by one number, the tenancy ratio, and whose reported earnings have been whipsawed by one relationship, its Vodafone Idea receivables. The underlying machine is excellent: contracted rent, ~55% margins, ~20% returns, net cash, and a resumed dividend. The two open questions are quality of growth (can it fill towers, not just build them, and lift the tenancy ratio?) and customer health (is Vi truly stabilised, or one funding scare from re-provisioning?). This is not a high-growth story; it is a defensive, cash-returning infrastructure annuity with a specific, well-defined risk. Track four things each quarter — core/rental revenue, net co-location additions, the tenancy ratio, and Vi's payment status. They will tell you whether the annuity is quietly compounding or merely holding.
