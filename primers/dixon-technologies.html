00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Dixon Technologies is India's largest electronics manufacturing services (EMS) company — a "contract manufacturer." It does not really sell its own brand; it builds electronics for other companies' brands. When you buy a Motorola or Xiaomi phone assembled in India, a Google Pixel, a boAt earbud, a Samsung or Xiaomi television, an LED bulb, a washing machine or a set-top box, there is a very good chance it rolled off a Dixon line. Its identity is simple: India's default factory for consumer electronics — the biggest, broadest and fastest-scaling contract manufacturer in the country.

Four facts frame everything:

  • Explosive scale, built in a few years. Revenue went from ₹4,400 Cr (FY20) to ₹49,586 Cr (FY26) — an ~11× jump in six years, a ~50% five-year growth rate. FY26 alone grew ~28%, EBITDA jumped ~69% to ₹2,580 Cr, and net profit rose ~33% to ₹1,644 Cr. Almost all of that engine is now mobile phones.
  • Thin margins, but a superb return on capital. EMS is a low-margin business — Dixon earns only about a ~5% operating margin. But because it holds very little capital per rupee of sales (customers often own the design, and inventory turns fast), it earns an eye-catching ~42% return on capital (ROCE) and ~37% ROE, and is essentially debt-free. Understanding why thin margin ≠ low return is the whole point of this primer.
  • India's #1 smartphone maker. In calendar 2025 Dixon assembled the single largest share of smartphones made in India — roughly 19% of production, up from ~11% a year earlier — displacing Samsung's own captive plant from the top spot for the first time. Mobiles & EMS are now ~90% of revenue.
  • Riding a once-a-generation policy wave. Its rise sits directly on top of the government's Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme, "Make in India," and global "China+1" supply-chain diversification. That wave is the growth runway — and Dixon is its biggest single beneficiary.
In plain terms

Think of Dixon as a "kitchen for hire" for the electronics industry. Big brands — Motorola, Xiaomi, Samsung, Google, boAt — want their gadgets made in India (cheaper, closer to the customer, and politically required), but many don't want to run their own giant factories. So they hand the recipe and the parts to Dixon, and Dixon does the cooking: assembling, testing and packing millions of phones, TVs and appliances. Dixon keeps a small slice of each device's price — often just a few percent — but it makes that slice on enormous volume, using very little of its own money because the brand owns most of the design and often the components. The two things that turned this quiet appliance-and-lighting maker into a ₹76,000-crore company: (1) the government paid brands to manufacture phones in India (the PLI scheme), which flooded Dixon with orders, and (2) Dixon is now trying to climb up-market — from cheap assembly toward higher-value design and components — to earn a fatter slice. That climb is the real investment story.

01

What "EMS / contract manufacturing" actually is

Read this once and the entire business — and its strange economics — clicks.

Most people assume a phone or TV is made by the brand on the box. Usually it isn't. The global electronics industry runs on a division of labour: brands own the customer, the design and the marketing; specialist factories own the making. Those factories are called Electronics Manufacturing Services (EMS) companies, or "contract manufacturers." The most famous is Foxconn, which builds Apple's iPhones. Dixon is India's answer to that model.

OEM vs ODM — the single most important distinction

Not all contract manufacturing is equal. There are two flavours, and the difference between them is the difference between a thin margin and a fat one:

Why ODM earns so much more

In OEM ("make to print"), the brand did the hard, valuable thinking — Dixon is just hands and machines, so it earns a labourer's cut. In ODM, Dixon supplies the brains too: it designed the product, so it captures the value of the design, not just the assembly. Historically Dixon's washing machines, LED lighting and some TVs were ODM (design-led, higher-margin), which is why those legacy segments earned more per rupee than mobiles. Its mobile business, by contrast, is mostly OEM — brands like Motorola and Xiaomi bring their own designs — which is exactly why booming mobile volumes have diluted Dixon's blended margin even as revenue exploded. The strategic mission, running through this whole primer, is to climb from OEM toward ODM and components to earn a bigger slice of each device.

What Dixon physically does on the floor

  • PCB assembly (PCBA) — populating the "green board" (the printed circuit board) with chips and components using surface-mount machines. This is the technical heart of any electronic device.
  • Box-build / final assembly — fitting the board, display, battery and casing into a finished product, then functional testing.
  • Testing & quality — every unit is tested; yield and defect rates directly drive whether Dixon makes money on a contract.
  • Sourcing & logistics — buying components (a huge working-capital job) and shipping finished goods, often to the brand's warehouses.
02

The thin-margin, high-ROCE model — the puzzle explained

The single most counter-intuitive idea in the whole investment case.

The number that confuses everyone about Dixon: it earns only about a 5% operating margin — razor-thin — yet a ~42% return on capital, which is spectacular. How can a business that keeps almost nothing on each sale be one of the most capital-efficient in the market? The answer is the key to understanding every EMS company on earth.

Margin is not the same as return

Profit margin asks: of each ₹100 of sales, how much do you keep? Dixon keeps ~₹5. That sounds bad. But return on capital asks a different question: for each ₹100 of your own money tied up in the business, how much profit do you earn a year? And Dixon ties up remarkably little of its own money per rupee of sales — so those thin margins are earned over and over as capital cycles through quickly. The formula that unlocks it: Return ≈ Margin × Asset Turnover. A supermarket earns thin margins but high returns because it turns its stock over constantly. Dixon is the electronics version of that.

Why Dixon needs so little capital per rupee of sales

  • The customer often owns the components. In many mobile contracts the brand supplies or nominates the expensive parts (chipsets, memory, displays), so Dixon doesn't fund that inventory — it just charges a conversion fee. Less of its cash is trapped in the product.
  • Inventory turns fast. An assembly line takes parts in and ships finished goods out in days-to-weeks; capital doesn't sit idle.
  • Factories are shared and asset-light-ish. One plant flexes across many customers and products; capex per rupee of revenue is modest versus, say, a steel mill or a chip fab.
  • Negative-ish working capital in parts of the business. Where Dixon gets paid before it must pay suppliers, customers effectively fund its growth.

A low margin multiplied by a high turnover produces a very high return. This is the mathematical signature of every good contract manufacturer — and the reason "5% margin" is not the warning sign it first appears to be.
The catch that keeps it honest: thin margins mean there is very little cushion. A small cost slip — a spike in memory-chip prices, a rupee move, a quality/yield problem, a customer squeezing price — can wipe out a big chunk of profit, because you were only keeping ~5% to begin with. That is exactly what happened in Q4 FY26, when rising memory prices and weaker phone demand compressed the margin and knocked ~36% off quarterly profit even as revenue held. Thin-margin businesses live or die on volume, execution and cost control — never on pricing power.

So the whole game for Dixon is: keep volumes exploding (to spread fixed costs and hold ROCE high), while lifting the margin over time by moving into design (ODM) and making its own components. Do both and you get a rare combination — a fast-growing, high-return, and margin-expanding business. Fail on either and it's just a low-margin assembler at the mercy of its customers.

03

The company and who controls it

From a small Noida TV-and-DVD workshop to India's electronics factory floor.

Dixon is a founder-built, professionally-run business. Sunil Vachani started it in 1993 making colour televisions and, later, DVD players in Noida. For two decades it was a modest, unglamorous contract manufacturer of TVs, lighting and appliances. The transformation into a national champion came only in the last few years, when the smartphone-manufacturing wave — turbo-charged by government incentives — hit its assembly lines.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter: founder Sunil Vachani (Executive Chairman) and family, holding ~28.7% (trimmed from ~32% as the group monetised part of its stake). Day-to-day the business is run by a professional management team led by MD & CEO Atul Lall, a long-tenured operator who is widely credited with the manufacturing execution.
  • Institutional base: a heavily institution-owned float (domestic and foreign funds), reflecting the stock's status as the marquee listed way to own India's electronics-manufacturing theme.
  • Balance sheet: near debt-free — modest gross debt against strong cash generation, a small net position either way. This lets Dixon fund an aggressive JV/capex programme without leverage.

The segment map — one giant vertical, four small ones

Dixon used to be a diversified appliance maker. Today it is, overwhelmingly, a phone factory.

Dixon reports across four verticals, but the shape has changed dramatically. As recently as FY25, mobiles were ~85% of the business; by FY26 the Mobile & EMS division alone is ~90% of revenue, and everything else — the TVs, appliances and lighting that built the company — has shrunk to a rounding error by comparison. This concentration is both the growth engine and a core risk.

The strategic answer is to broaden within mobile/EMS (telecom, IT hardware, wearables) and to deepen into components — so growth doesn't depend on any one brand's smartphone shipments.

05

The mobile ramp — how a TV maker became India's #1 phone factory

The transformation that rewrote the whole company in five years.

In FY20, Dixon barely made phones. By CY2025 it assembled roughly one in five smartphones produced in India — the single largest share of any manufacturer, ahead even of Samsung's own captive plant, per Counterpoint Research. That is the fastest scale-up in the history of Indian electronics manufacturing, and it explains essentially all of Dixon's revenue explosion.

Who Dixon builds phones for

  • Motorola — historically Dixon's largest single customer (over ~45% of revenue in FY25). A huge driver of the ramp — and, as we'll see, the sharpest concentration risk.
  • Xiaomi — a very large customer (~22% of FY25 revenue), one of India's top-selling smartphone brands.
  • Samsung — Dixon assembles for the global #1 brand too (~15% of FY25 revenue), a notable win given Samsung's own manufacturing.
  • realme, Vivo, Oppo, Transsion — Dixon works across the Chinese/Android brand ecosystem that dominates India's mass market.
  • Google Pixel — Dixon assembles Google's flagship Pixel phones in India (via a Compal/Google arrangement) — a marquee, premium credential.
Why the brands handed Dixon the work

Three forces pushed global brands into Dixon's arms at once. (1) The government made it pay: the PLI scheme (next section) literally wrote cheques to companies that assembled phones in India, and import duties made shipping finished phones in expensive — so brands localised fast. (2) They didn't want to build their own plants: running a giant factory is capital-heavy and off-strategy for a brand that would rather spend on chips and marketing — so they outsourced to a specialist. (3) Dixon had the scale and the track record: two decades of contract manufacturing meant it could take on millions of units with credible quality. The result: a flood of OEM assembly orders that turned a ₹4,400 Cr company into a ₹49,600 Cr one in six years.

The flip side of this OEM-assembly boom is the margin dilution described earlier: it's high-volume, low-value-add work. That is precisely why the next three sections — the policy tailwind, the components climb, and the JVs — matter so much. They are Dixon's plan to make the volume durable and the margin richer.

06

PLI, Make-in-India & China+1 — the mega-tailwind

The structural force under everything — Dixon's equivalent of a decade-long following wind.

You cannot understand Dixon without understanding the government policy that made it. India decided, as a matter of national strategy, that a country importing tens of billions of dollars of electronics should make them at home instead. The chief instrument was the Production Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme, and the global backdrop was "China+1" — multinationals seeking a second manufacturing base outside China. Dixon is the biggest listed beneficiary of both.

What the PLI scheme actually does

PLI is disarmingly simple: the government pays you a cash incentive — a percentage of your incremental sales — for manufacturing eligible products in India. Make more phones here than last year, and you get a rebate on the extra. It flips the economics of local assembly from marginal to attractive, and it did so at national scale for smartphones from 2020.

  • Smartphone PLI (2020–2026): the flagship scheme that ignited the whole boom. It concluded on 31 March 2026 — its window is what pushed brands to shift volumes to Indian contract manufacturers like Dixon, and it is the direct cause of Dixon's mobile ramp.
  • IT Hardware PLI: extends the same logic to laptops and tablets — a large import category India wants to localise next. Dixon's push here (laptops/AIOs for HP and others) targets ₹4,000 Cr+ of revenue, roughly a 4× jump.
  • Components PLI (the ₹22,900 Cr scheme): the newest and, strategically, most important — incentivising the parts inside the device (displays, cameras, PCBs, sub-assemblies). This is the policy engine behind Dixon's components climb (next section).
Why "China+1" and import-substitution matter for the runway

Two demand forces sit under Dixon for years, not quarters. Import substitution: India still imports a vast share of the value inside its electronics — every phone assembled here that was previously imported is new domestic manufacturing revenue, and the country has barely started on the harder stuff (components, semiconductors). China+1: after supply shocks and geopolitical tension, global brands want a manufacturing base that isn't China — and India, with scale, cost and now incentives, is the leading candidate. Dixon is positioned as the Indian partner of choice for a brand that wants to build here. That combination — a government paying to localise, plus a world diversifying away from China — is a structural tailwind that few businesses anywhere enjoy. It is the single biggest reason to be bullish, and the reason the stock commands a rich multiple.

The honest wrinkle: incentive schemes are, by design, temporary. The smartphone PLI ended in March 2026; a business that leans on subsidy must prove it can stand once the subsidy tapers. Dixon's answer — deepening into components and design so its economics improve regardless of PLI — is exactly the right strategic response, and executing it is the whole test of the next five years.
07

The climb up the value chain — into components

The margin-expansion thesis: stop just assembling phones, start making the parts inside them.

If assembly is thin-margin, the obvious way to earn more is to make the expensive bits yourself. A smartphone's cost is dominated by its components — the display, the camera modules, the circuit board, memory and chips. Today those are largely imported and Dixon merely bolts them together. The next act — "backward integration" — is Dixon building the components itself, capturing more of each device's value and lifting its structurally-low margin.

The further right Dixon climbs, the more of each device's value it captures — and the harder it is for a customer to switch away. This is the core margin-expansion thesis.

What Dixon is actually building

  • Display modules (JV with HKC): government-approved JV to make mobile and IT/automotive displays — Phase 1 targets ~24 million mobile displays a year, scaling toward ~54–55 million over two years, with commercial mobile-display output expected from ~Q4 FY27. Displays are one of the single most expensive parts of a phone — making them locally is a big value-capture prize.
  • Camera modules (via QTech): expanding capacity from ~70–80 million to ~180–190 million units, targeting ~₹2,500 Cr of revenue.
  • PCBA (printed circuit board assembly): the electronic heart of the device — a natural, high-value backward-integration step Dixon already does at scale and is deepening.
  • Precision/mechanical components & sub-assemblies — the surrounding parts (enclosures, connectors) that feed its own assembly lines.
Why this is the whole bull thesis in one move

Remember the core puzzle: Dixon earns great returns but thin margins. Components are the lever that fixes the margin without hurting the return. If Dixon makes the display and camera that go into a phone it also assembles, it earns money on the parts and the assembly — and a device that's mostly "Dixon inside" is far harder for a customer to move to a rival. Success here would re-rate the economics: higher blended margin, stickier customers, and a bigger slice of a device that used to be mostly imported. The risk is equally clear — components are capital-heavy (this is where Dixon's capex and JV cash goes), technically demanding, and take years to reach good yields. The margin-expansion thesis is real, but it is a multi-year build, not a switch.

How to read this pattern

Notice the logic. Some deals (Vivo, Compal/Google, Padget/HP) lock in customers and volume — securing the demand side. The risk in a JV-heavy strategy is complexity and minority-partner alignment — many moving parts, some with foreign partners subject to regulatory scrutiny — but it is a faster route to the top of the value chain than building everything alone.

The industry and the rivals

A young, fast-growing Indian EMS sector — Dixon is its clear leader.

India's EMS industry barely existed at scale a decade ago; the PLI wave created it almost overnight. The market is now a race between a handful of Indian contract manufacturers and the captive plants of global giants — with Dixon the largest and broadest of the home-grown players.

Where Dixon's edge really lies

In a thin-margin, execution-driven industry, the edge is scale and trust, not a secret product. Dixon wins because it can take on enormous volumes reliably, across many product categories, for many customers at once — spreading fixed costs and earning the credibility that gets it the next big contract. Its breadth (phones, TVs, appliances, and now components) is unusual and gives it more shots on goal than a single-category rival. The competitive threat is real, though: EMS is a volume game with modest switching costs at the pure-assembly level, so a customer can move orders to Bhagwati or its own plant if Dixon slips on price or quality. The components climb is, in part, a moat-building exercise — the more of the device Dixon makes, the harder it is to leave.

How to think about the ~53× multiple

At ~₹76,000 Cr market cap on ~₹1,644 Cr of FY26 profit, Dixon trades near 53× trailing earnings — a rich price for a business that keeps only ~5% of each sale. That multiple is not paying for the current margin; it is paying for a story: years of continued volume growth (mobiles, IT hardware, telecom), plus the margin expansion promised by the components/ODM climb, on a high-ROCE, near-debt-free balance sheet. Bulls argue you're buying the clearest listed proxy for India's electronics-manufacturing decade, run by proven operators, with real components optionality on top. Bears argue a thin-margin assembler dependent on a few customers and a now-expired subsidy is priced for perfection — and that any stumble in volumes, margins or the components ramp could de-rate a 53× stock hard. The quarter-to-quarter volatility (Q4 FY26's ~36% profit drop on memory-price and demand pressure) is a live reminder of how little cushion a ~5%-margin business has.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine national champion — priced for it to keep climbing the value chain.

Dixon is one of the defining beneficiaries of India's push to make its own electronics: the largest EMS player, the #1 smartphone maker, growing revenue and returns at a blistering pace, and now reaching up the value chain toward higher-margin components and design. The investment debate is almost entirely about margin, concentration and price — not about growth.

The bull case

  • Structural tailwind — PLI, Make-in-India, China+1 and import substitution give years of volume runway; Dixon is the biggest listed beneficiary.
  • Scale leadership — India's #1 smartphone maker and broadest EMS player, with the customer roster and credibility to win the next big contract.
  • Margin-expansion optionality — the climb into displays, cameras, PCBAs and ODM design can lift a thin margin and deepen the moat.
  • High-return, debt-free model — ~42% ROCE, near-zero net debt, self-funding an aggressive capex/JV programme.
  • New verticals — IT hardware (laptops/tablets), telecom/networking and wearables broadening the base beyond phones.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~53× earnings on a ~5%-margin business leaves no room for error; vulnerable to a sharp de-rating.
  • Customer concentration — a few phone brands (Motorola ~45%, Xiaomi ~22% of FY25 revenue) drive the business; a big client cutting or diversifying orders bites hard.
  • Thin-margin fragility — memory-price spikes, rupee moves or yield problems can hit profit disproportionately (see Q4 FY26).
  • Post-PLI question — the smartphone PLI ended Mar 2026; economics must stand on their own as subsidy tapers.
  • Execution & capex risk — the components climb is capital-heavy, technically hard and multi-year; JV complexity adds moving parts.
The bottom line

Dixon is a genuine national manufacturing champion riding a structural, policy-backed wave, with a rare mix of hyper-growth and high returns and a real plan to fix its one weakness — the thin margin. The business quality and runway are not in serious doubt. What is in doubt is price and margin trajectory: a ~53× multiple demands that volumes keep compounding and that the components/ODM climb actually lifts the margin, all while a handful of customers stay loyal in a business with little pricing power. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers — mobile/IT volumes (is the flywheel still spinning?), blended margin (is the value-chain climb working?), and customer mix (is it broadening beyond Motorola?). Get those right and the compounding justifies the premium; stumble on any, and a thin-margin assembler priced like a growth machine has a long way to fall.
