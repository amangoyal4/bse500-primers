00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

JM Financial is a diversified, home-grown financial-services group built around one of India's most respected investment banks. Founded by the late Nimesh Kampani and now run by his son Vishal Kampani, it advises corporates on mergers, runs their IPOs and share sales, trades and brokes equities, lends money, manages wealth and mutual funds, and — through a specialist arm — buys and resolves other lenders' bad loans. It is not a bank; it is a collection of financial businesses under one listed roof, some earning fees for advice and some earning a spread on money they lend out.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The advisory franchise is the crown jewel. JM Financial is a perennial top-tier Indian investment bank — in FY26 it closed ~41 capital-market transactions worth ~₹95,000 Cr and sat on a filed pipeline of ~55 IPOs worth ~₹1,40,000 Cr. This is a high-skill, capital-light, fee-based business: it needs brains and relationships, not a big balance sheet, and it earns strong returns on the little capital it uses.
  • The lending arm is the sore spot. Alongside the advisory jewel, JM ran a capital-heavy wholesale / real-estate lending book that hit asset-quality trouble — and in March 2024 the RBI barred its NBFC (JM Financial Products) from financing against shares and debentures, including IPO funding, after finding lax lending. The curbs were lifted in October 2024, but the episode reframed the group. It is now deliberately shrinking and de-risking that book: the non-core loan pile fell to ~₹2,304 Cr from ~₹5,751 Cr a year earlier.
  • The whole story is a capital-allocation pivot. FY26 consolidated PAT hit a record ~₹1,202 Cr, up 46%, even as net revenue slipped ~2% — the profit growth came from de-risking and mix, not a revenue surge. Management is steering capital away from low-return wholesale lending toward the capital-light, high-return engines — investment banking, wealth, and asset management. Yet the stock trades near 10× earnings and roughly book value, and is down ~27% over the year despite the record profit — a market not yet convinced the pivot is real.
In plain terms

Picture a prestigious advisory firm — the one companies call to sell themselves or go public — that also, over the years, grew a moneylending arm on the side. The advisory desk is brilliant, capital-light and high-return. The lending arm tied up a lot of the group's own capital, earned mediocre returns, and eventually attracted a regulator's attention. JM Financial's plan is to keep feeding the advisory desk (and its cousins in wealth and fund management) while starving the lending arm down to a smaller, safer, more retail shape. The bet: that as the bad-old lending book melts away, the group's blended return on capital climbs and the market stops valuing a fine investment bank as if it were a shaky NBFC.

01

How a financial house makes money: fees vs spread, light vs heavy

No finance degree needed — just the one distinction that runs through the entire company.

Every financial firm earns money in one of two fundamentally different ways, and JM Financial does both. Understanding the difference is the key that unlocks the whole primer, because JM's strategy is literally to move itself from the second kind of earning to the first.

The two ways a financial firm earns — and why it matters here
ModelHow it earnsCapital & return profile
Fee / advisory
(capital-light)		Charges a fee for a service — advising on a merger, managing an IPO, running someone's wealth, managing a mutual fund. The client's money moves; JM's own balance sheet barely does.Uses very little of the firm's own capital, so returns on that capital are high. But fees are cyclical — they boom with the deal market and dry up in a downturn. This is JM's investment banking, wealth and AMC.
Spread / lending
(capital-heavy)		Lends out money and earns the spread — the gap between what it charges borrowers and what it pays for funds. To lend, it must first raise and deploy large sums of its own and borrowed capital.Ties up huge capital, so returns on that capital are structurally lower — and if borrowers default, the losses land on JM's own book. This is JM's NBFC / wholesale mortgage lending. Steadier in theory, but this is where the trouble came from.

The distinction sounds academic until you attach a rupee to it. A rupee of shareholder capital sitting in the advisory desk supports a business that throws off fees with almost nothing on the balance sheet behind it — so that rupee earns a high return. The same rupee parked in the wholesale mortgage book earns a modest lending spread and carries the risk that the borrower doesn't pay it back. Same rupee, wildly different productivity. A diversified financial group's real quality is decided by which of these two buckets most of its capital and profit sits in.

Why "capital-light" is the phrase to hold onto

When you hear analysts say JM is "pivoting to capital-light," here is what it concretely means: the firm is trying to earn a bigger share of its profit from businesses that don't require it to put its own balance sheet at risk — advising, broking, distributing, managing money for a fee — and a smaller share from businesses where it has to lend out its own capital and hope to be repaid. Capital-light income is more volatile quarter to quarter, but it earns far more per rupee of equity and it can never blow a hole in the balance sheet the way a bad loan can. The re-rating case for the stock is simply this migration, made visible in one rising number: return on equity.

The units you'll see repeatedly

  • AUM (assets under management) — the pool of client money JM manages or advises on, in wealth and mutual funds. JM earns a small annual fee on it, so a growing AUM is a growing annuity. Its recurring wealth AUM reached ~₹32,000 Cr in FY26; mutual-fund average AUM ~₹14,900 Cr.
  • Loan book / AUM (lending) — the total money JM has lent out through its NBFCs. Bigger is not always better here: JM is deliberately shrinking the risky part.
  • ROE (return on equity) — profit as a percent of shareholders' capital. For a diversified financial, this is the master gauge of quality; JM's ~11.7% in FY26 is the number the pivot is meant to lift.
  • ARC (asset reconstruction company) — a specialist that buys bad loans from banks at a discount and tries to recover more than it paid. JM owns one. More on this in section 05.

One holding company, four distinct businesses — read this once and the whole group clicks into place.

From April 2025 JM re-cut its reporting to reflect how it really runs. Strip away the jargon and the group is four engines bolted to one chassis. Two are capital-light and prized; two are capital-heavy and being reshaped. Here is the whole company on one page.

The four engines, decoded

  • Investment Banking & Capital Markets — the business JM is famous for. It advises companies on selling themselves (M&A), taking themselves public (IPOs), and raising equity (QIPs) and debt. It also runs an institutional equities and broking operation. Almost pure fee income; almost no balance sheet. Section 03.
  • Wealth, AMC & Distribution — managing money for high-net-worth families, running JM's mutual funds, and a retail broking/distribution arm. Earns annual fees on a growing pool of client assets. Section 06.
  • Lending / NBFC — where JM puts its own capital to work as loans. Historically a wholesale, real-estate-heavy book; now being run down and re-pointed toward granular, retail affordable-home-loans. The engine that drew the RBI action. Section 04.
  • ARC & Alternatives — JM's asset reconstruction company buys distressed loans from banks and works to recover value, plus a private-credit and alternatives platform. Capital-heavy and lumpy, but counter-cyclical — it feeds on the distress that hurts everyone else. Section 05.
Why one group holds such different businesses

These four engines look unrelated, but they share a spine: a corporate-and-capital-markets nerve centre. The same relationships that let JM advise a promoter on an IPO also let it lend to that promoter, manage the family's newly liquid wealth, and — if a deal goes wrong somewhere in the market — buy the distressed debt through its ARC. A diversified financial house is really one relationship network monetised four different ways. The catch is that a network built for advice does not automatically make a good lender — and the gap between those two skills is exactly what the last few years exposed.

03

The crown jewel: JM as an investment bank

The reputation, the deal flow, and why it earns so well on so little capital.

Strip JM Financial to its essence and you find an investment bank — one of a small handful of Indian firms trusted to shepherd the country's largest and most complex capital-markets transactions. This is the business that carries the name, the reputation, and — quietly — the best economics in the group.

What an investment bank actually does

An investment bank sits between companies that need capital or advice and the investors who supply capital. JM's advisory and capital-markets work splits into a few core services:

  • M&A advisory — advising a company on buying another, selling itself, or merging: valuation, structuring, negotiation. JM charges a success fee, typically a slice of the deal value.
  • Equity capital markets (ECM) — running IPOs (a company's stock-market debut), QIPs (quick share sales to institutions), rights issues and block trades. JM prices the offering, lines up investors, and manages the process; it earns a fee on the money raised.
  • Debt capital markets (DCM) — helping corporates raise money through bonds and debentures rather than equity.
  • Institutional equities & broking — research and trade execution for large investors, which also feeds the deal machine with market intelligence and distribution muscle.

Two things make this business special. First, it is capital-light: JM does not lend the money in an IPO, it arranges the raise and takes a fee, so a rupee of the firm's capital supports many rupees of advisory revenue. Second, it is a reputation and league-table game: a company choosing who will run its ₹5,000 Cr IPO picks a bank it trusts to price it right and place it well, and that trust is built over decades. JM's decades-long presence in Indian dealmaking is a moat that a new entrant cannot buy.

The catch every investment bank lives with: cyclicality

The advisory engine's superpower — earning big fees on tiny capital — comes with a matching weakness: those fees swing violently with the market. In a booming year of IPOs and mergers, the deal desk mints money; in a bear market, when companies postpone listings and buyers sit on their hands, the same desk can go quiet for quarters. JM's FY26 shows the flavour of it — a record annual profit for the group, yet a Q4 in which net revenue actually fell ~12% year on year as market conditions softened. The crown jewel is genuinely high-return, but it is never a steady annuity; it is a franchise that harvests hard when the capital-markets sun shines and waits out the clouds.

04

The lending book and the RBI year

The capital-heavy leg that made JM famous for the wrong reasons — and is now being unwound.

If investment banking is the reputation JM earned over forty years, the lending book is the reputation it nearly lost in one. Understanding this engine — what it was, what went wrong, and what it is becoming — is essential, because it is the source of both the group's discount and its opportunity.

What the lending arm was

Over the 2010s JM built a sizeable NBFC lending operation, weighted toward wholesale and real-estate credit — large loans to developers and corporates — alongside a capital-markets lending line (lending against shares and, notably, financing investors to bet in IPOs). Lending is capital-heavy by nature: JM had to raise and deploy thousands of crores of its own and borrowed money, earning a spread and carrying the credit risk. When property markets and corporate credit ran into stress, a wholesale real-estate book is exactly the kind that suffers — lumpy, concentrated, hard to exit in a hurry.

The RBI action

In March 2024, the Reserve Bank of India ordered JM Financial Products (the group's NBFC) to cease and desist from any financing against shares and debentures, including sanctioning or disbursing loans against IPOs of shares and against subscription to debentures. The RBI cited serious deficiencies: JM had, the regulator found, helped a set of customers bid for IPOs and debentures using borrowed money, with a credit-assessment process it called superficial and loans extended against inadequate security. The restrictions were lifted in October 2024, roughly seven months later, after remediation. Separately, market regulator SEBI raised concerns around a debt issue and later reached a settlement.

Why this landed so hard: the RBI did not question JM's advisory competence — it questioned its lending discipline. For a firm whose entire brand is judgement and prudence in capital markets, being told its loan underwriting was superficial was a reputational blow far larger than the size of the book involved. It is the event that recast JM, in the market's eyes, from "premier investment bank" to "investment bank with a regulatory problem in its NBFC."

The genuine surprise, stated plainly

Here is the fact that catches most people off guard: the very same house that advises companies on their IPOs was also, through a different arm, financing speculators to punt on those IPOs — and it was that financing arm the RBI halted. The advisory jewel and the arm the regulator stopped sat under one roof, sharing a name. That single-roof structure is the crux of the whole JM story: the prestige of the bank and the risk of the lender are not two companies, they are two engines of one listed entity, and the market has been pricing the whole thing at the risk level of the weaker engine.

A smaller, more retail, better-underwritten lending arm is both safer and — because it releases capital — a source of fuel for the capital-light engines the group would rather grow.

05

ARC & alternatives: profiting from other lenders' bad loans

The counter-cyclical specialty most first-timers have never heard of.

Tucked inside JM Financial is a business that does something quietly contrarian: it buys bad loans. Its asset reconstruction company (ARC) purchases distressed and defaulted debt from banks — at a steep discount to face value — and then works to recover more than it paid. It is capital-heavy and unpredictable, but it has a rare property: it feeds on exactly the distress that damages everyone else.

How an ARC works

  • A bank has a loan that has gone bad and wants it off its books. It sells that loan to JM's ARC for, say, 40 paise on the rupee — taking its loss and moving on.
  • JM's ARC now owns the claim. It restructures the borrower, seizes and sells collateral, negotiates a settlement, or pushes the case through insolvency — whatever recovers the most.
  • If the ARC recovers 60 paise on a rupee it paid 40 for, the 20-paise gap (less costs and time) is its profit. Get the pricing wrong, or the recovery drags for years, and the maths can go the other way.

JM has treated the ARC as a core specialty rather than a sideline — during FY26 it raised its stake in the ARC from roughly 58% to about 80%, taking firmer control of the platform. Alongside it sits a private-credit and alternatives business, lending to and investing in situations too bespoke for a bank.

The counter-cyclical twist worth internalising

Most of JM's engines love a boom: IPOs flow when markets are euphoric, wealth grows when portfolios rise. The ARC is the odd one out — it does its best buying when credit is in crisis and banks are desperate to dump bad loans cheaply. In a sense, JM holds a small built-in hedge: when the deal desk and the lending book struggle in a downturn, the ARC's shopping list gets longer. The complication is that this is a capital-heavy hedge — resolving distress ties up money for years and the eventual recovery is genuinely hard to value in advance — which is why, even as JM took majority control of the ARC, the group's wider direction is still to lean toward the capital-light engines. The ARC is a specialty to be run well, not the growth engine the equity story is built on.

Valuation honesty: distressed assets are the hardest thing on any financial's balance sheet to value from the outside. Recoveries are lumpy, timelines slip, and the carrying value rests on management's estimates of what a pool of bad loans will eventually fetch. When you value JM's ARC stake, treat it as a range, not a point — it is real value, but the least precise number in the group.
06

Wealth, asset management & distribution

The second capital-light engine — a growing annuity of fees on client money.

The quieter half of JM's capital-light ambition is its wealth-and-asset-management cluster: managing money for wealthy families, running mutual funds, and distributing financial products to retail investors. None of it requires JM to risk its own balance sheet; all of it earns a small annual fee on a pool of client assets that, managed well, only compounds.

The three parts

  • Wealth management — advising high-net-worth and ultra-high-net-worth families on where to put their money, for a recurring fee. JM's recurring wealth AUM reached ~₹32,000 Cr in FY26, up ~26%, with the adviser headcount up sharply (relationship managers +~43% to ~1,015) and the branch network expanding. Growth here is a land-grab for advisers and clients.
  • Asset management (mutual funds) — JM runs its own mutual funds, earning a fee on the assets it manages. Average AUM rose to ~₹14,900 Cr from ~₹11,400 Cr a year earlier. Sub-scale versus the giants, but a genuine capital-light fee stream.
  • Retail broking & distribution — the platform through which individual investors trade and buy products, feeding the AUM engines and deepening the group's reach into India's fast-growing retail investor base.

The appeal of this cluster is the mirror image of the lending book's problem. Where a loan ties up capital and can default, a rupee of AUM ties up almost none of JM's own capital and simply pays a recurring fee for as long as the client stays. As India's household savings shift from bank deposits and gold into market products, the pool of fee-earning assets grows structurally — and JM's decades of relationships give it a running start at capturing them.

Why wealth is the pivot's natural partner

Wealth management is not just another capital-light business — it is the one that compounds. A single IPO fee is earned once; a wealth relationship pays a fee every year and tends to grow with the client's portfolio and with each new family the advisers win. That is why JM is spending on advisers and branches now: it is buying an annuity that thickens over time and steadies the group's earnings against the deal desk's cyclicality. If the investment bank is the pivot's high-octane engine, wealth and AMC are its flywheel — smaller today, but the part that keeps turning when the deal market pauses.

The Kampani house, the group, and who controls it

A founder-built franchise now in its second-generation transition.

JM Financial is, at heart, a Kampani-family institution. It was built by Nimesh Kampani, one of the most respected figures in modern Indian finance, and is now led by his son Vishal Kampani (Vice Chairman & Managing Director). The firm's story is inseparable from the family's, and its current chapter — a strategic reset after the lending troubles — is also a generational one.

The pivot is largely an exercise in changing the weights of these pieces without breaking the relationships that connect them.
08

The capital-light pivot: the whole thesis in one migration

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to this company's history.

Every part of this primer has been building to one point. JM Financial's value does not hinge on any single business booming; it hinges on where the group's capital and profit sit — and on a deliberate migration from one kind of rupee to another. Watch that migration and you are watching the entire thesis.

Sort JM's own shareholder capital into two piles. In the capital-light pile — investment banking, wealth, asset management — a rupee supports fee income and earns a high return with almost nothing at risk on the balance sheet. In the capital-heavy pile — wholesale lending and the ARC — a rupee earns a modest spread, ties itself up for years, and can be lost outright if a borrower defaults or a distressed pool disappoints. For most of the 2010s, JM shovelled capital into the second pile. The RBI year was the reckoning. The plan since is to reverse the flow.

The device that only fits JM Financial: the two-rupee test

Take one rupee of JM's shareholder capital and ask where it earns its keep. In the advisory desk it underwrites fees on India's biggest IPOs and barely touches the balance sheet — a high-return rupee. In the old wholesale mortgage book it earned a thin spread and, too often, met a write-off and a regulator — a low-return, high-risk rupee. JM's entire re-rating case is the migration of rupees from the second kind to the first: ~₹3,447 Cr moved in FY26 alone. That is why profit can hit a record while revenue falls — you are watching a firm swap bad rupees for good ones. And it is why the number that settles this thesis is not revenue or even PAT, but return on equity: ROE is the one figure that captures both halves of the swap at once — capital leaving the low-return pile and profit rising on a leaner base. Watch ROE climb from ~11.7% toward the mid-teens and the pivot is real; watch it stall and it is just a story.

This reframes the seemingly odd FY26 scoreboard. A ~2% dip in net revenue with a ~46% jump in profit is not a contradiction — it is the signature of a de-risking financial: the group gave up some low-quality lending income and kept far more of what remained, because it stopped feeding a book that consumed capital and courted losses. The market, so far, is reading the falling revenue and the past scandal more loudly than the rising return on capital. That gap between what the numbers are doing and what the price implies is the opportunity the bulls are pointing at.

The industry and the rivals

A different competitor in every engine — and no one who fights JM on all four.

JM Financial has no single rival, because it is really four businesses. In each engine it faces a different, often stronger, specialist. Its edge is not being the biggest in any one — it is the breadth of the franchise and the relationships that span all four.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, JM's closest structural cousins are the other diversified capital-markets houses — Motilal Oswal, Nuvama, 360 One, Edelweiss — firms that, like JM, blend advisory, wealth and some balance-sheet businesses rather than sticking to one lane. Second, JM's real competitive question is not "can it out-muscle a bulge-bracket bank on one mega-deal?" but "can it convert its four-engine breadth into a rising return on capital while the specialists keep pressure on each engine?" Breadth is JM's differentiator and its burden at once: it touches more of the market than any single-line rival, but it is rarely the outright leader in any engine except the investment bank at its core.

The one number that settles the thesis: consolidated ROE

At ~10× earnings and ~1.2× book, JM is not priced as a premier investment bank; it is priced as a diversified financial the market still half-distrusts. What flips that is not the deal count or even PAT — it is consolidated return on equity, read alongside the capital-light share of profit (is IB + wealth + AMC becoming the bulk of earnings?), the pace of the wholesale wind-down (is the non-core book still shrinking toward zero?), and asset quality in what lending remains (no fresh credit shocks). ROE is the single figure that fuses all four: it rises only if capital keeps leaving the low-return book and the remaining engines earn well and nothing new blows up. If ROE climbs from ~11.7% through the mid-teens over the next couple of years, the discount to book is unjustified and the stock re-rates. If ROE stalls near where it is, the market's caution was right. Everything else is narrative; ROE is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A repaired franchise asked to prove its return on capital, not just its pedigree.

JM Financial enters FY27 with its balance sheet visibly de-risked, its regulatory overhang lifted, a record profit behind it, and a clear strategic direction toward capital-light. What it has not yet won is the market's belief that the improvement is structural rather than a one-year clean-up. The debate below is really one debate: will the return on capital keep climbing?

The bull case

  • A genuinely strong investment bank — top-tier IPO/M&A franchise riding India's deal boom; a capital-light, high-return core that a new entrant cannot replicate.
  • The pivot is working in the numbers — non-core book cut ~60% in a year, ~₹3,447 Cr of capital freed, record PAT (+46%) on a leaner base.
  • Regulatory overhang lifted — the RBI restriction was removed in Oct 2024; the SEBI matter settled; the reputational cloud is clearing.
  • Growing capital-light annuities — wealth AUM +26%, mutual-fund AUM rising; recurring fee income to steady the cyclical deal desk.
  • Cheap on the numbers — ~10× earnings, ~1.2× book, down ~27% in a year; room to re-rate if ROE climbs.

The bear case

  • Regulatory-trust risk persists — a firm whose lending was called superficial by the RBI carries a lasting scrutiny premium; a fresh stumble would sting doubly.
  • Capital-markets cyclicality — the crown-jewel fee engine swings hard; a bear market or IPO drought (Q4 FY26 already softened) cuts profit fast.
  • Pivot-execution risk — shrinking a heavy book and scaling capital-light is gradual and unproven at scale; ROE could stall in the low teens.
  • ARC & distressed valuation risk — lumpy, hard-to-value recoveries; a majority-owned ARC concentrates that uncertainty on JM's book.
  • Competition on every front — Kotak/Axis/ICICI in IB, 360 One/Nuvama in wealth, the AMC giants, and dedicated ARC peers all pressure the individual engines.
The bottom line

JM Financial spent forty years earning a reputation as one of India's finest investment banks, then spent one year reminding everyone it had also become a lender — and not always a careful one. The company it is trying to become is the first thing without the second: a capital-light house of advisory, wealth and fund fees, with a small, safe, granular lending arm and a distressed-debt specialty run on the side. FY26 was the first year the scoreboard tilted that way — a record profit built on de-risking rather than on chasing revenue. The next test is concrete and checkable: whether the run-off of the wholesale book continues to zero and whether consolidated ROE keeps stepping up, quarter after quarter, through the mid-teens. Watch that return-on-equity line climb — or fail to — and you will know whether the market's discount was a mistake or a warning.
