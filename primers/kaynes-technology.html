Kaynes Technology, the board-builder that is learning to package chips

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Kaynes Technology is an electronics manufacturing services (EMS) company — a specialist that designs and physically builds electronic products, and the circuit boards inside them, on contract for other companies' brands. But calling it "an EMS firm" undersells the twist. Kaynes deliberately avoids the cheap, high-volume, thin-margin end of the trade (mass consumer gadgets, mobile phones) and instead concentrates on complex, high-reliability, heavily-certified electronics for demanding customers — industrial systems, cars, aerospace and defence, railway safety and signalling, medical devices. And it is now doing something almost no other Indian EMS peer is attempting: stepping past board assembly into semiconductors — building one of the country's first plants to package and test silicon chips.

Three facts frame the whole story:

  • Fast growth at unusually rich margins for the trade. Revenue grew ~33% in FY26 to ~₹3,602 Cr, EBITDA up ~40% to ~₹574 Cr — an operating margin of ~15.8%, roughly double the low-single-digits a mobile-heavy assembler earns. The order book stands at ~₹8,366 Cr, giving multi-year visibility. Section 03 explains why the end-market mix earns that premium.
  • A genuine leap into chip packaging. Through subsidiary Kaynes Semicon, the company has commissioned an OSAT plant at Sanand, Gujarat — the "back end" of chip-making, where bare silicon is packaged into finished, usable chips and tested. The Prime Minister inaugurated it in April 2026; it was India's second operational chip unit under the government's semiconductor programme. More plants (Telangana, plus a Tamil Nadu PCB/component complex) are on the drawing board — a capex programme running into thousands of crores.
  • A price that already believes. At roughly ₹32,000 Cr market cap on ~₹364 Cr of FY26 profit, the stock trades near ~88× earnings. That multiple isn't paying for the boards Kaynes builds today; it is paying for a chip-packaging business that has barely started earning. The debate, in one line: how much of a not-yet-proven semiconductor future is already in the share price.
In plain terms

Picture the electronics inside a train's braking controller, an insulin pump, or a car's power-steering unit. Someone has to buy the chips and components, assemble the circuit boards, load the software, test the whole thing to exacting safety standards, and ship it. Kaynes is that someone — a hired builder for products whose makers can't afford a single field failure. That's a better business than assembling cheap gadgets, because reliability and certifications keep rivals out and let Kaynes charge more. The new chapter is bolder still: rather than only buying chips to put on boards, Kaynes wants to package the chips themselves. If that works, it climbs one rung further up a supply chain India has spent decades importing. If it stumbles, it will have spent a fortune learning a very hard new craft — and the share price is priced as though success is close to certain.

01

What "EMS" actually is — the outsourced-building business

No engineering degree required — just one idea: making electronics is itself a service you can hire.

Nearly every modern object with a plug or a battery — a car, a ventilator, a smart electricity meter, a factory robot, a signalling box beside a railway line — runs on one or more printed circuit boards (PCBs) covered in chips and components. The company whose logo is on the outside rarely builds those boards itself. It hands the physical making to a contract manufacturer. That contractor is an Electronics Manufacturing Services (EMS) firm, and Kaynes is one of India's more capable examples.

Why brands outsource the making

An OEM (original equipment manufacturer — the brand that owns the product and the customer) is usually excellent at inventing a product, marketing it, and servicing buyers. It is often reluctant to sink capital into assembly lines, to manage a thousand component suppliers, or to hold the factory certifications that regulated products demand. So it contracts the build out to a partner whose entire craft is exactly that. The EMS firm earns its keep three ways: buying power (it purchases components for many customers at once, so it buys cheaper), shared factories (one line serves many clients, spreading fixed cost), and process rigour (the quality systems and certifications an aerospace or medical customer insists on).

The one accounting quirk to hold onto

In most industries, the price a company charges is mostly its own skill and effort. In contract electronics, a large chunk of the invoice is simply the cost of components the builder bought and is re-billing to the client. If a railway controller carries ₹850 of chips and connectors and Kaynes charges ₹1,000 for the finished, tested unit, only about ₹150 reflects Kaynes' own value — and out of that it must cover labour, plant and overheads. This is why EMS margins look thin next to software or pharma, and why the whole discipline is about widening that value-added slice: designing more of the product, working in industries where quality barriers keep prices firm, and — Kaynes' boldest move — capturing the chip inside the board, not just the board.

02

The value chain — and the extra rung Kaynes is climbing

From a customer's drawing to a shipped, tested system — and where the semiconductor step slots in.

An electronics build flows through a sequence of steps, each adding a little value. Most EMS firms live in the middle of this chain, assembling and integrating. Kaynes is unusual because it is stretching itself outward in both directions at once — backward into making the bare boards (PCB fabrication) and forward into a whole new layer that sits underneath the board: the packaging of the silicon chips that get soldered onto it.

Build-to-print versus design-led

  • Build-to-print (pure contract). The customer supplies a finished design; Kaynes builds it. Lower-margin, and easier for the client to shift to a rival, since they own the drawings.
  • Design-led / ODM. Kaynes' own engineers do the conceptual design and process engineering. Stickier and better-paid — the customer is now tied to Kaynes' know-how, and moving away means re-engineering. Kaynes markets itself explicitly as offering "conceptual design to life-cycle support," not just soldering.
  • Own the layer below. The frontier move: rather than merely buying a chip and mounting it, package the chip in-house through OSAT. This doesn't just widen the margin slice — it plants Kaynes in an entirely different, scarcer industry with its own economics (section 06).
Why widening the slice is the whole game

Because bought-in components dominate the invoice, the only durable way an EMS firm lifts profitability is to own more of what's inside the price — design content, in-house boards, specialised testing, and demanding end-markets where certification keeps competition thin. Kaynes has pursued every version of this: design-led work for regulated industries, a captive PCB build-out, and now chip packaging. Each step is an attempt to keep more of every rupee that flows across its factory floor rather than passing it straight through to a component vendor.

03

High-mix, high-reliability — why Kaynes earns more than commodity EMS

The single most important thing to understand about Kaynes before the semiconductor story even starts.

If you remember one distinction about Kaynes, make it this: it is a high-mix, high-reliability manufacturer, not a high-volume commodity one. "High-mix" means it builds a large variety of different, complex products in modest quantities — a defence avionics module here, a railway signalling board there, a medical-grade power supply, an EV battery-management unit — rather than a torrent of one identical consumer item. That choice is the reason its margins sit near 15–16% while the mobile-and-appliance assemblers scrape by in low single digits.

Why hard, regulated work pays better

  • Certification is a moat. To build for aerospace, defence, railways or medical, a supplier must hold demanding qualifications (functional-safety standards, quality systems, traceability) and prove years of reliability. Winning that trust is slow and expensive — which is precisely why, once won, the work is less contestable and better-priced. A new entrant cannot simply undercut on price the way it can for a generic consumer board.
  • Failure is unthinkable, so quality is paid for. A cheap earbud that dies is an annoyance; a railway signalling fault or a defence-system glitch is a catastrophe. Customers in these fields pay for engineering support, redundancy and testing rigour rather than shaving the last rupee — the opposite of the mobile-assembly game, where a few price-crushing giants dictate wafer-thin terms.
  • Complexity favours the capable. High-mix, low-volume production is operationally awkward — frequent line changeovers, small batches, deep engineering involvement. That awkwardness is a filter: it deters commodity players and rewards firms that have built the systems to handle variety profitably.
The margin gap in one comparison: Kaynes runs at roughly ~15–16% EBITDA margin. India's largest EMS company by scale, Dixon, historically earns low-single-digit EBITDA margins because it is anchored in mobile phones and consumer electronics — colossal volumes, brutal pricing. Same broad industry, very different economics, driven almost entirely by what you choose to build and for whom.
The counter-intuitive bit

It feels natural to assume the biggest, busiest factory is the best business. In contract electronics the reverse can be true: the firm chasing the hardest, smallest, most-certified jobs often earns more per rupee of sales than the one running the largest, fastest lines. Kaynes' bet is that reliability plus design content plus regulated end-markets is a sturdier, higher-quality growth engine than volume — and its margin profile, well above the commodity pack, is the evidence that the bet has been paying.

The company and who controls it

A first-generation Mysuru electronics business, listed in 2022, still tightly held by its founder.

Kaynes is not a conglomerate spin-off or a private-equity roll-up. It began in 1988 in Mysuru (Mysore), Karnataka, as a small electronics-manufacturing venture and grew, over more than three decades, into a national contract manufacturer with a dozen-plus plants. It remains a founder-run business, which shapes both its ambition and its concentration of control.

Ownership & management

  • Founder control. Ramesh Kunhikannan, the founder, is Managing Director and the dominant shareholder — holding on the order of ~53% personally, with the promoter group a little higher. This is a genuinely founder-led company, not a professionally-diffused one.

06

OSAT explained — the back end of chip-making

The crux of the whole story, explained from scratch: what it means to "package and test" a chip.

To understand why Kaynes' semiconductor move matters, you need one mental model of how a chip is actually made. The industry splits into a front end and a back end. The front end — the part everyone pictures, and the part that costs tens of billions of dollars — is the fab (fabrication plant), where circuits are etched onto large silicon wafers in ultra-clean rooms. The back end is where a raw wafer becomes something you can actually use. That back end is OSAT — Outsourced Semiconductor Assembly and Test, and it is exactly the rung Kaynes has stepped onto.

What "assembly and test" really means

  • Dicing. A finished wafer is a disc holding hundreds or thousands of identical circuits. It is sliced into individual dies — each die is a bare, fragile chip, too delicate to handle or connect directly.
  • Assembly (packaging). Each die is mounted onto a base, its microscopic contacts wired out to sturdy external leads (traditionally with fine gold or copper bond wires), and encased in a protective package. Only now is it a robust component that can be soldered onto a circuit board.
  • Test. Every packaged chip is electrically tested — good ones pass, bad ones are discarded — and then marked. Only tested, packaged, working chips are shipped to customers.

None of this etches new circuitry — the intelligence was created in the fab. But without assembly and test, a wafer is unusable. OSAT is the essential, lower-capital, labour-and-precision-intensive step that turns silicon into sellable chips. Globally it is a large industry of its own, led by specialists like ASE and Amkor; India, until very recently, had essentially none of it.

Why India wants domestic OSAT — and why it started here

Building a leading-edge fab is a multi-decade, tens-of-billions-of-dollars undertaking. OSAT is far more attainable — smaller cheques, more forgiving technology, and a natural fit for a country with manufacturing labour and a growing electronics base. So India's semiconductor strategy sensibly began at the back end: get packaging and testing running domestically first, reduce reliance on importing finished chips, and build the skills and supply chains that a fab ecosystem later needs. A packaged chip made in Sanand instead of imported keeps that value — and that capability — inside the country. This is why an OSAT plant, not a giant fab, is where an ambitious Indian manufacturer like Kaynes could realistically make its entry.

The non-obvious scale of it: Kaynes' Sanand line is designed to package on the order of 6.3 million chips a day at full capacity. That is roughly one Kaynes-packaged chip, every day, for every few hundred people on the planet — coming out of a company that a few years ago was best known for building instrument clusters and industrial control boards. The distance between soldering a chip onto a board and packaging the bare die inside that chip is not a small step sideways; it is a move into a different industry.
07

The semiconductor leap — Sanand, Telangana and the PCB build-out

The transformational-but-unproven bet: what has actually been built, what it cost, and what could go wrong.

The abstract idea of OSAT becomes concrete at Sanand, Gujarat, where Kaynes Semicon has commissioned a real, operating chip-packaging plant — and then extends into a second OSAT site in Telangana and a large PCB/component complex in Tamil Nadu. Taken together, this is a capex programme running into many thousands of crores, several multiples of the company's annual profit, aimed at capabilities Kaynes did not possess two years ago.

The strategic logic is coherent: Kaynes already builds the boards that chips sit on, already serves industries that consume power and control chips, and already holds the quality discipline OSAT demands. Packaging chips is an adjacent, if far harder, extension — and India's semiconductor programme is paying up to half the capital cost to make it happen. But coherence is not the same as certainty.

08

Make in India, and the semiconductor-localisation tailwind

The policy backdrop that turned a bold plan into a subsidised national priority.

Kaynes' expansion rides two overlapping waves of Indian industrial policy. The first, familiar one is electronics localisation — the "Make in India / China+1" push to build more electronics domestically instead of importing them, and to diversify global supply chains away from single-country dependence. The second, newer and more specific, is the semiconductor drive: a deliberate national effort to seed a domestic chip ecosystem, starting with the more attainable back-end (OSAT/packaging) before front-end fabs.

Three policy engines behind the story

  • Import substitution & China+1. India runs a large electronics trade deficit and wants that value — components, assembly, jobs, and now chip packaging — created at home; global brands, meanwhile, are spreading manufacturing beyond China. Both funnel demand toward capable Indian manufacturers.
  • The India Semiconductor Mission (ISM). The government's flagship chip programme co-funds semiconductor projects — offering, for approved plants like Kaynes' Sanand OSAT, capital subsidies reported at around half the project cost. That is an extraordinary de-risking of the capital outlay, and it is central to why an EMS firm could contemplate a chip plant at all.
  • Component-localisation incentives. Beyond assembling finished products, policy increasingly rewards making the components and boards that still get imported — directly supporting Kaynes' PCB/HDI build-out and its backward integration.
The nuance beneath the tailwind

Subsidies and policy momentum are real and material — a ~50% capital grant genuinely changes the maths of building a chip plant. But a careful reader treats government support as a catalyst, not a guarantee. It lowers the cost of building capacity; it does not create the customers, the yields, or the qualifications that turn capacity into profit. The durable prize is not the subsidy cheque — it is whether Kaynes ends up with a chip-packaging business that global and domestic customers actually rely on. Policy can get the plant built; only execution makes it pay.

The industry and the rivals

Two competitive arenas at once — a crowded EMS field, and a brand-new Indian OSAT race.

Kaynes competes on two fronts. In EMS, it sits among a fast-growing, richly-valued cohort of Indian contract manufacturers, differentiated by its high-reliability tilt. In semiconductors, it has entered a nascent Indian OSAT/packaging race alongside far larger industrial groups — and, globally, incumbents many times its size. Its position differs sharply in each.

10

Margins, cash and unit economics

Why Kaynes earns more than commodity EMS — and where the cash keeps disappearing.

Kaynes' profitability is a study in contrasts: margins well above the EMS commodity pack, but a growth engine that swallows working capital and a capex bill that dwarfs today's cash generation. Both halves matter, and they pull in opposite directions.

Why the margin is richer

  • Mix, mix, mix. The same logic as section 03: industrial, automotive, aerospace/defence, railways and medical work carries certification barriers and engineering content that keep pricing firm. A ~15–16% EBITDA margin is the reward for building hard things well, not cheap things fast.
  • Design content. Every rupee of ODM/design-led work adds Kaynes' own value on top of the pass-through components — and makes the customer harder to lose.
  • Operating leverage. As volumes rise across a fixed plant base, fixed costs spread thinner, nudging margins up even in a structurally component-heavy business.

Where the cash goes

  • Working-capital intensity. High-mix manufacturing and large order-book businesses like smart metering require Kaynes to buy and hold components, carry work-in-progress, and wait to be paid. Net working-capital days rose materially in FY26 (toward the ~110–120-day range from the high-80s), with smart meters a notable driver — a real drag on cash even as profits grow.
  • The capex mountain. On top of ordinary expansion, the OSAT plants and PCB complex demand thousands of crores. Even with subsidies covering a large share, Kaynes is deploying capital years ahead of the revenue it is meant to earn — which is why free cash flow lags reported profit.
  • Start-up drag. New semiconductor and PCB lines carry costs — depreciation, interest, ramp-up inefficiency — before they carry much revenue, weighing on near-term margins and profit.
The tension, stated plainly

The optimistic reading: a business compounding revenue at 30%-plus, at margins double the commodity trade, now bolting on a scarce, subsidised, high-value chip-packaging capability — profit should compound for years. The cautious reading: the same growth eats cash through inventory and receivables, the semiconductor build spends for several years before it earns, and reported profit can wobble in the meantime — as it did when Q4 FY26 net profit actually fell ~21% year-on-year even while revenue rose ~26%, on ramp-up and cost drag. Both readings describe the same company; which dominates depends on whether the new plants start earning before the cash strain and the elevated expectations run out of patience.

Financials & valuation

Rapid, richly-margined growth — carrying a valuation that leans heavily on a business barely earning yet.

Kaynes' financial record is strong and consistent: revenue roughly tripled in three years, margins held near the mid-teens, and profit grew alongside. The debate is not whether the core EMS business works — it plainly does — but how much of the share price rests on a semiconductor future that is still mostly a promise.

The one number that settles the thesis

At roughly ₹32,000 Cr market cap on ~₹364 Cr of FY26 profit — near ~88× trailing earnings — Kaynes is not priced on the boards it builds today. A high-reliability EMS business, however good, does not command 88× on its own. The premium is an implicit bet on the semiconductor and PCB layers becoming a large, profitable second engine. So the number that settles the case is specific and near-term: the revenue the semiconductor segment actually books against its ~₹1,000 Cr FY27 guide — and at what margin. That is the first hard evidence that the OSAT bet earns rather than merely spends. Alongside it, watch two core-business gauges: order-book conversion into revenue, and whether the ~15–16% EBITDA margin holds as new plants ramp and working capital stretches. Everything else is narrative; those are the meters.

A note on the figures: FY26 numbers here draw on the company's FY26 results and third-party summaries (Screener, broker notes, results coverage) and are approximate — revenue ~₹3,602 Cr, EBITDA ~₹574 Cr (~15.8% margin), PAT ~₹364 Cr, order book ~₹8,366 Cr. Market cap (~₹32,000 Cr) and P/E (~88×) are as of mid-July 2026 and move daily — the stock has been highly volatile, having fallen more than 50% from a 2025 high before recovering. The semiconductor/PCB capex and revenue-ramp figures are managed guidance and inherently uncertain; treat the OSAT timeline especially as a plan, not a fact, and verify against primary filings before relying on any single number.
12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely differentiated EMS business making an audacious, capital-heavy leap the market has already largely paid for.

Kaynes is a rare thing among Indian contract manufacturers: a profitable, high-reliability, design-led EMS firm attempting to vault into semiconductors while the core business still compounds. The core is real and the tailwinds are genuine; the argument is about the size of the semiconductor cheque, the years before it pays, and how much of that payoff is already in the price.

The bull case

  • Richer-margin core — ~15–16% EBITDA on high-reliability, certified end-markets (industrial, auto, aero/defence, rail, medical), well above commodity EMS.
  • Visible growth — ~₹8,366 Cr order book supports years of 30%-ish revenue growth before any semiconductor contribution.
  • A scarce second engine — one of India's first operating OSAT plants, ~50% subsidised, with a Japanese tech partner; a rung almost no EMS peer can reach.
  • Vertical integration — captive PCB/HDI fabrication captures component value onshore and hedges import dependence.
  • Founder conviction & policy backing — a committed founder-owner and full-throated ISM/Make-in-India support behind the semiconductor push.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — ~88× earnings prices in semiconductor success that has barely begun; the core EMS alone cannot justify the multiple.
  • Unproven, capital-heavy leap — OSAT is new to Kaynes (tech licensed from AOI), needs slow customer qualification, and could spend for years before earning.
  • Cash intensity — working-capital days rose sharply in FY26 (smart meters a driver); growth and capex both drain cash, so free cash flow lags profit.
  • Near-term profit wobble — Q4 FY26 PAT fell ~21% YoY on ramp costs; transformation is denting margins today.
  • Concentration & competition — founder-concentrated control; larger-balance-sheet rivals (Tata, CG Power) and global OSAT incumbents (ASE, Amkor) in the same race; component/forex exposure.
The bottom line

Kaynes has already done the hard, unglamorous work of becoming India's most credible high-reliability contract manufacturer — the kind trusted with railway signalling, defence electronics and even space and nuclear applications. That alone is a fine business. What lifts it to ~88× earnings is the wager that the same discipline can be carried one rung deeper, into packaging the silicon itself. The evidence will not arrive as a slogan; it will arrive as a line item. Watch the Sanand OSAT plant's first full year of chip revenue against its ~₹1,000 Cr FY27 target, and the margin at which those first Intelligent Power Modules — 17 dies to a package, already shipping to a US customer — turn into cash. When that line grows and earns, the valuation earns its keep; until it does, Kaynes is a superb board-builder being paid, in advance, to become a chipmaker.
