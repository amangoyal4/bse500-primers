Mastek, built on Britain and Oracle

A full walk through one of India's oldest software houses — a mid-cap digital-transformation and enterprise-applications firm that, unlike the Indian IT giants, earns most of its money in the United Kingdom rather than America, leans heavily on UK public-sector work, and has bought its way into a leading Oracle-Cloud practice. How it makes money, why its geography is both its moat and its biggest risk, and what actually decides a year.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Mastek Limited is a mid-cap IT services company — it sells the skilled human effort that helps large organisations replace ageing software, move onto modern cloud systems and build new digital products. Founded in 1982, it is one of India's oldest software firms, but it looks nothing like a Tata Consultancy or an Infosys. It is far smaller (FY26 revenue ~₹3,699 Cr, against TCS's ₹2.5-lakh-crore-plus), and its centre of gravity is not the United States but Britain: roughly 70% of revenue comes from the UK and Europe, a large chunk of that from UK government and public-sector bodies. Its two signature capabilities are digital transformation / application engineering and, through its Evosys arm, one of the world's larger independent Oracle Cloud implementation practices.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • It is a UK-and-Oracle mid-cap, not a US-and-everything major. Where a large-cap Indian IT firm earns half or more of its money in North America across every technology, Mastek is concentrated twice over — by geography (UK & Europe ~70%) and by platform (a deep Oracle Cloud franchise plus Salesforce and data/AI). That focus is its edge in specific niches and its single biggest risk if either the geography or the platform cools.
  • Much of its scale was acquired, not grown. Mastek's leap in digital and cloud came from buying Evosys (Oracle Cloud, 2020) and MST Solutions (Salesforce, in the Americas, 2022), among others. That inorganic strategy bought capability and geography quickly — but it also means the reported growth of recent years mixes organic work with acquired revenue, and the balance sheet carries acquisition-related goodwill and earn-outs.
  • FY26 was a steady, not spectacular, year. Revenue grew ~7% and net profit ~7.5% to ~₹404 Cr, with EBITDA margin holding near 15.8% — a recovery in tone after the mid-cap IT slowdown of 2023–24, but a long way from the ~20%+ growth of Mastek's best years. The order backlog rose a strong ~24%, and the company pinned its pitch on an AI-led services story. The stock trades on a modest ~13–14× earnings — the market pricing it as exactly what it is: a sub-scale, concentrated services firm that must prove it can grow organically again.
In plain terms

Mastek rents out expertise. A UK council, an NHS body, a Gulf ministry or an American manufacturer decides it needs to retire a creaking old system and run on modern cloud software — most often Oracle's, sometimes Salesforce's — and Mastek supplies the consultants and engineers who design, build and run that migration, billed by the hour or by the project. Its profit is what's left of the fee after it pays those people. Two things make Mastek distinctive among Indian IT names: it is unusually British, and it is unusually Oracle-shaped. Both were partly bought rather than built. FY26 showed the model steadying after a rough patch, but the question that decides the next few years is simple — can Mastek grow the core business under its own steam, without leaning on the next acquisition?

01

What Mastek actually sells: digital transformation, in plain words

No jargon needed — just the service, the buyer, and how a rupee of revenue is earned.

An IT services company does not sell a product off a shelf. It sells capacity and know-how: teams of engineers, consultants and project managers who do technology work an organisation cannot or would rather not do in-house. Mastek's slice of that world is "digital transformation" and enterprise applications — helping mostly large institutions modernise the core software they run their operations on. To follow the company you need only a handful of terms.

The only IT-services terms you need here
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for Mastek)
Digital transformationThe umbrella pitch: taking an organisation off old, on-premise, custom-coded systems and onto modern, cloud-based, standardised software and digital services. This is Mastek's core positioning.
Enterprise applications (ERP)The big software backbones a company runs on — finance, HR, supply chain, procurement. Oracle and SAP are the giants; implementing and running these is a huge services market and Mastek's Oracle heartland.
Cloud migrationMoving those enterprise applications from a company's own servers to a vendor's cloud (Oracle Cloud, Salesforce, Azure). A one-time project plus years of run-and-maintain work — recurring, sticky revenue.
Implementation partnerSoftware vendors (Oracle, Salesforce) don't install their own products at every client. They certify third parties like Mastek to do it. Being a top-tier partner is a licence to earn on the vendor's ecosystem.
Book-to-bill / backlogThe pipeline health check. New orders signed versus revenue billed; the 12-month order backlog (~₹2,849 Cr for Mastek) is the visible revenue already contracted for the year ahead.

Mastek groups its work into a few practices: Oracle Cloud applications (its largest and most distinctive), Salesforce and customer-experience work, data, analytics and AI, and digital and application engineering — building and maintaining bespoke software, cloud infrastructure and legacy modernisation. Underneath the labels the economics are the same: a project is scoped, staffed with a blend of on-site consultants (in the UK, US or Gulf) and lower-cost delivery engineers (largely in India), and billed. The gap between what the client pays and what the team costs — driven by utilisation, billing rates and the on-site/offshore mix — is the margin.

Where a rupee of Mastek revenue comes from

Every rupee is somebody's billable time. When a UK government department signs Mastek to move its finance systems to Oracle Cloud, Mastek assembles a team — a few senior consultants in Britain who face the client, a larger group of engineers in India who do the build — and charges for their hours or for the project milestones. The more of the work that can be done from lower-cost India (offshoring) without hurting quality, and the busier each person is kept (utilisation), the wider the margin. This is why an IT services firm's profitability is really a story about people: hiring, retention, wages, and how cleverly the pyramid of junior-to-senior staff is stacked. Mastek's ~15–16% EBITDA margin sits below the ~24–26% the large-caps earn — the price of being smaller, more project-heavy, and more concentrated.

Who buys, and why they keep buying

  • Public sector & government — UK central departments, the NHS, local councils; a legacy Mastek stronghold and its most defining customer base. Long contracts, high switching costs, but tied to public budgets and procurement politics.
  • Regulated enterprises — financial services, healthcare, retail and manufacturing that must modernise but cannot fail. They value a partner who understands compliance and won't break mission-critical systems.
  • Middle East governments & corporates — the Gulf's push to digitise state services and diversify away from oil created a fast-growing Oracle-Cloud market that Mastek's Evosys arm was built to serve.
02

The Britain concentration: the moat that doubles as the risk

The single most important structural fact about Mastek — and the one most likely to surprise a newcomer.

Say "Indian IT company" and most people picture a firm that earns the bulk of its money in the United States — because the giants do (North America is typically half or more of TCS, Infosys, Wipro). Mastek breaks that mental model entirely. Its largest market by far is the United Kingdom, and Europe alongside it; the Americas are a distant second. This inversion is the key to understanding both the company's strengths and its vulnerabilities.

This British tilt is a genuine heritage asset. Mastek has spent decades embedded in UK public-sector IT — it grew up serving central-government departments, the NHS, and local authorities, and it retains the security clearances, framework listings and delivery track record that let a firm win sensitive government work. In FY26 the UK business led the group, growing ~21.8% in rupee terms, with a striking ~95% surge in healthcare revenue and fresh wins in financial services. Government work is sticky: contracts are long, switching suppliers is painful and risky, and an incumbent that delivers tends to keep winning the follow-on. Few Indian mid-caps can match Mastek's depth in Whitehall and the NHS.

The concentration that runs the P&L

Here is the device to hold onto: for Mastek, a single national government's IT budget cycle moves more of the P&L than any one client logo does. Because ~70% of revenue is UK-and-Europe and a heavy slice of that is British public bodies, the things that decide a Mastek year are unusually specific — a UK spending review, an NHS digitisation push, the pound-sterling exchange rate, and the pace of Whitehall procurement. A US-diversified major absorbs a wobble in any one country; Mastek is exposed to the weather over Britain in a way almost no other listed Indian IT name is. When UK public spending flows, Mastek flies; when a government tightens or a procurement freeze hits, there is no large American book to cushion it.

And the currency point is not a footnote. With the majority of revenue in pounds and euros but a large part of costs in rupees, Mastek's reported growth and margins swing with GBP/INR. A strong pound flatters the rupee top line (as it did in FY26); a weak one does the reverse — so a chunk of any given year's "growth" can be exchange-rate arithmetic rather than more work won. Reading Mastek in constant currency is essential to separating real momentum from FX.

Why the concentration cuts both ways: the UK/public-sector depth is a real moat — clearances, frameworks and relationships that take years to build and shield Mastek from pure price competition. But the same concentration means a British fiscal squeeze, a change in government procurement priorities, or sustained sterling weakness hits Mastek harder and with less to offset it than any diversified peer. The stated remedy — grow the Americas and steady the Middle East — is sensible, but it is a work in progress, not a fact on the ground.
03

The Oracle Cloud engine: how Evosys defines the company

The platform bet that gives Mastek an identity larger than its size.

If geography is one axis of Mastek's concentration, platform is the other. Mastek is, above all, an Oracle Cloud house — one of the larger independent implementers of Oracle's Fusion / ERP / Cloud applications in the world. That standing came largely from a single 2020 acquisition, Evosys, and it shapes how the company competes.

The logic is straightforward. Software vendors like Oracle sell licences and subscriptions, but they do not personally install and configure their products at thousands of clients. They certify implementation partners to do it — and the top-tier partners inherit a steady flow of work as the vendor's own sales team wins new customers who then need someone to make the software actually run. Evosys arrived as an Oracle Platinum/Cloud specialist with well over a thousand Oracle Cloud customers across dozens of countries and a deep bench of certified consultants. Buying it vaulted Mastek from a UK-focused digital-services firm into a globally relevant Oracle-Cloud practice, with a large Middle East footprint attached.

The prize is Stage 3–4: an implementation is a one-off, but a well-run account becomes years of managed-services and expansion revenue. Depth in one platform lets Mastek claim expertise a generalist cannot.

Being platform-deep rather than platform-broad is a deliberate trade. A large-cap IT major offers everything, everywhere; Mastek instead says to an Oracle-Cloud buyer, "this is the thing we do better than almost anyone." That focus wins in the niche — reference clients, certified specialists and repeatable delivery methods — and it lets a small firm punch above its revenue in a specific corner of the market. The flip side is obvious: Mastek's fortunes are tied to the health of the Oracle Cloud applications cycle and to Oracle's own competitive position against SAP, Microsoft, Workday and the rest. A slowdown in enterprise Oracle migrations, or a share-shift away from Oracle, would land on Mastek more directly than on a diversified peer.

Why a mid-cap chooses depth over breadth

A firm Mastek's size cannot out-scale TCS or Accenture, so it competes on being the specialist a client trusts with a specific job. In Oracle Cloud — and, through MST Solutions, in Salesforce — Mastek can field consultants who have done the exact migration dozens of times, in the client's exact sector. That earns a seat at tables a generalist mid-cap never reaches, and it supports pricing that pure staff-augmentation can't. The strategic tension is that this same focus is a bet: the practice thrives only as long as enterprises keep moving onto Oracle's and Salesforce's clouds, and as long as those platforms keep winning. Mastek's job is to widen — into data, AI and more geographies — before any single platform cycle turns.

04

The founder, the professional managers, and the CEO churn

A 1982 promoter legacy run by hired executives — with a bumpy record at the top.

Mastek is one of India's original software entrepreneurs' stories, and its ownership and leadership carry both that heritage and a more complicated recent history. Understanding who founded it, who runs it, and how often that has changed tells you a lot about the governance texture an investor is buying.

Timeline

  • 1982
    Mastek founded by Ashank Desai and co-founders — one of the earliest Indian software product-and-services companies, well before the IT-export boom.
  • 1990s–2000s
    Builds a strong UK and public-sector franchise; develops insurance software that later becomes a major product line, and lists in India.
  • 2014–15
    Demerges the insurance products business as Majesco (subsequently US-listed and sold), leaving Mastek as a focused IT services company. A defining strategic split.
  • 2020
    Acquires Evosys (~US$65m), transforming Mastek into a leading Oracle Cloud implementation partner and adding a large Middle East business.
  • 2021
    Leadership turbulence at the top — CEO transition as the incumbent departs; Hiral Chandrana is appointed Global CEO. Founder Ashank Desai steps back toward the chair.
  • 2022
    Acquires MST Solutions (US Salesforce specialist), pushing into the Americas and the Salesforce ecosystem.
  • FY24
    The mid-cap IT slowdown bites — discretionary spending dries up, growth decelerates sharply across the sector; Mastek's momentum stalls.
  • FY26
    Steadier year: revenue ~₹3,699 Cr (+7%), PAT ~₹404 Cr (+7.5%), backlog +24%, 85+ AI-led deals; ₹16/share final dividend recommended.

Ownership & leadership

  • Promoter group: the founding Desai family and co-founders hold roughly ~36% (approx.), with the balance widely held by institutions and the public. This is a professionally managed company, not a tightly family-controlled one — a lower promoter stake than many Indian mid-caps. Confirm the exact percentage in the latest shareholding filing.
  • Founder-chairman: Ashank Desai remains the guiding promoter and non-executive presence — a link back to the 1982 origins and a stabilising figure through the leadership changes.
  • Executive leadership: day-to-day running sits with a professional CEO (Hiral Chandrana as Global CEO) and management team. This separation of ownership from management is healthy in principle — but only as good as the continuity of the people in the seats.
The governance point to watch: Mastek's past leadership churn is not a footnote. An IT services firm is, quite literally, its people and its client relationships — and repeated changes at the very top can unsettle both, especially in a business built on long, trust-heavy public-sector accounts. The counter-argument is that a strong founder-promoter anchor and a lower-drama recent stretch suggest the ship has steadied. For a minority investor, senior-management stability and key-client retention are the things to keep re-checking; they matter more here than in a product company where the product outlives the executive.
05

Growth bought, not just built: the acquisition machine

The defining strategy of modern Mastek — and the number it makes you work to find.

Much of what Mastek is today was purchased. The two capabilities that most distinguish it — a leading Oracle Cloud practice and a US Salesforce business — did not grow organically over years; they arrived by acquisition, and quickly. This inorganic strategy is the smart core of the recent story and, simultaneously, the reason its reported numbers need careful reading.

The deals that built modern Mastek
DealWhat it broughtWhy it mattered
Evosys (2020)An Oracle Cloud implementation specialist with 1,000+ Oracle customers and a big Middle East footprint; ~US$65m.The transformational one — turned Mastek from a UK digital firm into a globally relevant Oracle-Cloud partner overnight, and opened the Gulf.
MST Solutions (2022)A US-based Salesforce consulting partner (Arizona), strong in public sector, healthcare and manufacturing.Bought a Salesforce capability and an Americas beachhead in one move — addressing Mastek's under-weight US position.
Earlier / bolt-onsA string of smaller digital, data and niche capability acquisitions over the years.Filled gaps in skills and clients faster than organic hiring could — the standard mid-cap "buy the capability" playbook.

The appeal of buying growth is real: it is fast, it delivers ready-made clients and certified skills, and in a fast-moving technology market speed can matter more than cost. Evosys is the case study — Mastek could not have organically built a thousand-customer Oracle Cloud practice in the time it took to acquire one. But the strategy carries three standing costs that an investor must price in.

  • The numbers get muddier. When a chunk of a year's revenue growth comes from a newly consolidated acquisition, the headline "growth" overstates the health of the underlying business. The figure that cuts through — and the one management is less eager to lead with — is organic, constant-currency growth: how fast the business is expanding stripped of both acquisitions and FX. That is the true engine reading.
  • The balance sheet carries the price. Acquisitions create goodwill and intangibles, and are often funded partly with debt and earn-outs (deferred payments contingent on the acquired firm hitting targets). Mastek has used borrowing for deals — though it runs a broadly net-cash position at the group level (cash ~₹900 Cr-plus against modest gross debt) — and integration and earn-out payments can weigh on cash flow for years after the announcement.
  • Integration is where value is made or lost. Bolting on a firm is easy; blending its people, culture, delivery systems and client relationships into one company without losing the talent that made it worth buying is hard. The payoff of Evosys and MST is realised only if they are genuinely integrated, cross-sold and retained.
The device that fits Mastek specifically: separate the bought growth from the built growth

The honest way to read Mastek is to run every headline growth number through two filters before believing it. First, strip out acquisitions — is the pre-existing business actually bigger, or did a deal just get added on? Second, strip out sterling — did more work get won, or did a stronger pound inflate the rupee figure? What survives both filters is organic constant-currency growth, and that is the real test of whether Mastek is a compounding services franchise or an integrator that must keep shopping to show progress. FY26's ~7% reported growth, read through those filters, is the number that matters — modest, and the crux of the whole debate about the stock. A firm that has bought its way to relevance now has to prove it can grow the relevance it bought.

06

The Middle East swing: the growth engine that stalled

The region a newcomer never expects an "Indian IT" firm to depend on — and its recent wobble.

Here is the fact that surprises people who assume Indian IT lives on America and Britain alone: Mastek built a meaningful and, for a stretch, fast-growing business in the Middle East — the Gulf states' drive to digitise government services and diversify their economies away from oil created a wave of Oracle-Cloud and digital-transformation work, and Evosys came pre-built to ride it. For several years the region (reported within "AMEA" — Asia, Middle East, Africa) was one of Mastek's brighter growth stories.

That is exactly why the recent reversal is worth pausing on. In the past year, Mastek's AMEA share slipped — from roughly 13% toward ~11% and below of revenue — as Middle East geopolitical tensions disrupted decision-making and deferred projects. A region that was a tailwind became, at least temporarily, a drag. It is a vivid illustration of how a concentrated mid-cap feels macro shocks that a diversified major can shrug off.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with

A firm this size cannot be strong everywhere, so it ends up unusually exposed to specific places. Mastek's three geographies each carry a distinct kind of risk: the UK is exposed to fiscal and procurement politics, the Middle East to geopolitical shocks, and the Americas is the market where Mastek is sub-scale and still trying to establish itself. There is no large, boring, diversified book to average those out. So a single event — a Gulf conflict flaring, a British spending freeze, a US enterprise-spend pause — moves Mastek's numbers in a way that would barely register at a company ten times its size. The Middle East slowdown of the past year is the clearest recent proof: the same concentration that lets Mastek win big in a niche also means it rides the niche's weather without a cushion.

The strategic answer management is pursuing is rebalancing — leaning on the resilient UK book while it stabilises the Gulf and grows the Americas (where MST Solutions and Salesforce are the wedge). If it works, Mastek ends up with three legs instead of one dominant one and a wobbly third. If it doesn't, the company remains a largely UK-driven firm whose other geographies are as likely to subtract as to add in any given year. That rebalancing, more than any single quarter, is the multi-year project to watch.

07

Margins and the mid-cap squeeze: why size and cyclicality bite

The structural reasons Mastek earns less than a large-cap — and swings more.

Mastek's ~15–16% EBITDA margin is respectable, but it sits well below the ~24–26% the Indian IT majors earn, and it is more volatile. Two forces explain the gap: sub-scale economics and discretionary-spend cyclicality. Both are inherent to being a project-heavy mid-cap, and both were on full display in the 2023–24 slowdown.

On scale: a larger firm spreads its fixed costs — sales, delivery infrastructure, senior management, brand — across a much bigger revenue base, and its enormous, multi-year "run-and-maintain" annuity contracts give it a stable margin floor. A mid-cap like Mastek has a higher share of one-off project work, less annuity ballast, and thinner fixed-cost absorption. When a large project ends or a client pauses, utilisation drops and margin falls faster because there is less recurring revenue underneath to hold it up.

On cyclicality: a large share of digital-transformation work is discretionary — a client can defer a cloud migration or a new-build if budgets tighten. When enterprises cut IT spending, as they did through 2023–24 amid higher interest rates and macro caution, the discretionary projects are first to be paused. Mid-caps, weighted toward exactly that kind of work, felt the slowdown more sharply than the majors, whose annuity-heavy books held up better. Growth across the mid-cap IT cohort decelerated hard; Mastek's own momentum stalled in FY24 before the FY26 steadying.

Why the margin is thinner and the ride bumpier

Two things compound. First, Mastek carries a lot of project revenue and less of the recurring managed-services annuity that gives the giants a margin cushion — so when utilisation dips, profitability falls further and faster. Second, its client demand is skewed to discretionary transformation spend, which is precisely what corporates freeze first in a downturn. Add a heavy geographic concentration on top, and Mastek is structurally a higher-beta way to own Indian IT: it should grow faster than the majors in a good digital cycle and suffer more in a bad one. The task for management is to lift the recurring-revenue share (managed services, longer Oracle-run contracts) so the next downturn hurts less — turning some of the project volatility into annuity stability.

08

The AI question: opportunity, or margin threat?

Every IT services firm's defining swing factor for the decade — Mastek included.

No primer on an IT services company in 2026 can skip the generative-AI question, because it points in two directions at once. On one hand, helping clients adopt AI is a large new pool of work Mastek can sell into. On the other, AI tools that write and test code threaten to make some traditional services cheaper to deliver — compressing the very billable hours the industry runs on. Which force wins is the central uncertainty hanging over every firm in the sector.

Mastek has planted its flag firmly on the opportunity side with a "Lead with AI" strategy, and it has numbers to show for it: over 85 AI-assisted or AI-led deals closed across FY26, with the momentum continuing into FY27. The pitch is that Mastek uses AI both inside its delivery (to build faster and cheaper) and as a service (helping clients deploy AI on their Oracle, Salesforce and data estates). For a firm whose whole positioning is "modernisation," AI is the natural next chapter of the same story it already sells.

But the threat is not imaginary. If AI lets clients (or rival firms) do the same migration or maintenance with far fewer billable hours, then a services company that charges by the hour faces a pricing problem — the same "efficiency" it sells to clients can eat its own revenue. The industry's hoped-for escape is that AI expands the total work to be done (more systems to modernise, more AI to deploy, more data to wrangle) faster than it deflates the price of any single task. For a specialist like Mastek, being genuinely useful with AI on the platforms it already knows — Oracle, Salesforce — is the way to stay on the right side of that equation.

How to judge whether AI is helping or hurting Mastek

Don't take the deal-count headline at face value — 85 "AI deals" tells you demand exists, not that economics improved. The tell is in two places over the next few years. First, margin: if AI is a net efficiency gain, Mastek's EBITDA margin should hold or rise even as it passes some savings to clients; if it is a net deflation, margins slip as pricing falls faster than delivery cost. Second, growth: does AI-led work lift organic constant-currency growth above the sluggish mid-single-digits, or merely replace lost traditional revenue? An AI story that grows the deal count while margins and organic growth stay flat is a story about running to stand still. The next several years of those two numbers will answer the question the deal count cannot.

How Mastek differs from a TCS — and from a generalist mid-cap

Two contrasts define Mastek's place. Against a large-cap major (TCS, Infosys), the difference is scale and stability: the giants are US-diversified, annuity-heavy, higher-margin and far larger, so they grow steadily and defensively; Mastek is smaller, project-heavier, concentrated and therefore higher-beta — more upside in a good cycle, more pain in a bad one. Against a broad mid-cap generalist, the difference is focus: Mastek is deliberately UK-and-Oracle-Cloud-tilted rather than spread evenly, which wins it a specialist's seat in its niche but denies it the diversification a generalist enjoys. Mastek is neither the safe compounder nor the everything-everywhere generalist — it is a concentrated specialist, and it should be judged as one.

Financials & valuation

A steady recovery year on a modest multiple — the debate is durability of organic growth.

FY26 read as a stabilisation rather than a breakout: revenue up ~7% to ~₹3,699 Cr, net profit up ~7.5% to ~₹404 Cr, EBITDA margin steady near 15.8%, and a strong ~24% rise in the order backlog signalling firmer demand ahead. Mastek is not priced as a hot growth stock — it trades on a low-teens earnings multiple (~13–14×), the sort of rating the market gives a smaller, cyclical, concentrated services firm. The question in the price is not whether heroic expectations are baked in; it is whether Mastek can convert a recovering backlog into organic growth again.

The one number that settles the thesis: organic constant-currency growth

At ~13–14× earnings, Mastek does not need a re-rating to work — it needs to prove the core business is compounding. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is organic constant-currency revenue growth: growth stripped of both acquisitions and the tailwind (or headwind) of sterling. Read it alongside two companions — the EBITDA margin (is AI and mix lifting it toward the high-teens, or is pricing pressure holding it down?) and the 12-month order backlog (is the ~24% rise a durable pipeline or a timing bump?). If organic CC growth re-accelerates into double digits as the UK stays strong and the Middle East recovers, the low multiple looks like a mispricing and earnings compound. If it stays stuck in the mid-single-digits while the company leans on the next acquisition and a strong pound to show progress, the market's cautious rating is correct. Organic growth, ex-deals and ex-FX — that is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A concentrated specialist steadying after a slump — asked whether it can grow the core again.

Mastek enters FY27 in firmer shape than a year earlier: a resilient UK book, a rebuilt order backlog, a live AI-services pitch, a net-cash balance sheet, and a clear plan to rebalance toward the Americas while the Middle East heals. What it has not yet demonstrated is durable, self-generated growth in the core — the thing its modest valuation is really waiting on. That tension frames both cases.

The bull case

  • Cheap for a recovering franchise — ~13–14× earnings for a specialist with a strong UK niche, a ~24% backlog rise and improving demand; little optimism is priced in.
  • Deep, defensible UK/public-sector moat — clearances, frameworks and relationships that are hard to replicate and sticky through cycles; FY26 UK growth was strong (healthcare +95%).
  • Genuine platform specialism — a leading independent Oracle Cloud practice plus Salesforce, giving Mastek a seat generalist mid-caps can't claim.
  • AI as a real tailwind — 85+ AI-led deals and a credible "Lead with AI" story on the exact platforms Mastek already knows.
  • Optionality & firepower — net-cash balance sheet funds dividends, AI investment and the next bolt-on without strain; a proven acquirer.

The bear case

  • Double concentration — ~70% UK/Europe and a heavy Oracle tilt mean a British fiscal squeeze, a sterling slide or an Oracle-cycle turn hits harder, with little to offset it.
  • Sluggish organic growth — FY26's ~7% reported growth, stripped of FX and deals, is modest; the market is unconvinced the core compounds on its own.
  • Mid-cap cyclicality — project-heavy, discretionary-spend-exposed and lower-margin than the majors; the 2023–24 slump showed how fast momentum can stall.
  • Middle East wobble & sub-scale US — the Gulf growth engine stalled on geopolitics; the Americas is still small and unproven.
  • Acquisition & leadership risk — reported numbers flattered by deals and FX; past CEO churn in a people-and-relationships business is a governance watch-item.
The bottom line

Mastek is a focused, professionally run UK-and-Oracle digital-services firm, steadying after the mid-cap slump and priced as a cyclical the market is not yet willing to pay up for. Its strengths are real and hard to copy — a Whitehall-and-NHS pedigree, a leading Oracle Cloud practice, a clean balance sheet, and a fast-recovering backlog. Its burden is equally real: it is doubly concentrated, more cyclical than the giants, and its recent growth owes as much to acquisitions and a strong pound as to work won under its own steam. The next test is concrete and datable: whether organic constant-currency growth climbs back into double digits over the coming FY27 quarters as the UK book holds, the Middle East recovers, and the AI-led backlog converts — with margin heading toward the high-teens rather than slipping. Deliver that, and a low-teens multiple on a specialist franchise will look too cheap; stay stuck in mid-single-digit organic growth, and Mastek remains a capable integrator the market is right to rate with caution.
