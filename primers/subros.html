Subros, the company that keeps India's cars cool

A full walk through India's largest maker of automotive air-conditioning and thermal-management systems — how a car's AC actually works, why almost every rupee still rides on one customer called Maruti Suzuki, and why the shift to electric vehicles could quietly triple the amount of thermal hardware Subros sells into the very same car.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Subros makes the air-conditioning and thermal-management systems that go inside vehicles — the compressor, condenser, evaporator, heater and the whole HVAC (heating, ventilation & air-conditioning) box that cools, warms and de-fogs a car's cabin. It is India's largest automotive thermal-systems specialist, holding roughly 41% of the passenger-car AC market and about 42% of the truck AC/blower market. It is not a diversified auto-parts giant that also happens to make AC — cooling is the entire business, which makes it a rare pure-play on how much thermal hardware India's growing car fleet needs.

Three facts frame everything:

  • One customer towers over the rest. Subros supplies more than 70% of Maruti Suzuki's air-conditioning needs, and Maruti — directly and through Maruti's own vendors — has accounted for roughly 85% of Subros's revenue. That is a moat (a decades-deep, designed-in relationship with India's biggest carmaker) and the single biggest risk (if Maruti sneezes on volumes, Subros catches a cold). Everything the company is doing on diversification is aimed at that one number.
  • Electrification is an opportunity, not a threat. Unusually for an engine-era supplier, EVs make Subros's product more valuable, not obsolete. An electric car has no engine heat to spare and a battery pack that must be kept in a tight temperature band, so its thermal system is far more complex: thermal content per vehicle runs ~2.5–3× higher than a petrol car, and an electric compressor alone is worth ~3.5–4× its conventional cousin. New-energy systems (hybrid, EV, CNG) already make up ~25% of FY26 revenue.
  • FY26 was record revenue on squeezed margins. Revenue rose ~11.5% to a record ~₹3,756 Cr and PAT was ~₹166 Cr, but the EBITDA margin slipped to ~9.6% (from ~11%) as aluminium, copper and the rupee moved the wrong way. The stock trades near 29× earnings — a valuation that assumes the diversification and EV content story pays off while margins climb back to double digits.
In plain terms

Subros is the cooling department for India's vehicles. When you press the AC button in a Maruti, there is a very good chance the compressor, condenser and evaporator doing the work were built by this company in Noida or Gujarat. For forty years its fortunes have tracked one thing: how many cars Maruti sells and how many of them are ordered with air-conditioning. The bet today is that two forces widen that narrow base — a fleet that keeps adding AC content per car, and an EV transition that needs two to three times as much thermal hardware per vehicle — while the company deliberately pushes into trucks, buses, railways and the spare-parts aftermarket to stop living and dying by a single customer's monthly sales.

01

Car cooling 101: the loop that makes cold air

No engineering degree needed — just the handful of parts that make up the whole business.

A car air-conditioner is a small refrigerator running in a loop. A gas called refrigerant is pumped around a sealed circuit, and at each stage a different component does one job — squeeze it, cool it, expand it, or let it soak up heat from the cabin. Subros makes every part of that loop. Learn the four stages once and the entire product line falls into place.

The parts that matter (and why)
ComponentWhat it does (and why it matters to Subros)
CompressorThe heart of the system — it squeezes the refrigerant to high pressure, which is what drives the whole cooling loop. It is the highest-value single part and the hardest to make. Subros builds compressors under long technical collaboration with Japan's Denso. In an EV, the mechanical belt-driven compressor is replaced by an electric one, worth several times more.
CondenserThe radiator-like grille at the front of the car that dumps heat from the refrigerant to the outside air, turning the hot gas back into liquid. A precision aluminium heat-exchanger.
Evaporator & HVAC unitThe box behind the dashboard where the cold refrigerant absorbs heat from cabin air, so the blower can push chilled air at you. The HVAC unit bundles the evaporator, heater core, blower and air-mix flaps into one assembly.
Hoses, pipes & controlsThe plumbing and electronics that connect it all — refrigerant hoses, condenser/evaporator pipes, thermal-expansion valves and the control logic. Unglamorous, but Subros makes these in-house too, which is what lets it sell a complete system to a carmaker.
The one idea that runs the whole business: content per vehicle

Forget megawatts and market share for a second. The number that quietly decides Subros's fate is rupees of thermal hardware per vehicle — how much cooling kit ends up in the average car it supplies. A base hatchback with a simple manual AC carries a modest amount. Add automatic climate control, rear vents, better filtration or a bigger cabin and the content rises. Switch to a CNG, hybrid or full-electric powertrain and it jumps again, because the car now needs to cool a battery and power electronics as well as the people. Subros can grow even if India's total car sales are flat — simply because each car is worth more thermal content than the one before it.

Who buys automotive thermal systems

  • Passenger-vehicle OEMs — the carmakers themselves. Maruti Suzuki is the anchor; Subros also supplies Tata Motors, Mahindra & Mahindra and others. The AC system is designed into the car years before launch, which makes the supplier relationship sticky.
  • Commercial-vehicle & bus makers — truck and bus builders (Tata, Ashok Leyland, and the fast-growing electric-bus fleets) fitting driver-cabin AC and passenger climate systems.
  • Railways, refrigeration & the aftermarket — Indian Railways coach AC and maintenance contracts, transport-refrigeration for cold-chain trucks, and replacement parts sold through the spares channel long after a vehicle leaves the showroom.
02

What Subros actually makes: one system, sold whole

The integrated model — read this once and the company clicks into place.

Subros's edge is that it hands a carmaker a complete, validated thermal system rather than a bag of loose parts. It designs, makes and assembles the compressor, the condenser, the evaporator/HVAC unit and the connecting hardware in-house, then delivers the integrated package that a car is built around. Owning the whole loop is what lets it command a leading share — and it is why an OEM, once designed in, is slow to switch.

A sealed circuit: the refrigerant never leaves it, just changes pressure and temperature at each stage. Subros builds all four — which is why it can sell a warranted, ready-to-fit system, not components a carmaker must integrate itself.

The product lines, decoded

  • Compressors — the crown jewel. Technically demanding, capital-intensive, and the part with the highest value and the deepest moat. The Denso collaboration gives Subros a compressor technology edge that a new entrant cannot quickly replicate — and it is the single component that gains the most value in an electric vehicle.
  • Condensers & heat exchangers — the aluminium heart-and-lungs of the system. This is where metal prices bite: a condenser is largely aluminium, so the cost of the raw metal flows straight into the product's margin.
  • Evaporators, HVAC units, hoses & controls — the cabin-side assembly plus all the plumbing and electronics. Bundling these into one HVAC module is what turns Subros from a parts vendor into a system supplier — the reason OEMs give it the whole job.
Why "the whole system" is the moat

A carmaker designing a new model does not want to source a compressor from one firm, a condenser from another and an HVAC box from a third, then take responsibility for making them work together in a sealed loop that must never leak for a decade. It would rather hand the entire thermal problem to one supplier who guarantees it. That is exactly what Subros offers — and once its system is engineered into a platform, replacing it means re-validating the whole car. This is why a ~41% passenger-car AC share is durable rather than fragile: it is protected less by price and more by the sheer switching cost of being designed in.

03

The JV, the founders, and who controls it

A Suri-family company built on two Japanese partners.

Subros was born in 1985 as a joint venture between the Indian Suri family and two Japanese heavyweights — Denso Corporation (one of the world's largest auto-parts and thermal-systems makers) and Suzuki Motor Corporation (Maruti's parent). That founding structure is not trivia: it is why Subros has world-class compressor technology and a front-row seat at India's biggest carmaker, baked in from day one.

Timeline

  • 1985
    Subros incorporated as a JV of the Suri family with Denso and Suzuki — the technology partner and the customer's parent, in one cap table.
  • 1990s
    Scales alongside Maruti's rise as India motorises; becomes the default AC supplier as car ownership spreads.
  • 2000s
    Expands capacity and deepens localisation — making compressors, condensers and HVAC units in India rather than importing them.
  • 2010s
    Pushes beyond passenger cars into truck and bus AC, transport-refrigeration and Indian Railways coach cooling — the first serious diversification effort.
  • 2023–24
    Begins developing EV thermal systems — electric compressors, battery- and motor-cooling loops; wins its first big electric-compressor order.
  • FY25
    Anticipation builds around a mandatory truck cabin-AC norm for N2/N3 vehicles; Maruti places a multi-year ~₹1,280 Cr electric-compressor order.
  • FY26
    Record revenue ~₹3,756 Cr; new-energy systems ~25% of sales; truck AC up ~111%; new capex at Karsanpura and a greenfield plant at Kharkhoda near Maruti's new hub.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters — the Suri family (~36.8%): the founding Indian family retains management control with a stake near 37%. This is the promoter group that runs the company day to day.
  • The two Japanese anchors: Denso Corporation holds ~20% and Suzuki Motor Corporation ~12%. These are not passive investors — Denso is the technology lifeline (compressor and system know-how) and Suzuki is the parent of the biggest customer. Between them the two Japanese partners own roughly a third of the company, tightly aligning Subros with both its technology source and its largest buyer.
  • What this cap table buys: access to Denso's global thermal R&D (crucial as EV systems get more complex), and an inside track at Maruti Suzuki. The flip side is dependence — the same structure that guarantees the relationship also concentrates the risk on it.
The structure is the strategy — and the trap. Subros's founding DNA gives it a genuine, hard-to-copy edge: Denso's technology and a Suzuki-blessed line into Maruti. But those two advantages point at the same concentration. A supplier this closely wired into one carmaker's ecosystem earns steady, designed-in business — and inherits that carmaker's every model cycle, market-share wobble and pricing squeeze. The whole diversification push is an attempt to keep the benefits of this cap table while loosening its grip.
04

The Maruti question: one customer, roughly 85% of the story

The single most important number in the investment case — and the one it is trying to shrink.

Every analyst who looks at Subros lands on the same figure within five minutes. Subros supplies over 70% of Maruti Suzuki's air-conditioning requirement, and when you add up direct sales to Maruti plus sales routed through Maruti's own vendor chain, the single carmaker has represented roughly 85% of Subros's revenue. There is no way to understand this company without sitting with that number — because it is simultaneously the reason Subros is so profitable and predictable, and the reason its valuation carries a discount to less-concentrated auto-parts peers.

The third bar flips the view — inside Maruti's own AC sourcing, Subros already holds the lion's share, so the growth from this customer comes less from taking share and more from Maruti selling more cars and fitting more content per car.

Why is such a lopsided relationship allowed to persist? Because it is genuinely mutual. Subros's AC systems are engineered into Maruti's platforms years ahead of launch; Suzuki is a founding shareholder; and the two have co-evolved for four decades. For Maruti, Subros is a proven, local, deeply integrated supplier it would be disruptive to replace. For Subros, Maruti is a firehose of steady, high-visibility volume that most auto-parts firms would envy. The problem is not that the relationship is bad — it is that it is everything.

The device that only fits Subros: the same customer is the risk and the prize

Most concentrated suppliers face a simple trade — great customer, scary dependence. Subros's twist is stranger. Its biggest risk (85% of revenue riding on Maruti) and its biggest growth engine (electrifying Maruti's fleet) are the exact same customer. Maruti's move into hybrids, CNG and EVs does not dilute the concentration — it deepens it, because Subros's content per Maruti car rises as those cars gain more thermal hardware. So the honest framing is not "diversify away from Maruti" but "make each Maruti car worth more, while slowly building a second leg in trucks and rail." A single ₹1,280 Cr electric-compressor order from Maruti tells you which way that is pointing.

When Maruti has a strong model cycle, Subros hums; when Maruti loses share or cuts production, Subros feels it first.

05

Content per vehicle: how you grow when car sales don't

The quieter growth lever — and the one that works even in a flat market.

India's total car sales grow in the low-to-mid single digits in a normal year — not the kind of tailwind that excites investors. Subros's more interesting engine is premiumisation: the average car sold today carries far more thermal hardware than a decade ago, and that content keeps climbing regardless of whether unit volumes boom. More expensive, better-equipped cars simply need more cooling kit.

Mix-shift up this ladder lifts Subros's revenue per car even if total car sales barely move.

Three premiumisation trends push in Subros's favour at once. Richer variants — as buyers trade up to SUVs and higher trims, automatic climate control, rear AC vents and better cabin filtration become standard, all of which add content. Alternative fuels — CNG and hybrid vehicles (a Maruti stronghold) carry extra cooling demands. And the EV step-change — the biggest jump of all, covered next. The common thread is that Subros does not need India to buy dramatically more cars; it needs each car to keep getting thermally richer, which is exactly the direction the market is moving.

Why premiumisation beats volume for a parts maker

Unit growth is capped by how many cars India can buy in a year — a slow-moving number. Content growth has no such ceiling: a supplier can keep adding value inside each vehicle as specifications rise. For Subros, a market where buyers trade up to better-equipped, alternative-fuel and eventually electric cars is worth more than one where they simply buy a few percent more of the same basic hatchbacks. It is the difference between selling more units and selling more system per unit — and the second is where the margin and the durability of demand actually live.

06

The EV thermal prize: why electrification helps, not hurts

The counter-intuitive heart of the growth case.

The instinct with any engine-era auto supplier is to worry that EVs make it obsolete. For a spark-plug or exhaust maker, that fear is fair. For a thermal-systems maker it is backwards. An electric vehicle is, in cooling terms, a far harder machine than a petrol car — and a harder cooling problem means more hardware, more value, and a bigger role for a specialist like Subros.

An EV must cool its battery and power electronics, warm the cabin without waste engine heat (often via a heat pump), and keep the pack in a narrow temperature band — so total thermal content runs 2.5–3× an ICE car, and the electric compressor alone is worth 3.5–4× the old belt-driven unit. The same car park, electrified, is worth multiples more thermal hardware to Subros.

Why so much more? A petrol car gets cabin heat almost for free from the engine, and its AC compressor is spun by a belt off that engine. An EV has neither. It must cool a large lithium battery (which degrades and even catches fire outside a safe temperature window), cool the motor and power electronics, and heat the cabin electrically — often with a heat pump to avoid draining range. Each of those is a thermal loop with its own pumps, valves, exchangers and an electric compressor at the centre. The result is a system that is not just bigger but architecturally more complex — precisely the kind of integrated thermal problem Subros exists to solve.

A genuine surprise: electrification could multiply content on the very same customer

Here is the fact that reframes the whole company. Subros's largest exposure — Maruti — is also entering EVs and expanding hybrids and CNG. If a future Maruti EV carries ~2.5–3× the thermal content of today's petrol version, Subros can grow its rupees-per-Maruti-car dramatically without adding a single new customer. The ~₹1,280 Cr electric-compressor order Subros won from Maruti is the first concrete proof of this: the same concentration that scares investors is the channel through which the richest content upgrade in the company's history could flow. Electrification does not dilute the Maruti bet — it super-charges it, for better and for worse.

The catch worth naming: the EV thermal prize is real but back-ended. India's EV penetration in passenger cars is still low, the electric-compressor order ramps to only a few hundred crore a year at peak, and new plants (Karsanpura, Kharkhoda) must be built and localised before the content shows up in profit. This is a multi-year build, not a next-quarter earnings lever — the thesis is about where content per vehicle is heading, and how fast Subros can localise the parts to keep the margin.
07

Beyond the car: trucks, buses, rail and the aftermarket

The second leg — small today, but growing fast and deliberately.

If passenger-car AC (still ~93% of revenue) is the trunk, Subros is busy growing branches. The logic is simple: every rupee earned outside Maruti-linked passenger cars is a rupee that dilutes the concentration and, in several cases, comes at a better or more recurring margin. The four diversification legs

  • Commercial-vehicle & truck AC — the standout near-term story. A government mandate requiring air-conditioned cabins for medium/heavy trucks (N2/N3) created an instant addressable market. Subros, already the leading truck AC/blower supplier, is riding it hard — truck AC up ~111% in FY26 — and targeting several hundred crore of annual truck-AC sales.
  • Bus air-conditioning — climate systems for city and intercity buses, including the growing electric-bus fleet. Subros holds roughly 16% of the bus AC market, a segment that scales with public-transport electrification.
  • Railways & refrigeration — coach air-conditioning for Indian Railways plus long-term annual maintenance contracts (a small but growing, recurring, high-margin AMC pipeline), and transport-refrigeration for cold-chain vehicles carrying food and pharma.
  • Aftermarket & non-auto — replacement compressors and AC parts sold long after the vehicle is bought, plus early moves into home-AC components and even hydrogen-fuel-cell bus thermal solutions. These broaden the base and, in the aftermarket's case, add demand that does not depend on new-vehicle sales at all.
Why the second leg is worth more than its size suggests

At ~7% of revenue, the non-passenger-car businesses look almost like a rounding error. But they matter out of proportion to their size for two reasons. First, they attack the concentration problem directly — trucks, buses and rail have nothing to do with Maruti's monthly car sales, so every point of revenue they add makes Subros a little less hostage to one customer. Second, several of them — railway AMCs, aftermarket spares — carry recurring, service-like economics rather than the feast-or-famine of new-vehicle orders. A company judged on how well it diversifies should be watched here: the pace at which this ~7% climbs is a direct read on management's most important strategic goal.

08

Margins and the metals: why aluminium and copper set the ceiling

The commodity sensitivity that makes or breaks a given year.

Subros is a good business with a modest margin, and understanding why the margin is modest — and volatile — is essential. A thermal system is, physically, a lot of metal and refrigerant: condensers and evaporators are largely aluminium, wiring and windings use copper, and the loop runs on refrigerant gas. When those input prices rise, or the rupee weakens against the currencies Subros imports parts in, the margin compresses before the company can pass costs through to price-sensitive carmakers.

FY26 EBITDA margin slipped to ~9.6% from ~11%, dented by higher aluminium and copper prices and adverse currency moves. Management's stated ambition is to rebuild toward sustained double-digit margins through localisation, scale and a richer product mix — the single financial gate the thesis rests on.

Two structural facts keep the margin modest. First, Subros sells into an industry — automotive OEMs — that is famously ruthless on price; a supplier rarely dictates terms to a carmaker buying millions of units. Second, its bill of materials is metal-heavy and partly import-linked, so a commodity spike or a weaker rupee lands on the P&L quickly while price recoveries lag. The offsets are equally structural: rising localisation (making more of each system in India cuts import cost and FX exposure), scale as volumes grow, and mix — higher-value EV and premium content carries better economics than a basic hatchback AC.

Why return on capital is the number that flatters the margin

A ~9.6% EBITDA margin sounds unexciting until you look at how little capital it takes to earn it. Subros converts that thin margin into a return on capital employed near 20%, because it is not an asset-heavy plant business so much as a design-and-assemble one with high asset turns. That is the quiet quality in the model: even in a margin-pressured year, the capital efficiency stays respectable. The bet for shareholders is that localisation and EV/premium mix lift the operating margin back to double digits without eroding those returns — turning a decent business into a distinctly better one.

09

Rivals, technology and the localisation race

A global-major industry where Subros defends home turf.

Automotive thermal management is dominated worldwide by a handful of scaled specialists — the same names that supply the global carmakers. In India, Subros is the leader, but it competes against the local arms and technology of these global majors, and increasingly against the in-house thermal ambitions of the EV makers themselves. Its defence is depth in India: local manufacturing, local engineering, and a forty-year customer web that a newcomer cannot quickly build.

The competitive edge that is hard to replicate: localisation plus a partner-owner

Two things protect Subros from the global majors on its home turf. First, localisation: Subros makes its systems in India, which means lower cost, faster response to Indian carmakers, and less exposure to import duties and currency swings than a supplier shipping parts from abroad. Second — and this is the quirk — its most fearsome global rival, Denso, is also its shareholder and technology partner in India. Where a standalone Indian supplier would be out-engineered on EV heat pumps and electric compressors, Subros gets to plug into Denso's global R&D. The race it must win is to localise the new EV thermal components — electric compressors and battery-cooling hardware — fast enough to keep both the cost advantage and the margin as the product mix electrifies.

The wildcard is the EV makers' own vertical integration. Some global EV manufacturers design significant portions of their thermal system in-house, and a few Indian EV entrants may try to follow. Subros's answer is that thermal integration is genuinely hard, that most volume carmakers (Maruti chief among them) prefer a proven local partner to building a thermal division from scratch, and that its Denso-fed technology keeps it on the right side of the make-versus-buy decision. That answer holds best exactly where Subros is strongest — the high-volume mass market — and is least certain at the technology frontier, which is why the localisation race matters so much.

Financials & valuation

Record revenue, a margin dip, and a multiple that assumes the mix improves.

FY26 was a year of growth blunted by costs: revenue hit a record, but the margin fell and reported profit rose only modestly. The market values Subros not on this year's numbers but on where content per vehicle, diversification and margins are heading. The debate is whether the mix improves fast enough to justify the price.

The one number that settles the thesis: content per vehicle × new-energy mix

At ~29× earnings, Subros is not priced as a sleepy, single-customer parts maker; it is priced as a company whose content per vehicle is going to climb. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is thermal content per vehicle — read through the rising new-energy revenue share (past ~25% and climbing) and the EBITDA margin recovering toward double digits. If EV, hybrid and CNG systems keep lifting the value of hardware in each car while localisation defends the margin, the multiple is defensible even on a flat passenger-vehicle market. If content stalls and margins stay stuck below 10%, a ~29× multiple on a Maruti-concentrated supplier looks generous. Watch the mix and the margin together — one without the other does not settle it.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A leader in a narrow niche, betting that the niche gets richer per vehicle.

Subros enters FY27 as the clear leader in Indian automotive cooling, with a genuine content-growth story in EVs and premiumisation, a fast-growing truck-AC line, and a Denso-backed technology edge — set against a stubborn dependence on one customer and a margin that commodities keep pushing around. The real question is no longer whether Subros leads its niche; it is whether that niche becomes valuable enough per vehicle, fast enough, to justify the price.

The bull case

  • Content per vehicle rising — premiumisation, CNG/hybrid and especially EVs lift the thermal hardware in each car, growing revenue even on flat unit sales.
  • EVs are an upgrade, not a threat — ~2.5–3× thermal content per EV and a ~3.5–4× electric compressor; the ₹1,280 Cr Maruti order is first proof.
  • Truck-AC mandate tailwind — a regulatory switch created an instant market where Subros already leads; truck AC up ~111% in FY26.
  • Denso partnership & localisation — global thermal technology from a ~20% shareholder, made locally for cost and speed.
  • Leadership + capital efficiency — ~41% PV AC share and ~20% ROCE; a designed-in supplier that is hard to displace.

The bear case

  • Maruti concentration — ~85% of revenue tied to one carmaker; a weak Maruti model cycle or share loss hits Subros first and hardest.
  • Thin, commodity-driven margin — aluminium, copper and refrigerant prices plus FX push the ~9.6% EBITDA margin around; pricing power over OEMs is limited.
  • EV prize is back-ended — India's PV EV penetration is still low; the content upgrade ramps over years, not quarters.
  • Rich valuation — ~29× earnings on a single-customer supplier prices in the mix improving smoothly; a margin miss can de-rate it.
  • Global majors & in-housing — Valeo, Mahle-Behr, Sanden/Hanon and some EV makers' own thermal ambitions contest the technology frontier.
The bottom line

Subros has spent forty years as the quiet cooling department for India's cars — indispensable to Maruti, technologically anchored by Denso, and largely invisible to the driver who just wants cold air. The investment case is that the humble AC system is about to become far more valuable per vehicle: premium variants, CNG and hybrids, and above all EVs each pile more thermal hardware into the same car, and Subros is the supplier best placed to build it. The offsetting truth is that almost the entire base still rests on one customer, and the margin bends to metal prices the company cannot control. The next few years turn on two checkable prints: the new-energy share climbing well past 25% and the EBITDA margin clawing back above 10%. Watch those two lines move together — they will tell you whether the richer-per-vehicle future arrived on schedule, or stayed one model cycle away.
