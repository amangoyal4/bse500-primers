PTC India, the power market's middleman

A complete walk through India's largest electricity-trading company — the intermediary that buys power from generators and sells it to state utilities for a margin measured in fractions of a paisa per unit. How a business can run a ₹16,000-crore top line yet keep only a few hundred crore of it, why its most valuable franchise is the electricity that crosses India's borders, and why the market prices the whole thing below the cash and stakes sitting on its books.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

PTC India Limited — formerly Power Trading Corporation of India — is the country's largest power-trading company. It does not generate a single unit of electricity and it does not own the wires. It sits in the middle: it buys power from generators (thermal, hydro, wind, solar, and from across India's borders) and sells it on to state distribution utilities and large buyers, taking a wafer-thin trading margin on every unit that passes through its books. Think in terms of a wholesaler of electrons, not a factory or a grid operator.

Two look-alikes to clear out of the way first. This is PTC India Ltd (NSE: PTC), the power trader. It is not PTC India Financial Services (PFS) — a separate, separately listed NBFC that PTC part-owns (its lending affiliate; more in §07–08). And it is not PTC Industries (a Lucknow-based castings/titanium maker) or the American software firm PTC Inc. Everything below is about the power-trading parent.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The huge revenue line is almost all pass-through. PTC reports ~₹16,000 Cr of revenue because accounting makes it book the full value of the electricity it trades. But nearly all of that is the cost of the power itself, handed straight to the generator. What PTC actually keeps is the trading margin — in FY25 about 3.37 paise per unit of electricity — which, across ~82.8 billion units, is a few hundred crore. The top line is a mirage; the margin per unit is the business.
  • It is a low-capital, cash-generative intermediary — the opposite of a generator. A power station like NTPC sinks tens of thousands of crore into plant and equipment. PTC's model needs comparatively little fixed capital: its balance sheet is dominated by receivables (money discoms owe it) and cash. That makes it cash-rich, high-payout and dividend-friendly — FY25 paid ₹11.70/share, a double-digit dividend yield — but also low-growth in the market's eyes.
  • FY25 flattered by a one-off. Reported net profit jumped to ~₹976 Cr, but a large slice came from selling the PTC Energy wind portfolio to ONGC (a ~₹457 Cr post-tax gain at the standalone level). Strip that out and recurring profit is closer to FY24's ~₹500 Cr. The stock trades on a single-digit P/E and below its book value — a deep-value, sum-of-the-parts profile, not a growth story.
In plain terms

Picture the wholesale desk that stands between a power plant and a state electricity board. The plant wants to sell surplus units; the state board needs to buy them; PTC arranges the contract, takes on the settlement and payment risk, and clips a tiny fee per unit for making the trade happen. It never owns the electricity for more than an instant and never owns the plant at all. Its entire profit is that clip — a couple of paise on a unit that might sell for around two rupees — multiplied by an enormous volume. The company is cash-rich and pays most of it out, because there is little to reinvest in. The debate over the stock is not about growth; it is about whether power exchanges, where buyers and sellers increasingly meet without a middleman, slowly make that clip smaller.

01

Power trading 101: who needs a middleman for electricity?

No engineering needed — just who makes power, who buys it, and where the trader fits.

Electricity is unusual: it must be consumed the instant it is produced, and India's grid is a patchwork of generators, transmission companies and state distribution utilities (discoms) that rarely line up neatly. A plant in Odisha may have surplus at midnight; a discom in Maharashtra may be short at 7 p.m. Bridging those mismatches — in place, in time, and in creditworthiness — is what power trading does. To follow PTC you need six terms.

India's electricity is sold three ways, and the split is the single most important backdrop for a trader. The overwhelming majority still moves under long-term PPAs — discoms tied directly to plants for decades. A slice moves through the short-term market — bilateral deals (where a trader like PTC is useful) and, increasingly, power exchanges (where buyers and sellers match directly, no trader needed). PTC lives in the short-term-bilateral and long-term-broking pools; the exchange pool is the part that threatens to bypass it.

Why a middleman survives at all

If a discom could just call a plant directly, why pay PTC? Three reasons a trader still earns its clip. First, credit: many discoms are poor payers, and a generator would rather sell to a well-capitalised trader that guarantees payment than chase a state board. PTC absorbs that counterparty risk and backs it with payment-security mechanisms. Second, matching: PTC aggregates many small surpluses and shortfalls into workable contracts, which a single plant or discom cannot do alone. Third, cross-border and long-term structuring: some deals — importing hydropower from Bhutan, or a 25-year PPA spanning states — are complex enough that an experienced intermediary is genuinely useful. Where those frictions are high, the trader is paid. Where they collapse — a liquid exchange with cleared payments — the trader is not.

02

The margin per unit: reading past the ₹16,000-crore illusion

The single most important idea in the case — and it is specific to a power trader.

Open PTC's accounts and the first number you see is revenue of roughly ₹16,000 Cr. It is almost meaningless. Because PTC books the full value of the electricity it trades as revenue, that line is over 95% pass-through — money that lands in one door and leaves through the other, straight to the generator. The number that actually describes the company is what it keeps per unit. And what it keeps is astonishingly small.

Do the arithmetic and the scale of the illusion becomes clear. A core margin of ~3.37 paise across ~82.75 BU is roughly ₹279 Cr of gross trading-margin income — from a ₹16,000-crore revenue line. That is why the standalone total operating income that PTC itself highlights is only about ₹718 Cr (FY25, +17% year-on-year): the trading margin plus two other real earners — surcharge income (the interest PTC charges when discoms pay late, a surprisingly large contributor) and consultancy income (~₹50 Cr) from advising on power projects. The ₹16,000 Cr is the electricity; the ₹718 Cr is the company.

The device that only fits a power trader: volume × paise, not revenue × margin %

Forget "revenue grew" or "margins expanded" — both mislead here. The number that runs PTC India is units traded × paise kept per unit, full stop. Two levers, and only two: how many billion units flow through the desk, and how many paise stick to each one. In FY25 that was ~82.75 BU × ~3.37 paise. Nudge the margin from 3.4 to 3.0 paise and no revenue line will show it — yet a tenth of the trading profit has quietly vanished. Push volume from 75 to 83 BU, as FY25 did, and profit rises even if the per-unit take is flat. A generator is judged on capacity and plant load factor; a bank on net interest margin; a power trader on this one two-part product — and almost nobody reading the headline revenue figure realises how little of it is real.

This is also why headline revenue can swing without telling you anything about the health of the business. If power prices rise, the pass-through value rises and revenue balloons — while the paise kept per unit may be unchanged or falling. If prices soften, revenue shrinks even as volumes and margin hold. For a pass-through intermediary, billion units and paise-per-unit are the only vital signs. Hold that lens for the rest of this primer.

03

The regulated take: why the margin has a legal ceiling

The genuine surprise — PTC's fee is not just thin, it is capped by law.

Here is the fact that startles most people meeting this company: PTC cannot simply charge what it likes. Power trading in India is a licensed activity, and the Central Electricity Regulatory Commission (CERC) sets a hard ceiling on the trading margin a licensee may take. The trader's core product has a statutory price cap. That single rule shapes the entire economics.

Under CERC's trading-margin regulations, for short-term trades the margin is capped at roughly 7 paise per unit where the sale price is up to ₹4/unit, and about 4 paise per unit where the sale price is higher. PTC's realised core margin — ~3.37 paise in FY25 — sits comfortably inside that ceiling, because competition among traders pushes the achieved margin below the legal maximum anyway. So PTC is squeezed twice: by a regulator on the upside and by rival traders on the downside.

Why a capped fee is both a floor and a cage

The cap cuts two ways. On one hand it is a protection: because trading is licensed and margin-capped, the field cannot be swamped by reckless price wars to zero, and a handful of scaled, creditworthy licensees dominate. PTC's licence and two-decade track record are a real barrier — you cannot simply set up a power-trading desk overnight. On the other hand it is a cage: no matter how well PTC executes, it cannot earn its way to a fat margin on the core trade, because the law forbids it. The only routes to more profit are more volume, more surcharge (which itself depends on discoms paying late), and adjacent income like consultancy and its financial-services stake. That is a narrow set of levers, and it is why the market values the trading business modestly.

The surcharge point deserves a beat, because it is counter-intuitive. When a cash-strapped discom pays PTC late, PTC charges a late-payment surcharge — effectively interest on overdue bills — and this can be a meaningful chunk of operating income. In other words, part of PTC's earnings comes from the very financial weakness of its customers. It is real money and contractually protected, but it is not the sign of a healthy, fast-paying market, and reforms that improve discom payment discipline would, oddly, shrink one of PTC's income lines even as they make the sector safer.

04

The company, the promoters, and who really controls it

A government-born trader with almost no promoter holding — an unusual cap table.

PTC India has an unusual origin and an even more unusual ownership structure. It was created not by an entrepreneur but by the state, to solve a specific problem — and today its "promoters" between them hold barely a sixth of it, leaving institutions and the public firmly in control of the register.

Timeline

  • 1999
    Incorporated as Power Trading Corporation of India by the Government of India, to develop a market for surplus power and de-risk private generation by acting as a creditworthy off-taker.
  • 2004
    IPO and listing on NSE & BSE (scrip code 532524), establishing PTC as the flagship of a nascent power-trading industry.
  • 2006–08
    Diversifies: sets up PTC India Financial Services (PFS) to lend to power projects, and later PTC Energy to own generation (chiefly wind) — the seeds of the later "why is a trader owning assets?" debate.
  • 2010
    PFS lists separately; the group takes an early stake in the power-exchange story before divesting its IEX holding in 2017 — ironically stepping back from the venue that would later challenge trading.
  • Jan 2022
    Governance episode: three independent directors of PFS resign alleging board-level lapses, triggering SEBI scrutiny across the group and a sharp de-rating (see §08).
  • FY25
    PTC Energy sold to ONGC (wind portfolio, ~₹925 Cr equity value / ~₹1,179 Cr enterprise), booking a large one-off gain and refocusing on core trading; record reported PAT ~₹976 Cr; dividend ₹11.70/share.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters — four state power giants, tiny stakes: PTC was promoted by NTPC, Power Grid (PGCIL), Power Finance Corporation (PFC) and NHPC. But each holds only around 4%, so the combined promoter holding is roughly 16%. There is no dominant controlling shareholder — unusual for an Indian company, and a double-edged trait (professional independence, but also weaker ownership accountability).
  • Institutions & public hold the balance: the large free float (~84%) is dominated by domestic and foreign institutions and retail investors. It was precisely these institutional holders who, in past years, pressed management to exit non-core ventures (wind, lending) and return to the trading knitting.
  • The group's other listed limb: PTC India Financial Services (PFS) — the power-sector NBFC — is a majority-owned, separately listed affiliate. Its performance, and its 2022 governance troubles, flow back to PTC both financially and reputationally (§07–08). Verify the exact current PFS shareholding in the latest filing.
The structure is the story. A trader promoted by the very generators (NTPC, NHPC) and financiers (PFC) it deals with, yet controlled by none of them, gives PTC deep sectoral credibility and a quasi-official standing — which is exactly why it became the government's nominated cross-border trader (§05). But the near-absent promoter holding, an ill-fated diversification into owning assets and lending, and a 2022 governance blow-up are the reasons the market has long applied a holding-company-style discount to it. Read the credibility and the discount together — both are real.
05

The cross-border moat: the electricity that crosses India's borders

The one part of the business a rival genuinely cannot copy overnight.

If PTC's domestic trade is a commoditised, capped-margin business open to any licensee, its cross-border trade is the opposite: a government-nominated franchise, built on inter-governmental treaties, that a competitor cannot simply buy its way into. This is the most defensible thing PTC owns, and it flows from its state origins.

PTC is the long-standing, government-designated Indian entity for trading power with India's neighbours. It imports hydropower from Bhutan (from projects such as Tala, Chhukha, Mangdechhu) under bilateral arrangements between the two governments; it trades with Nepal in both directions as Nepalese hydro capacity swings seasonally; and it exports to Bangladesh. Because these flows sit inside diplomatic and treaty frameworks — not open commercial tenders — the incumbent nominated trader enjoys a position that is structurally hard to dislodge.

Why the border trade matters more than its size suggests

Measured in billion units, cross-border is a minority of PTC's volume. But it punches above its weight for two reasons. First, defensibility: domestic trading margin is capped and contested by a dozen licensees, whereas the cross-border franchise rests on government nomination and treaty relationships that took decades to build and cannot be tendered away in a hurry. Second, optionality: as Nepal and Bhutan bring large new hydro capacity online and as regional grid integration (the "One Sun One World One Grid" and South Asian grid ambitions) advances, the pool of cross-border power that needs an experienced intermediary should grow. That is the one part of PTC where the volume lever and the margin lever both point up, and where a rival cannot arrive tomorrow with a cheque and a licence.

06

The exchange threat: being designed out of the trade

The structural question that governs the multiple — can a middleman survive a marketplace?

Every intermediary faces the same existential question: what happens when buyers and sellers can find each other directly? For a power trader, that "direct marketplace" already exists and is growing — the power exchanges, dominated by the Indian Energy Exchange (IEX), with PXIL and the newer HPX alongside. On an exchange, a discom and a generator match anonymously, the exchange clears the trade and guarantees payment, and no bilateral trader is needed. This is disintermediation, and it is the core bear case on PTC.

The short-term market is exactly where growth is happening, and it is the slice most exposed to exchanges. As more short-term volume migrates onto IEX's screen — day-ahead, real-time and term-ahead markets — the addressable pool for a bilateral trader's paise-per-unit clip is pressured on both volume and price. An exchange charges a fee too, but it is a technology-and-clearing platform with structurally lower costs, and its liquidity is self-reinforcing: the more volume it has, the tighter its prices, the more volume it attracts.

Directional, not precise: over time, liquid exchanges have taken share of the short-term market from bilateral trading, and a trader's margin is both capped (§03) and undercut by the exchange alternative. This is the structural headwind the valuation reflects.

Two things soften the story without cancelling it. First, PTC itself uses the exchanges — it transacts on IEX as one channel among many — so it is not wholly a victim of the venue. Second, the parts of PTC's book least touched by exchanges are its most defensible: long-term PPAs (which exchanges do not serve) and cross-border (which they cannot). The realistic reading is not that PTC vanishes, but that its domestic short-term franchise faces slow structural erosion, which caps growth and keeps the multiple low — while its long-term and cross-border franchises endure.

The uncomfortable framing to sit with

An intermediary is worth most when the friction it removes is large, and worth least when a marketplace removes that friction more cheaply. Power exchanges are, in effect, industrialising the friction PTC is paid to solve: they clear payment, they match counterparties, they set a transparent price. For the short-term trade, that is a direct substitute for what a bilateral trader does. PTC's defence is to concentrate where the friction is still real and high — creditworthiness for weak discoms, the structuring of decades-long PPAs, and the treaty-bound cross-border flows an exchange cannot list. Whether that defensible core is enough to hold the trading profit flat while the contested part erodes is the single structural question a buyer of the stock is really taking a view on.

07

The holding company underneath: cash, a lending stake, and a cleaned-up wind exit

Why the sum of PTC's parts is a live part of the investment case.

PTC is not only a trading desk. Over the years it accreted a set of side-holdings that make it look, on the balance sheet, part operating company and part investment vehicle. Understanding those parts — and management's move to simplify them — is essential, because the market's deep-value tag rests on the gap between what PTC owns and what it is priced at.

  • The core trading business — capital-light, cash-generative, but low-growth and capped-margin (§02–03). This is the engine, but not where the "hidden value" argument lives.
  • A pile of surplus cash and investments — because trading needs little reinvestment, PTC throws off cash it cannot productively redeploy, which is why the payout ratio and dividend yield are so high. Net debt is low and, after the wind exit, the balance sheet is cash-heavy.
  • The PFS stake — PTC's majority holding in the separately listed PTC India Financial Services carries a market value that a sum-of-parts investor adds to the trading business. It is also the source of the governance overhang (§08), so the market discounts it heavily.
  • PTC Energy — now monetised — the ~288 MW wind portfolio that institutional investors had long wanted gone was sold to ONGC in FY25 for roughly ₹925 Cr of equity value (~₹1,179 Cr enterprise value). The sale removed a non-core distraction, booked a large one-off gain, and added cash — the clearest single act of the "focus on core" agenda.

Why the parts are the argument

The bull case on PTC is rarely about trading growth — it is arithmetic. Add the value of the trading business (however modestly you rate it), the listed PFS stake, and the surplus cash swollen by the wind sale, and the total tends to sit above the market capitalisation. In other words, the market prices PTC at a discount to the pieces it holds — the classic holding-company markdown, aggravated here by the governance history and by the low-growth trading core. The catalysts that could close that gap are concrete and nameable: a higher, sustained payout or buyback that hands the surplus cash back to owners; a clean-up or monetisation of the PFS stake; and a durable end to the governance overhang. None require the trading business to suddenly grow — they require the balance sheet to be simplified and returned. The wind exit was the first move; the question is whether it is the last.

08

The 2022 governance episode — told straight

The overhang that a first-timer must weigh squarely, neither dismissed nor exaggerated.

No primer on this group is complete without the events of early 2022, because they are the single biggest reason the market applies a discount to it. The episode centred on PFS — the listed financial-services affiliate — but it reflected on the whole PTC group's governance and its board's oversight.

In January 2022, three independent directors of PTC India Financial Services resigned together, publicly alleging governance and compliance lapses at the board and management level — including a contested senior appointment and process failures they described as violations of the Companies Act. The market reaction was severe: PFS shares fell sharply, and the affair drew SEBI scrutiny of the group. Regulatory proceedings followed against certain group office-holders; some orders were subsequently challenged and, at the appellate level, partly set aside. The details are legally involved and have moved through multiple stages — a reader should treat the exact current status as something to verify against the latest filings and orders.

Why it still matters for the parent: even though the flashpoint was at PFS, PTC India is the majority owner and the group's flagship, so the affair dented confidence in the whole structure's governance and independent-director culture — at a company that already has almost no controlling promoter to enforce discipline. Governance risk here is not hypothetical; it has crystallised once, and the market has not fully forgiven it.
How to weigh it without over- or under-reacting

The honest read is balanced. On one side, this was a real, public, regulator-involving governance failure at a group with weak ownership control — precisely the kind of event that justifies a permanent discount and demands ongoing vigilance on board quality, related-party dealings and capital allocation. On the other, the subsequent agenda — exiting non-core assets, the PTC Energy sale, pressure from institutional holders for higher payouts — reads as a group being pushed, however belatedly, toward tighter focus and better shareholder alignment. A buyer of the stock is underwriting the view that the worst is disclosed and behind it, and that the clean-up continues; a sceptic is right to insist that governance be demonstrated, quarter after quarter, before the discount narrows. The metric that would settle it is not financial at all — it is a clean, uneventful stretch of board conduct and consistent capital returns.

09

The industry and the rivals

A licensed field with a clear leader in volume — and a marketplace circling it.

Power trading in India is a licensed business with a limited set of serious players, because a trader needs a CERC licence, deep balance-sheet strength to carry discom credit risk, and long relationships on both sides of the grid. PTC is the largest and oldest of the bilateral traders, but it competes against both other traders and — increasingly — the exchange venues that bypass traders entirely.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two features define this market. First, among traders the competition is on creditworthiness, relationships and scale, not on price — because the margin is capped and largely commoditised, the winner is whoever the generators trust to pay and whoever the discoms trust to supply. PTC's size, age and quasi-official standing are genuine advantages there, especially cross-border. Second, and more important, the sharpest competitor is not another trader at all — it is the exchange, which competes with the entire trading model rather than with PTC specifically. A power trader can beat every rival desk and still see its addressable market quietly narrow as liquidity migrates to a screen. That is the distinctive competitive bind of this business.

10

Financials & valuation

A cash-rich intermediary priced below its book — the debate is durability and distribution, not growth.

PTC's numbers must be read on two levels: the near-meaningless gross revenue, and the modest-but-real operating income and per-unit margin that actually describe it. FY25's reported profit was flattered by the PTC Energy sale, so the true picture is a steady, low-single-digit-growth trading business sitting on a lot of cash and a lending stake — and a share price that reflects deep skepticism about all of it.

The one number that settles the thesis: the sustained payout on a stable core margin

PTC is cheap on every conventional lens — under 10× earnings, below book, a ~14% yield. So the case does not need a re-rating engine; it needs two things to hold. The metric that proves or breaks it is the durable dividend/buyback stream the business can fund without shrinking — which in turn rests on the core trading margin per unit staying near its ~3.37 paise level as volume grows. Track those together, quarter by quarter: is the per-unit margin holding against exchange encroachment (§06), and is management actually returning the surplus cash (§07) rather than hoarding or misallocating it? If the margin holds and the payout is sustained and lifted, a sub-book, sub-10× valuation on a cash-rich intermediary is hard to justify and the discount should narrow. If the per-unit margin drifts down as short-term volume migrates to the screen, and cash sits idle, the market's skepticism is vindicated and the yield is a value trap. Volume times paise, times what actually reaches shareholders — that is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, cash-rich middleman — asked whether it can defend its clip and return its cash.

PTC India enters FY26 simplified and liquid: the wind assets sold, the balance sheet cash-heavy, a double-digit dividend paid, and volumes growing. What it cannot promise is the one thing the whole model rests on — that a marketplace does not slowly render the middleman less necessary, and that its own governance and capital discipline stay clean. That tension frames both cases.

The bull case

  • Cheap on plain arithmetic — under 10× earnings and below book, on a capital-light, cash-generative business with a ~14% dividend yield.
  • Sum-of-parts discount — trading business + listed PFS stake + surplus cash (swollen by the wind sale) plausibly exceed the market cap; catalysts are payout, buyback and stake clean-up.
  • Cross-border moat — the government-nominated Bhutan/Nepal/Bangladesh franchise is treaty-backed, defensible, and set to grow with regional hydro and grid integration.
  • Scale & licence barrier — largest, oldest bilateral trader; creditworthiness and relationships that new entrants cannot quickly assemble.
  • Self-help underway — non-core wind exited, institutional pressure driving focus and higher returns of capital.

The bear case

  • Structural disintermediation — power exchanges keep taking short-term share; the trader's addressable pool narrows and its clip is contested.
  • Capped, thin margin — CERC limits the upside by law; competition pins realised margin below even that. Little room to earn more per unit.
  • Governance overhang — the 2022 PFS episode crystallised real risk at a group with almost no controlling promoter; trust must be re-earned.
  • Value trap risk — cheap can stay cheap if surplus cash sits idle or is redeployed poorly, and if the PFS stake keeps discounting the whole.
  • Discom & policy dependence — earnings lean on stressed state utilities (including the odd truth that surcharge income rises when they pay late).
The bottom line

PTC India is a well-established wholesaler of electrons — cash-rich, capped-margin, and priced by a market that half-expects a marketplace to erode it. The self-help is real: the wind exit simplified the story, the balance sheet is liquid, and the dividend is large. But the core trade earns a legally-limited few paise per unit that power exchanges are quietly learning to charge less for, and the group's governance was found wanting once within living memory. The next test is concrete and datable: whether, through FY26, the core trading margin holds near ~3.37 paise as volumes rise, and whether management converts its swollen cash pile into a sustained, lifted return to shareholders rather than a new diversification. Defend the clip and hand back the cash, and a sub-book, sub-10× valuation looks like a mispriced utility of the grid's plumbing. Let the margin thin on the exchange's advance while the cash sits idle, and the high yield is simply the market paying you to wait for a business the market itself is designing around.
