Thangamayil Jewellery, the jeweller that owns its towns

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Thangamayil Jewellery is a gold-jewellery retailer — showrooms full of chains, bangles, necklaces, wedding sets, coins and, increasingly, diamond and silver pieces — concentrated in the temple towns and districts of southern Tamil Nadu, radiating out from its home city of Madurai. It is not a national brand and does not try to be. It is a regional champion: in Madurai and the surrounding tier-2 and tier-3 towns it is often the most trusted name a family thinks of when a wedding, a festival or a birth calls for gold. Its FY26 was, by its own description, its best-ever year — revenue up ~73%, profit nearly tripling — riding two forces at once: a fast-expanding store count and a gold-price rally that lifted the value of everything on its shelves.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A jeweller earns far less than the sticker price suggests. When a customer pays ₹6 lakh for a necklace, most of that money is the gold itself, which the jeweller merely passes through. Thangamayil keeps the making charges (labour) plus a slim margin over the gold value. That is why a jeweller doing ₹8,514 Cr of revenue nets only ~₹352 Cr — a ~4% net margin is normal, not a warning sign. The business is a high-turnover, thin-slice retail model bolted onto a very expensive raw material.
  • Its moat is trust and density, not scale. Gold is a high-value, high-anxiety purchase — customers fear being cheated on purity or weight, so they go to the name their family has always used. Thangamayil has spent decades earning that reputation across a specific cluster of southern-Tamil-Nadu towns and packing it with showrooms. That local density is genuinely hard for a national chain to replicate town by town — which is the whole investment case, and the subject of section 04.
  • The stock is priced as a growth compounder, not a value play. At ~₹21,460 Cr market cap and roughly 60× earnings, the market is paying up for the store-rollout-plus-gold-boom story to continue. That multiple only makes sense if new stores keep maturing profitably and same-store volumes keep growing — not if the "growth" turns out to be mostly the gold price, which lifts every jeweller and can just as easily fall.
In plain terms

Thangamayil is best understood as a trusted local shopfront sitting on top of a giant pile of gold. The pile of gold is worth a fortune but earns the company almost nothing directly — it belongs, economically, to the customer who will eventually buy it. What Thangamayil actually sells is convenience, trust and craftsmanship: the assurance that the purity is honest, the weight is right, and the design is what a Madurai wedding expects. It makes money on the making charges and a thin retail margin, turns its inventory fast, and grows by opening more shopfronts in towns where its name already carries weight. The risk hiding in a record year is that a rising gold price flatters the revenue line while telling you very little about whether the company is actually selling more jewellery.

01

Jeweller economics 101: where the money actually is

No finance background needed — just the handful of ideas that make a jeweller tick.

The single most important thing to understand about a jewellery retailer is that most of its revenue is not really its own. Gold is a pass-through cost. If you strip a necklace's price into parts, the overwhelming majority is the market value of the metal — which the jeweller bought and is simply reselling — and only a thin sliver is the money the jeweller keeps. Get that one idea and the whole business model, and its odd-looking financials, snap into focus.

The four levers of a jeweller's revenue
LeverWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Gold priceThe per-gram market price of gold. It sets the value of everything in the store. When it rises, revenue rises even if not a single extra gram is sold — a flattering, and misleading, tailwind.
Grammage (volume)The actual weight of jewellery sold — grams and kilos out the door. This is the real demand signal, stripped of price. Volume growth means more customers buying more jewellery; price growth just means gold got dearer.
Making chargesThe labour/craftsmanship fee, charged as a % of gold value or a flat rate per gram. This is genuine value-add the jeweller keeps. Higher-design, studded and branded pieces carry richer making charges.
Store count & SSSGHow many showrooms, and how fast sales grow at stores open more than a year (same-store-sales-growth). New stores add revenue; SSSG tells you the existing base is healthy — but SSSG is inflated by gold price too, so grammage-led SSSG is what counts.
The economics that decide everything: making charges + margin over gold

A jeweller's gross profit comes from two places. First, the making charge — the fee for turning raw gold into a finished ornament. Second, a small margin over the gold value — the retailer buys metal at one price and sells the finished piece at a modest mark-up, and on old stock a rising gold price can hand it an inventory gain on top. Add those up and a jeweller's gross margin on a plain-gold chain might be low single digits to high single digits; on a diamond-studded or branded piece it can be much fatter. That is why mix matters more than the headline: a jeweller that sells more studded jewellery and richer designs earns more per rupee of gold than one shifting plain 22-carat chains. Revenue tells you the gold flowed; the making-charge and studded mix tell you how much of it stuck.

Why the balance sheet looks so heavy

A jeweller must stock what it sells, and what it sells is gold — so its shelves and vaults hold enormous value at all times. Thangamayil carried roughly ₹2,954 Cr of inventory at FY26 exit, most of it gold and silver. That inventory has to be financed, which is why jewellers run meaningful borrowings and lean on a clever instrument called a gold-metal loan (section 07). The upshot: a jeweller is a working-capital-intensive retailer where the raw material is money-like, price-volatile, and expensive to hold — so inventory turns (how fast stock sells and is replaced) and hedging discipline (protecting against a gold-price fall on unsold stock) are not footnotes; they are the difference between a good year and a painful one.

One shopfront, several products — with very different economics.

To a shopper, Thangamayil is a bright, trusted showroom for gold. To an analyst, it is a product mix whose composition quietly drives profitability. The same square foot of retail floor earns very different money depending on whether it is moving plain gold coins, an intricate wedding set, or a diamond-studded piece.

Why mix is the quiet margin lever

Two jewellers can report identical revenue and earn very different profits. The one selling more studded and designer pieces — where the making charge is a larger slice and customers pay for craft, not just metal — keeps more of every rupee. Thangamayil's core is wedding-led plain gold, which is a footfall magnet but a thinner-margin product. The strategic question for the next few years is whether it can nudge its mix toward studded and higher-design jewellery without losing the trusted, value-for-money positioning that made it the default choice in its towns. Push mix too hard and it starts to look like the national premium chains it undercuts; push too little and margins stay pinned to the plain-gold floor.

03

The family, the city, and who controls it

A Madurai founding family that turned a local name into a listed chain.

Thangamayil is a family business in the most literal sense — the name itself is heritage. It grew out of a long-standing Madurai jewellery house built by the Ba. Ramesh founding family, who took a trusted local retail name, corporatised it, expanded it across the surrounding districts, and listed it. The promoters still run and control the company, which for a trust-based regional retailer is a feature, not a bug: the family name is the brand.

Timeline

  • 2000s

  • 2010

  • FY24–FY25

  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Ba. Ramesh founding family and associated entities hold roughly 61.6% (approx.) of the company, with — per the company's FY26 disclosures — no fresh encumbrances and effectively nil pledged. A committed, unlevered promoter holding is exactly what you want in a trust-brand retailer.
  • Geographic identity: the company is deeply Tamil Nadu — headquartered in Madurai, dense across the southern districts (Madurai, Tirunelveli, Dindigul, Sivakasi, Rajapalayam, Karur and the temple-town belt). Its planned Chennai push is the first serious test of whether the brand travels beyond the towns where its name is already a household word.
  • Financing: the balance sheet carries meaningful borrowings (~₹774 Cr at FY26) against a large gold inventory (~₹2,954 Cr) — normal for a jeweller, and largely working-capital and gold-metal-loan in nature rather than long-term project debt. This is a retailer that funds metal, not factories.
Why the family control matters here: in most industries a tightly held, family-run company invites governance scepticism. In regional jewellery it is closer to the point of the whole thing — customers are buying trust, and the founding family's name over the door is the collateral behind that trust. The flip side is ordinary family-business risk: succession, related-party dealings, and capital-allocation discipline as the chain scales. Watch how professionally the expansion beyond the heartland is executed.
04

The regional-density moat: why owning your towns beats being everywhere

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a regional jeweller.

The instinct is to assume the biggest jeweller wins — that Tanishq's national scale, ad budget and design must eventually roll over a small-town regional name. For gold, that instinct is partly wrong, and understanding why is the crux of Thangamayil. Gold retail is not one national market; it is hundreds of hyper-local trust markets, and in its own cluster of southern-Tamil-Nadu towns, Thangamayil is the incumbent everyone already trusts.

Here is the mechanism. Buying gold is a high-value, low-frequency, high-anxiety transaction. A family buys serious gold a handful of times in a lifetime — a wedding, a birth, a big festival — and spends lakhs each time. Their overriding fear is being cheated: on purity (is it really 22-carat?), on weight, and on the buy-back value when they return to exchange old gold. That fear makes them intensely loyal to a name their family has used and trusts. In a town where Thangamayil has been the trusted jeweller for a generation, a new Tanishq or Kalyan showroom does not automatically win — it has to earn trust that the incumbent already has, in a purchase customers make too rarely to experiment with lightly.

The device that only fits Thangamayil: the one-trusted-jeweller catchment

Picture southern Tamil Nadu not as one market but as a grid of small-town catchments, each of which can profitably support only a limited number of large, trusted jewellers — often really just one dominant local name plus a couple of challengers. Thangamayil's edge is that across a dense cluster of these catchments it is that one name, and it has packed the cluster with showrooms so a rival has to fight for trust town-by-town, catchment-by-catchment, against an incumbent with decades of head start. A national chain can out-spend Thangamayil on advertising a hundred times over and still find that in Dindigul or Rajapalayam the wedding gold goes to the local name. That is the moat: not size, but trusted density in places too small and too relationship-driven for scale alone to conquer. The whole thesis rides on whether that town-by-town trust holds — and whether Thangamayil can rebuild it in new catchments like Chennai, where it is the challenger, not the incumbent.

This is why the thesis is not "buy the gold price." A gold rally lifts every jeweller's revenue equally — Titan, Kalyan, Joyalukkas, GRT and Thangamayil all report fatter numbers when gold climbs, so the rally tells you nothing about which of them is winning. Thangamayil's distinctive edge only shows up in the metrics the gold price can't fake: growing grammage at existing stores (are more people buying, not just paying more?), profitable new-store adds in and around its heartland, and a studded/making-charge mix that lifts margin. The moat is real; the question is always whether the numbers prove it is still holding.

A genuine surprise: the thing that would most threaten Thangamayil is not a national chain arriving — that competition is decades old and it has held its ground. The subtler threat is the company diluting its own moat by expanding into large cities like Chennai, where it has no incumbency, no trusted-name advantage, and must slug it out with Tanishq and Joyalukkas on their turf, spending heavily to buy trust it gets for free at home. Growth outside the heartland is where a high-ROCE regional jeweller can quietly turn into an ordinary one.

05

The record-year illusion: gold price vs grammage

How to read a jeweller's "+73%" year without being fooled by it.

FY26 revenue rose about 73%. That looks spectacular — and it is a genuinely strong year — but a first-time reader must learn the one habit that separates jeweller analysts from the crowd: ask how much of the jump is more jewellery sold, and how much is simply gold getting dearer. Because gold is a pass-through, a rising price inflates the revenue line mechanically, without a single extra gram leaving the store.

In a year when gold rose sharply, the price effect can be the single biggest contributor to a "+73%" headline. The two bars that actually prove the business is winning — new-store adds and same-store grammage growth — are usually smaller than the price bar, and are the only parts that survive when gold stops rising.

Why does this matter so much? Because a gold-price-driven revenue jump is borrowed, not earned. If gold gives back a chunk of its gains next year, the same mechanical arithmetic runs in reverse — revenue can fall even as the company sells the same amount of jewellery, and margins can take an inventory hit on stock bought at higher prices. A jeweller whose "growth" was mostly price is a leveraged bet on the gold market wearing a retailer's clothes. A jeweller whose growth was mostly grammage and new stores is compounding a real business. The two look identical on the revenue line and could not be more different underneath.

The one habit to carry away

Every time a jeweller reports a blow-out quarter, find the volume/grammage disclosure and the same-store growth ex-gold-price, and weigh them against how much gold moved that quarter. If grammage grew and new stores are maturing, the business is genuinely winning share and footfall. If revenue soared but grams were flat, you are looking at a gold-price photograph, not a growth engine. For Thangamayil specifically, the encouraging read is that FY26's revenue growth came alongside real store additions and strong festive/wedding footfall — not price alone — but the discipline is to keep checking, because the gold tailwind is the easiest thing in this business to mistake for skill.

06

Store expansion and the new-store maths

Growth by shopfront — and why where and how well matter more than how many.

A regional jeweller grows in two ways: sell more at existing stores, and open new ones. Thangamayil ended FY26 with 66 showrooms and has flagged a next phase of expansion around Chennai from FY27. New stores are the visible growth engine — but a store is only good news if it matures into a profitable, high-return outlet rather than sitting as expensive floor space stuffed with borrowed gold.

Here is the mechanism a new jewellery store follows. On day one it is a cost centre: it has to be stocked with a full showroom of gold — lakhs of grams financed by debt or gold-metal loans — before it sells a single chain. For the first months to a year or two it builds footfall and local trust; revenue ramps, and only once it covers the carrying cost of its inventory and its operating costs does it start earning its keep. A store in a heartland town where the Thangamayil name is already trusted matures fast. A store in a new city where the brand is unknown can take much longer, cost more to stock and advertise, and — in the worst case — never reach the returns of a heartland outlet. That is why new-store ROCE (return on the capital tied up in each store, including its gold inventory) is the number that separates disciplined expansion from empire-building.

Why the Chennai step is the one to watch

Expanding deeper into the heartland is low-risk, high-return, and largely proven — it is simply more of what already works. The Chennai move is different in kind: it takes Thangamayil out of the towns where incumbency does its work and into a large, competitive metro where it is the outsider. If those stores mature at ROCE close to the heartland's, it proves the brand and model travel, and the growth runway extends dramatically — the bull case. If they bleed capital and never reach heartland returns, the company will have swapped a wonderful regional economics for an ordinary national one, funded with gold-inventory debt. Same expansion headline; opposite investment outcome. The maturation curve of the first Chennai stores is a genuine, checkable signal.

07

Gold inventory, gold-metal loans and hedging

The plumbing that keeps a jeweller solvent — and the risk that can sink one.

A jeweller's biggest asset and biggest headache are the same thing: gold inventory. Thangamayil held roughly ₹2,954 Cr of it at FY26 exit. Holding that much of a price-volatile metal creates two problems — how to finance it, and how to protect against its price falling before it sells. The industry's answer to both is elegant, and worth understanding because it is where a jeweller can quietly get into trouble.

The metal on the shelves (~₹2,954 Cr) dwarfs the debt behind it (~₹774 Cr) — normal for a jeweller, and a sign the inventory is funded largely by working capital and gold-metal loans rather than heavy long-term debt. The financing and hedge structure behind that pile, not its size, is what decides whether a gold-price swing helps or hurts.

The gold-metal loan — borrowing metal, not money

Instead of borrowing cash to buy gold, a jeweller can take a gold-metal loan (GML): a bank lends it physical gold, which it turns into jewellery and sells, and it repays the loan in gold (or the rupee value) later, paying a modest lease/interest rate. The beauty of this is a natural hedge. Because the jeweller owes gold, not rupees, it is broadly indifferent to the gold price on that borrowed metal — if gold rises, the value of what it sold rises but so does what it owes; the two offset. GMLs let a jeweller carry a huge gold inventory while neutralising much of the price risk on it, which is exactly what a thin-margin, high-turnover retailer needs.

Why hedging discipline is a survival skill, not a nicety

The danger in jewellery is the un-hedged gold — the metal a jeweller owns outright, bought with cash or equity, sitting on the shelf. If the gold price falls sharply before that stock sells, the jeweller books a real loss on inventory it already paid for. In a rising-price year like FY26 this works gloriously in reverse — old stock bought cheap is sold dear, and the inventory gain flatters profit (a big part of why net profit nearly tripled). But that same mechanism is a loaded gun in a falling market. A well-run jeweller keeps most of its metal exposure hedged — via gold-metal loans and other instruments — so that its profit comes from making charges and turnover, not from betting on the gold price. The quality tell is a jeweller whose earnings are steady across gold cycles, not one that soars when gold rises and slumps when it falls. Inventory turns and hedge coverage are the metrics that reveal which kind you own.

This is why inventory turns — how many times a year the stock sells and is replaced — matter so much. Faster turns mean less capital tied up per rupee of sales, less financing cost, and less exposure to a price reversal on ageing stock. A jeweller that turns its inventory briskly and hedges its owned metal is running a retail business; one that lets inventory pile up un-hedged is, whether it admits it or not, running a leveraged bet on gold. For Thangamayil, with ~₹2,954 Cr of stock against ~₹774 Cr of borrowings, the financing and hedge structure behind that inventory is one of the most important — and least glamorous — things to track.

The nuance most first-timers miss: a chunk of Thangamayil's FY26 profit surge came not from selling more jewellery but from the revaluation of gold it already held as prices climbed. That is a real gain, but it is a gold-market gain, not a retail one — and it does not repeat, and can reverse, if gold flatlines or falls. Separating the durable, making-charge-and-volume profit from the one-off inventory gain is central to judging what this business truly earns through the cycle.
08

The competition closing in on the towns

National chains want Thangamayil's catchments — the moat is tested, not settled.

Thangamayil's heartland is attractive precisely because it is profitable, and profitable markets attract entrants. The big national and pan-south chains — Titan's Tanishq, Kalyan Jewellers, Joyalukkas, GRT, Lalitha, Senco — have all been pushing franchise and owned stores deeper into exactly the tier-2 and tier-3 towns where regional players like Thangamayil have long ruled. The moat described in section 04 is real, but it is under continuous, well-funded assault.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two forces run at once. The headwind is that national chains with deeper pockets and better design are franchising into Thangamayil's towns, and each new Tanishq or Kalyan showroom chips at the incumbent's monopoly on local trust. The tailwind is formalisation: across India, organised branded jewellers are steadily taking share from the unorganised, small family-jeweller base — driven by hallmarking, transparency and financing schemes — and Thangamayil, as a trusted organised regional, is a beneficiary of that shift even as it fends off the nationals. So the same market is getting more competitive at the top and more winnable at the bottom. Thangamayil's job is to keep converting unorganised-market share in its heartland faster than the nationals convert its share — a race it has been winning at home and must now prove it can run away from it.

09

Regulation and the gold backdrop

The rules and the metal — two external forces a jeweller cannot control.

A jeweller sits at the intersection of two things it does not control: government policy on gold and the gold price itself. Both can help or hurt in a single stroke, and both have moved a lot in recent years — mostly, so far, in Thangamayil's favour.

Why hallmarking is quietly the jeweller's friend

It sounds like a compliance burden, but mandatory hallmarking is one of the best structural things to happen to an organised jeweller. For decades a customer's biggest fear — being sold under-carat gold by a corner jeweller — was also the unorganised sector's hiding place. Enforced purity certification drags that whole grey market into the light, narrowing the trust gap that made local jewellers competitive on price. A brand like Thangamayil, which built its name on being the trustworthy option, gains as the rules make trustworthiness the legal minimum and customers migrate to organised names. Regulation here is doing part of the moat-widening for free — as long as Thangamayil stays firmly on the compliant, transparent side of it.

10

Financials & valuation

A genuine record year — priced as if records are the new normal.

FY26 was, by the company's own account, its strongest year: revenue up ~73% to ~₹8,514 Cr, net profit up ~196% to ~₹352 Cr, on a store base of 66 and a fast-rising gold price. The market has already re-rated the stock hard for it — to roughly 60× earnings. Whether that multiple is deserved comes down to what actually drove the numbers.

The one number that settles the thesis: grammage-led SSSG plus new-store ROCE

At ~60× earnings, Thangamayil is not priced as a cheap regional retailer catching a gold tailwind — it is priced as a compounder that will keep opening profitable stores and growing real volume for years. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the pair the gold price cannot fake: same-store-sales growth driven by grammage (volume), not price, read alongside the ROCE at which new stores — especially the Chennai ones — actually mature. If same-store grams keep rising and new stores hit heartland-like returns while net debt and inventory stay under control, the multiple is defensible and the compounding is real. If SSSG turns out to be mostly gold price, or new-city stores drag returns down, then a ~60× stock is paying growth-compounder prices for what is really a gold-levered regional retailer — and the re-rating runs the other way. Revenue is the applause; grammage-led SSSG and new-store ROCE are the scoreboard.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A superb regional franchise — asked to prove the record year was skill, not just gold.

Thangamayil enters FY27 at the top of its game: 66 stores, a record year behind it, an unlevered promoter family, a genuine local moat, and a next chapter aimed at Chennai. The real debate is whether the business can keep compounding real volume and profitable stores once the gold-price tailwind fades — and whether its trusted-density edge survives outside the towns that made it.

The bull case

  • Trusted regional density — decades of local trust and dense store coverage across southern Tamil Nadu that national chains find hard to dislodge town-by-town.
  • Formalisation tailwind — hallmarking and organised-retail share gains keep pulling customers from unorganised local jewellers toward trusted brands like Thangamayil.
  • Store-led growth runway — a proven heartland roll-out model with room to densify further, plus optional upside if the Chennai expansion works.
  • Mix & operating leverage — room to lift studded/making-charge mix, and a fixed store base that turns extra sales into disproportionate profit.
  • Clean control — committed founding family, effectively nil pledge, aligned with the brand that is the asset.

The bear case

  • Gold-price dependence — a big slice of the record revenue and profit rode a rising gold price; a sustained fall reverses the maths and can hit inventory value.
  • Full valuation — ~60× earnings prices in years of flawless compounding; any grammage or new-store disappointment can trigger a sharp de-rating.
  • Regional concentration & expansion risk — reliance on one region, and a Chennai push where it is the challenger, not the trusted incumbent.
  • National-chain pressure — Tanishq, Kalyan and Joyalukkas franchising into its towns, spending heavily to buy the trust Thangamayil earns for free.
  • Working-capital & hedging risk — a large gold inventory funded by debt/gold-metal loans; a hedging or turns misstep is costly in a thin-margin model.
The bottom line

Thangamayil is a rare thing — a small-town retailer with a real, hard-to-copy moat, built on the one currency that matters when a family spends a wedding's worth of gold: trust. FY26 showed that moat throwing off record numbers. But a record printed during a gold-price rally has to be read carefully, because the metal did some of the work, and the metal does not always cooperate. The next chapter turns on two things you can actually check: whether same-store grammage keeps climbing when the gold tailwind fades, and whether the first Chennai showrooms mature at returns anywhere near the heartland's. Those two prints — grams sold at home, ROCE earned away from home — will tell you whether you own a durable regional-trust compounder or a gold-levered retailer wearing its costume. Watch the grammage and the Chennai maturation curve; everything else is the gold price talking.
