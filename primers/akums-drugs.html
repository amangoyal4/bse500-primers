Akums Drugs, the factory behind the brands

A full walk through India's largest contract maker of finished medicines — the company that develops and manufactures tablets, capsules, syrups and injectables for more than fifteen hundred other pharma brands, including 26 of the country's 30 biggest. Why breadth and a giant library of ready-made formulations are the moat, why the contract business earns a thin spread while its own APIs and brands earn more, and why a 2024 IPO star has spent two years asking the market a hard question about who really owns the customer.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Akums Drugs and Pharmaceuticals is a company most Indians have used without ever seeing its name. It is a CDMO — a contract development and manufacturing organisation — and by revenue, capacity and client count it is the largest maker of finished pharmaceutical formulations in India. In plain language: hundreds of pharma companies do not own the factories that make their medicines; they hire Akums to develop the product, run it through the plant, and hand back finished tablets, capsules, liquids and injectables in the client's own branded pack. Akums does this for a roster of over 1,500 companies at once, across more than 60 dosage forms, from ten manufacturing sites.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Its customers are the pharma brands themselves. In FY24 Akums manufactured products for 26 of the 30 largest pharma companies in India and held roughly a 30% share of the domestic CDMO market. The medicines carry someone else's brand on the strip; the actual chemistry and manufacturing were often done in an Akums plant. That is the whole idea — it is an invisible factory whose clients are the visible brands.
  • The contract business is high-scale but thin-margin; the owned pieces earn more. The flagship CDMO arm is roughly three-quarters of revenue at a low-teens EBITDA margin. Around it Akums runs its own APIs (the active drug ingredients), its own branded and generic formulations, and a trade-generics arm — parts where it captures more of the value but which have, at times, lost money. Group operating margin sits near 12%.
  • It is a recent, and bumpy, listing. Akums IPO'd in August 2024, then spent much of the next two years well below its issue price as investors chewed over customer concentration, thin contract economics and a strange near-zero reported profit in FY24. FY26 revenue was ~₹4,359 Cr with reported net profit of ~₹256 Cr; the stock still trades on a punchy multiple that assumes the CDMO engine keeps compounding.
In plain terms

Behind almost every branded medicine sits a factory — but the brand rarely owns it. Akums is that factory, at national scale. A pharma company that wants to launch a cough syrup or a fixed-dose tablet can either spend years and crores building a plant, a formulation team and a regulatory dossier, or it can walk into Akums, pick a ready-made formulation off the shelf, slap its own brand on the pack, and be selling in months. Akums earns a manufacturing fee on each strip — a modest slice, because the client owns the brand and the pricing. Its bet is that being the biggest, broadest, fastest option makes it the default choice for the entire industry. The catch, and the reason the stock has been argued over, is that a factory-for-hire is only as strong as its customers' loyalty — and its best customers are exactly the ones large enough to build a plant of their own one day.

01

CDMO 101: the factory the brand doesn't own

No pharma background needed — just who makes the medicine, and who owns it.

When you buy a strip of tablets, two very different companies were usually involved: the one whose brand is on the pack and sells it to your chemist, and the one that actually made the tablet. Very often they are not the same. The maker-for-hire is called a contract manufacturer, and when it also develops the product — designs the formula, works out the coating, stabilises the liquid, prepares the regulatory paperwork — it graduates to a CDMO: contract development and manufacturing organisation. Akums is the largest such business in India for finished formulations. Five terms carry the whole primer.

The only pharma-manufacturing terms you need

The structural fact that creates the whole industry: brands don't want factories

A pharma brand's real assets are its brand equity, its doctors' relationships and its distribution — not steel and stainless. Building and running a formulations plant is capital-heavy, regulation-heavy and low-return; keeping it busy across dozens of dosage forms is harder still. So a large share of the industry outsources the making and keeps the selling. That is the demand Akums lives on. Every time a mid-sized brand decides it would rather not own a tablet line, or a big brand needs a dosage form its own plants can't run, or a new company wants to launch without a five-year factory build, the call goes to a CDMO. Akums' pitch is simply that it is the one that can say yes to almost any request, in almost any format, faster than anyone else.

Who pays Akums, and for what

  • The pharma brand (the CDMO client) — the core customer. It brings a product idea or picks an existing Akums formulation, and pays a development-plus-manufacturing fee for finished, packed medicine in its own brand. It keeps the brand, the price and the margin on the shelf; Akums keeps a conversion spread.
  • The end patient — indirectly — never a direct Akums customer, but the ultimate reason volumes exist. India's rising chronic-disease load and deep, price-sensitive medicine market drive the sheer number of strips that must be made each year.
  • Akums' own brands and export buyers — where Akums sells medicine under its own name (branded and generic formulations, domestic and international) or exports finished product and APIs. Here it is the brand owner, not the hired hand — a different, higher-value game explored in sections 03 and 06.
02

The three-part moat: capacity × breadth × dossier library

Why the biggest formulations CDMO is hard to dislodge — and what "biggest" actually buys.

In a business where the client owns the brand and can, in theory, take its volume elsewhere, what stops it? Akums' answer is scale of a very particular kind. Its defensibility is not one thing but the product of three: how much it can make, how many formats it can make, and how many ready-to-launch formulations and dossiers it already holds. Multiply them and you get a supplier a brand cannot easily replace with a phone call.

Any one lever is copyable; the three together are not. A rival can build a tablet line, but matching Akums' capacity and 60-plus formats and a 4,000-strong dossier library at once is a decade-long, capital-heavy undertaking.

Take each lever in turn. Capacity is the least glamorous but the hardest to fake: roughly 49.23 billion units of annual capacity across around ten facilities (as at the FY24 IPO) means Akums can absorb a big client's whole order and a hundred small ones without turning work away. Breadth is the differentiator most rivals lack — a brand that can hand its tablets, its syrup, its ointment and its injectable to one partner saves enormous coordination cost, and Akums' 60-plus dosage forms make it that partner. And the dossier library is the quiet compounder: over 4,100 commercial formulations already developed means a client often does not need anything invented — it picks a proven product off the shelf, brands it, and launches. Every dossier added widens the menu and lengthens the lead.

The one construction that fits only Akums: value = capacity × breadth × library

Most industrial moats are a single wall — a low-cost plant, a patent, a brand. Akums' is a volume, in the geometric sense: units of capacity, multiplied by the number of dosage formats, multiplied by the depth of the formulation-and-dossier shelf. A competitor that matches only capacity is a job shop; one that matches only breadth but lacks scale can't take the big orders; one with a good library but few formats can't be the one-stop partner. Akums is the rare Indian player pushing all three dials at once — which is why "largest CDMO" is not a vanity label but the actual mechanism of defence. Shrink any one factor and the product — the reason a brand stays — shrinks with it.

03

The four businesses under one name

CDMO is the engine — but three other arms decide how much of the value Akums actually keeps.

It is tempting to read Akums as "a CDMO," full stop. It is really four businesses stacked on a shared manufacturing base, and they differ sharply in how much margin flows to Akums versus its clients. Understanding the stack is understanding the investment case, because the cheap-but-thin part and the richer-but-smaller parts pull the group margin in opposite directions.

The four arms

  • CDMO (contract development & manufacturing) — the flagship, roughly three-quarters of revenue (~78% in FY25). Akums makes finished medicine for other brands and earns a conversion-plus-development fee. High volume, sticky, but a thin spread because the client owns the brand and the pricing. Its adjusted EBITDA margin (~14% in FY25) is actually the best of the group — proof that scale, not just ownership, can pay.
  • Branded & generic formulations (own label) — medicines Akums sells under its own brands, both in India and internationally. Here Akums keeps the brand margin, not just the making fee, so each rupee is worth more; the domestic own-brand book grew ~9% and the international book ~14% in FY25. Smaller than CDMO, but strategically the way Akums climbs the value chain.
  • Active Pharmaceutical Ingredients (API) — Akums makes the raw drug molecules that feed its formulations, and sells some externally. Owning APIs secures supply and can lift margin over a cycle, but it is a tougher, more commoditised, capital-heavy business that has at times lost money and dragged the group. Turning the API arm toward break-even is a stated priority.
  • Trade generics & other — unbranded generic medicine sold into the trade channel. Historically loss-making, it was deliberately downsized and turned EBITDA-positive in FY25 — a small but telling example of management pruning value-destroying volume rather than chasing top line.

The tension that runs the P&L: thin-and-huge versus thick-and-small

The four arms are in a quiet tug-of-war over the group margin. CDMO is enormous but thin — it sets the top line and, because of its scale, still delivers the group's best segment margin. Own-brand formulations are richer per rupee but far smaller, so they lift quality-of-earnings only slowly. API and trade generics have historically been a drag, not a lift — which is why the FY25–26 story is less about CDMO heroics and more about Akums stemming losses below the flagship. The single most useful way to read the company is: is the mighty CDMO engine holding its margin and are the three smaller arms moving from drag toward contribution? When both happen, the thin base and the thick edges start compounding together.

04

The company, the Jains, and the 2024 listing

A two-decade family build from a single plant to the country's largest formulations CDMO.

Akums is not a legacy conglomerate spin-off; it is a from-scratch family enterprise that grew, over roughly two decades, into the scale it has today. It was founded and is run by the Jain family — brothers Sanjeev and Sandeep Jain — who still control the company through the promoter vehicle after a 2024 public listing. Knowing that lineage explains both the operational focus of the business and the governance questions a new public shareholder should keep in view.

  • Founder-operators still at the helm: Sanjeev Jain has led the business since inception in 2004 — a two-decade operating tenure. This is a company run by the people who built it, which cuts both ways: deep operating knowledge, but also the concentration and related-party watchpoints typical of a founder-controlled firm.

But founder-controlled pharma companies carry the usual minority-shareholder homework: watch related-party dealings, capital allocation into the loss-making arms, and whether the interests of the trust and the public float stay aligned as Akums pushes into its own brands and exports.
05

The surprise: your medicines were probably made here

The single fact that reframes the company for anyone seeing it for the first time.

Here is the thing that startles almost everyone who meets Akums for the first time. The branded medicines Indians pull off a chemist's shelf — from companies whose names everyone knows — were, in a great many cases, physically made in an Akums plant, not the brand-owner's. In FY24 Akums manufactured for 26 of the 30 largest pharmaceutical companies in India. The strip says one company; the tablet inside was very often made by another — this one.

That is not a quirk; it is the business model working exactly as designed. A CDMO succeeds precisely by being invisible: the whole value to a brand is that the medicine reaches the market in its pack, with its name, indistinguishable from something it made itself. So a company you have never heard of turns out to sit behind a large slice of a market you use constantly. Akums' ~30% share of India's domestic CDMO market means that, statistically, a meaningful share of the outsourced-manufactured medicine in the country passed through its lines.

Sit with the counter-intuition

The most valuable pharma-manufacturing footprint in India belongs to a company whose name will never appear on a single strip it makes. That is genuinely strange — brand recognition is usually where value lives — and it flips the normal logic of the sector. Akums has, in effect, chosen to be the utility behind the industry rather than a name in front of it. The upside is enormous, sticky, industry-wide demand. The vulnerability is the flip side of the same coin: because the brand loyalty belongs to the client, not to Akums, its position rests entirely on being the most capable, reliable, broad supplier — never on a consumer who insists on it. It is powerful and anonymous at the same time, and both halves are the point.

06

The margin ladder: why a huge business earns a thin slice

The most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a formulations CDMO.

People instinctively judge a manufacturer by its revenue. For a CDMO that is the wrong lens, because most of the money that flows across Akums' books belongs, economically, to its clients. What Akums actually keeps is a conversion spread — the sliver between what a client pays for finished medicine and what it costs Akums to develop, make and pack it. Read the company through that spread, and through the ladder of businesses that widen it, and the whole thing clicks into focus.

Now climb the ladder that widens that slice. Plain trade generics sit at the bottom — near-commodity, historically loss-making, deliberately shrunk. CDMO sits above: thin per unit, but at Akums' scale it is the group's best-margin segment (~14% adjusted EBITDA). Higher still is own-brand formulation, where Akums keeps the brand margin the CDMO client normally takes. And regulated-market exports — the €200 mn European contract being the flagship — sit at the top, where quality barriers keep pricing firmer. The strategy is not to abandon the thin base but to tilt the mix up the ladder over time.

The number that settles the thesis: CDMO utilisation × segment margin × mix, read against concentration

Akums does not live or die on revenue. It lives on how full its plants run, at what CDMO margin, with how much of the mix climbing toward own-brand and regulated export — measured against how dependent it is on a handful of big clients. That composite is the scoreboard. High utilisation keeps unit costs down and the ~14% CDMO margin intact; a richer mix lifts the group above that thin base; and a broadening client roster reduces the risk that any one departure dents the whole. If utilisation and CDMO margin hold while own-brand and export creep up and no single client is too large to lose, the low group margin is a floor that rises. If a big client in-sources, utilisation slips, and the margin ladder stalls at the bottom rung, the thin base is simply thin. Plants full, margin held, mix climbing, no client too big — that is the four-part gauge to watch every quarter.

07

The IPO and the puzzle of the ₹1 crore year

Why a company earning hundreds of crores of operating profit once reported almost none — and what it teaches.

Anyone glancing at Akums' record hits an oddity that looks alarming until it is explained: in FY24, on revenue of about ₹4,178 Cr, the company reported net profit of roughly ₹1 crore — essentially nothing — and then in FY25 reported ~₹344 Cr. Did the business collapse and rebound? No. The FY24 near-zero was driven overwhelmingly by a large non-cash accounting charge tied to pre-IPO financial instruments, not by the operating business, which was profitable throughout. It is a textbook lesson in reading reported profit around an IPO with care.

Before a company lists, it often issues instruments to investors — preference shares or convertibles — that accounting rules require to be fair-valued through the profit and loss each year. As the equity value rises ahead of a hot IPO, the accounting loss on those instruments can balloon, crushing reported net profit even while the factories hum and cash comes in. Once the instruments convert at listing, the charge disappears and reported profit snaps back to reflect the underlying business. That, in essence, is the FY24-to-FY25 jump — an accounting artefact of listing, not an operating swing.

The reading discipline this forces on you

The FY24 figure is a warning label on reported profit around any IPO: a single accounting line can make a healthy company look broken, or the reverse. The honest way to judge Akums across its listing is on adjusted and cash-based earnings — on an adjusted basis, profit was broadly stable through FY24 and FY25 (in the region of ₹220–234 Cr), which is the real operating trajectory the reported ₹1 Cr obscures. It also flags a subtler point for a new public shareholder: the equity-value gymnastics that produced the charge were features of a pre-IPO capital structure, now resolved — but they are a reminder to read the notes, not just the number in bold.

The IPO context that colours everything since: Akums listed in August 2024 to real enthusiasm, then spent much of the following two years trading below its issue price as the market digested customer concentration, thin contract economics and this very accounting noise. The company's operating story kept improving; the share price lagged. That gap — improving business, sceptical tape — is the backdrop against which the current valuation debate (section 10) plays out.
08

Why domestic-formulations CDMO is its own animal

Akums is not the CDMO you may be picturing — and the difference is the whole risk-and-reward shape.

"CDMO" has become an investor buzzword, usually attached to companies making APIs or complex intermediates for global innovators — the export-led, patent-cliff, regulated-market story. Akums is a different creature: primarily a domestic finished-formulations CDMO, serving Indian brands for the Indian market. The distinction is not pedantic; it shapes margins, competition, regulatory exposure and growth optionality.

This is why Akums' €200 mn European supply contract, with deliveries slated from 2027, matters out of proportion to its size. It is the company stepping off the purely-domestic rung onto the regulated-export ladder — where quality barriers are higher, pricing is firmer, and a successful track record can compound into more contracts. It is also where the regulatory stakes rise: winning European and, eventually, US business means submitting to the tougher inspection regimes that can lift a CDMO's rating or, if an inspection goes badly, sink it.

Why the category label changes the whole risk profile

Get this distinction wrong and you will misjudge the stock in both directions. Read Akums as a high-margin global API CDMO and you will overpay, expecting margins it does not structurally earn. Read it as only a low-margin domestic job shop and you will miss the option it is buying — that a proven, giant, broad Indian formulations base can be levered into regulated-market exports and its own brands, where the economics are richer. Akums is the domestic scale leader reaching for the export-CDMO rung. The domestic base is the reliable, thin, sticky bulk; the export and own-brand push is the higher-margin call it is placing on its own capability. Judge each on its own terms.

09

The risk stack: concentration, quality, and the in-sourcing question

A clear-eyed catalogue of what can go wrong for a factory-for-hire.

Every feature that makes Akums powerful has a shadow. A factory-for-hire's risks are specific and worth naming plainly, because they are exactly what has kept the market cautious since the IPO. Five sit at the top of the stack.

The one risk that defines the category

Strip the list down and the defining risk is #1: your best customers are the ones most able to replace you. A small brand will never build a plant; a top-five pharma company can, and some do bring select volumes in-house. Akums' entire defence against this is to be so broad, so reliable and so cost-efficient at scale that in-sourcing rarely pencils out — that it is cheaper and safer for even a large client to keep handing Akums the work. That defence is real, and the 26-of-top-30 roster suggests it holds. But it must be re-earned every year, and it is why plant utilisation and client breadth are not soft metrics here — they are the health of the whole model.

10

Financials & valuation

A steady operating business on a demanding multiple — the debate is durability of the margin story, not this year's number.

Akums' financials tell a consistent story once the FY24 accounting noise is set aside: revenue in the low-₹4,000-crore range, an operating margin near 12%, a near-debt-free balance sheet, and returns on capital in the mid-teens. What makes the stock a debate is not the operating record but the price the market puts on it — a multiple far richer than the thin group margin would suggest, resting on the belief that the CDMO engine and the mix-climb keep compounding.

So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the CDMO segment margin (that ~14%) sustained on rising plant utilisation, while own-brand and regulated-export mix climbs and no single client grows too large to lose. If that composite holds and improves, earnings grow into the multiple and the thin base becomes a rising floor. If CDMO margin slips, a big client in-sources, or the export/own-brand climb stalls, a rich multiple on a thin margin is an uncomfortable place to be. The scoreboard is not revenue growth — it is margin durability times mix-climb, watched against concentration, quarter by quarter.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

The scale leader of a structurally growing outsourcing market, asked whether it can climb the value ladder faster than its multiple.

Akums enters its next chapter as the clear domestic leader of a business that should structurally grow — Indian pharma keeps outsourcing manufacturing, and Akums is the broadest partner to hand it to. Its self-help levers are real: stem the API and trade-generics losses, tilt mix toward own-brand and regulated exports, convert the European foothold into a track record. What it cannot escape is that a factory-for-hire's fortunes rest on customers it does not own, on a margin with little cushion, and on a clean inspection record. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Structural outsourcing tailwind — Indian brands keep handing manufacturing to CDMOs, and Akums is the largest, broadest partner to receive it; the market grows and the leader gains share.
  • A moat that is genuinely hard to copy — capacity × 60-plus dosage forms × a 4,100-strong formulation library; 26 of the top 30 as clients is not easily replicated.
  • Mix-climb optionality — every rupee shifted toward own-brand and regulated exports (the €200 mn Europe deal) is a richer rupee; the ladder is the earnings-quality lever.
  • Self-help on the drags — trade generics already turned EBITDA-positive; getting API to break-even is pure operating leverage on the group.
  • Clean balance sheet — near debt-free, mid-teens ROCE, founder-operators who built and know every plant.

The bear case

  • Concentration & in-sourcing — the best clients are the ones most able to build their own plants; a large departure hits utilisation and margin directly.
  • Thin, cushion-less margins — a low-teens spread leaves little room to absorb input-cost spikes or pricing pressure; scale is the only shield.
  • Regulatory tail-risk — an adverse USFDA/CDSCO inspection at a key plant could halt output and de-rate the stock overnight.
  • API drag & input costs — a loss-making API arm and raw-material swings can weigh on the group until break-even is reached.
  • Rich multiple, founder-control — ~40× earnings on a ~12% margin leaves no comfort if the mix-climb stalls; watch related-party and capital-allocation discipline.
The bottom line

Akums is the anonymous backbone of India's medicine cabinet — the country's largest formulations CDMO, powerful precisely because no patient sees its name, and vulnerable for the same reason. The business is real, clean and structurally advantaged; the operating record, once the FY24 accounting noise is stripped out, is steady and improving. But the market already pays a full price for the story, and the story only works if the composite gauge keeps trending the right way. The next concrete test is datable: whether CDMO segment margin holds near ~14% on rising utilisation through FY27 while own-brand and the first European deliveries (from 2027) lift the mix — and whether the client roster keeps broadening rather than concentrating. Deliver that, and a thin margin becomes a rising floor beneath a widening moat. Miss it, against this multiple, and the market will remember that a factory-for-hire is only ever as strong as the customers who choose to keep hiring it.
