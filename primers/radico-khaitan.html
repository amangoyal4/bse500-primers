00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Radico Khaitan makes and sells spirits — whisky, vodka, brandy, rum and gin — under the Indian regulatory category called IMFL (Indian-Made Foreign Liquor). It began life in 1943 as the Rampur Distillery, a maker of bulk industrial-grade alcohol, and over three decades transformed itself into a branded consumer-goods company that owns some of India's best-known liquor labels. It is one of the country's four largest domestic spirits makers, with roughly 7% of the IMFL market, behind the two foreign-owned giants (Diageo's United Spirits and Pernod Ricard).

Three facts frame everything:

  • A branded-liquor major that owns its shelf. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) Radico sold 36.6 million cases of its own IMFL brands, generating net revenue of ₹6,050 Cr (up ~25%), EBITDA of ~₹1,018 Cr (up ~52%) and net profit of ~₹604 Cr (up ~75%). Its portfolio spans mass-market whisky (8PM) to a single malt (Rampur) that has sold at ₹5 lakh a bottle.
  • Premiumisation is the whole story. Radico's "Prestige & Above" (P&A) segment — its higher-priced, higher-margin brands — reached 45.6% of own volumes but 70.3% of IMFL revenue in FY26. As the mix keeps shifting up-market, realisation and margins rise faster than volume. That mix-shift is exactly what has driven the stock's re-rating to a rich ~85× earnings.
  • It operates in one of India's hardest regulated industries. Alcohol in India is a state subject: every state sets its own excise, pricing, distribution and licensing — some run a full retail monopoly, a few ban liquor outright, and direct advertising is prohibited. This makes alcobev a fragmented, licence-heavy, working-capital-intensive business — and a moat for the incumbents who have learned to navigate it.
In plain terms

Think of Radico as a company that spent 30 years climbing a ladder. At the bottom rung it was a commodity alcohol supplier — it distilled raw spirit and sold it in bulk to other liquor makers, earning thin, cyclical margins. It then started bottling its own brands, and over the last two decades pushed relentlessly up the value ladder: from cheap whisky, to a category-defining vodka (Magic Moments), to a luxury single malt (Rampur) that competes with Scotch. Every rung up the ladder, the same bottle of alcohol earns dramatically more. The entire investment case is a bet that Radico keeps climbing — and that the tangle of Indian liquor laws keeps out anyone who might crowd the ladder.

01

Alcobev 101: how the IMFL business actually works

No industry background needed — just the handful of terms that drive everything.

A spirits company is, at heart, a branded-fast-moving-consumer-goods (FMCG) business wrapped in a heavy layer of regulation. It buys agricultural raw material, distils it into alcohol, blends and bottles it into branded drinks, and sells them through a tightly controlled state distribution system. A few terms unlock the whole thing.

The vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters)
IMFL"Indian-Made Foreign Liquor" — the regulatory bucket for Western-style spirits (whisky, vodka, rum, brandy, gin) distilled in India. It excludes beer, wine and "country liquor". This is Radico's core market.
ENA"Extra Neutral Alcohol" — the pure, near-flavourless base spirit (from molasses or grain) that virtually all IMFL is built from. It is Radico's biggest raw material and a product it makes and sells itself. Its price is the industry's key input cost.
A caseThe industry's unit of volume — 9 litres of packaged liquor (e.g. 12 × 750ml). Radico's volumes are quoted in "million cases". FY26: 36.6m own cases.
Prestige & Above (P&A)The higher price bands — roughly ₹500+ a bottle and up. The opposite of "Regular"/mass. The share of volume that is P&A is the single most-watched number in Indian alcobev.
Gross vs net revenueBecause state excise duty is enormous, the "gross" price a consumer pays is a multiple of what the company keeps. Companies report net revenue (after excise) — the number that actually reflects the business.
Own brands vs royalty vs bottling — three different ways to earn

Not all "sales" are equal. A spirits company can earn in three very different ways. (1) Own brands — it makes and sells its own label (e.g. Magic Moments); it keeps the full margin but bears all the brand-building cost and risk. (2) Royalty / franchise — it lets another company make and sell under one of its brands in a state where it lacks a distillery, taking a fee per case; low effort, thin economics. (3) Bottling / contract — it distils or bottles for someone else (its old bulk-supplier roots), earning a commodity margin. Radico's whole strategic journey has been shifting weight from (3) toward (1) — from being a spirit supplier to owning the brand on the shelf. That is where the value is.

The simple economics of a bottle

  • Volume × realisation. Revenue is cases sold multiplied by the net price kept per case. Radico grows both — selling more cases and selling a richer mix that keeps more per case.
  • Fixed brand + distribution costs. A big chunk of cost (advertising within the legal limits, sales infrastructure, brand teams) is broadly fixed, so as premium volumes scale, margins expand — classic operating leverage.
  • ENA is the swing cost. The price of the base alcohol (grain or molasses) is the biggest variable input; when it falls, margins jump, and vice-versa. Owning your own ENA capacity (as Radico increasingly does) tames this swing.
02

The business model: the value ladder

One picture explains Radico's entire strategy — climbing the price ladder while owning more of the supply chain.

Radico's model rests on two moves that reinforce each other: trading up the price ladder (regular → prestige → luxury), and integrating backward into its own alcohol (making the ENA and malt it used to buy). The first lifts what each bottle earns; the second protects the margin on that bottle from the input-cost cycle.

The price ladder — where the value lives

Radico organises its portfolio into value tiers. Each tier up carries a higher price, a fatter margin, and a stickier, more brand-loyal customer. The strategic goal is simple: sell proportionally more of the drinks near the top.

The further up the ladder, the higher the realisation and the harder a rival is to copy — a single malt takes years of ageing and reputation. The backbone (own alcohol) is what lets Radico protect margins while it climbs.
Why backward integration matters so much here

ENA — the base alcohol — is Radico's single biggest raw material. When grain and molasses prices spike, an alcobev company that buys its ENA on the open market gets squeezed. Radico has spent heavily to make its own ENA and malt, most recently at its Sitapur (UP) grain distillery, which management says gives it a margin advantage of roughly ₹6–9 per litre versus buying outside. For a premium portfolio that needs consistent, high-quality grain spirit, owning the source does two things: it stabilises the margin through the input-cost cycle, and it guarantees supply and quality for the luxury brands that can't afford a bad batch. This is the difference between a branded marketer and a genuinely integrated spirits maker.

Two engines: own brands and "non-IMFL"

  • Branded IMFL (the core) — the consumer business: making and selling Radico's own labels. This is where premiumisation plays out and where nearly all the profit growth comes from.
  • Non-IMFL / bulk (the legacy backbone) — selling surplus ENA, country liquor and by-products. Lower-margin and more commodity-like, but it keeps distilleries fully utilised and generates cash. Management guides this to grow a modest ~7–8% a year — steady ballast, not the growth story.
03

The company and who controls it

From a loss-making bulk distillery bought for ₹16 lakh to a ₹52,000-crore branded major.

Radico is a family-promoted business run by the Khaitan family — Chairman Dr. Lalit Khaitan and his son, Managing Director Abhishek Khaitan, the architect of the premiumisation strategy who was inducted into the global "Whisky Hall of Fame" for the Rampur single malt. The story is a genuine transformation: a commodity distillery reinvented, over three decades, into a consumer-brands company.

  • 2011–2018

Ownership & balance sheet

  • Promoters: the Khaitan family holds ~40.2% (Mar 2026). Founder-led alignment, but a moderate stake by Indian-promoter standards — the float is large and institutionally owned.
  • Institutions: heavy institutional backing — FIIs ~22.8% and DIIs ~18.4% (Apr 2026) — reflecting the market's appetite for the premiumisation theme.
  • Balance sheet: Radico carries modest borrowings (~₹498 Cr at FY26 per Screener) after a heavy capex cycle, but is guiding toward a debt-free position in FY27 as cash flows from the premium mix ramp up. It earns a healthy ROCE of ~24% and ROE of ~20%.

Why a brand stable beats a single hit

Owning a spread of brands across every category and price tier is a structural advantage in Indian alcobev. First, it lets Radico place a brand in every state's price slabs — a state that taxes premium whisky punitively might still be open to a mass brandy, so the portfolio always has a door in. Second, it creates trade-up ladders within the company: a consumer who starts on 8PM can be moved to 8PM Black, then to a single malt, all inside Radico. Third, the luxury brands (Rampur, Jaisalmer) act as a halo that lifts the perceived quality of the whole stable — the same reason a carmaker races a supercar. The risk mirror-image: managing dozens of SKUs across 30+ state regimes is operationally brutal, and a few brands (like some ageing regular labels) can quietly decline while the premium ones sprint.

05

Premiumisation — the defining investment thesis

This is the one idea to internalise: it explains Radico's margins, its growth, and its valuation.

If you remember one thing about Radico — or about the entire Indian spirits sector — make it this: the same litre of alcohol earns radically more as you move it up-market. India is a young, urbanising, rising-income country where consumers are trading up from cheap whisky to branded premium and luxury spirits. This "premiumisation" mix-shift is the structural engine of the sector, and Radico is one of its purest plays.

The mechanism — why mix-shift beats volume growth

Selling more cases grows revenue in a straight line. Selling a richer mix of cases grows revenue and margin at the same time — because premium bottles carry both a higher price and a higher gross margin, while much of the cost base is fixed. That's why analysts obsess over the P&A share: it is the leading indicator of both realisation and profitability.

The bull case in one mechanism

Because premium and luxury spirits carry structurally higher margins, every point of P&A mix gain lifts the blended margin — without needing a single extra case sold. Stack that on top of volume growth and own-ENA cost control, and profit compounds much faster than revenue. Radico's own FY27 guidance spells the strategy out: it is targeting ~20% P&A volume growth, ~25% luxury growth, ~125 bps of EBITDA-margin expansion, and only 3–5% growth in the mass "Regular" segment. In plain English: grow the profitable end fast, let the low-margin end drift — and the mix does the heavy lifting. That is the compounding the market is paying ~85× earnings for.

The catch — and the bear's counter — is that premiumisation is the entire sector's playbook now. Diageo's United Spirits sold off 32 mass brands in 2022 to focus almost wholly on P&A; Pernod Ricard is premium-heavy by design; Allied Blenders is trading up too. So Radico is competing for the same premium consumer against deep-pocketed global marketers. The thesis isn't "premiumisation happens" — it's "Radico wins its share of premiumisation faster than rivals, and defends the margin".

06

The Rampur & luxury story — the halo at the top

Why a small single-malt business matters far more than its revenue suggests.

At the very top of Radico's ladder sits Rampur Indian Single Malt — a whisky distilled and aged at the original Rampur distillery in the foothills of the Himalayas, now sold across the US and 100+ countries, with limited editions retailing near ₹5 lakh a bottle. It is small in revenue but strategically outsized, and it is the clearest signal of Radico's ambition to be taken seriously as a global premium spirits maker, not just an Indian volume producer.

Why "luxury" earns its place on the balance sheet

  • Margin. A single malt or craft gin sells at multiples of a mass whisky's price for a similar litre of base cost — the richest margins in the portfolio.
  • Halo. A world-recognised Indian single malt (and awards for Jaisalmer gin) lifts the perceived quality of every Radico brand — the classic luxury flywheel that makes the mid-premium brands more credible.
  • Exports & hard currency. Luxury spirits travel — Rampur's US and international distribution earns foreign revenue outside the Indian state-excise maze, a rare escape from domestic regulation.
  • Structural scarcity. Single malt requires years of ageing in barrels; a rival can't simply decide to launch one next quarter. That time-moat is why Radico's early malt-maturation investment matters.
The India single-malt moment

For decades, "premium whisky" in India meant imported Scotch. In the last few years Indian single malts — led by Amrut, Paul John and Radico's Rampur — have won global awards and a genuinely aspirational following, both at home and abroad. This is a structural opportunity: rising Indian affluence plus national pride in a home-grown luxury product. Radico's edge is that it controls the whole chain — it grows the demand with its brand, distils and ages the malt at its own distilleries, and increasingly secures its own grain-spirit and malt supply (Sitapur). The bet is that Indian single malt becomes a durable global category, and Rampur is a top-three name in it. The risk: luxury is a long game, capital sits in barrels for years before it sells, and the volumes are still tiny relative to the whisky mass market.

The luxury portfolio is small in cases and large in meaning — it is Radico announcing that it intends to be measured against Scotch, not just against the shelf below it.
07

The regulatory maze — why alcobev is a hard business

The single biggest thing that makes Indian liquor different from ordinary FMCG.

You cannot understand a company like Radico without understanding that alcohol in India is governed state-by-state, not nationally. The Constitution puts liquor on the State List, and simultaneously (Article 47) directs states to work toward prohibition. The result is a patchwork of 30+ different regimes, punishing taxes, advertising bans, and — in some states — the government literally being the only legal seller. This is the industry's defining structural feature, and it cuts both ways.

The regulatory obstacle course
FeatureWhat it means for a company like Radico
State excise & pricingEach state sets its own excise duty and often controls the retail price. A company can't freely pass on cost increases; it must negotiate price hikes state-by-state, sometimes waiting years. Alcohol sits outside GST, so there's no uniform national tax.
State monopoliesSeveral states run distribution/retail themselves — e.g. Tamil Nadu's TASMAC is the sole wholesaler/retailer. In those markets the state is your only customer, dictating which brands get listed and at what margin.
Prohibition statesBihar, Gujarat, Nagaland, Mizoram ban liquor outright — entire large markets simply closed. Policy can flip with an election, adding or removing markets overnight.
Advertising banDirect advertising of alcohol is prohibited. Brands rely on surrogate advertising (promoting same-name water, soda, music, glassware) — and regulators (ASCI, CCPA) are actively tightening even that. Building a brand is far harder than in normal FMCG.
Licensing & working capitalManufacturing, warehousing and selling each need state licences; payments from state corporations can be slow, tying up working capital. It is a licence-heavy, cash-hungry business.
Why the maze is also a moat

Every feature above is a headache — but collectively they are a barrier to entry that protects the incumbents. A new brand can't just buy national TV ads and blitz the country; it must painstakingly win listings, licences and price approvals in state after state, with one hand tied behind its back on marketing. That is exactly why the Indian spirits market is dominated by a handful of players who have spent decades building state-level relationships and distribution — Radico among them. The flip side, and the permanent risk, is that the same states that protect you can hurt you at will: a sudden excise hike (Maharashtra raised duties sharply in 2025), a pricing freeze, a retail-model overhaul (Karnataka's 2026 pricing shake-up), or a prohibition drive can dent a whole state's economics with no warning.

The recurring policy risk to watch: because states depend on liquor for a huge slice of their tax revenue, they lean on the industry when budgets are tight — raising excise faster than companies can raise prices squeezes margins. Radico's premiumisation helps here (premium consumers absorb price hikes better than mass consumers), but state-excise volatility is a permanent feature of the business, not a passing risk.
08

Raw materials, the input cycle & the capex build

The other half of margins — what goes into the bottle, and who makes it.

A premium mix lifts the top line, but margins also depend on input costs. Radico's biggest raw materials are ENA (from grain or molasses), glass bottles, and packaging, and their prices move on their own cycles — grain on the monsoon and crop, molasses on the sugar cycle, glass on energy. Managing this input cycle, and increasingly owning the inputs, is the second lever of the margin story.

What drives Radico's cost of goods
InputWhy it matters & what moves it
Grain ENAThe base for premium/grain spirits (vodka, premium whisky, malt). Priced off broken rice / maize — moves with the crop and the monsoon. Now increasingly made in-house at Sitapur.
Molasses ENAThe cheaper base for mass spirits — priced off the sugar cycle and government ethanol-blending policy (which competes for the same molasses).
Glass & packagingA meaningful, energy-linked cost. Premium bottles/packaging cost more but are a smaller share of a premium bottle's higher price.

The Sitapur bet — why own the still?

Historically Radico bought much of its grain ENA on the open market, exposing its margins to every grain-price spike. Its big recent capex — the Sitapur (UP) grain distillery, plus expanded malt maturation — is a deliberate move to make that spirit itself. Management estimates captive production gives a margin advantage of ₹6–9 per litre versus outside sourcing, and it secures consistent, high-quality supply for exactly the premium brands that are driving growth. This capex is the reason Radico carries some debt today — but with the premium mix now throwing off strong cash, the company is guiding toward a debt-free balance sheet in FY27. In effect, Radico spent heavily to convert an uncontrolled variable cost (bought ENA) into a controlled asset (its own distillery), trading near-term leverage for durable, cycle-proof margins.

The industry and the rivals

A large, growing, premiumising market — dominated by two foreign giants Radico is chasing.

India is one of the world's largest spirits markets by volume and among the fastest-growing by value, thanks to premiumisation. But it is an oligopoly: two foreign-owned giants control roughly two-fifths of it between them, with a cluster of Indian players — Radico foremost among them — competing for share. Note: Radico is a spirits company; the beer market (United Breweries/Heineken) is a separate business it does not play in.

The strategic question

Radico's opportunity and its challenge are the same fact: the premium and luxury whisky segment — the fastest-growing, highest-margin part of the market — is dominated by Diageo and Pernod. Radico has won decisively in vodka and brandy and has a credible luxury single malt, but the biggest prize (premium Indian whisky) is where the giants are strongest and where their marketing budgets, Scotch access and distribution are hardest to match. The bull case is that Radico keeps out-executing on the premium trade-up with sharper local brands and better cost control; the bear case is that as everyone chases the same premium consumer, competition intensifies, marketing spend rises, and Radico's share gains slow.

How to think about the ~85× multiple

At ~₹52,000 Cr market cap on ~₹604 Cr of FY26 profit, Radico trades around 85× trailing earnings — a steep multiple even by premium-FMCG standards, and richer than most global spirits peers. That price makes sense only if you believe the premiumisation engine keeps compounding profit at 20%+ for years, margins keep expanding toward management's targets, and the balance sheet turns net-cash. Bulls argue a structurally growing, category-leading, self-integrating Indian spirits champion with a genuine luxury franchise deserves a scarcity premium. Bears argue the multiple already prices in near-flawless execution, leaving no room for a bad state-excise cycle, a grain-cost spike, or a premium-competition squeeze — and an 85× stock can de-rate hard on any wobble. Both can be right at different points; the numbers to track each quarter are P&A/luxury growth and EBITDA margin.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely transformed business — priced for the transformation to continue.

Radico has done the hard thing: it turned a commodity distillery into a premium consumer-brands company, and FY26 showed the financial payoff arriving in force. The business-quality debate is largely settled; the open questions are valuation and whether the premium engine keeps its pace against the giants.

The bull case

  • Premiumisation runway — young, urbanising, richer consumers still trading up; P&A already 70% of IMFL revenue and guided to keep growing ~20%.
  • Category leadership — #1 vodka (Magic Moments), leading brandy (Morpheus), a credible luxury single malt (Rampur) rivals can't quickly copy.
  • Margin expansion — mix-shift plus captive ENA (₹6–9/L edge) driving EBITDA up faster than revenue; ~125 bps guided for FY27.
  • Deleveraging — heavy capex behind it; premium cash flow taking the balance sheet toward debt-free in FY27.
  • Regulatory moat — the state-licence maze that torments new entrants protects the incumbents like Radico.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~85× earnings prices in years of flawless compounding; little margin for error.
  • Premium competition — Diageo and Pernod own the premium-whisky prize and have deeper pockets and Scotch access.
  • State-excise & policy risk — sudden excise hikes, price freezes, retail-model changes or prohibition drives can hit whole states overnight.
  • Input-cost cycle — grain/molasses/glass spikes can compress margins between annual state price revisions.
  • Advertising ban & regular-segment drag — brand-building is legally handicapped; some mass brands quietly decline while premium sprints.
The bottom line

Radico Khaitan is a high-quality, structurally advantaged Indian spirits champion in the middle of a genuine premiumisation-led re-rating — with category-leading brands, a luxury halo, integrated supply, and a self-funding, deleveraging balance sheet. The business risk is moderate and mostly external (state policy, input costs, giant competitors); the valuation risk is real and specific to the ~85× multiple. The story over the next few years will be written in a few numbers each quarter — P&A and luxury growth (is the mix still marching up?), EBITDA margin (is it expanding on plan?), and net debt (does it reach debt-free?). Deliver those and the compounding can justify the premium; stumble on any, and a stock priced for perfection has a long way to fall.
