Ola Electric Mobility, India's high-burn electric-scooter pioneer

A full walk through the company that put electric scooters on India's roads at scale — how a loss-making EV manufacturer actually works, why it sells direct to buyers with no dealers (and how that broke its service), the make-or-its-own-batteries "Gigafactory" bet the whole future rides on, and why a first-mover that once owned the market now fights to sell one scooter in twelve.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Ola Electric designs and manufactures electric two-wheelers — mainly the S1 range of electric scooters, and now the Roadster electric motorcycles — at its own giant plant near Krishnagiri, Tamil Nadu. It sells them direct to buyers (online and through its own experience centres, not through independent dealers), and it is trying to build the single most valuable part of an EV — the lithium-ion battery cell — in its own "Gigafactory". It was, for a while, India's clear leader in electric scooters. It is also, still, deeply loss-making.

Four facts frame everything that follows:

  • First-mover leader, now losing the lead. Ola went from roughly a third of every electric scooter sold in India in FY25 to under ~8% by June 2026, overtaken by TVS, Bajaj, Ather and Hero as they arrived in force. Volume roughly halved; revenue fell from ₹4,514 Cr (FY25) to ~₹2,245 Cr (FY26).
  • A cash-burning growth company. It has never made an annual profit. FY26 net loss was ~₹1,833 Cr (better than FY25's ₹2,276 Cr, but still huge); it burned ~₹775 Cr of operating cash in the year, and its auditors flagged the "going concern" assumption — accountant's language for "this depends on more funding arriving".
  • The one genuinely bright number: gross margin. Even as it shrank, Ola's gross margin rose to ~30.6% for FY26 and ~38.5% in the March quarter (from ~13.7% a year earlier). Per scooter, the economics got dramatically healthier — the problem is it is selling far fewer of them.
  • The whole future rides on a battery factory. Ola's biggest bet is making its own 4680 "Bharat Cell" instead of importing cells from China — the largest cost in any EV. If the Gigafactory works at scale, it becomes a structural margin and cost weapon. If it doesn't, it is an enormous drain of cash the company can't spare.
In plain terms

Picture a company that arrived first at a party everyone else eventually showed up to. Ola Electric proved Indians would buy electric scooters, built the biggest two-wheeler EV factory in the country, and grabbed the largest slice of a brand-new market. Then the established two-wheeler giants — the Bajajs and TVSs with decades of dealers, mechanics and trust — walked in, and buyers who'd been burned by Ola's patchy service drifted to them. So Ola is now doing two hard things at once: fixing a damaged brand while it is shrinking, and spending scarce cash to build its own battery cells — the move that could either rescue its economics or finish its runway. The stock, listed in 2024 at ₹76 and now around ₹40, is the market trying to price exactly that gamble.

01

The electric-scooter opportunity

No EV knowledge required — just why two-wheelers, and why India, and why now.

India buys more two-wheelers than almost anywhere on earth — well over 15 million scooters and motorcycles a year. They are how ordinary India moves: cheap, nimble, essential. That makes the two-wheeler the natural front line of India's shift to electric — and it is shifting fast. Electric two-wheelers ("e-2Ws") went from a rounding error to a mainstream choice in barely five years.

Two-wheelers are ~57% of all EVs India registers, and e-2W volumes are still growing ~50% a year — yet only ~7-8% of new two-wheelers are electric, so the runway is enormous. The category is winning; the fight is over who wins the category.

Why an electric scooter makes sense to a buyer

  • Running cost. Electricity to "fuel" a scooter costs a fraction of petrol — roughly the difference between ~20 paise and ~2 rupees per kilometre. For a daily commuter, that adds up fast.
  • Simplicity. No engine oil, no clutch, far fewer moving parts. Instant torque makes it easy and quick off the line in city traffic.
  • Government push. India actively subsidises the switch — first via FAME-II, then the PM E-Drive scheme, plus a Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) that pays manufacturers for making EVs and components locally. These subsidies lowered sticker prices and rewarded local production.
The catch buyers weigh

Against those pluses sit the classic EV anxieties: a higher upfront price than a petrol scooter, worry about how far a charge lasts ("range anxiety"), the life and replacement cost of the battery, and — crucially in India — whether the thing can be serviced quickly when it breaks. That last point is not a footnote. In a market where a two-wheeler is often a household's only vehicle, a scooter stuck waiting weeks for a spare part is a broken promise. Ola learned this the hard way; it is the hinge of section 05.

So the opportunity is real and large: a giant, price-sensitive, still-mostly-petrol two-wheeler market, tilting electric with government money behind it. The question every player faces is not "will India go electric" but "who will Indians buy their electric scooter from, and can that maker actually make money doing it".

02

How an EV maker actually earns — and why Ola loses money

The economics of building and selling a scooter, in plain arithmetic.

An EV manufacturer is, at heart, a factory business with a technology skin. It buys components, assembles vehicles, and sells them for more than they cost to build. The gap between the selling price and the direct cost of building each scooter is the gross margin — and understanding that single number explains both Ola's crisis and its flicker of hope.

The three layers of an EV maker's P&L
LayerWhat it is (and Ola's reality)
Gross marginSelling price minus the direct cost of the scooter (cells, motor, parts, factory labour). The battery cell alone is the single biggest cost. Ola's gross margin was ~30.6% in FY26 — a big improvement, and the crux of its self-help story.
Operating costsEverything above the factory floor: R&D, the sprawling store-and-service network, marketing, salaries, technology. For a young EV maker these are heavy and largely fixed — they don't shrink just because you sold fewer scooters.
Capital spendingCash sunk into building capacity — the vehicle factory, and above all the cell "Gigafactory". This doesn't hit the profit line directly, but it drains the bank account and is why cash burn is the number that can actually kill the company.
The loss-making-growth-company logic

Here is why a company can be worth billions while losing thousands of crores. A growth EV maker deliberately spends far more than it earns today — on factories, R&D, cells and a service network — betting that volume will eventually rise high enough that fixed costs get spread thin and each scooter turns profitable at scale. Investors are buying the future business, not the current one. That model works only if two things hold: the company keeps growing into its scale, and it doesn't run out of cash before it gets there. Ola's danger is that in FY26 the first stopped happening — volumes fell — while the cash kept draining. That is the whole reason a first-mover leader trades below its IPO price.

Ola's distinctive design choice: vertical integration

Most two-wheeler makers assemble vehicles from parts they buy in — importing battery cells (mostly from China), sourcing motors and electronics from suppliers. Ola's founding idea was the opposite: make as much of the vehicle in-house as possible — battery packs, motors, vehicle frames, and eventually the battery cells themselves. The logic is that whoever controls the cell controls the cost and the margin of the whole EV. It is an ambitious, capital-hungry, high-risk strategy — and it is what makes Ola genuinely different from every rival in section 08.

03

The company and who controls it

From cab-hailing to cell-making — one founder's very large bet.

Ola Electric is a founder-driven company, and the founder is a large part of the story. Bhavish Aggarwal — who co-founded the Ola ride-hailing app in 2010 — spun out an EV ambition in 2017, built it into a separate company, took it public in a high-profile 2024 IPO, and remains its chairman, managing director and central promoter. His personality, urgency and public combativeness are woven through the company's history — for better in the early sprint, and for worse in some of its recent controversies.

Ownership & control

  • Promoter: Bhavish Aggarwal and his entities hold roughly ~34-35% (as of Mar 2026), after he sold a small (~2.2%) slice in late 2025 to repay a promoter loan — a sale that landed while the stock was near record lows and drew criticism.
  • Early backers: the cap table has carried marquee names — SoftBank, Temasek, and others — from its venture years, plus public shareholders since the 2024 IPO. A ₹780 Cr QIP (a share sale to institutions) in June 2026, priced with a ~₹37.74 floor, added fresh capital but diluted existing holders.
  • Founder concentration is the point. Strategy, capital allocation and the bet-the-company cell factory all trace back to one person's conviction. That drove the early lead; it also means key-person and governance risk sit unusually high for a company this size.
A structural quirk worth naming: Ola Electric and the Ola ride-hailing app are separate companies under the same founder — a source of endless public confusion. The listed entity you can buy, OLAELEC, is the EV maker only. The cab business is private and unrelated to these results.
04

The product range

Scooters first, now motorcycles — and increasingly powered by Ola's own cells.

Ola's revenue is overwhelmingly the S1 family of electric scooters, sold across a wide price ladder to cover the mass market and the premium buyer. In 2025 it added electric motorcycles (Roadster) to widen the addressable market, and began putting its own 4680 Bharat Cell inside its top products.

Why the motorcycle matters

India buys far more motorcycles than scooters — bikes are the workhorse of small-town and rural India. Electrifying scooters, where Ola started, taps only part of the two-wheeler market; the Roadster is Ola's move into the larger half. It also lets Ola reuse its cells, motors and software across more products — spreading its heavy R&D over a bigger base. The risk is obvious: launching a new vehicle category, against entrenched motorcycle brands, while the core scooter business is shrinking and cash is tight, is a lot to carry at once.

One product truth underlies all of this: an electric two-wheeler is a software-and-battery product wearing a scooter's body. Ola leans hard into this — over-the-air updates, a big touchscreen, connected features — which is a genuine differentiator versus a legacy petrol maker, but also a source of the software glitches and reliability complaints that fed the service crisis.

05

Selling direct — and the service crisis it caused

The choice that made Ola distinctive is the same one that nearly broke its brand.

Almost every scooter in India is sold through independent dealers — local businesses that own the showroom, hold stock, register the vehicle, and, crucially, run the service workshop. Ola deliberately skipped all of that. It sold direct to consumers — order online or at an Ola-owned "experience centre", no third-party dealer in between. On paper this is elegant: Ola keeps the dealer's margin, owns the customer relationship, and controls the brand. In practice, it created the single biggest reputational wound in the company's history.

Why cutting out dealers backfired on service

A traditional dealer network isn't just a sales channel — it is a ready-made, nationwide repair-and-spares system, financed and staffed by thousands of local business owners. By going direct, Ola had to build that entire service-and-spares machine itself, from scratch, at the same time it was flooding the market with hundreds of thousands of new scooters. Sales outran service. Customers reported scooters sitting for weeks awaiting parts, unanswered complaints, disputed bills and closed-without-fixing tickets. What looked like a smart way to save the dealer's cut turned into a promise the company couldn't keep — and in two-wheelers, service is the product's second half.

How bad it got — and the regulator's role

  • A flood of complaints. India's Central Consumer Protection Authority (CCPA) acted after the national consumer helpline logged over 10,000 complaints about Ola's after-sales service in roughly a year — issues spanning overcharging, refunds, documentation and unresolved repairs.
  • Claims that didn't hold up. Ola publicly claimed it had resolved ~99% of complaints; the CCPA's own inquiry reportedly found the opposite for a large sample of complainants. Multiple show-cause notices followed through late 2024, and the Karnataka High Court directed Ola to comply with the probe.
  • A regulatory pile-on. Separately, SEBI (the markets regulator) issued an administrative warning over a founder social-media post, and — the strangest episode — a gap between claimed sales and government registrations (below) drew scrutiny from transport authorities.
The genuine surprise a generalist should take away: in February 2025 Ola announced it had sold ~25,000 scooters in the month — but the government's VAHAN registration database showed only ~8,600 registered. Ola explained the gap as a registration backlog tied to changing its registration vendors, and RTOs inspected showrooms (seizing some vehicles sold on trade certificates). Whatever the cause, it taught investors a lasting lesson: for this company, the number that counts is not what Ola says it sold — it's what the VAHAN portal says got registered. That external, un-spinnable scoreboard became the market's trusted gauge of Ola's real demand.

Ola's fix has been to overhaul service — more company-owned service centres, a shift toward a "Network Partner" model that brings in local partners for sales and service (quietly re-admitting some of the dealer logic it once rejected), better spares logistics, and heavy software effort to cut breakdowns at the source. Whether trust returns is a slow, quarter-by-quarter question — and trust, once lost in a mass-market product, is expensive to rebuild.

06

The cell factory — the bet the whole company rides on

The most valuable, most difficult part of an EV — and Ola is trying to make it itself.

Everything else in this primer is context for this section. The lithium-ion cell is the single most expensive component in an electric vehicle — commonly 30-40% of the entire cost — and India imports almost all of them, overwhelmingly from China. Ola's defining strategic gamble is to make its own cells at a "Gigafactory" in Krishnagiri, Tamil Nadu, rather than buy them. Get it right and Ola controls its own cost, margin and supply. Get it wrong and it has poured scarce cash into the hardest manufacturing problem in the industry.

No other Indian two-wheeler maker attempts to own Stage 1 (the cell). It is Ola's biggest differentiator — and its biggest execution risk.

Where the Gigafactory actually stands

  • It is real and running — at small scale. Ola has commissioned cell production of about 2.5 GWh, is scaling toward ~5-6 GWh, and targets 20 GWh by the second half of 2027. Thousands of vehicles carrying its own 4680 cells are already on the road — real-world proof, not just a lab claim.
  • A genuine first. Ola says it is the first Indian company to win BIS certification for an indigenously developed large-format LFP cell (the 46100/4680 formats) — a credible technical milestone in a field India has almost entirely outsourced.
  • Beyond scooters. Ola has opened the 4680 cell to other businesses and to home energy storage — an attempt to turn a captive cost centre into a product line of its own.
Why making cells is so hard — and so tempting

Cell manufacturing is a chemistry-and-precision business, not an assembly business. It demands enormous upfront capital, years to reach reliable high yields, punishing quality tolerances, and deep process know-how that the incumbents (in China, Korea, Japan) spent decades acquiring. Plenty of well-funded companies have burned cash learning it. So why try? Because if Ola can make good cells at scale cheaper than it can import them, it permanently lowers the cost of its single largest input — a margin advantage no rival buying cells on the open market can match, and one that also earns extra government PLI money for local production. The improvement in gross margin during FY26 is partly the early hint that vertical integration is starting to bite. The unresolved question is whether Ola can fund the ramp to 20 GWh from a shrinking, loss-making vehicle business — building the country's hardest factory on the country's tightest budget.

07

The market-share collapse

A first-mover that owned the category is now fighting to stay in the top five.

The most brutal chart in Ola's story is its monthly market share. At its peak Ola sold roughly one in three electric scooters in India. By mid-2026 it was closer to one in twelve. Nothing about the EV opportunity shrank — the e-2W market grew ~50% year on year — Ola simply lost the share it had, to rivals who arrived late but arrived strong.

That is the Ola scissors: the health of each scooter it sells has improved dramatically at the exact moment the number of scooters it sells has cratered. Ola in FY26 became a company that finally figured out how to make money per unit — right as it stopped selling enough units for that to matter. Every rival's turnaround is "sell more, then fix margins"; Ola's is the reverse, and stranger: it fixed the margins first and now has to win back the volume. Which blade of the scissors wins is, quite literally, the investment case.

Why did the share fall so fast?

Three forces compounded. (1) The service crisis poisoned word-of-mouth in a market where a neighbour's bad experience travels fast. (2) The giants arrived. Bajaj (Chetak) and TVS (iQube) — brands Indians have trusted for generations, with dealer and service networks already blanketing the country — went from dabbling to competing hard. (3) Ola pulled back on price and grew choosier, deliberately trading some volume for margin as subsidies changed and losses mounted. The first was a self-inflicted wound; the second was inevitable; the third was a conscious, and contested, choice.

08

The rivals

Late-arriving giants with dealers, mechanics and deep pockets.

When Ola started, its main rivals were startups and its own scale. Today its rivals are some of the most powerful two-wheeler companies on earth, plus a well-run EV-native peer. The competitive picture flipped from "Ola vs small players" to "Ola vs everyone who already knows how to sell and service two-wheelers in India".

The competitive truth Ola has to face

In a two-wheeler market, distribution and service are as decisive as the product. TVS, Bajaj and Hero spent decades building the one thing Ola tried to skip: a dense network of dealers and mechanics that buyers already trust. Ola's counter-weapon is technology and vertical integration — its own cells, its own software, a cost structure it controls end-to-end. So the contest is a genuine clash of models: the incumbents' distribution moat versus Ola's manufacturing moat. Ather is the wild card — the one rival playing roughly Ola's game (EV-native, tech-led) but with a cleaner brand and rising share, proving the startup model can work when service and trust hold.

Subsidy dependence cuts both ways: the whole category leans on government support — FAME, PM E-Drive, PLI. Changes to subsidy rules can reshape pricing overnight, and they hit everyone. But they matter more to a loss-making player like Ola, whose PLI earnings for local cell and vehicle production are a real prop under its improving margins — a dependency that is a tailwind today and a risk if the policy shifts.

How to think about a loss-maker's market cap

At roughly ₹18,800 Cr (share price ~₹40 in July 2026, versus the ₹76 IPO price and a brief post-listing high near ₹150), Ola's market value has no earnings to divide into — you can't compute a meaningful P/E on losses. So the market is pricing something else: the odds that Ola turns its improving unit economics and its cell factory into a profitable, decent-sized EV business before the cash forces another raise on bad terms. The bull sees a first-mover with a real manufacturing edge, a healed margin, and a shrinking burn — an EV franchise the market has written off too early. The bear sees a brand-damaged, share-losing company that must keep raising money at depressed prices, valued on a turnaround that hasn't shown up in the volume line. The valuation is not a multiple; it is a probability the market is arguing about every day.

The metric that settles it: not revenue, not even margin — those can improve while the company slowly disappears. The number that decides Ola's fate is monthly VAHAN market share: does the line stop falling and start climbing? Ola has already proven, via the gross-margin jump, that it can make each scooter profitable. The entire remaining question is whether it can sell enough of them. If monthly registrations stabilise and turn up while gross margin holds above ~30% and the cash burn keeps shrinking toward zero, the turnaround is real. If share keeps sliding, healthy per-unit economics on a vanishing unit count won't save it.
10

The path to profit

What actually has to go right, in order.

Ola's management case is that FY26 was the trough — the year it stopped chasing volume at any cost, fixed its per-unit economics, cut its burn, and set up for a healthier climb. There is real evidence for that (margins up, Q4 cash-positive, losses narrowing). But a path to profit needs several things to line up, and the order matters.

The turnaround, step by step
StepWhat has to happenStatus (FY26)
1. Stop the bleedingNarrow cash burn toward break-even so it isn't racing a shrinking bank balanceImproving — FY26 burn ~₹775 Cr (from ₹2,391 Cr); Q4 operating cash positive
2. Fix unit economicsGet gross margin high enough that scale can turn it into real profitLargely done — ~30.6% FY26, ~38.5% in Q4
3. Stabilise volumeHalt the market-share slide; win buyers back with better service and productNot yet — share still ~8% and below year-ago; the open question
4. Scale the cell factoryRamp the Gigafactory to 20 GWh and feed cheaper own-cells into vehiclesEarly — ~2.5 GWh live, 20 GWh targeted H2 2027
5. Fund it allRaise enough capital to reach break-even without ruinous dilutionPartial — ₹780 Cr QIP done; auditors still flag going-concern

Notice the sequence. Ola has made real progress on steps 1 and 2 — the financial repair. Steps 3, 4 and 5 — the demand, the factory and the funding — are where the story is undecided. The company has, in effect, proven it can build a profitable scooter; it has not yet proven it can sell that scooter in the numbers profit requires, nor that it can fund the cell ramp from a business still losing money.

Ola fixed the margin before it fixed the market — an unusual order that leaves the hardest, most external test for last: getting Indians to buy its scooters again.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A distressed first-mover with a real edge — priced as a coin-flip on its own turnaround.

Ola Electric is genuinely different from every other name in Indian mobility: an EV-native pioneer that tried to own the whole stack, from the battery cell to the customer. That ambition built an early lead and a real manufacturing edge — and also a cash-burning, brand-bruised, share-losing company whose survival still depends on execution and funding. The debate is not about whether India goes electric; it is about whether this company earns the right to lead it.

The bull case

  • Real manufacturing edge — the only Indian e-2W maker making its own cells; a cost lever no rival buying cells can match.
  • Margins have turned — gross margin ~30.6% (Q4 ~38.5%); each scooter is now profitable at the gross line.
  • Burn is shrinking fast — operating cash burn down two-thirds YoY; Q4 FY26 was operating-cash positive.
  • Huge, growing market — e-2W volumes up ~50%/yr with penetration still under ~8%; a first-mover brand with scale intact.
  • Optionality — motorcycles (Roadster) and selling cells/energy storage to others widen the story if execution holds.

The bear case

  • Share in free-fall — from ~30% to ~8%; the only major OEM shrinking while the market booms.
  • Damaged trust — the service crisis and CCPA scrutiny left a brand wound that's slow and costly to heal.
  • Going-concern flag — persistent losses and negative cash flow; dependent on further fundraising to keep going.
  • Bet-the-company cell ramp — cell manufacturing is brutally hard; funding 20 GWh from a shrinking business is a stretch.
  • Formidable rivals + subsidy risk — TVS, Bajaj, Hero and Ather with dealers, service and deep pockets; policy changes can reset pricing.
The bottom line

Ola Electric is a rare thing on the Indian market: a company that has already solved the problem most turnarounds are still chasing — it made its product profitable per unit — and yet remains in genuine danger, because it did so by shrinking. The self-help chapter (margins up, burn down, cells shipping) is largely written and real. The chapter that decides everything is the one Ola controls least: persuading buyers, one VAHAN registration at a time, to choose an Ola again. The next milestones are concrete and dated — the Gigafactory's climb toward 20 GWh by late 2027, and full-year cash break-even in the vehicle business. But the number to watch first sits on a government dashboard, updated monthly: the day Ola's e-2W market-share line stops pointing down is the day this turnaround stops being a hope and starts being a fact.
