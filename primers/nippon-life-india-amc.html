Nippon Life India AMC, India's passive-and-retail powerhouse

A complete walk through the asset manager of Nippon India Mutual Fund — India's fourth-largest fund house and, by a wide margin, its largest passive/ETF player and one of its most granular, retail-heavy franchises. Why a book built on tens of millions of small folios, record SIPs and a dominant ETF share is a distinctive kind of AMC — high-volume and sticky, but structurally lower-yielding than an equity-led rival; how a Japanese life insurer rescued and rebranded a scandal-hit Reliance fund house into a clean, trusted, market-share-gaining machine; and why the two clouds over the whole model remain the stock market and SEBI's power to cut fees.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Nippon Life India Asset Management is the company that runs Nippon India Mutual Fund — India's fourth-largest fund house, managing about ₹7.25 lakh crore of quarterly-average mutual-fund assets (₹7.73 lakh crore including all mandates; roughly US$90 billion) for an extraordinary 23.8 million unique investors — more than one in every three mutual-fund investors in the country. It is majority-owned (71.9%) by Nippon Life Insurance of Japan, which bought out Reliance Capital and rebranded the once-troubled Reliance Mutual Fund in 2019. In FY26 (year to March 2026) it earned ₹2,709 crore of operating revenue and ₹1,529 crore of net profit (+19%), on a return on equity of about 34% and almost no capital employed.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a capital-light annuity on a mountain of money. An AMC needs almost no physical assets — no factories, barely any balance sheet, no debt. It manages money and skims a fee. That is why Nippon earns a ~34% return on equity, converts nearly all its profit to cash, and pays out over 90% as dividend. It has grown QAAUM 30% in a year — the fastest of India's top-10 AMCs.
  • Its differentiator is scale of reach, not scale of assets. Nippon is India's undisputed No. 1 in passive investing — a 21.4% share of ETF assets, 45% of all ETF folios and 52% of ETF exchange volumes — and it runs one of the deepest, most granular retail bases in the country: 23.8 mn investors, 39.4 mn folios, and ₹1.43 lakh crore (20%+) in smaller-town "B-30" India. That is a genuinely different franchise from an equity-and-metro-led rival — and it cuts both ways (see §01, §03).
  • The tailwind is India's savings revolution. Households are shifting money out of bank deposits and gold into mutual funds, mostly through automatic monthly SIPs. Nippon runs a systematic (SIP) book of ~₹3,720 crore a month — an annualised ~₹44,700 crore — with a 9.84% SIP market share, and 77% of new business now comes in digitally. But the same regulator (SEBI) that oversees this boom can also cut the fees — and that, plus the market itself, is where the risk lives.
In plain terms

Think of Nippon India AMC as a toll booth on India's savings highway — but the one that has deliberately built the widest road, with the most lanes and the most cars, rather than the single most expensive lane. It doesn't own the cars (the money belongs to investors) and it doesn't build the road (markets rise and fall on their own). It simply charges a tiny toll — well under 0.5% a year — on every rupee that passes through its funds. What makes Nippon distinctive isn't a premium fee; it's volume and reach: it collects a small toll from an enormous number of small vehicles, including India's biggest fleet of ultra-cheap "passive" ETFs and the largest crowd of first-time, small-town savers. That gives it huge granularity and stickiness — but because so much of its traffic is low-fee passive money, its average toll is lower than an equity-heavy peer's. The two things that can go wrong are the same as for any AMC: the market falls (shrinking the pile the toll is charged on) or the government lowers the toll (SEBI cutting expense ratios). Everything else in this primer is a variation on those forces — plus the remarkable story of how a Japanese insurer rescued this toll booth from its previous owner.

01

The passive & retail-folio franchise — what makes Nippon different

Not the biggest AMC, but the one that touches the most Indians and dominates the future's cheapest products.

If HDFC AMC's edge is the quality (high-fee, individual, equity) of its money, and ICICI Pru's is its lead in high-margin active equity, Nippon's defining trait is different again: it is the franchise of breadth and passive leadership. It reaches more investors, holds more folios, penetrates smaller-town India more deeply, and — above all — owns the fast-growing passive/ETF segment more completely than any rival. This is the single most important thing to understand about the company, because it explains both why the business is so resilient and why its economics look a little different from the pure equity houses.

Why each of these is genuinely distinctive

  • Passive/ETF leadership — the No. 1 spot in the future's product. Globally, asset management has moved relentlessly toward cheap index funds and ETFs, and India is an early version of that story. Nippon is the Indian leader: a 21.4% share of ETF assets, ~45% of all ETF folios, ~52% of ETF exchange volumes, and passive AUM that crossed ₹2.50 lakh crore in FY26. When Indians rushed into gold and silver ETFs during FY26, Nippon captured the bulk of it (its gold + silver ETFs alone are ~₹85,000 Cr). Owning the passive segment is a structural asset almost no rival can match.
  • The largest retail folio base in India. Nippon serves 23.8 million unique investors across 39.4 million folios — over one in three MF investors in the country, and it led the industry (with HDFC) in folio additions in FY26. A huge, granular, individual base is worth far more than a handful of large corporate mandates: it is stickier, more diversified, and re-buys automatically each month.
  • Deep B-30 / small-town penetration. "B-30" means the towns and districts beyond India's 30 biggest cities. Nippon runs ₹1.43 lakh crore (20.1% of its book) in B-30, growing 28% a year — faster than the industry. Small-town money tends to be smaller-ticket, longer-held SIP money: the granular, hard-to-replicate base every AMC covets.
Why a passive-and-retail franchise is distinctive — and why it's double-edged

A granular, folio-heavy, passive-leading book is a fortress of resilience: money arrives from millions of tiny sources, rarely leaves in a hurry, and grows the fee base whether markets are calm or wild. It gives Nippon reach that a metro-and-equity rival simply cannot buy. But — and this is the honest nuance — it comes at the cost of yield. Passive/ETF money is the cheapest kind of asset there is: an index rupee earns barely a tenth of what an active-equity rupee earns. So the very thing that makes Nippon distinctive (its huge, dominant passive book) also drags down its blended fee versus an equity-led house. The result is an AMC that is unusually resilient and unusually well-positioned for the future of the industry, but that earns a lower average margin today. Understanding that trade-off — reach and stickiness bought with a lower yield — is the master key to Nippon (see §03).

02

How an asset manager actually earns money

No finance degree required — just the one equation that runs the whole business.

An AMC is one of the purest business models in the market: it is paid a percentage fee on the assets it manages, charged continuously, day after day, for as long as the money stays invested. There is no product to manufacture, no inventory, no receivables to chase. The entire company can be summarised in a single line.

Revenue ≈ Average Assets Under Management × the fee rate (the "yield on AUM").

So there are only two ways to grow revenue: manage more money (grow AUM) or earn a higher fee on it (lift the yield, mainly by holding more equity and less passive/cash). Everything an AMC does — launching funds, delivering returns, expanding distribution, marketing SIPs — is ultimately in service of those two levers.

Why this is such a beautiful business model

Three features make asset management structurally attractive. (1) It is capital-light. The AMC manages investors' money, not its own — so it needs very little balance sheet, which is why ROE can be ~34% (and higher for lighter peers). (2) It has operating leverage. A fund manager who runs ₹5,000 Cr costs about the same as one who runs ₹50,000 Cr, so once AUM crosses a scale threshold, almost every extra rupee of fee drops to profit — which is why Nippon's operating profit grew 24% in FY26 while revenue grew ~21%. (3) The fee is recurring and sticky. Money in a fund doesn't have to be re-sold every year; it stays and keeps paying the fee, and SIP money often stays for years. Add it up and you get a high-margin, high-return, low-capital annuity — as long as AUM keeps growing and the fee isn't cut.

Where the costs go

Nippon's expense base is small and mostly people. The big buckets are employee costs (fund managers, analysts, a large sales force), fees paid to distributors (the independent advisers and platforms that bring in the money), and administration/technology. Because these are largely fixed, the gap between the ~37 bps it earns and the far smaller sum it spends is what makes the model print money. Nippon's edge on cost is its digital scale — 77% of new business now arrives through apps and online, which keeps the cost of acquiring each of those tens of millions of folios remarkably low.

03

Yield on AUM, the mix, and the passive nuance

The single idea that separates a high-yield AMC from a high-volume one — and why Nippon is the latter.

Not all AUM is equal. An equity fund can charge many times more than an index ETF or a cash fund for managing the same rupee, because active equity investing requires more skill, carries more risk, and investors will pay for it. So an AMC's profitability is dictated less by how much money it runs than by what kind of money it runs. This is the master key to understanding why Nippon — despite its enormous reach — earns a lower blended yield than an equity-led rival, and why that is a deliberate feature of its passive-heavy model, not a flaw.

Why the lower yield is a feature, not just a weakness

It is tempting to read Nippon's ~37 bps blended yield — below ICICI Pru's ~48 bps or HDFC AMC's ~47 bps — as simply "worse." That misses the point. Nippon has made a strategic bet on the direction the whole industry is travelling. Passive is where global asset management ended up, and India is following: index funds and ETFs will be a much larger share of the market in a decade than they are today. By dominating passive now — 45% of folios, 52% of volumes — Nippon has claimed the segment that its rivals will have to fight to enter later, and it has done so with the scale (and therefore the cost efficiency) to actually make money at ETF-level fees, which sub-scale players cannot. The trade-off is real: lower yield today. But the franchise is positioned for the future of the industry rather than only its present — and it still earns ~34% returns on capital. The bull sees a moat in the fastest-growing segment; the bear sees a structurally lower-margin book. Both are describing the same fact.

04

Reliance → Nippon: the rescue, the rebrand, the recovery

How a Japanese life insurer bought a scandal-shadowed fund house and rebuilt its trust — the story no other listed AMC has.

Nippon India AMC is the only major listed Indian fund house with a genuine turnaround-and-ownership-transition story at its heart, and understanding it is essential to understanding the stock. This company was, until 2019, Reliance Mutual Fund — part of Anil Ambani's ADAG (Reliance Group), which by 2018–19 was in deep financial distress. What happened next — a clean Japanese parent buying control, rebranding, and painstakingly winning back trust, flows and market share — is a distinctive asset that the other AMCs, born inside blue-chip banks, simply do not carry.

  • 2018–19

  • Oct 2019
    The rescue & rebrand. Nippon Life Insurance buys out Reliance Capital's stake to become majority owner; the fund house is renamed Nippon India Mutual Fund (effective 28 Sep 2019) and the AMC becomes Nippon Life India Asset Management.   • 2020–25
    The trust recovery. Under stable ownership, flows return, folios and SIPs grow rapidly, and market share climbs back off its lows — helped by aggressive digital and passive/ETF expansion.
  • FY26
    Market share reaches 8.89% — the highest since June 2019, QAAUM ₹7.25 lakh Cr (+30%, fastest of the top-10), PAT ₹1,529 Cr (+19%). The recovery is, on the numbers, complete.
Why the ownership transition matters to a shareholder today

Three things flow from this history. (1) The parent is a source of stability, not a risk. Where Reliance Capital was a distressed borrower whose troubles bled into the fund house, Nippon Life is one of Japan's largest and oldest life insurers — a patient, deep-pocketed, reputationally cautious owner. That swap from a shaky Indian promoter to a rock-solid Japanese one is precisely what let trust return. (2) The recovery is a repeatable capability, not luck. Winning back share after a crisis of confidence — the hardest thing an asset manager can do, because trust is the entire product — demonstrates a franchise resilience that a never-tested rival hasn't proven. (3) It still carries a faint discount. Some investors remember the ADAG chapter, and Nippon has occasionally attracted regulatory scrutiny (see §09); a lingering "show me" caution is part of why it can trade at a slightly different multiple than a bank-parented peer, even as its numbers now stand comparison with any of them.

Ownership at a glance (~Mar 2026)

  • Nippon Life Insurance (Japan) — promoter, ~71.9%. The sponsor and controlling shareholder; also the trustee via Nippon Life India Trustee. A blue-chip global insurer behind the wheel.
  • Public / institutions — ~28%. The free float is meaningful (unlike some newly-listed peers), giving the stock genuine liquidity and index representation.
  • Balance sheet: debt-free, cash-rich, capital-light — it funds itself and returns the great majority of profit as dividends (FY26 payout ~91.5%).

06

The SIP & financialisation engine

The once-in-a-generation force that feeds every Indian AMC — and Nippon's granular, digital take on it.

The single biggest reason to own an Indian AMC has nothing to do with any one fund's returns. It is a structural, decade-long shift in where Indians put their savings — out of bank deposits, gold and property, and into financial assets, above all mutual funds bought through automatic monthly SIPs. This "financialisation of savings" is the tide lifting the entire industry, and Nippon — with the widest retail reach and the most digital-native acquisition engine — is one of the biggest boats on it.

The numbers that tell the story

Three linked shifts drive it. First, a generation of Indians is discovering investing for the first time, helped by smartphones, digital KYC and fintech apps that make starting an SIP a two-minute job — a shift Nippon rides better than most, with 77% of its new business now digital and a record 5.04 million digital purchases + new SIPs in a single quarter (+44%). Second, the share of household savings going into markets has risen sharply while the share sitting in bank deposits has fallen. Third, mutual-fund penetration — AUM as a share of GDP — is still only ~21% in India versus 100%+ in developed markets, so the runway is long.

Why the SIP is the magic ingredient

A Systematic Investment Plan is an instruction to invest a fixed sum (say ₹1,000 or ₹5,000) into a fund automatically every month. For the AMC this is transformative, because it turns lumpy, sentiment-driven investing into a predictable, recurring, sticky river of inflows that keeps coming even when markets fall — which is exactly when new investors are tempted to stop, and exactly when continued buying pays off most. Nippon runs a systematic (SIP) book of ~₹3,720 crore a month — an annualised ~₹44,700 crore — with a 9.84% SIP market share. Crucially, Nippon's SIP investors are unusually numerous and small: it is the granular, tens-of-millions-of-folios kind of SIP money, spread across small-town India, that churns the least and compounds the most. For an AMC, a rising SIP book is the closest thing to a subscription business — and Nippon's is one of the most granular in the country.

The honest caveat: SIP flows are resilient but not unbreakable. In a prolonged bear market, SIP "stoppage ratios" rise as some investors pause or cancel, and monthly flows can plateau. The structural story is intact for years, but it doesn't make Nippon's earnings immune to a bad market — it just cushions them, and Nippon's granular base cushions them a little more than most.
07

Distribution & the retail advisory base

Manufacturing funds is only half the job — someone has to reach 24 million investors.

An AMC "manufactures" funds, but it needs a distribution army to place them with millions of investors. How money reaches an AMC — and how much it must pay away to whoever sold the fund — shapes both its growth and its margins. Nippon's channel mix is distinctive in its own way: it leans heavily on the vast network of independent mutual-fund distributors (MFDs) and on its own digital direct engine, rather than on a single captive bank — the natural mix for a franchise built on reaching small investors everywhere.

The Nippon distribution angle — reach without a captive bank

  • No captive bank — an independent-adviser franchise. Unlike HDFC AMC (HDFC Bank) or ICICI Pru (ICICI Bank), Nippon has no parent bank branch network to lean on. Instead it has built one of India's deepest independent-MFD relationships (~40% of AUM) — a wider, more entrepreneurial distribution base that reaches into towns a bank branch network never would. This is a strength for granularity and B-30 reach.
  • A digital-direct machine. Nippon punches above its weight online: 77% of new business is now digital, and its ETF platform gives it a natural pull with self-directed, app-native investors. Direct/digital money carries no commission, so it quietly improves margins as it grows.
  • The trade-off vs bank-owned peers. Not having a captive bank means Nippon can't cross-sell into a giant, trusting deposit base the way ICICI Pru or HDFC AMC can. It has offset this with sheer breadth of advisers and digital reach — but it is a structurally different, more distributed go-to-market.
Direct vs regular — the margin dial

Every fund comes in two flavours: a "regular" plan sold through a distributor (who is paid a commission out of the fee), and a "direct" plan bought by the investor with no middleman (and so no commission). The more money that comes direct, the less the AMC pays away, and the more of the fee it keeps. As Indian investors grow more confident and go direct through apps, this quietly improves AMC economics — a slow tailwind for net yields that partly offsets the fee pressure elsewhere. Nippon's high digital share means a healthy and rising slice of its money is direct — an important cushion given its lower gross yield.

08

Market-linked earnings: the built-in leverage

Why an AMC's profits are a geared bet on the market — softened, in Nippon's case, by the passive mix.

Here is the feature that most distinguishes an AMC from a bank or an insurer: because revenue is a percentage of AUM, and because AUM moves with markets, an AMC's earnings are geared to asset prices. When markets rise, Nippon's fee base inflates automatically — no extra effort required. When they fall, the fee base deflates just as automatically. This is the double-edged sword baked into the model — with a Nippon-specific twist.

The Nippon twist — lower yield cuts both ways here too

Because Nippon earns a lower blended fee (~37 bps) with a big passive slice, a rupee of AUM swing moves its revenue slightly less than it would move an equity-led peer's — its earnings are a touch less geared to the top of the equity market than ICICI Pru's or HDFC AMC's. But the flip side is that passive/ETF AUM still rises and falls with its underlying indices, so Nippon is very much a broad bet on Indian (and, via gold/silver ETFs, commodity) markets. The bigger nuance is Nippon's own investment book: like its peers, it invests surplus cash in markets, so mark-to-market swings on those investments can add volatility to reported quarterly "other income" — you saw the reverse of this in FY26, when strong markets and buoyant flows lifted results. The takeaway: an AMC is partly a leveraged, fee-collecting position on markets; Nippon's version is a slightly-less-geared, broader-market one, with the SIP flow providing the floor and AUM compounding the long-term climb.

What to hold in mind: when you buy Nippon, you are partly buying a diversified, fee-collecting position on Indian markets — equities and the commodity/index ETFs it dominates. The granular SIP flow provides a floor and AUM compounding provides the climb, but don't expect the share price to be calm when markets are volatile — the earnings simply aren't, by design.
09

SEBI, expense ratios & the fee squeeze

The regulator holds the dial on the AMC's revenue line — and Nippon carries a live SEBI notice.

Everything above depends on one number the AMC does not fully control: the fee it is allowed to charge. India's market regulator, SEBI, caps the "Total Expense Ratio" (TER) a fund may levy — and it has a long, consistent history of pushing those caps down to protect investors. For an AMC, a TER cut is a direct hit to the top line with no offsetting effort possible. This is the permanent cloud over every fund house — and, for Nippon specifically, there is an additional regulatory item to watch.

The bigger the scheme, the lower the fee it may charge — so as flagship funds swell, the blended yield naturally drifts down even without a rule change.

There are three squeezes on the fee line, stacked on top of each other — and a fourth, Nippon-specific, item:

  • The regulatory ratchet. SEBI periodically lowers TER caps and, from FY26–27, overhauled the framework (a "Base Expense Ratio" model, tighter brokerage caps). Analysts read the latest changes as broadly earnings-neutral for large AMCs, but the direction of travel is unmistakably down.
  • The size ratchet. Even with no rule change, the caps fall as funds grow — so Nippon's own success in gathering assets slowly compresses its blended fee.
  • The passive ratchet. Index funds and ETFs charge a fraction of an active fund — and Nippon, as the passive leader, is most exposed to this. Its very strength (a huge, growing passive book) is also the biggest structural drag on its blended yield.
  • The Nippon-specific item: a live SEBI show-cause notice. Nippon has attracted regulatory scrutiny — including a small TER-related penalty in the past and, more recently, a SEBI show-cause notice alleging non-compliance with certain investment provisions. It is not yet quantified and may prove immaterial, but it is a real, company-specific overhang worth tracking and a faint echo of the "show me" caution the ADAG chapter left behind.
Why it's a cloud, not a thunderstorm — for now

Fee compression is real and permanent, but it has so far been a slow grind rather than a cliff, and the industry has repeatedly grown AUM fast enough to more than offset a lower fee rate. The maths is simple: if AUM grows ~20–30% but the fee rate slips a few percent, revenue still rises comfortably — exactly what Nippon delivered in FY26 (revenue +21.5% on a lower-yielding mix). Nippon's defences are its scale in passive (it can profitably run ETFs at fees that would bankrupt a sub-scale rival), its rising direct/digital share (keeping more of each fee), and its sheer volume growth. The danger scenario is a step-change: a sharp, one-off TER cut plus a weak market plus an accelerating passive shift all at once — with the SEBI notice as an added, idiosyncratic wildcard. That combination is the bear's nightmare, and part of why an AMC never quite escapes a valuation discount for regulatory risk.

How to think about the ~49× multiple

At a market cap of about ₹74,000 crore (~₹1,160/share, June 2026) on ₹1,529 Cr of FY26 profit, Nippon trades near 49× trailing earnings — roughly a 30% premium to the peer median, and a level that only makes sense if you underwrite years of fast AUM growth outrunning fee compression. The bull argues: this is the fastest-growing of the giants, the undisputed leader in passive (the segment of the future), with the widest retail reach in India, a clean Japanese parent, ~34% ROE and almost no capital needs — scarcity of this quality and this growth deserves a premium. The bear argues: the market is paying an equity-house multiple for a lower-yielding, passive-shaded book, the multiple leaves no room for a market correction, a sharper-than-expected TER cut, or the SEBI notice turning material — any of which could de-rate a ~49× stock quickly. As so often, both are right at different points in the cycle, which is why the numbers to track each quarter are AUM growth, the equity vs passive mix, net flows, and the blended yield.

The industry and the rivals

A consolidating oligopoly with a huge tailwind — where Nippon owns the passive corner.

Indian asset management is a scale-driven oligopoly: the top handful of AMCs control the bulk of the assets, and their lead compounds because size begets distribution begets more size. The industry is riding the financialisation wave together, but competing hard on brand, performance and — increasingly — fees. Nippon is the No. 4 anchor of that structure, and the clear leader in one whole corner of it: passive.

The competitive tension to watch

Two dynamics matter for Nippon specifically. First, the passive race it is winning. Passive is the way global asset management went — fees collapsing toward zero — and India is an early version of that story. Where rivals see passive as a low-margin threat to be defended against, Nippon has leaned in and become the runaway leader; its ~45% folio share and ~52% volume share are a genuine, scale-based moat in the segment most likely to grow fastest. Second, the yield gap. Nippon must keep proving that a lower-yielding, higher-volume model can compound profit as well as an equity-led one — which so far it has, growing PAT 19% on a mix its rivals would call low-margin. The strategic question for the next five years is whether Nippon's bet — own the future's cheap products at scale, reach the most investors, and let volume beat margin — outperforms ICICI Pru's and HDFC AMC's bet on premium active equity. It is one of the more interesting divergences in Indian finance.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A recovered, fast-growing, passive-leading franchise — priced for it to keep executing.

Nippon India AMC is one of the more distinctive compounding stories in Indian finance: a capital-light, ~34%-ROE annuity on a structurally growing pool of savings, led by the No. 1 passive franchise and the widest retail reach in the country, rescued and rebuilt under a clean Japanese parent. The investment debate is about price, the market cycle, the yield model, and the regulator — the business quality itself is no longer in question.

The bull case

  • Passive leadership — No. 1 in ETFs (21% assets, ~45% folios, ~52% volumes); owns the segment most likely to grow fastest.
  • Widest retail reach — 23.8 mn investors (1 in 3 in India), 39.4 mn folios, deep B-30 penetration; granular, sticky, digital-native.
  • Fastest-growing giant — QAAUM +30%, market share at a post-2019 high; the recovery is complete and momentum is with it.
  • Clean, strong parent — Nippon Life of Japan replaced a distressed promoter; trust rebuilt, capital abundant.
  • Capital-light economics — ~34% ROE, ~65% operating margins, debt-free, ~90%+ payout — a cash machine.

The bear case

  • Lower blended yield — ~37 bps vs ~45–48 bps for equity-led peers; the passive tilt structurally caps the fee rate.
  • Rich valuation — ~49× earnings, a premium to peers, for a lower-margin book; little room for error.
  • Market-linked, geared earnings — an AUM-based fee means profits fall in a correction.
  • TER / passive fee-cut risk — SEBI can lower fee caps, and Nippon's own passive dominance is the biggest yield drag.
  • Company-specific overhang — a live SEBI show-cause notice and lingering memory of the ADAG era.
The bottom line

Nippon India AMC is a structurally advantaged, capital-light leader of arguably the best theme in Indian finance — the movement of household savings into markets — with a genuinely different franchise from its listed peers: the widest retail reach and the undisputed lead in passive. Its turnaround under a clean Japanese parent is the most compelling ownership story of any listed AMC. The business risk is low; the valuation, yield-model and cyclical risks are real. The story over the next few years will be written in four numbers each quarter — AUM growth (is the pile still swelling, fastest of the pack?), net flows / SIPs (is the granular annuity still filling?), the equity-vs-passive mix (can the lower-yield book still compound profit?), and the blended yield (is fee compression manageable?). Get those right and the growth justifies the premium; a market correction stacked on a sharp TER cut and a bad SEBI outcome, and a ~49× stock has room to fall.
