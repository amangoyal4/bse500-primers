00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Billionbrains Garage Ventures Ltd is the legal parent company behind the brand everyone knows as Groww — the mobile app that lets ordinary Indians buy stocks, mutual funds and derivatives in a few taps. Groww is India's largest retail stockbroker by active clients, ahead of Zerodha, Angel One and Upstox, and it listed on the NSE and BSE on 12 November 2025 in one of the year's most high-profile IPOs (issue price ₹100; NSE: GROWW · BSE: 544603).

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is the #1 broker, built on a mobile-first, low-cost model. Groww ended FY26 with roughly 12.9 million NSE active clients (~28% market share) and 16.7 million active users across its whole platform. It overtook Zerodha not by cutting price alone, but by making investing feel as simple as ordering food — winning millions of first-time investors from small towns.
  • It's very profitable — and the money isn't where you'd think. FY26 revenue was ~₹4,645 Cr at a ~59% EBITDA margin and ~₹2,083 Cr net profit. Delivery trades on Groww are largely free; the profit comes from derivatives (F&O) brokerage, interest on client funds and margin lending — not from your long-term SIPs.
  • Its biggest risk and its biggest opportunity are both regulatory/structural. A SEBI crackdown on retail derivatives has shrunk the F&O pool that drives most broker revenue — the single largest swing factor. Against that, the deep tailwind of India's savings shifting into markets gives it a decade-long runway. It trades at a rich ~60× earnings, so the debate is entirely about durability.
In plain terms

Think of Groww as the "investing app your cousin downloaded first" — the default place a young, first-time Indian investor opens a demat account, buys their first mutual fund, and eventually starts trading. Groww's genius was simplicity: no jargon, no branch, no paperwork, a clean green app. That won it the biggest customer base in the country. But a broker that charges almost nothing for delivery trades has to make its money elsewhere — mostly from active traders doing options, from lending against shares, and from parking client cash. So the health of the business tracks two things closely: how many people trade actively (which the regulator is trying to cool down), and how many new products Groww can sell the same giant customer base (mutual funds, loans, payments).

01

Discount-broker economics 101: how a "free" app earns

No finance degree required — just the handful of terms that drive everything.

A stockbroker is a toll booth on the flow of trades. Every time you buy or sell a stock or an option, the trade passes through a broker, who charges a small fee — and also earns on the cash and margin that sit around the transaction. The "discount" model, which Groww and Zerodha pioneered in India, throws out the old percentage-of-trade commission and charges a tiny flat fee per order instead. Five terms tell you almost the whole story.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for Groww)
Active clientA customer who has traded at least once in the last year (NSE's official definition). This is the headline scoreboard. Groww is #1 at ~12.9 million (~28% share). More active clients = more trades = more fees.
Delivery vs intraday vs F&ODelivery = buying a share to keep (Groww charges ₹0 or a tiny fee). Intraday = buy & sell same day. F&O = futures & options (derivatives) — high frequency, and the real money-maker at ₹20/order.
ADTO / volumes"Average Daily Turnover" — how much money flows through the platform per day. Groww's retail cash ADTO was ~₹13,800 Cr and derivatives premium ADTO ~₹16,500 Cr in Q4 FY26. Higher volumes = more brokerage.
Take rateThe slice of each rupee traded that the broker keeps as revenue. Discount brokers run a very thin take rate — the model only works at massive scale, which is why the #1 by volume wins.
Float / net interestInterest the broker earns on client money parked with it (idle cash, margin deposits) and on money it lends to clients. Invisible to the customer, but a huge, high-margin revenue line.
The "free trades" paradox, resolved

New investors love that Groww charges ₹0 for delivery equity trades (buying stocks to hold) and 0% commission on direct mutual funds. So how is it one of India's most profitable fintechs? Because the loss-leader wins the customer, and the customer then generates money elsewhere. Free delivery brings millions of first-timers in cheaply. A slice of them start trading options (charged ~₹20/order — small per trade but done many times a day by active traders). Many leave cash idle in the account or borrow to trade — Groww earns interest on both. It's the classic platform playbook: acquire cheaply at the top of the funnel, monetise the active minority underneath.

02

How Groww actually makes money

Follow the rupee — the revenue mix is the single most useful thing to understand here.

Groww's revenue is dominated by transaction-linked income: broking fees, interest on margin lending, and float income together make up roughly 87–88% of total income. Within that, equity derivatives (F&O) are the biggest single driver — around 55% of revenue in recent quarters. Understanding this mix is essential, because it tells you exactly what makes the numbers move.

The four money machines, decoded

  • Broking fees — a flat ₹20 per order on F&O and intraday (delivery is free). Tiny per trade, enormous in aggregate because active traders place many orders a day. This is the visible "brokerage" line and the most volume-sensitive.
  • Net interest / float income — Groww holds large balances of client cash and margin deposits; it earns interest on those balances. Because there's almost no cost attached, this is extremely high-margin and grows quietly with the client base and market activity.
  • Margin Trading Facility (MTF) — Groww lends customers money to buy more shares than their cash allows, charging ~15% p.a. Its MTF book roughly quadrupled year-on-year to ~₹2,814 Cr in Q4 FY26 and now contributes ~5% of revenue (up from ~2%). A fast-growing, interest-rate-style earnings stream.
  • The newer businesses — Groww Mutual Fund (AMC), consumer lending/credit, and payments. Small today but strategically vital: they monetise the same customers without depending on trading volumes, which is how Groww hopes to de-risk the F&O concentration (see §06).
The one-line risk map: because ~55% of revenue is F&O brokerage and most of the rest is trading-linked interest, Groww's profits are geared to how much retail India trades derivatives. When the regulator restricts that activity (§07), it hits Groww's largest revenue line directly. The whole diversification strategy exists to reduce this single dependency.
03

The company and who controls it

From four Flipkart engineers to a listed fintech giant — and a famously expensive tax bill.

Groww is a founder-led, first-generation start-up success. In 2016, four Flipkart employees — Lalit Keshre (CEO), Harsh Jain, Ishan Bansal and Neeraj Singh — quit to build a mutual-fund app for ordinary Indians. It launched in 2017, added stockbroking in 2020, and rode India's retail-investing boom to the #1 spot. The legal entity that owns it all is Billionbrains Garage Ventures Ltd; "Groww" is the consumer brand.

The "reverse flip" and the FY24 loss — why the accounts look odd

Like many Indian start-ups, Groww had originally incorporated its holding company in the US (Delaware) to raise dollar venture capital. To list in India, it had to bring the parent back home — an "inbound merger" completed in March 2024. Moving domicile out of the US triggered a large American exit tax of ~$160 million (₹1,340 Cr). That one-time, non-operating charge turned an otherwise profitable year into a reported net loss of ~₹805 Cr in FY24. It is crucial to read this as a plumbing cost of relocating, not a sign of a struggling business: strip it out and FY24 operations were profitable, and FY25 profit rebounded to ~₹1,824 Cr. FY25 and FY26 are clean of this item.

Ownership & management

  • Founder-promoters — Lalit Keshre and his three co-founders retain a meaningful stake (Keshre alone ~9%); the promoter group held ~27% after listing. Owner-operators running the business they built — generally a positive alignment signal.
  • VC backers, now partly cashed out — Peak XV, Y Combinator, Ribbit Capital, Tiger Global, ICONIQ and Satya Nadella were pre-IPO investors. Several sold part of their holdings in the IPO's offer-for-sale (Peak XV alone booked a ~52× gain) and have continued trimming post-listing — a normal but watch-worthy overhang of VC supply.
  • Balance sheet — well-capitalised and cash-generative; the broking model is asset-light, though the growing MTF and lending books do consume capital and add balance-sheet risk.
04

The #1-by-active-clients franchise

How Groww overtook Zerodha — and why the scoreboard matters.

In a business defined by scale, Groww is the leader. It sits at #1 among all Indian brokers by NSE active clients — the metric the regulator and the market watch most closely — having overtaken the once-dominant Zerodha. Being biggest matters more here than in most industries: at a wafer-thin take rate, the platform with the most trades enjoys the best economics and can keep prices low, reinforcing its lead.

Two different "user" numbers — don't confuse them

Groww quotes 16.7 million "active users" — its own platform metric, which counts anyone using any product (stocks, mutual funds, etc.). The NSE separately reports ~12.9 million "active broking clients" — the standard, regulator-defined count of people who actually traded in the last year. Both are real and useful: the larger number shows the size of the top of the funnel (people Groww can cross-sell to); the smaller number is the count that drives broking revenue and the "#1 broker" claim. When you compare Groww to rivals on market share, use the NSE broking-client number.

Why leadership compounds: a discount broker earns a tiny amount per trade, so profit depends on doing enormous volume cheaply. The biggest player spreads its fixed technology and compliance costs over the most trades, giving it the best unit economics — which lets it keep prices low and marketing efficient, which wins even more clients. That self-reinforcing loop is why the #1 spot in a low-cost platform business is so valuable, and why the market pays up for the company that holds it.
05

The mobile-first, low-cost disruption flywheel

The first defining force — how simplicity, not just price, built the biggest base.

Groww didn't win by being the cheapest — Zerodha got to zero-brokerage delivery first. Groww won by being the simplest, most trustworthy on-ramp for the first-time, small-town Indian investor. It treated investing like a consumer app, not a trading terminal, and in doing so unlocked a customer segment the incumbents under-served: young people in Tier-2 and Tier-3 cities opening their first ever demat account. That is the heart of the bull case.

The further right, the higher the revenue per user and the more diversified the earnings — but you can only get there by winning Stage 1 cheaply and at scale, which is exactly what Groww's simplicity does.

Why the flywheel spins — three reinforcing effects

  • Low customer-acquisition cost (CAC). A clean, jargon-free app with painless digital onboarding spreads largely by word of mouth and network effects — your friends and family are already on Groww. Low CAC means Groww can profitably acquire the small-ticket, first-time investor that a branch-based or terminal-first rival can't serve economically.
  • Simplicity as the moat. For someone investing ₹500 in their first mutual fund, the fear is complexity, not fees. Groww removed the fear. That trust is sticky: the first app that successfully holds your money tends to become your default, and switching brokers is a hassle most casual investors never bother with.
  • Network effects & brand. As Groww becomes the obvious answer to "which app should I use to invest?", each new cohort of young investors defaults to it — a self-reinforcing brand advantage that is very hard for a challenger to unseat once it takes hold.
The genuinely disruptive insight

Traditional brokers optimised for the serious trader — powerful terminals, percentage commissions, relationship managers. Groww realised the far larger prize was the hundreds of millions of Indians who had never invested at all, intimidated by the whole thing. By making the first step trivially easy and nearly free, it converted savers into investors at scale. The catch, which §07 explores, is that the customers who are cheapest to acquire (long-term SIP holders) are not the ones who generate the most revenue (active F&O traders) — so Groww's growth in users and its growth in profit don't always move together.

06

Beyond broking: the "super-app for money"

The second defining force — diversification that de-risks a pure-broking business.

Groww's strategic answer to "what if trading slows down?" is to become a full-stack financial super-app — using its giant, trusting customer base to sell products whose revenue does not depend on trading volumes. Four adjacencies matter: asset management, wealth, lending/credit, and payments. Each is small today, but together they are the story of how Groww hopes to convert a volatile broking business into a durable financial-services franchise.

Why "boring" beats "exciting" here

Broking revenue is exciting — it soars in a bull market and when derivatives are hot, and it slumps when volumes dry up or the regulator tightens. Asset-management fees and lending interest are boring — they compound slowly and steadily on a growing pool of assets, largely regardless of whether markets are frantic or quiet. Groww is deliberately trying to add more of the boring, so that a bad year for F&O doesn't wreck the whole company. The bet is that owning India's largest investor base is the hard part, and selling that base a mutual fund, a loan and a payments product is comparatively easy. If it works, Groww re-rates from "a broker" to "a financial platform." If the new businesses stay sub-scale, it remains, at heart, a leveraged bet on retail trading activity.

Reality check: the diversification is real but early. Groww AMC is a rounding error next to the incumbents and still loses money; lending is promising but small. For now, transaction-linked income (~87–88% of revenue) still runs the show — so treat the super-app as an option on the future, not the base case, when thinking about valuation.
07

The SEBI F&O clampdown — the single biggest swing factor

The third and most important force — the regulator holds the tap that feeds most of Groww's revenue.

If you understand only one risk to Groww, understand this one. Because derivatives (F&O) drive ~55% of revenue and most of the rest is trading-linked, Groww is acutely exposed to how much retail India is allowed and inclined to trade options. And India's market regulator, SEBI, has spent the last two years deliberately trying to cool exactly that activity — because studies showed the vast majority of retail F&O traders lose money. This is the same clampdown that hit exchange operators like BSE; for a broker geared to retail derivatives, it lands even harder.

What SEBI actually did (from Oct/Nov 2024)

  • Fewer weekly expiries. Each exchange may now offer weekly-expiry options on only one benchmark index (NSE kept Nifty; discontinued Bank Nifty and other weekly contracts). Weekly expiries were the casino days that generated the most frantic — and most brokerage-rich — retail volume.
  • Bigger contract (lot) sizes. The minimum contract value was raised to ~₹15 lakh (from ₹5–10 lakh), pricing many small retail punters out of a single trade.
  • "True-to-label" charges & upfront premium. Rules requiring upfront collection of option premium and passing exchange charges through transparently — removing hidden cross-subsidies that had fuelled cheap, high-volume trading.

The mechanism: fewer expiry days + bigger lots = fewer, larger trades by fewer people = less ₹20-per-order brokerage. It hit the whole industry (Zerodha lost ~550k active clients in 2025); Groww, being most retail-derivatives-exposed, felt it directly on its biggest revenue line.
Why this is the number-one thing to watch

Here's the chain of cause and effect in plain English: SEBI restricts retail options trading → fewer people place fewer, bigger option trades → Groww collects fewer ₹20 order fees → its largest revenue line shrinks. This is not a one-off; the regulator has signalled it will keep leaning against retail speculation, and further tightening is always possible. That makes SEBI policy the dominant swing factor for Groww's near-term earnings — more important than the market level, competition, or anything management controls. It is the demand-side equivalent of what "China" is for a solar company: an external force that can move the whole P&L. The silver lining the bulls point to: when frantic F&O cools, some of that money migrates to cash equities, mutual funds and ETFs — which is precisely why Groww's super-app diversification (§06) is strategically urgent, not optional.

The honest tension: Groww's remarkable Q4 FY26 numbers (revenue +88% YoY) partly reflect a recovery in market activity and MTF/interest income after the initial shock — but the structural ceiling on retail derivatives is now a permanent feature of the landscape. Bulls believe diversification + rising cash-equity and SIP volumes more than offset it over time; bears believe a business still ~55% dependent on F&O is one more regulatory circular away from an earnings shock. Watch derivatives volumes and F&O revenue share every quarter — if they keep falling while AMC/lending rise, the de-risking is working.
08

The financialisation of India's savings

The structural tailwind under everything — a decade-long runway, if it holds.

Against the F&O headwind sits a powerful, multi-year tailwind: Indians are moving their savings out of gold, property and bank deposits and into financial markets. Demat accounts and mutual-fund SIPs have exploded — yet penetration remains low versus developed markets. Groww, as the largest on-ramp for new investors, is a direct leveraged play on this shift. This is the single best reason to own the stock for the long run.

The logic is demographic and structural. India has a young, digitally-native, rising-income population that is only beginning to invest in equities. Each year brings a fresh cohort of first-time investors — and Groww is the default app many of them download first. Even if any single investor trades little, the sheer number of new accounts, the steady drip of SIP money, and the gradual deepening of each relationship (from one mutual fund to stocks to loans) compounds the platform's value over a decade.

Why penetration, not this quarter's volumes, is the long-game

Short term, Groww's earnings swing with trading activity and SEBI policy. But the structural case is that the pool of Indian investors is still small and growing fast — the number of people who will eventually hold a demat account is a large multiple of today's. As the largest, cheapest, simplest gateway, Groww captures a big share of every new cohort. If it converts even a fraction of those users into SIP holders, MTF borrowers and eventual wealth clients, the base compounds regardless of any one year's derivatives volumes. This is the tailwind that lets bulls look past the F&O clampdown: the addressable market is expanding faster than the regulator can restrain any single product. The risk is that penetration saturates in the segments Groww already dominates, or that a market downturn scares off the newest, least-committed investors — the ones most likely to quit at the first loss.

Financials & valuation

Fast growth, fat margins — and a price that assumes both continue.

Groww's financials read like a scaled-up fintech: rapid revenue growth, unusually high margins for a broker, and strong profitability once you look past the FY24 tax noise. The debate is not whether the business is good — it clearly is — but whether ~60× earnings is the right price for something so exposed to a regulator and a market cycle.

How to think about the ~60× multiple

At a market cap of roughly ₹1.24 lakh Cr on ~₹2,083 Cr of FY26 profit, Groww trades around 60× trailing earnings — the kind of multiple reserved for companies expected to compound rapidly for many years. That price only makes sense if you believe three things at once: (1) the financialisation tailwind keeps adding users and assets for a decade; (2) the super-app diversification succeeds enough to reduce the F&O dependence; and (3) the SEBI clampdown doesn't structurally cap earnings. Bulls argue the #1 platform in a booming, under-penetrated market with 59% margins deserves a scarcity premium. Bears argue that a business ~55% geared to a product the regulator is actively suppressing, priced at 60× with a still-loss-making AMC and a post-IPO VC-selling overhang, has almost no cushion if any one of those three assumptions wobbles. A stock this richly priced can de-rate hard on a single soft quarter — which is why the swing factors in §07 matter so much to the valuation.

10

The rivals

A consolidating oligopoly of discount brokers — with new fronts opening.

Indian retail broking has consolidated into a handful of large discount players, with the old full-service brokers relegated to niches. Groww leads, but the competitive set is strong and the battle is shifting from pure broking to the wider "financial super-app" arena — where fintech giants and banks also compete.

The competitive tension to watch

Two dynamics matter. First, the core is commoditising: everyone charges roughly ₹20/order or free delivery, so brokerage is no longer where you win — the fight moves to interest income, lending, AMC and engagement. Second, everyone is copying the super-app playbook: Angel One, banks and payments giants all want to be the one app you use for money. Groww's edge is its enormous, young, trusting base and its brand as the beginner's app — but that edge has to be defended by continuously out-executing on product and by making the diversification (§06) actually work. The strategic question for the next five years: can Groww convert its lead in users into a durable lead in profit per user before rivals catch up on simplicity and before the regulator reshapes the pool again?

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely excellent platform — priced for it to keep winning on every front.

Groww is one of the cleanest ways to own India's retail-investing boom: the #1 platform, high margins, a founder team that built it, and a real diversification strategy. The investment debate is almost entirely about price, regulation and durability — not whether the business is good.

The bull case

  • #1 by active clients — the largest, still-growing base in a low-cost platform business where scale compounds.
  • Structural tailwind — the financialisation of Indian savings gives a decade-long runway of new investors and SIP flows.
  • Fat, fintech-style margins — ~59% EBITDA on an asset-light model, with high-margin float/interest income.
  • Optionality from the super-app — AMC, lending and payments can add durable, non-trading revenue and re-rate the stock.
  • Aligned founder-owners — first-generation builders running the company they created.

The bear case

  • SEBI F&O clampdown — ~55% of revenue rides on retail derivatives the regulator is actively suppressing; further curbs are the dominant risk.
  • Rich valuation — ~60× earnings leaves no margin for error; vulnerable to a sharp de-rating on any soft quarter.
  • Diversification still early — the AMC is sub-scale and loss-making; new lines don't yet move the needle.
  • Cyclicality — broking revenue swings with the market; a bear phase scares off the newest, flightiest investors.
  • Post-IPO overhang & competition — continued VC selling, plus Angel One, banks and fintechs copying the playbook.
The bottom line

Groww is a structurally advantaged, highly profitable platform sitting on top of one of the best long-term trends in India — more people investing more money. The business risk is low; the valuation and regulatory risk is real and concentrated. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers: derivatives revenue share (is the F&O dependence falling?), AMC/lending scale (is the super-app actually working?), and active-client growth (is the lead widening?). Get those right and a 60× multiple compounds into something reasonable; get the F&O swing factor wrong and a richly-priced stock has a long way to fall.
