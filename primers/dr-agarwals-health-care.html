Dr. Agarwal's Health Care, India's eye-care roll-up

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Dr. Agarwal's Health Care runs eye hospitals and clinics — and almost nothing else. It is India's largest single-specialty ophthalmology chain: a network of eye-care centres that perform very high volumes of surgery (above all cataract, plus LASIK, retina, glaucoma and cornea), run out-patient consultations and diagnostics, and sell spectacles, contact lenses and eye-care pharmacy products alongside. Its customers are ordinary patients — an ageing parent needing a clouded lens replaced, a young professional getting LASIK, a diabetic being screened for retinal damage — paying cash, through insurance, or via government schemes. It listed in February 2025, backed by global investors TPG and Temasek, and is controlled by the founding Agarwal family.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a single-specialty chain that grows by acquisition. The Indian eye-care market is a cottage industry — thousands of standalone ophthalmologists and small family clinics. Dr Agarwal's strategy is a roll-up: buy regional eye hospitals, open new clinics, and consolidate that fragmentation under one brand and one standardised surgical system. It exited FY26 with 288 facilities across 10 countries after adding 57 new centres in a single year.
  • One procedure drives the volume. Of the 323,245 surgeries it performed in FY26, the single largest slice is cataract — a high-throughput, largely day-care, ageing-driven operation where a clouded natural lens is swapped for an artificial one. That concentration is not a weakness; it is the engine. A country that is getting older will need more cataract surgery every year for decades, which is what turns eye-care into a structural growth story rather than a cyclical one.
  • The financials are compounding, and priced for it. FY26 revenue rose ~21.6% to ₹2,080 Cr, EBITDA ~22% to ₹614 Cr (a 28.9% margin), and PAT jumped ~52% to ₹168 Cr. Same-store growth ran near 14%. The stock trades on a rich recent-IPO multiple — a market value near ₹16,100 Cr (approx.) on ₹168 Cr of profit — pricing in years of clean acquisition-led compounding.
In plain terms

A general hospital treats every part of the body and has an eye department tucked inside it. Dr Agarwal's inverts that: the whole company is the eye department, replicated as a chain. Doing only one thing lets it run eye surgery like a production line — the same standardised cataract procedure, the same equipment, the same protocols, in centre after centre — which drives down cost and drives up throughput in a way a general hospital's eye wing never can. Growth then comes from two levers stacked on top of each other: adding centres (buying family eye clinics and opening new ones with PE money) and ripening the ones it already has (a new clinic loses money at first, then matures into a high-margin one). The bet is that an ageing India keeps the surgical volume rising while the network keeps widening — and the risk is that buying dozens of clinics a year is far harder to integrate cleanly than it looks on a slide.

01

Eye-care 101: the handful of procedures that run the business

No medical degree needed — just the terms that drive the revenue.

Ophthalmology — medicine of the eye — sounds specialist, but the commercial engine rests on a small set of procedures with very different volumes and economics. A modern eye-hospital chain earns from surgery first, out-patient consultations and diagnostics second, and retail (spectacles, lenses, pharmacy) third. To follow Dr Agarwal's, you need five things.

The procedures and terms that matter
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Cataract surgeryReplacing a clouded natural lens with an artificial intra-ocular lens (IOL). The bulk of surgical volume in India and for Dr Agarwal's. High-throughput, largely elective and day-care (patient walks in and out the same day). Ageing-driven — nearly everyone develops cataracts eventually.
Refractive (LASIK)Reshaping the cornea with a laser to remove spectacle dependence. A premium, self-pay, cash procedure — younger patients, higher ticket, no insurance needed. Fewer volumes than cataract but richer per case.
Retina / glaucoma / corneaThe "medical retina" and specialty side — diabetic retinopathy, macular disease, glaucoma management, corneal transplants. Rising with diabetes and lifestyle disease; more complex, higher-value, and stickier (repeat visits).
ARPOB / revenue per surgeryAverage revenue per operation — the price × mix metric. In FY26 Dr Agarwal's average revenue per surgery was ~₹42,900, up ~8.5% — the number that tells you whether the case-mix is shifting toward premium (LASIK, premium IOLs) or staying value.
Optical & pharmacyThe retail attach — selling spectacles, contact lenses and eye-drops to the same patients. Lower-margin but high-footfall, and it monetises the out-patient traffic that surgery alone doesn't.
The economics that decide everything: cost per surgery at scale

An eye surgery is a small, standardised, repeatable procedure — and that is precisely why volume is the whole game. A surgeon and a well-drilled team can perform many cataract operations in a day; each additional case spreads the fixed cost of the theatre, the equipment and the building over more revenue. So the metric that governs profitability is throughput per centre: a busy eye hospital running high surgical volumes earns a fat margin, while a half-empty one bleeds. This is the deep reason single-specialty works — by doing one procedure again and again, a focused eye chain reaches the throughput that makes each surgery cheap to deliver and profitable to sell, which a general hospital's occasional-use eye theatre never can.

Who pays for eye care

  • Self-pay / cash patients — the premium end. LASIK and premium intra-ocular lenses are elective lifestyle choices people pay for directly. High realisation, no reimbursement risk.
  • Insurance — a growing share as health cover deepens in urban India; cataract is a common covered procedure.
  • Government schemes — state and central health schemes fund large volumes of basic cataract surgery, especially for the elderly poor. High volume, thin price — a floor of demand but a drag on average realisation where the mix tilts that way.

What Dr Agarwal's actually sells: one patient, four ways to earn

The integrated eye-care journey — read this once and the model clicks.

Dr Agarwal's monetises a single patient at several points along one journey. Someone walks in for blurred vision; they get a consultation and diagnostic scans; they may be booked for surgery; they buy spectacles or drops on the way out; and if they have a chronic condition (glaucoma, diabetic retina) they come back for years. Owning the whole path — not just the operating theatre — is what lets the chain squeeze full value from every footfall.

The revenue lines, decoded

  • Surgery — the profit core. Cataract is the volume anchor; refractive (LASIK) and specialty retina/cornea are the higher-value, richer-margin cases. The whole model is built to keep surgical theatres busy, because throughput is what makes eye surgery profitable.
  • Consultation & diagnostics — the funnel. Every surgery starts as an out-patient visit and a diagnostic scan. This is lower-ticket but it is the feeder: no consult traffic, no surgery pipeline. It is also where a new clinic first builds a catchment before the surgical volumes ramp.
  • Optical & pharmacy retail — the attach. Spectacles, contact lenses and eye-care pharmacy sold to patients already in the building. Lower margin than surgery, but it captures spend that would otherwise leak to an outside optician, and it monetises the large out-patient base that never reaches the operating theatre.
Why owning the whole journey matters

A standalone ophthalmologist typically captures only the consultation and the surgery, then waves the patient off to buy spectacles elsewhere and hopes they come back. A chain that owns the diagnostics, the theatre, the optical counter and the follow-up captures the full lifetime value of that eye — often across the patient's whole family, since eye conditions cluster and referrals spread by word of mouth. That is the quiet advantage of scale in a fragmented market: the same footfall that a solo doctor monetises once, an integrated chain monetises repeatedly. The catch is that running an optical retail and pharmacy operation well is a different discipline from surgery — retail execution can dilute margins if it is run loosely rather than as a genuine business.

The family, the PE owners, and the 2025 IPO

A 1957 Chennai clinic, professionalised with TPG and Temasek money.

Dr Agarwal's is unusual in Indian healthcare: a genuine multi-generational medical family — the Agarwals, going back to the ophthalmologist Dr. J. Agarwal and carried forward by Dr. Amar Agarwal and the next generation — that has been paired with institutional private-equity capital to scale a single-specialty chain nationally and abroad. The clinical brand is old (founded 1957); the corporate machine around it is new.

Ownership & structure

Why the ownership shape matters: this is a founder-family brand riding on top of PE-funded consolidation. But it also creates the two structural tensions that define the stock: a PE-selldown overhang above the price, and the ever-present question of whether a doctor-led culture survives being scaled by a spreadsheet.
04

The focused-factory: why doing only eyes is the strategy

The core idea that separates a single-specialty chain from a general hospital.

The instinct of most healthcare investors is that breadth is safety — a hospital that treats everything has many revenue streams. Dr Agarwal's is a bet on the opposite: that narrowness is strength. By doing only eyes, it becomes a "focused factory" — a term from healthcare management for a facility that performs one type of procedure so repeatedly that it beats a generalist on cost, speed and outcomes at the same time.

A dedicated eye hospital runs its theatres at high, standardised throughput; a general hospital's eye department shares theatres and staff with a dozen other specialties, so it never reaches the same volume, cost or repeatability. Focus is the moat.

The focused-factory logic compounds in three ways. Cost — standardising one procedure lets the chain buy intra-ocular lenses and equipment in bulk, train teams narrowly and deeply, and run theatres near-continuously, so the unit cost of a surgery falls. Outcomes — a surgeon who does thousands of the same operation tends to deliver better, more consistent results, which builds the brand and the referrals. Speed — a patient booked into a dedicated eye theatre isn't queued behind emergencies and unrelated surgeries, so day-care throughput stays high. Do all three and you have something a general hospital structurally cannot replicate in its eye wing.

The focused-factory, stated plainly

Imagine the difference between a restaurant with a 40-item menu and one that serves a single dish perfected over decades. The specialist buys ingredients cheaper, cooks faster, and makes the dish better — because it does nothing else. Dr Agarwal's is that specialist for eye surgery. Every rupee of capital, every hire, every protocol points at one organ. That single-mindedness is why a focused eye chain can undercut a general hospital on price and out-perform it on outcomes at the same time — a combination that usually forces a trade-off. It is also why the model travels: the same standardised playbook can be dropped into an acquired family clinic or a brand-new city, which is exactly what makes the roll-up in the next section possible.

05

The roll-up engine: new clinics as a maturity curve, not a revenue number

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to this company.

Every acquisitive chain reports rising total revenue, and investors cheer when it grows. For Dr Agarwal's, total revenue is almost the least informative number, because it blends two very different populations of clinics that sit at opposite ends of a curve. A new or freshly acquired eye clinic loses money for a while; a mature one prints a fat margin. The group's reported profit is just a weighted average of where its centres happen to sit on that ramp. The thesis is not "revenue went up" — it is "the new-clinic cohort is ripening faster than the next batch is dragging."

A new centre starts sub-scale and loss-making, ramps through break-even as it builds a catchment, and settles into a high-margin steady state. The reported group margin (~28.9%) is simply the blend of many clinics at different points on this curve.

Here is the whole company in one mechanism. In FY26 the group opened and absorbed 57 new centres — and management flagged that newly opened greenfield clinics carried a drag of around ₹30 Cr, money spent building catchments that haven't matured yet. That drag is not a problem to be hidden; it is tomorrow's margin bought at today's cost. Each of those 57 centres is a margin liability now that is designed to become a margin asset in three to four years. Meanwhile the older clinics — already mature — grew same-store at around 14% and carried the profitability. The group margin still expanded to 28.9% despite absorbing that new-clinic loss, which is the entire tell.

The device that only fits Dr Agarwal's: the ₹30-crore drag is the product, not the leak

Most companies you judge by whether costs are falling. Here, a chunk of "cost" — the ~₹30 Cr of losses from brand-new clinics — is actually the deliberate output of the strategy: the price of planting the next cohort of centres that will be printing 30%-plus margins in FY29. So the correct way to read this business is to mentally split it in two: a mature estate compounding same-store at ~14% and throwing off cash, funding a growth estate of loss-making new clinics that will graduate into the mature estate. Total revenue tells you nothing about whether that machine is working. What tells you is the combination: is the mature same-store growth strong, is average revenue per surgery rising (₹42,900, +8.5% in FY26), is the group margin holding or expanding while the company keeps adding 50-plus centres a year, and are the acquisitions actually maturing into the margin the model promises? If all four are true at once, the roll-up compounds. If the new clinics don't ripen — or if each new batch of acquisitions is bought too dear — the drag never converts, and you are left paying a premium multiple for a chain that runs to stand still.

This is why the roll-up framing matters more than the headline. A market of thousands of standalone ophthalmologists is exactly the kind of fragmentation a disciplined consolidator can compound into for a decade — buying a respected family eye hospital, keeping its surgeons, stamping the standardised system on it, and folding it into a national brand with better procurement and marketing. The reward is scale in a business where scale genuinely lowers cost. The danger is equally specific: buy too fast, overpay, and fail to integrate, and the goodwill piles up while quality and surgeon loyalty quietly erode. The roll-up is the opportunity and the defining risk — the same activity, judged entirely on execution.

06

Cataract and the ageing wave: why one procedure is a structural tailwind

The demographic engine that turns eye-care into a decades-long growth market.

The genuine surprise about an eye-hospital chain is how much of it rides on a single procedure tied to a single, unstoppable trend. Cataract — the clouding of the eye's natural lens — is overwhelmingly a condition of ageing. As a population gets older, the number of cataracts needing surgery rises almost mechanically. India is ageing and its access to affordable surgery is widening at the same time, which stacks two growth vectors on top of each other.

The insight a generalist misses about eye-care

Hospitals are usually thought of as cyclical, capex-heavy and policy-exposed. A high-volume cataract chain behaves more like a demographic annuity: the number of ageing eyes needing a clouded lens replaced climbs every year regardless of GDP, elections or interest rates, and the procedure is largely elective day-care, so it doesn't need the heavy in-patient infrastructure of a general hospital. That is the quiet structural beauty of the model — a business whose core demand is set by a demographic curve you can see coming decades out. The flip side, and the reason it isn't a free lunch, is that this very attractiveness has drawn a crowd of well-funded competitors chasing the same ageing eyes.

A genuine surprise: the highest-growth pocket in FY26 wasn't volume-cataract at all — it was the premium end. Robotic-assisted cataract procedures grew ~87% year-on-year, and high-end cataract and refractive work outpaced the base. So the company is not just riding an ageing India for more surgeries; it is simultaneously trading patients up the value ladder — from basic subsidised cataract toward premium lenses and laser procedures — which is what lifted average revenue per surgery to ~₹42,900. Two growth engines, volume and mix, running at once.

The Africa footprint: the same model, a different continent

The unusual geography that most Indian hospital chains don't have.

Almost every listed Indian hospital is an India-only story. Dr Agarwal's is not: it runs eye hospitals across several African countries alongside its Indian network — a footprint that spans 10 countries in total. Africa is a minority of revenue (roughly 10%, with ~90% from India) but it is a genuine second leg, and it tells you something about how transferable the focused-factory playbook really is.

The logic is that many African markets look like India did a generation ago in eye care: a large, under-served population, a big untreated cataract backlog, and a fragmented supply of care — but far fewer scaled, standardised providers. A chain that has already industrialised high-volume cataract surgery in India can carry that exact system abroad, where the competitive field is thinner and the clinical need is acute. It is the roll-up logic exported: fragmentation plus a standardised operator equals room to consolidate.

Why Africa is a real option and a real complication

Africa gives Dr Agarwal's something rare among Indian hospital stocks — a large, structurally under-penetrated market where its core competence (cheap, high-quality, high-volume cataract surgery) is exactly what's missing, and where it already has operating experience rather than a slide-deck ambition. That is a genuine long-run optionality most of its domestic rivals simply don't have. But it is also a source of complexity the market tends to under-price: multiple currencies, varied regulation, forex translation, and the sheer managerial stretch of running clinics on two continents. At ~10% of revenue it is a promising second engine, not the main story — and investors should size it as an option, not a certainty.

Same-store maturity and ARPOB: reading the engine correctly

How to tell whether the roll-up is actually compounding or just getting bigger.

Because the group is a blend of clinics at different ages, the numbers that matter are the ones that strip out the noise of "just adding centres." Two do most of the work: same-store growth (how fast the existing, mature estate is growing on its own) and average revenue per surgery / ARPOB (whether the case-mix is climbing the value ladder). Together they tell you if the underlying business is healthy independent of the acquisition treadmill.

The FY26 pattern is about as clean as a roll-up can show. More surgeries (volume up ~14.5% to 323,245) and richer surgeries (revenue per case up ~8.5% to ₹42,900) both contributed, which means the ~14% same-store growth is real underlying demand and mix improvement — not an accounting artefact of consolidating acquired revenue. Crucially, the group EBITDA margin still expanded to 28.9% while carrying the ~₹30 Cr new-clinic drag — evidence that the mature estate is comfortably out-earning the cost of planting the next cohort.

The pair of numbers that separates good roll-ups from bad ones

Any consolidator can grow reported revenue simply by buying more clinics — that proves nothing. The test of quality is whether the clinics it already owns keep growing (strong same-store) and whether it is earning more per patient over time (rising ARPOB), because those are the only ways to grow that don't depend on writing another cheque. In FY26 both were healthy — double-digit same-store and mid-single-digit per-surgery price growth. Watch this pair every quarter: if same-store growth fades toward low single digits, or revenue per surgery stalls, it means the organic engine has weakened and the company is leaning on acquisitions to mask it — the classic way roll-ups start to disappoint even as headline revenue keeps rising.

The industry and the rivals

A fragmented market being consolidated by several well-funded chains at once.

The Indian eye-care market is enormous and overwhelmingly fragmented — dominated by thousands of independent ophthalmologists, small family clinics and the eye departments of general hospitals. That fragmentation is the whole opportunity, but it also means Dr Agarwal's is far from the only one who has spotted it. The competitive set is really "everyone chasing the same consolidation," from PE-backed chains to the incumbent solo doctor next door.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, the market is big and fragmented enough that several chains can consolidate at once without a knife-fight — for now this is a land-grab across a huge under-organised field, not a share-stealing war. Dr Agarwal's is the scale leader, but ASG, Centre for Sight and Maxivision are all pursuing the same fragmentation with real capital, and the more of them there are, the more competition there is for acquisitions — which bids up the price of the family clinics everyone wants to buy. Second, the toughest competitor isn't a chain at all; it's the trusted local eye surgeon a community has used for twenty years. Consolidating a cottage industry means either buying those doctors or winning their patients, and both are slow, relationship-heavy work that a spreadsheet can't shortcut.

The one gauge that settles the thesis: margin holding up while the network keeps expanding

At a market value near ₹16,100 Cr on ₹168 Cr of profit, Dr Agarwal's is not priced as a hospital — it is priced as a compounding platform that will consolidate a fragmented market for a decade. The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A scale leader in a structural market — asked to prove it can consolidate cleanly at a full price.

Dr Agarwal's enters FY27 as the largest organised eye-care chain in a fragmented, ageing-driven market, with real margins, a proven focused-factory model and a second leg in Africa. The frank debate is not whether eye-care is a good business — it plainly is — but whether an acquisition-led chain can keep integrating dozens of clinics a year without eroding quality, and whether the returns on that spending justify a premium recent-IPO multiple with a PE block still looking to exit.

The bull case

  • Structural demand — an ageing India plus a large cataract backlog makes core volume grow with demographics, not the economic cycle.
  • Focused-factory margins — ~28.9% EBITDA, high for healthcare, from standardising one procedure at scale; expanding even while absorbing new-clinic losses.
  • Long consolidation runway — thousands of independent clinics to acquire; the scale leader with PE firepower and a national brand.
  • Healthy organic engine — ~14% same-store growth and rising revenue per surgery (₹42,900, +8.5%) mean growth isn't only bought.
  • Africa optionality — a genuine second market where the focused-factory playbook is scarce and the need is acute.

The bear case

  • Integration / roll-up risk — the defining danger: buying dozens of clinics a year while keeping quality, brand and surgeon loyalty consistent, without goodwill outrunning profit.
  • Rich valuation — a ~90×-plus multiple prices in years of flawless compounding; any stumble in margin or maturity can de-rate it sharply.
  • Crowded consolidation — ASG, Centre for Sight, Maxivision and others bid up the same acquisitions; competition raises the price of growth.
  • Cataract & scheme dependence — heavy reliance on cataract volumes and exposure to insurance/government-scheme pricing pressure.
  • Surgeon & PE-selldown risk — doctors are the real asset and can walk; and a large residual TPG/Temasek block overhangs the stock.
The bottom line

Dr Agarwal's has turned a 1957 family eye clinic into the closest thing India has to an industrialised, national eye-surgery platform — high margins, a demographic tailwind that compounds for decades, and a fragmented market wide enough to consolidate for years. What it must now prove, quarter after quarter, is the one thing a roll-up can never take for granted: that the 50-plus centres it adds each year actually ripen into the margins the model promises, faster than the next batch drags, and that it isn't overpaying to buy that growth in an increasingly crowded field. The cleanest place to watch it is a single, checkable pattern — group EBITDA margin still expanding even as the network and the new-clinic drag keep growing. If that line keeps climbing, the maturity machine is real and the premium is earned. If it flattens while acquisition cheques get bigger, the roll-up has started running to stand still — and the market has priced almost none of that in.
