00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Punjab National Bank ("PNB") is India's second-largest public-sector bank — a 130-year-old, government-controlled lender that gathers deposits from savers across the country and lends to households, farmers, small businesses and corporates. After the 2020 amalgamation that folded Oriental Bank of Commerce and United Bank of India into it, PNB became the country's largest PSU bank after SBI, with a ~₹29.70 lakh crore global business (deposits + advances), roughly 10,000+ branches and a customer base above 18 crore.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It survived the biggest fraud in Indian banking history. In 2018 the ₹14,357-crore Nirav Modi / Mehul Choksi Letter-of-Undertaking scam — fraudulent guarantees issued off-the-books at a single Mumbai branch — detonated inside PNB. Combined with the wider bad-loan crisis, gross NPAs spiked to a staggering 18.38% (from 3.15% a year earlier) and the bank posted a ₹12,283-crore loss in FY18, the largest ever by an Indian bank at the time, followed by a further ₹9,975-crore loss in FY19. The recovery from that near-death event is PNB's defining story.
  • The turnaround is now complete. FY26 is a different institution: a record net profit of ₹16,904 Cr, gross NPAs of just 2.95%, net NPAs of a negligible 0.29%, a fortress ~97% provision coverage, and a Q4 return on assets of 1.06%. Cleaning the book is what turned two years of deep losses into a record profit.
  • It is ~70% owned by the Government of India. That is the double-edge the whole valuation turns on: sovereign backing makes its deposits ultra-safe and cheap, but state ownership, social-lending mandates, the fraud's scar tissue and still-lagging return ratios keep the stock at a P/E of ~7× and below book value (~0.82× P/B) — a steep discount to private peers like HDFC and ICICI, and even a modest discount to PSU peer Bank of Baroda.
In plain terms

Think of Punjab National Bank as a ₹17-lakh-crore deposit machine wired to a ₹12.6-lakh-crore loan book, run by the government. It pays savers a little, lends that money out for more, and pockets the gap. A decade ago the machine was catastrophically broken — a headline-grabbing fraud and a mountain of bad corporate loans pushed bad debts above 18% and drove the bank to record losses. It has since been painstakingly rebuilt: bad loans are back below 3%, net bad loans are almost zero, and the bank now earns a record ~₹16,900 crore a year. The open questions are whether it can defend thin margins in a tough rate cycle, whether it can close the return-ratio gap with better-run PSU peers, and whether the market ever stops treating "PNB" as shorthand for the great banking scandal.

01

Bank economics 101: how a bank actually earns

No finance degree required — just the numbers that drive everything.

A bank is, at its core, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors) and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap between what it earns on loans and pays on deposits — scaled to its assets — is the engine. A handful of terms tell you almost the whole story, and they matter triply for a bank like PNB, whose recent history is a case study in what happens when the "loss" side of the equation runs out of control.

The only banking units you need
TermWhat it means (and PNB's number)
NIINet Interest Income — interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings. The core revenue line. PNB: ~₹10,380 Cr in Q4 FY26 (−3.5% YoY) — pressured by the margin squeeze.
NIMNet Interest Margin — NII as a % of interest-earning assets. The "spread", standardised. PNB: ~2.57% global for FY26 (Q4 domestic 2.61%, down from 2.96%) — the pressure point of the year.

GNPA / NNPAGross / Net Non-Performing Assets — the % of loans that have gone bad. The single best gauge of lending discipline. PNB: 2.95% / 0.29% — a world away from 18.38% in 2018.
PCRProvision Coverage Ratio — how much of the bad loans the bank has already set money aside against. PNB: ~97.14%, meaning almost all recognised bad loans are pre-funded — one of the highest in the industry.
CAR / CET-1Capital Adequacy — the equity cushion versus loans, the buffer against losses. PNB: CRAR 17.74%, Tier-1 15.15% — comfortably above the regulatory floor.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. So a bank gets richer four ways — lend more (grow advances), fund cheaper (more CASA, lower deposit cost), lose less (low NPAs and low credit cost), and earn fees (cards, insurance, forex, distribution). PNB's story is the most dramatic illustration of the third lever in Indian banking: for two years the "loan losses" line — swollen by a single ₹14,000-crore fraud on top of the wider crisis — didn't just dent profit, it obliterated it, driving the bank to record losses. Fix the bad loans and the same interest spread suddenly drops through to profit — which is exactly what happened.

Why "loan losses" are the swing factor for a PSU bank

  • Provisioning is the hidden lever. When a loan turns bad, the bank must set aside money to cover the expected loss — a provision, charged straight against profit. In FY18–FY19 PNB was provisioning tens of thousands of crores at once; as the book cleaned up, that charge collapsed and profit surged. Credit cost (provisions as a % of loans) is the number that made — and unmade — the bank.
  • Slippages are the leading indicator. A "slippage" is a loan freshly turning bad. PNB's fresh slippages are now modest against a ₹12.6-lakh-crore book — the early-warning gauge that the book is healthy, not just historically cleaned.
  • The rate cycle sets the margin. Because most of PNB's loans reprice quickly when the RBI cuts rates, but deposits reprice more slowly, a rate-cut cycle squeezes NIM first — loan yields fall before funding costs do. That is precisely the pinch showing up in FY26's falling margin.
02

How the money flows: deposits → loans → margin

Read this once and the whole bank clicks into place.

A bank is a conveyor belt for money. Savers' deposits come in one end; loans go out the other; the spread between the two is harvested as net interest income. Two things govern how much profit drips out: how cheap the deposits are (the CASA mix) and how much more the loans yield than the deposits cost (the margin). For PNB, the belt runs on one of the largest and stickiest deposit franchises in the country — its historic strength — but the spread being harvested is currently getting thinner.

When the RBI cuts rates, Stage-3 loan yields fall almost immediately while Stage-1/2 deposit costs lag — so the spread narrows first. Holding on to a large, low-cost CASA base is PNB's main defence of the margin.

Why the CASA mix is the whole game

A current account pays 0% and a savings account ~3%; a fixed deposit pays 6.5–7.5%. The more of your funding that sits in low-cost CASA, the cheaper your money — and the wider your margin on the same loan. PNB's CASA share of ~37% — roughly ₹6.09 lakh crore, of which savings deposits alone are ₹5.30 lakh crore — is a genuine strength: PNB has historically had one of the largest, most granular retail deposit bases among PSU banks, built over decades across the Hindi heartland. But CASA is growing slower (~6.3% in FY26) than costlier term deposits (~10.9%), so the mix is drifting the wrong way — a slow, structural headwind to margin that PNB shares with the whole sector.

Volume up, spread down — why both can be true at once

It sounds contradictory that a bank can grow loans ~13% and still see profit growth slow to ~1.6%. Here's why: net interest income = volume × spread. In FY26 PNB pushed volume up hard (advances +12.7%), but the spread (NIM) shrank because loan yields fell with RBI rate cuts while deposit costs stayed sticky and CASA grew slowly. The two roughly cancelled on the interest line — so the record profit was actually powered by the third and fourth levers instead: sharply lower loan-loss provisions and a big jump in fee income (non-interest income +15.2%). The near-term investment question is whether volume and fees keep out-running the margin squeeze, or whether the squeeze finally catches up.

03

The company and who controls it

A 130-year-old institution, majority-owned by the state.

Punjab National Bank was founded in 1894 in Lahore — the first Indian bank to be started purely with Indian capital, with Lala Lajpat Rai among its founders — and nationalised in 1969 along with India's other large banks. It carries deep roots in the freedom movement; the accounts of leaders including Jawaharlal Nehru and Indira Gandhi were once held here. Today it is a professionally-run public-sector bank whose controlling shareholder is the Government of India (~70%), headquartered in New Delhi (Dwarka), with a footprint anchored in northern and eastern India.

Ownership & management

  • Government of India ~70%. The state is the promoter and controlling shareholder. This anchors the stock's identity as a "PSU bank" and drives both its funding advantage and its valuation discount.
  • Public & institutional float ~30%. The balance is held by domestic institutions (LIC, mutual funds ~16%), foreign investors (~6%) and retail (~7%). With the government at ~70%, periodic dilution risk — a government stake sale (OFS) or a fresh QIP to shore up capital or meet minimum-public-shareholding norms — hangs over minority holders.
  • Board-driven, government-appointed leadership. Like all PSU banks, top management (MD & CEO, executive directors) is appointed via the government's process, with fixed tenures — a governance model that differs sharply from the founder- or professional-CEO continuity of private banks. The current MD & CEO, Ashok Chandra, is driving the "PNB 2.0" digital-and-AI transformation.
  • A financial-services group. Alongside the bank sit subsidiaries and stakes in housing finance (a large stake in listed PNB Housing Finance), a life-insurance JV, an AMC (PNB MetLife / PNB mutual-fund distribution ties), and sponsored regional rural banks — a source of fee income and latent value.
04

The Nirav Modi fraud: India's biggest banking scam

The single event that defines PNB — how ₹14,357 crore walked out of one branch, and why it matters for a shareholder.

No other Indian bank carries a scar like this one. In January 2018, PNB disclosed that it had been defrauded of ₹14,357 crore (~US$2 billion) through fraudulent Letters of Undertaking (LoUs) — the single largest banking fraud in Indian history. Orchestrated by fugitive diamond magnate Nirav Modi and his uncle Mehul Choksi (of Gitanjali Gems), with the collusion of PNB insiders, the scam is essential to understanding both PNB's near-death experience and the durability of its recovery today.

How the fraud worked — in plain English

A Letter of Undertaking is a guarantee: PNB promises an overseas branch of another Indian bank that "if our customer doesn't pay, we will." On the strength of that guarantee, the overseas bank hands the customer short-term foreign-currency credit. It is a normal trade-finance tool. The fraud was in how these LoUs were issued at PNB's Brady House branch in Fort, Mumbai:

  • The core system was bypassed. A handful of PNB employees — notably deputy manager Gokulnath Shetty — issued LoUs directly over the global SWIFT messaging network to overseas branches of banks like Allahabad Bank, Axis Bank and Union Bank, without recording them in PNB's Core Banking System (CBS). Because SWIFT and CBS were not integrated, the guarantees existed in the outside world but were invisible on PNB's own books.
  • No collateral, rolled over for years. The LoUs were issued without the required cash margin or sanction, then rolled over repeatedly from as early as 2011 — fresh LoUs raised to repay maturing ones, a classic revolving fraud that ballooned unnoticed.
  • It unravelled when the insiders left. When the colluding employee retired and new staff refused to issue fresh LoUs without margin, the scheme collapsed. The perpetrators had already fled India in early 2018.
The mechanism that mattered: the fraud was not really a "bad loan" — it was a controls failure. Because PNB had guaranteed the money to other banks, it was legally on the hook to honour those LoUs when they were called. That is what turned a criminal scam into a ₹14,000-crore hole in PNB's own accounts.

Where the recovery stands (mid-2026)

  • The perpetrators are cornered but not extradited. A London court has also held Nirav Modi personally liable for further sums.
  • The book is fully provided-for. Crucially for a shareholder today, the fraud losses were fully provisioned years ago. Any recoveries now flow back as write-backs — pure upside to profit, not a plug for an open hole.
Why this is section 04, not a footnote

You cannot understand PNB's investment case without this event. It explains three things at once: why the stock is cheap (the "PNB scam" brand and governance scar keep a discount on the multiple even years later); why the balance sheet is now over-provisioned (a ~97% provision coverage ratio is partly the legacy of being forced to over-prepare after being badly burned); and why controls and technology are now central to strategy (SWIFT-CBS integration, AI-based fraud detection and the "PNB 2.0" overhaul are direct responses). The reputational rebuild — proving that the controls failure was fixed and cannot recur — is as important to the re-rating as the financial clean-up.

05

From a ₹12,000-crore loss to a record profit

The turnaround mechanism — how a lender goes from the largest loss in Indian banking history to a record ₹16,904 Cr profit.

Rewind to FY18. On top of the Nirav Modi fraud, PNB was carrying years of aggressive, poorly-underwritten corporate lending (infrastructure, steel, power) that all turned sour at once. Gross NPAs hit 18.38%, and the bank posted a ₹12,283-crore loss — the largest ever by an Indian bank at the time — then lost a further ₹9,975 crore in FY19. Understanding how a bank climbs out of a hole that deep is the key to the entire PNB investment case, because a bank's earnings are governed less by how much it lends than by how little of what it lent goes bad.

The mechanism: why cleaning bad loans creates profit

A bank's profit is roughly net interest income + fees − operating costs − loan-loss provisions − tax. In the crisis years, the provisions line was so vast — the bank was forced to set aside money against the fraud plus tens of thousands of crores of bad corporate loans — that it wiped out the entire spread the bank earned, pushing it deep into losses. Why this drives the "re-rating"

A "re-rating" means the market pays a higher multiple for the same earnings because it trusts them more. For years, investors valued PNB at a deep discount because its profits were not just fragile — they had been proven capable of turning into record losses overnight. Now that GNPA is under 3%, net NPA is a negligible 0.29%, provision coverage is ~97% and the fraud is fully behind it, those earnings look far more durable. The bull thesis: a bank earning a record ~₹16,900 Cr, with a clean book and ~13% ROE, should trade closer to book value — yet PNB trades at ~0.82× book. The bear counter is that PNB's returns still lag better-run PSU peers (§09), and the "scam bank" scar and PSU-discount reasons (§10) never fully go away.

Why this is the section that matters: almost every good number in PNB's FY26 results exists because the provisioning burden fell from crisis-era levels to normal. The franchise didn't suddenly get better at lending; it stopped hemorrhaging money on old loans and the fraud. Watch slippages, credit cost and recoveries every quarter — plus any recovery from the Nirav Modi/Choksi asset auctions, which is pure write-back upside.
06

The 2020 amalgamation: three banks into one

The consolidation that made PNB India's second-largest PSU bank.

On 1 April 2020 — as part of India's mega PSU-bank consolidation — the government folded two other public-sector banks, Oriental Bank of Commerce (a solid Delhi/north-India lender) and United Bank of India (a Kolkata-based eastern-India bank), into Punjab National Bank. Overnight, PNB vaulted to ~₹18 lakh crore of total business, over 11,000 branches and a customer base above 18 crore — cementing it as the country's second-largest public-sector bank after SBI.

Why the government did it

  • PSU consolidation. India had too many small, sub-scale state banks. The 2020 mega-merger collapsed ten PSU banks into four strong anchors; PNB was designated one of those anchor banks — the surviving entity into which the others merged — meant to create a globally competitive lender, cut duplicated costs and diversify risk.
  • Geographic complementarity. PNB's traditional strength was the north and Hindi heartland; OBC deepened its Delhi/Punjab/Haryana franchise, while United Bank brought a strong eastern-India (Bengal, north-east) network PNB largely lacked. The combined bank gained a genuinely pan-India footprint.
  • Scale & a bigger deposit franchise. The merger gave PNB the deposit base, branch reach and balance-sheet heft to sit second only to SBI — and one of the largest low-cost deposit franchises in the system.
The amalgamation's legacy in today's numbers

Because this was a government-engineered merger executed just as the pandemic hit, the integration was a genuine feat — branches, IT systems, roughly 18 crore accounts and three different loan books had to be fused into one. That digestion is now largely behind PNB: systems were unified, and the extra bad loans the merged banks brought in (United Bank in particular was NPA-heavy) have long since been provisioned. The lasting effect is scale and geographic balance — today's PNB is a genuinely national bank spanning north, east and beyond. It also means comparisons to pre-2020 figures can mislead: the FY21-onward balance sheet is three banks in one, which is why the numbers step up sharply from that point.

07

Deposits, CASA and the margin squeeze

The live pressure point — how a bank earns, and why the spread is under strain right now.

With the bad-loan problem solved, the swing factor for PNB's core earnings has shifted to the margin. And the margin is being squeezed from both sides: the RBI's rate-cut cycle drags loan yields down quickly, while a deposit war — banks competing hard for a shrinking pool of household savings — keeps funding costs stubbornly high. FY26's global NIM slid to 2.57%, with domestic Q4 NIM down to 2.61% from 2.96% a year earlier — nearly a 35-basis-point compression that dragged Q4 NII down ~3.5% even as the loan book grew ~13%.

Why margins are hard to defend right now

  • Loans reprice faster than deposits. A large share of PNB's loans are linked to external benchmarks (the RBI repo rate). When the RBI cuts, those loan rates drop almost immediately — but the bank's existing fixed deposits keep paying their old, higher rates until they mature. So in a rate-cut cycle, income falls before costs do, and NIM compresses first.
  • The deposit war. Indian households are increasingly moving savings into mutual funds, equities and higher-yield products. That shrinks the pool of cheap bank deposits, so every bank — public and private — must offer higher fixed-deposit rates to attract funds. Higher deposit costs eat directly into the spread.
  • CASA is growing slower than term deposits. PNB's cheap CASA grew ~6.3% in FY26 while costlier term deposits grew ~10.9% — so the funding mix is quietly drifting toward pricier money, a slow structural drag on margin even though the CASA share (~37%) remains a relative strength.

Why the deposit franchise is the ultimate moat

Anyone can lend money; the hard, valuable part of banking is gathering deposits cheaply and reliably. A bank with a sticky, low-cost deposit base can lend profitably through any cycle; one that has to "buy" deposits at high rates is always squeezed. PNB's edge here is real: as a government-majority bank with a 130-year brand and one of the deepest branch networks in the Hindi heartland, its deposits are perceived as ultra-safe, so households park money with it even at slightly lower rates — giving it a ₹17-lakh-crore, ~37%-CASA funding base that few peers can match. That is a genuine moat. But it is being tested by a savings market that increasingly prizes returns over safety. Watch the cost of deposits and CASA ratio: if they stabilise as the rate-cut cycle ends, NIM can recover; if CASA keeps lagging, the margin stays under pressure.

08

The RAM shift and the PNB One / PNB 2.0 push

Moving the loan book toward higher-yield, granular lending — and distributing it digitally.

The other half of PNB's post-crisis strategy is a deliberate shift in the loan mix toward what PSU banks call the "RAM" book — Retail, Agriculture and MSME. The bad-loan crisis was a corporate-lending disaster — a handful of large borrowers (and one enormous fraud) blew a hole in the book. The lesson learned across every PSU bank was the same: grow the granular, secured RAM book, where losses are small and diversified, and be far more selective with big corporate loans. That mix shift is both safer and higher-yielding — retail and MSME loans typically earn more than wholesale corporate loans.

PNB One and the "PNB 2.0" transformation

PNB One is the bank's unified mobile-banking super-app — the single digital front door for its ~18-crore customer base. It sits at the centre of the broader "PNB 2.0" transformation programme under MD & CEO Ashok Chandra, a heavy push into digitalisation and artificial intelligence. It matters for three reasons:

  • Cheaper distribution. Onboarding customers, opening accounts and disbursing small retail loans through an app costs a fraction of doing it through a branch. With 95%+ of transactions already digital, PNB can grow its customer count without proportionally growing cost — even as it selectively adds ~250 new branches for reach.
  • Deposit and cross-sell reach. A good app helps gather low-cost CASA deposits and cross-sell cards, insurance and investments — feeding both the funding side (cheaper deposits) and the fee side, where non-interest income jumped 15.2% in FY26.
  • Controls and fraud defence. After the Nirav Modi episode, technology is not just about growth — AI-based fraud detection and fully integrated systems are a direct answer to the controls failure that caused the scam. Rebuilding trust in the plumbing is part of the strategy.
Why the retail shift is a quiet margin defence

Here's the elegant part: the same strategy that made PNB safer also makes it more profitable per rupee lent. Retail and MSME loans (home, auto, personal, small-business) typically yield more than large corporate loans, which are priced razor-thin because big companies can shop around. So by tilting the book toward RAM — which grew ~12% versus a slower corporate book — PNB is quietly lifting its average loan yield, which partly offsets the deposit-cost pressure squeezing its margin. Digital (PNB One) makes that shift cheaper to execute at scale. The risk to watch is unsecured personal lending, where a downturn can bite — but PNB's book remains dominated by secured housing, vehicle and agri loans.

09

The lagging return ratios — and the catch-up thesis

Why PNB is cheaper than Bank of Baroda even after the turnaround — and what would close the gap.

PNB has done the hard part — the book is clean. But a clean book is necessary, not sufficient. The number that separates a good bank from a great one is return on assets (ROA) — how much profit a bank squeezes from each rupee of its balance sheet. Here PNB still trails its closest PSU peer. PNB's ROA reached ~1.06% in Q4 FY26 (with the full year lower); Bank of Baroda already runs at ~1.0%+ on a full-year basis, and SBI higher still. That gap — and whether PNB can close it — is the crux of the stock's relative valuation.

Why PNB's returns lag — the mechanics

  • A thinner net interest margin. PNB's NIM (~2.57% global) sits below Bank of Baroda's (~2.9%) and SBI's. Because NIM is the single biggest driver of ROA for a bank, even a 30-basis-point gap on a ₹17-lakh-crore balance sheet is a very large amount of foregone profit. This is the core reason PNB's returns lag.
  • Historically higher cost and slower productivity. As the bank that absorbed the most consolidation and the deepest crisis, PNB has carried a larger, less-productive branch/employee base to digest. Its cost-to-income and per-branch profitability have trailed the best PSU peers — though FY26 saw operating expenses actually fall ~2.5%, a sign the efficiency drive is biting.
  • The over-provisioning drag turns into an advantage. PNB's ~97% provision coverage is higher than it strictly needs to be — a legacy of the fraud. That depressed profits on the way up (more money set aside), but it now means very little future provisioning is required, and recoveries flow straight to profit. This is a lever that could help close the gap.

The catch-up thesis, and why the market is sceptical

Until PNB's full-year ROA durably matches its peers', the discount is arguably deserved.

10

Government ownership and the PSU-discount debate

Why a cleaned-up, record-profit bank still trades below book value — and whether that ever changes.

Here is the puzzle at the heart of the PNB stock: it just posted a record profit, has a fortress ~97% provision coverage and net NPAs of almost zero — yet the market prices it at a P/E of ~7× and ~0.82× book, while private peers HDFC Bank and ICICI Bank trade at ~18–20× earnings and 2–3× book. That gap — the "PSU discount" — is not an accident. It reflects real structural concerns about government-owned banks, sharpened in PNB's case by the fraud, and whether it narrows is a major driver of the stock's return.

Why PSU banks trade at a discount

  • Governance & non-commercial mandates. The government is both owner and policymaker. PSU banks can be asked to support social schemes, farm-loan waivers, priority-sector lending and national-priority projects — lending that may not maximise shareholder returns. Management is government-appointed on fixed tenures.
  • The scar tissue — and PNB's is the deepest. Institutional investors remember the FY18–FY19 crisis; PNB, as the bank at the epicentre of the largest fraud in Indian history, carries the heaviest reputational discount of the lot. Even a clean book today does not fully erase that memory.
  • Dilution risk. With the government at ~70%, there is persistent overhang from potential equity raises or government share sales (OFS) that could dilute minority holders — and PSU banks have historically diluted at low valuations to shore up capital.
  • Slower structural growth & lagging returns. Private banks have taken market share for two decades on service, technology and underwriting, and PNB's returns still trail even PSU peers (§09). The market extrapolates that.
The re-rating case vs the value-trap case

The bull ("closing the gap"): PNB's fundamentals now stand up — record profit, sub-3% GNPA, ~97% coverage, strong capital. If it sustains this and closes the ROA gap with Bank of Baroda, the market should stop pricing it like a fragile, scandal-prone lender and re-rate it from ~0.82× book toward, or above, book value — a large move even without earnings growth. Government interest in higher PSU valuations (it makes divestment easier) supports this.

The bear ("value trap"): PSU banks have looked "cheap" for a decade and the discount has never fully closed, because the structural reasons — government control, mandate risk, dilution, the fraud scar and thinner returns — never go away. A clean book is necessary but not sufficient; without a durable return record and a change in governance, the discount is arguably the market correctly pricing lower-quality, government-directed earnings.

How to hold both ideas: PNB can be a genuinely healed, cheap bank and a stock whose discount only partly closes. The realistic middle case is that a proven-durable clean book plus a closing ROA gap earns a modest re-rating (toward ~1× book) plus a dividend — a decent return — rather than full convergence to private-bank multiples. The swing factor is credibility: every quarter of clean, stable results chips away at the discount; any fresh asset-quality wobble, controls lapse or dilutive raise widens it again.

Financials & valuation

Clean book, record profit, still-thin returns, at a striking discount.

PNB's financials read like a healed but not-yet-elite PSU bank: strong loan growth, a genuinely clean balance sheet, a record profit — but a thin margin, returns that still lag peers, and a valuation that prices in heavy scepticism. The debate is less "is it a healthy bank" (it plainly is now) and more "how durable and how high can the returns go, and will the discount ever close".

How to think about the valuation

PNB trades around a ~₹1.23 lakh crore market cap (~₹107/share) on FY26 earnings — roughly ~6.7× earnings, ~0.82× book value (book ~₹130), with a ~2.8% dividend yield. PNB's sub-book valuation is the market applying a large "PSU discount" — pricing in government control, mandate/dilution risk, the fraud scar and still-lagging returns (§09, §10). The bull case is that a durable clean book plus a closing ROA gap earns a re-rating toward 1×+ book (a substantial move) plus a dividend; the bear case is that the discount is structural. Unlike a private bank, the debate here is more about the multiple than the earnings.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A healed, cheap PSU giant — the debate is durability, margins, returns and the discount.

Punjab National Bank has done the hardest thing in Indian banking: it survived the largest fraud in the country's history and a near-existential bad-loan crisis, and climbed back to a clean book and a record ~₹16,900 Cr profit. The investment debate now is about whether the returns can rise and stay high (can it defend margins and close the ROA gap with peers?) and whether the market ever pays up for a government-owned bank still tagged by its scandal.

The bull case

  • Clean, de-risked, over-provided book — GNPA 2.95%, NNPA 0.29%, PCR ~97%; the fraud and crisis are fully provided-for and behind it.
  • Record profitability & a strong deposit franchise — record ₹16,904 Cr profit, ~37% CASA, ₹17-lakh-crore deposit base, second-largest PSU bank.
  • Very cheap valuation — ~6.7× P/E, ~0.82× book for a ~13% ROE bank; cheaper than even Bank of Baroda, so a re-rating is a big return.
  • Catch-up optionality — Q4 exit ROA already ~1.06%; falling costs, a richer RAM mix and fraud-asset recoveries could close the gap with peers.
  • Recovery upside — any cash from the Nirav Modi / Choksi asset auctions flows back as pure write-back profit.

The bear case

  • Margin squeeze — NIM slipped to 2.57%; rate cuts and the deposit war keep the thinnest margin among large PSU peers under pressure, capping core profit growth.
  • Lagging returns — full-year ROA and NIM still trail Bank of Baroda and SBI; the market wants a sustained track record before it re-rates.
  • The deepest scar — as the "scam bank", PNB carries the heaviest reputational and governance discount; any controls lapse would reopen the wound.
  • Dilution overhang — with the state at ~70%, equity raises or OFS could dilute minority holders, often at low valuations.
  • Cyclical & governance risk — asset quality can turn in a downturn; government-appointed management and mandate risk cap the quality premium.
The bottom line

Punjab National Bank is a genuinely healed public-sector giant trading at a fraction of private-bank valuations — and a shade below even its closest PSU peer. The business risk is far lower than it was a decade ago: the book is clean, capital is strong, provisioning is fortress-like, and the fraud that nearly sank it is fully behind the balance sheet. The three live questions are: can it defend the thin margin through the rate cycle; can it close the return-ratio gap with Bank of Baroda and SBI; and will a durable clean record finally narrow the discount that the scam still imposes. The story over the next few years will be written in a handful of numbers each quarter — NIM and cost of deposits, slippages and credit cost, ROA versus peers, and any fraud-asset recovery. Deliver on those and a cheap, clean, systemically important bank can re-rate and pay you a dividend while you wait; let margins, returns or controls slip, and it stays what the market fears — a permanently discounted PSU.
