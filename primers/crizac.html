Crizac, the toll-booth between agents and universities

A complete walk through a Kolkata-born, technology-led business that sits in the middle of the global student-migration flow — matching thousands of local recruitment agents to Western universities, taking a commission on every student who enrols, and running one of the most cash-generative, asset-light models on the Indian market. It listed only in July 2025, so its public track record is short. And nearly all of its money still comes from one country's universities.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Crizac Limited runs a B2B education platform — a piece of software and a set of relationships that connect two groups who need each other but struggle to find each other at scale: local student-recruitment agents scattered across India, Africa and the rest of Asia, and universities in the UK, Canada, Ireland, Australia and New Zealand that want to fill seats with international students. Crizac is the exchange in the middle. An agent uses its platform to submit a student's application to a partner university; if that student enrols and pays, the university pays Crizac a commission, and Crizac shares part of it with the agent. Founded in Kolkata in 2011, it listed on the NSE and BSE in July 2025.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a commission toll-booth, not a school. Crizac owns no campus, teaches no student, and carries almost no physical assets. It earns a cut of tuition — paid by the university, not the student — for delivering an enrolled international student through its agent network. That makes it remarkably asset-light and high-margin: in FY26 it turned ~₹1,042 Cr of revenue into ~₹219 Cr of net profit, a net margin above 20% and an EBITDA margin around 27%, while sitting on net cash rather than debt.
  • Almost all of the money comes from one country. The overwhelming majority of Crizac's revenue — by most estimates around 90–95%+ — is tied to UK universities. That single fact is the company's greatest strength (deep relationships, scale, a proven corridor) and its single greatest vulnerability (when Britain tightens student-visa rules, Crizac's volumes feel it directly). FY26 was still a strong growth year; the strain showed up in the FY27 first-quarter revenue dip.
  • It is a young listed company doing two things at once. It is compounding the core UK-heavy B2B engine and spending its IPO-era cash pile on bolt-on acquisitions to build a direct-to-student (B2C) arm and open new destination and source markets. The IPO itself (July 2025, ~₹860 Cr) was entirely an offer for sale — existing owners cashing out, no new money into the company — and the stock has since traded well below its issue price. The public track record is barely a year long.
In plain terms

Picture the global business of sending students abroad. A family in Hyderabad or Lagos wants their child at a British university but has no idea how to navigate applications, offers and visas; a mid-sized university in Manchester wants those students but cannot possibly recruit in seventy countries itself. In between sits a sprawling, informal trade of local recruitment agents. Crizac's insight was to become the plumbing for that trade — one platform where an agent lodges an application, one counterparty the university deals with instead of thousands of small agents, one place the commission is collected and split. Because it takes a slice of tuition rather than paying for classrooms or salaries-per-student, almost every extra enrolment drops a high-margin sliver to the bottom line. The catch is that its plumbing is plumbed mostly to Britain — and Britain has been turning the tap.

01

The business, in one page: what an "education B2B platform" actually is

No jargon — just the players, the product, and who pays whom.

Every year millions of students try to study in a country other than their own. That flow — from source countries like India, Nigeria, Bangladesh and Nepal toward destination countries like the UK and Canada — is enormous, fragmented, and hard to organise. Crizac makes its living by organising a slice of it. To follow the company you only need six terms.

The one structural fact that shapes everything: Crizac is paid by the university, not the student

This is the hinge of the whole model, and it is easy to miss. A student never pays Crizac a rupee. Crizac's customer is the university, which pays a commission out of the tuition it collects, as a cost of acquiring an international student it would otherwise struggle to reach. That single design choice explains almost everything downstream: why the business is asset-light (no student-facing cost to carry), why it is cash-generative (universities pay commissions; agents are paid only afterward, out of money already received), and why the whole enterprise ultimately rides on how many international students the destination countries are willing to admit. Crizac is a middleman on a flow it does not control — it can widen its share of the flow, but not the size of the river.

The three parties, and what each wants

  • The student & family — want a place at a good overseas university and help getting there. They deal with a local agent, not with Crizac. Demand is driven by aspiration, currency, job prospects abroad, and — crucially — whether the destination country is issuing visas.
  • The agent (counselling partner) — wants deal-flow, fast offers, reliable payment and a wide choice of universities to place students with. Crizac's platform gives them one login to reach many institutions. The agent is loyal to whoever pays promptly and gives the widest menu.
  • The university — wants to fill seats with paying international students without running a recruitment operation in seventy countries. It pays a commission for that convenience, and it values a partner that can be trusted to send genuine, visa-worthy applicants — not just volume.
02

The commission mechanism: follow the money, and the cash

The single most important thing to understand — get this and the financials make sense.

Crizac's economics are unusually elegant, and they come down to the order in which money moves. The university pays Crizac first, for a student who has already enrolled and paid tuition; Crizac pays the sourcing agent second, out of money it already holds. That sequence is what turns a simple matchmaking fee into a business that generates cash before it spends it.

Crizac collects from the university before it settles with the agent — so growth is funded by the model itself, not by borrowing. This is what "negative working capital" means in practice.

Two features of this mechanism are worth dwelling on, because they explain the quality of the earnings.

It only gets paid for results

Crizac's revenue is tied to enrolments, not applications. An agent can lodge a thousand hopeful applications; Crizac earns on the ones that turn into a paying, admitted student. This acceptance-based model protects it from the classic education-agent problem of inflated, low-quality application volumes: padding the pipeline with weak applications does nothing for revenue, so the incentive is to send students who will actually get in and go. Universities value exactly this discipline, which is part of why they keep paying commissions rather than recruiting alone.

It gets paid before it pays out

Because the university pays Crizac and Crizac then pays the agent, the company is structurally cash-generative and carries little or no debt. It does not need to fund a big receivable to grow; each enrolled student brings cash in ahead of the cash going out. By the first quarter of FY27 the company was sitting on a net cash position of over ₹560 Cr with a return on equity near 29% — the financial signature of a business that turns commissions into cash without heavy reinvestment. That cash pile is now the fuel for its acquisition strategy (section 08).

Why the "paid by the university, paid first" design is the whole moat

Strip away the technology talk and Crizac's durable advantage is a position, not a product. It has spent more than a decade becoming a trusted counterparty to universities — accredited, compliant, able to deliver genuine enrolments at scale — and simultaneously the widest, most reliable payer for agents. Each side is more valuable to Crizac because the other is there: universities like it because it aggregates thousands of vetted agents; agents like it because it offers many universities and pays on time. A new entrant has to build both sides at once, against an incumbent that already sits in the middle of the flow and gets paid before it spends. That two-sided position — plus the compliance track record universities insist on — is more of a barrier than the software itself.

The asymmetry to hold in mind: broad on the left, narrow on the right

Crizac's network is deliberately wide where it can be and unavoidably narrow where it matters most. On the supply side it is spread across dozens of countries and thousands of agents — genuinely diversified, and getting more so as Africa grows. On the demand side, where the commission is actually paid, it is concentrated on the UK. So the reassuring breadth of the source map is not the same as safety: a business can recruit students from seventy countries and still have its revenue decided by the immigration policy of one. Read the two maps separately, and weight the destination one.

A Kolkata family business that went public in 2025 — with a twist worth understanding.

Crizac is a promoter-family company that grew quietly for over a decade before its 2025 listing. Understanding who owns it, and the unusual shape of its IPO, tells you a lot about the incentives on the cap table.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the founding Agarwal family — including Pinky Agarwal, Manish Agarwal and Dr Vikash Agarwal, alongside other family and associated promoters — collectively hold roughly 80% of the company (about 79.9% as of mid-2026). The public float is correspondingly small, which can amplify share-price moves. Confirm the exact promoter percentage in the latest shareholding filing.
  • The IPO was 100% offer-for-sale (worth pausing on): the entire ~₹860 Cr raised in July 2025 went to selling shareholders, not into the company. No fresh capital was raised, no new plant or product was funded by the listing — because a cash-generative, asset-light business does not need external money to grow. The listing gave early owners liquidity and a public currency, not a war chest; the war chest was already the internally-generated cash.
  • Institutions are still light: as of mid-2026, FIIs held ~1.6% and DIIs ~3.2% — modest for a listed company, reflecting both the small float and the market's wait-and-see stance on a newly public, single-corridor business.
Why the OFS shape and the short track record matter: a listing that raises no money for the company is neither good nor bad on its own — for a debt-free, cash-rich business it is logical. But it means the IPO was, in effect, a sale by insiders, and the market has repriced the shares well below the ₹245 issue level since. Combined with a public history barely a year long, that leaves investors judging Crizac on a very short reel of reported quarters and a promoter group that still controls ~80% of the votes. There is less public financial history here than for most names of comparable size — a reason for extra care, not a verdict.

The unit economics: why the margins are so high — and what could move them

The heart of the investment case, and it is specific to a commission platform.

The reason Crizac is interesting as a business — and expensive to replicate — is the shape of its profit and loss. It has almost no cost of goods: no campus, no inventory, no fleet of salaried counsellors per student. Its main costs are the commission it shares with agents, its technology and its people. What is left after paying the agent is an unusually fat, cash-backed margin. The whole case rests on whether that margin persists as the company scales and diversifies.

Read that alongside the cash. A 21% net margin would be impressive on its own; what makes it unusual is that the profit converts to cash rather than being locked in receivables or reinvested in hard assets. Because the university pays before the agent is paid (section 02), growth releases cash instead of consuming it — which is why the company sits on net cash and posts a return on equity near 29%. High margin and high cash conversion and high return on capital is a rare trio; it is what a genuinely asset-light platform looks like when it works.

The device that only fits a commission platform: revenue is a share of tuition it never touches

Here is the mechanism in one line. Crizac's revenue is a percentage of tuition that flows between a student and a university it is not party to — it books the commission, pays the agent a slice, and keeps the rest, with almost nothing in between. There is no raw material to buy, no factory to run, no product to hold. So the two questions that decide the margin are simply: how much of the commission does it have to give the agent (the split), and how many enrolments flow through per rupee of fixed cost (the operating leverage). FY26 improved on both. The risk to the margin is not a cost spike — there is barely a cost base to spike — but a squeeze on the take: universities trimming commissions in a tighter market, or agents demanding a bigger share as competition for them intensifies. Watch the commission take-rate and the agent split, not a cost line.

The flip side of a light model is that there is little to protect the flow if it falls. Crizac cannot warehouse students or diversify into a hard asset when the corridor tightens; its earnings are only as large as the volume of enrolments moving through, times the take on each. That is exactly why the concentration on a single destination — the subject of the next section — is not a footnote but the central risk. A high margin on a volume you do not control is a good business only for as long as the volume holds.

06

The UK concentration: the one number that defines the risk

Treated straight — this is the single most important sentence in the whole primer.

For all its network breadth on the supply side, Crizac's revenue rests on a remarkably narrow base on the demand side: the great majority of its money — by most estimates around 90–95%+ — comes from UK universities. This is not a nuance to be softened. It is the defining feature of the risk profile, and any honest reading of the company has to sit with it first.

Estimates of the exact UK share differ across sources (commonly cited between ~90% and ~97%), but every version tells the same story: this is one of the most destination-concentrated businesses of its size on the market. Treat the precise figure as approximate; treat the concentration as certain.

Why is it so UK-heavy? Because that is where Crizac started, where it built its deepest university relationships over a decade, and where the international-student economics have historically been most attractive to recruit into. Concentration of this kind is often the by-product of doing one thing very well for a long time. The problem is that a strength built by depth becomes a vulnerability the moment the single market it depends on changes its rules — and Britain's international-student policy has been in flux.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with

A concentrated business can grow strongly for years and still carry a fragility that never shows up until it does. Crizac's UK dominance delivered the FY26 growth and is the reason the FY27 first quarter turned down when UK visa sentiment cooled — the same corridor produced both. So the health of this company can never be read from a single good year. You have to ask a second question every quarter: what is the UK doing to international-student visas, and is the destination mix broadening? A policy tightening in one country can turn a compounding growth story into a flat or shrinking one for reasons entirely outside the company's control, precisely because the company chose — reasonably — to be very good at one corridor rather than mediocre at five.

Read this as the base rate, not a scare: the UK remains a large, structurally attractive destination, and Crizac is a scaled, trusted operator within it — that is real. But an investor cannot treat FY26's growth as a clean run-rate while ~90%+ of revenue depends on the visa and enrolment policy of a single government. The concentration is the reason the stock trades where it does, and the reason management is spending its cash to diversify. Both facts belong in the same sentence.
07

The visa-policy weather: the external force that writes the year

Crizac manages around it, but does not author it — the core of the risk profile.

If Paradeep's year is written by the monsoon and the subsidy, Crizac's is written by immigration policy in its destination countries. The number of student visas a country is willing to issue — and the rules attached to them — sets the size of the pool Crizac fishes in. When the UK, Canada or Australia tighten, the effect on volumes is direct and fairly quick.

The recent backdrop has been unusually policy-heavy. Across the major English-speaking destinations, governments have moved to cool record post-pandemic student inflows — through measures such as restrictions on students bringing dependants, tighter scrutiny of the post-study work routes that make the destinations attractive, higher financial thresholds, and in some cases outright caps on numbers. Because a large share of the reason students choose these countries is the ability to work or settle afterward, changes to those rules feed straight back into demand — and therefore into Crizac's enrolments and commissions.

Notice how the forces compound. A benign visa environment (force 1) is worth far more when Crizac is diversified across destinations (force 2 low) and source demand is strong (force 3). The worst configuration is a visa clampdown in the one country that supplies almost all the revenue, while the company is still mostly single-corridor — which is close to the situation the FY27 guidance is navigating. Management's response, sensibly, is to attack force 2: reduce the concentration so that no single government's policy can write the whole year.

The uncomfortable summary a first-timer should internalise

Crizac's job is to run an excellent two-sided platform and then live with the immigration decisions of a handful of Western governments — decisions made for domestic political reasons that have nothing to do with Crizac and everything to do with its revenue. It can improve its odds: broaden destinations, deepen the agent base, move into direct-to-student and adjacent services. What it cannot do is guarantee any single year, because the biggest input to that year — how many students a country will admit — is set in Westminster, Ottawa and Canberra, not in Kolkata. FY26 was a year the policy weather was, on balance, still kind. FY27's guidance reflects a year it turned. A disciplined investor prices the good years knowing the model does not control whether they repeat.

08

Diversification & deals: spending the cash to widen the base

The self-help strategy — reduce the single-corridor risk before it forces the issue.

Management clearly understands the concentration problem, and its answer is to deploy the company's cash into acquisitions that widen the business on three fronts at once: new destination markets, new source regions, and a move from pure agent-based B2B toward direct-to-student (B2C) and adjacent services. FY26 into FY27 has been a burst of bolt-on dealmaking to that end.

Why the acquisition route makes sense here — and what to watch

Building a new destination corridor or a B2C counselling brand from scratch is slow; buying an operator that already has the relationships compresses years into a transaction. For a business whose central risk is concentration and whose central asset is cash, spending that cash to diversify is exactly the right instinct. But bolt-on acquisitions carry their own homework: the returns depend on paying sensible prices, integrating cultures and systems, and not letting a spree of small deals blur the focus that made the core so profitable. The prize is a Crizac that is less hostage to one government's visa policy; the risk is a Crizac that dilutes a superb single-corridor model into a mediocre multi-corridor one. The next few years of margins and organic growth will show which it is becoming.

09

The industry and the rivals

A fragmented global trade with a few scaled platforms and a long tail of agents.

International student recruitment is a huge but famously fragmented business, historically run by countless small local agents. The structural trend Crizac rides is consolidation onto platforms — universities increasingly prefer to deal with a few large, compliant aggregators rather than thousands of individual agents. That trend creates the opportunity and also the competition.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, Crizac does not compete mainly on price to the student — the student pays nothing — but on the quality of its two-sided network: the breadth of universities it can offer agents, the reliability and speed with which it pays them, and the trust and compliance record that keeps universities commissioning it. The best-connected, most-trusted aggregator wins the flow. Second, the competitive threat runs in two directions: bigger, more diversified platforms like IDP and ApplyBoard above it, and the risk of disintermediation below it, as students go direct or universities build their own recruitment. Crizac's answer to both is the same — deepen the network, add services, and edge into B2C — which is why the diversification of section 08 is competitive strategy as much as risk management.

10

Financials & valuation

A high-quality, cash-rich compounder on a de-rated multiple — the question is the corridor, not the accounts.

On the numbers alone, Crizac looks like a rare thing: fast-growing, very high-margin, debt-free and cash-generative. Yet the stock trades well below its IPO price and on a modest earnings multiple. That gap between quality-of-business and rating is the whole valuation story, and it comes down almost entirely to the market's discount for the UK concentration and the newly public, short-track-record status.

The way to read the valuation is to separate two questions the market is fusing. The first is quality: is this a genuinely good business? On margins, cash conversion, return on equity and the two-sided network moat, the answer is clearly yes. The second is durability: can that quality survive a UK visa clampdown and a diversification that is still young? That is genuinely uncertain, and it is what the de-rated multiple and the sub-issue-price stock are pricing. A low multiple on a high-quality business is either an opportunity or a warning; here it is the market saying the corridor risk is real and the public history too short to be sure.

The one relationship that settles the thesis: destination mix, read with enrolment growth

At a low-teens multiple, Crizac does not need a re-rating to reward an owner — it needs the enrolment volume to hold and the destination mix to broaden. So the metrics that prove or break the case are the UK revenue share (is it falling as the acquisitions and new corridors kick in?) and organic enrolment growth (is the core still compounding beneath the M&A?), read together, quarter by quarter, against the visa-policy backdrop. If the UK share drifts down while enrolments keep growing and the ~20%+ net margin holds, the cheap multiple looks like a mispricing of a quality compounder. If a UK clampdown drags volumes while diversification stalls, the FY26 numbers will read as a high-water mark for a single-corridor model. Volume times take-rate, spread across more than one country — that is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality, cash-rich platform asked one question: can it widen the base before the corridor narrows?

Crizac enters FY27 in strong financial shape — debt-free, cash-rich, high-margin, with a proven core and an acquisition engine aimed squarely at its biggest weakness. What no student-recruitment platform can promise is the one thing that decides everything: how many students the destination governments will let in. The FY27 guidance for a flattish year with a second-half recovery is the company's own acknowledgement that the near term is in policy's hands. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Exceptional economics — ~27% EBITDA and ~21% net margins, ~29% ROE, net cash and negative working capital; a genuinely asset-light platform that converts profit to cash.
  • A real two-sided moat — ~16,000 agents and 170+ universities, plus a decade-long compliance and payment record universities trust; hard to replicate.
  • Structural demand — a large, rising pool of Asian and African students who aspire to study abroad; the flow Crizac taps is growing over the long run.
  • Cash-funded diversification — four bolt-on deals seeding B2C and new destination/source markets, all paid from internal cash, aimed at the concentration risk.
  • Cheap on quality — a low-teens multiple and a sub-issue-price stock for a business growing profit ~40% in FY26; little optimism is in the price.

The bear case

  • Single-corridor concentration — ~90%+ of revenue tied to UK universities; one government's visa policy can write the whole year, as the FY27 first-quarter dip showed.
  • Visa-policy cycle — dependant limits, post-study-work scrutiny and caps across the UK, Canada and Australia can cut volumes quickly and are wholly outside the company's control.
  • Short public track record — listed only in July 2025, on a 100% offer-for-sale; a barely one-year reel of reported quarters to judge it on.
  • Disintermediation & competition — bigger platforms (IDP, ApplyBoard) above, and the risk students go direct or universities recruit in-house below.
  • Integration & governance — a spree of small acquisitions to execute, and a promoter group holding ~80% with a small public float that can amplify moves.
The bottom line

Crizac is a genuinely high-quality business — a cash-rich, high-margin toll-booth on the global flow of students into Western universities, with a two-sided network that took a decade to build and would take a decade to copy. The self-help is real: cash-funded moves into direct-to-student counselling and new corridors that attack the very concentration that holds the stock back. But the one force that writes each year — how many students the destination countries admit — remains outside the company's gift, and today almost all of the revenue leans on a single country whose immigration policy has turned restrictive. The next test is concrete and datable: whether the UK revenue share falls and organic enrolment growth holds through the FY27 policy cycle as the acquisitions integrate. Widen the base before the corridor narrows, and the cheap multiple looks like a mispricing of a quality compounder; fail to, and FY26 will read as the good year a single-corridor model is built to enjoy between the hard ones. Judge it, above all, by the destination map — and give the short public history the caution it deserves.
