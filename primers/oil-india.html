00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Oil India Limited (OIL) is India's second-largest national oil-and-gas exploration & production (E&P) company — an upstream business and a Maharatna, one of the elite state-owned enterprises granted extra financial autonomy. Its job is the very first link of the oil chain: it explores for, drills and pumps crude oil and natural gas out of the ground — mostly from its historic fields in Assam and the North-East, plus Rajasthan — and sells the crude to refiners at internationally-linked prices and the gas at government-regulated prices. It is the smaller sibling of state giant ONGC, and sits before the refiner-marketers like Indian Oil, BPCL and HPCL that buy crude and turn it into fuel.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It sells barrels, not fuel — so the crude price is everything. Oil India's earnings are, at their core, production volume × realisation − the cost of finding and lifting the oil. Because it sells crude at prices linked to international Brent, the global oil price is the single biggest swing factor in its profit. FY26 (year ended March 2026) shows this plainly: consolidated net profit rose ~7% to ₹7,551 Cr, but the standalone upstream business was softer because its crude realisation fell to $69.04/bbl (from $78.09 in FY25) — a lower oil price, mechanically, means lower profit.
  • It owns a refinery too — the NRL differentiator. Unlike pure-upstream ONGC, Oil India owns a 69.63% majority stake in Numaligarh Refinery Ltd (NRL) in Assam, giving it a downstream refining-margin leg. NRL had a bumper FY26 — PAT up ~90% to ₹3,057 Cr on a fat $13.43/bbl refining margin — and is mid-way through tripling its capacity from 3 to 9 MTPA, the single biggest growth-and-capex project in the group.
  • A cheap, high-dividend PSU with a growth build-out. Oil India is 56.66% owned by the Government of India, paid ₹11.50/share in dividends for FY26 (a ~2.7% yield), and trades at barely 10× earnings and ~1.2× book — the classic "cyclical-cheap" oil valuation. But it is also spending heavily: record drilling, the NRL expansion, pipelines and a ~₹20,000 Cr push into renewables, green hydrogen and CBG through 2040.
In plain terms

Think of Oil India as the hunter, not the cook. The refiner-marketers (Indian Oil, BPCL, HPCL) are the "cooks" — they buy crude and turn it into the petrol and diesel you pump. Oil India is one rung earlier: it drills holes in the ground in Assam and Rajasthan, brings up crude oil and natural gas, and sells the raw barrels to those cooks. Its fortune therefore rises and falls with the global oil price: when Brent is high, each barrel it lifts is worth more; when Brent falls, so does its profit — almost mechanically. Two things complicate the picture. First, when oil spikes, the government has at times slapped a windfall tax on domestic crude to skim the excess (that tax was scrapped in December 2024). Second, its gas is sold at a price the government fixes, not a free-market price. And uniquely, Oil India also owns a refinery (NRL) it is busy tripling in size — so it isn't purely a barrel-hunter; it has a second, downstream leg growing underneath it.

01

Upstream E&P economics 101: how a barrel-hunter earns

No petroleum-engineering degree required — just where Oil India sits and the handful of levers that drive it.

To understand Oil India you first have to see where it sits in the oil chain. The industry runs in three stages — upstream (find & pump the crude and gas), midstream (transport it), and downstream (refine and sell the fuels). Oil India is fundamentally an upstream company: its "product" is the raw crude oil and natural gas it brings to the surface, and its customers are the refiners and gas buyers who take it from there. That single fact — that it sells the input to the fuel industry, not the finished fuel — is what makes it behave completely differently from an Indian Oil or a BPCL.

The simple profit equation

Roughly: Oil India's upstream profit ≈ (Volume produced × realisation) − (finding & lifting costs) − taxes/levies. Because volume moves slowly (you can only drill so many wells a year) and costs are fairly stable, the realisation — the oil price — is the term that jumps around most, and it drags profit with it. That's the whole game of an E&P company: it is a leveraged bet on the commodity price, sitting on a base of reserves it must keep replacing. Two twists specific to India cap the upside: a windfall tax can claw back part of the gain when oil spikes, and the gas half of production is sold at a government-fixed price, not a market one. Both are covered next.

Why upstream is a different animal from downstream

  • An E&P company likes high oil prices; a refiner-marketer often fears them. When crude rises, Oil India's realisation and profit rise. For a downstream OMC like BPCL, a crude spike raises its raw-material cost and — if pump prices are frozen — can crush its marketing margin. They sit on opposite sides of the same barrel.
  • Reserves are the balance sheet's real asset. Oil India's long-term value is the oil and gas still underground. It must "replace" what it produces each year by finding new reserves — the reserve replacement ratio (RRR), ~1.02 in FY26, means it just about found as much as it pumped. An RRR below 1 for long is a slow liquidation; above 1 is growth.
  • It's capital-hungry and hit-or-miss. Exploration wells can come up dry — those costs get written off (Oil India took exploration write-offs in FY26). This is why upstream earnings are lumpy and why the market pays a low multiple for the volatility.
02

The crude-price master driver

The single most important idea in the entire investment case — read this once and Oil India's earnings click into place.

If you remember only one thing about Oil India, make it this: its profit is a near-direct function of the international crude-oil price. Oil India sells its crude to Indian refiners at prices benchmarked to international Brent (with small quality adjustments). So when Brent goes up, each of the ~3.4 million tonnes it lifts fetches more; when Brent falls, the same barrels fetch less — and because its production volume and its costs move slowly, almost the entire change in the oil price flows straight to the bottom line. This is what analysts mean when they call an E&P stock a "leveraged play on the oil price."

The mechanism, in one paragraph

Imagine Oil India lifts one barrel that costs it, say, ~$35 to find and produce. If it sells that barrel at $78, it keeps ~$43 of gross margin; if the oil price drops so it sells at $69, it keeps ~$34 — a ~20% fall in margin from a ~12% fall in price. That amplification (a small % move in the oil price → a bigger % move in profit) is operating leverage on a commodity, and it works both ways. It's why Oil India's earnings can look volatile and hard to forecast even though the business itself — pumping oil from decades-old fields — barely changes year to year. The stock, in effect, is a way to own the oil price with a dividend attached.

A crucial nuance: because its Assam fields are mature, onshore and low-cost, Oil India stays profitable even when oil is cheap — it doesn't need $90 Brent to make money the way a high-cost deep-water producer might. That low cost base is a genuine strength. The flip side is that its growth in profit, quarter to quarter, is dominated by a variable it cannot control at all: the world oil price, set by OPEC+, global demand and geopolitics.

03

The windfall tax and the APM gas ceiling

The two policy levers that cap Oil India's upside — essential reader education on Indian upstream economics.

Oil India doesn't get to keep the full benefit of a high oil price, and it doesn't get a free-market price for its gas. Two government mechanisms sit between the commodity and Oil India's pocket — and understanding them is what separates a real grasp of the stock from a naïve "it's a bet on Brent" view.

1 · The windfall tax (SAED) — the cap on crude upside

When global oil prices spiked in 2022, domestic producers like Oil India and ONGC suddenly earned outsized profits on crude they pump cheaply at home. To skim off that "windfall," the government introduced a Special Additional Excise Duty (SAED) — a windfall tax — on domestically produced crude in July 2022. It was revised roughly every fortnight, moving up when oil rose and down when it fell, effectively capping how much of an oil spike a producer could keep.

  • How it worked: above a trigger oil price, a per-tonne levy kicked in on domestic crude, rising with the oil price — so past a point, extra dollars of Brent went largely to the exchequer, not the producer.
  • The good news for FY26: as oil prices normalised, the government abolished the windfall tax entirely, effective 2 December 2024. For the full FY26 year, Oil India kept the upside of its realisation without the SAED skim — a structural positive versus the 2022–24 period.
  • The residual risk: the mechanism is gone but the precedent isn't. If oil spikes hard again, a future government could reinstate a windfall levy. So the "unlimited upside to Brent" case always carries an implicit policy ceiling.
Why the windfall tax mattered so much

A windfall tax turns an E&P company's payoff from a straight line into a "capped" one: it keeps the full pain when oil falls, but hands over much of the gain when oil surges past the trigger. That asymmetry is exactly why, during 2022–24, Oil India's shares didn't rally as hard as the oil price might have suggested — investors knew the taxman was taking the top slice. Its removal in December 2024 uncapped the upside again, which is one reason the FY26 setup is more attractive than it looks from the headline realisation dip.

2 · The APM gas price — the ceiling on the gas half

Roughly half of Oil India's output by energy is natural gas, and here it has even less pricing freedom. Gas from "nomination" (legacy) blocks like Oil India's is sold at the Administered Price Mechanism (APM) price — a rate the government sets, not the market. For FY26, that APM price was raised to $7/mmBtu, with a floor of $4 and a ceiling of $7. So however high global gas prices go, Oil India's domestic gas is capped at $7/mmBtu; however low they fall, it's protected at $4.

  • Stability, not upside: the APM formula (linked to a basket of global benchmarks, with a floor/ceiling) makes gas revenue far steadier than crude — but it also means Oil India can't fully ride a global gas rally.
  • Why the ceiling exists: cheap domestic gas keeps power, fertiliser and city-gas (CNG/PNG) affordable — a consumer-protection choice by the government, at the producer's expense.
  • New gas gets more: gas from newer/difficult fields (deepwater, HPHT) is priced higher — a ceiling of ~$8.90/mmBtu for Apr–Sep 2026 — a small incentive for fresh exploration, though most of Oil India's gas is legacy APM.
The net picture: Oil India's crude is now un-capped on the upside (windfall tax gone) but fully exposed on the downside to Brent; its gas is boxed inside a $4–$7 government band. So the stock is best understood as a partly-hedged bet on the oil price — the crude leg gives the swing, the regulated gas leg gives the ballast.
04

Production, reserves and the fields

Where the oil actually comes from — and whether it can keep coming.

Oil India's production heartland is Upper Assam and the North-East — the very cradle of India's oil industry — supplemented by Rajasthan and a set of overseas assets. In FY26 it produced roughly 3.4 MMT of crude oil and ~3 BCM of natural gas, together about 6.64 MMTOE (down slightly from 6.71 MMTOE in FY25). The dip is a story worth telling, because it shows exactly what an upstream operator can and can't control.

Output rose steadily FY21→FY25, then dipped marginally in FY26 — not from geology, but from a ~0.33 MMTOE production loss (0.23 gas from weak customer offtake, 0.10 crude from a 10–15 day economic blockade in the eastern producing area in Q3). Daily crude rate actually hit a decade high.
Why production dipped even as drilling hit a record

FY26 was, operationally, Oil India's most active year ever: it drilled 74 wells (highest-ever annual tally) and completed 307 workovers (also a record), and its daily crude rate touched a decade high. Yet full-year volume still slipped, because two things outside the drill-bit intervened: a 10–15 day economic blockade in the eastern producing region (a recurring North-East risk — local agitations can physically halt operations) cost ~0.10 MMTOE of crude, and weak gas offtake (customers taking less gas during shutdowns) cost ~0.23 MMTOE. This is the quiet truth of onshore E&P in the North-East: the geology and effort were there, but logistics and local disruption capped the result. It's why the growth story is really about sustaining and lifting volume against these frictions.

The reserve base — the fuel tank for the future

An E&P company is only as good as the oil and gas it still has underground. Oil India's 2P (proven + probable) reserves stand at 190 MMTOE domestic (about 121 MMTOE gas + 69 MMT crude) plus 41 MMTOE overseas, for ~231 MMTOE total — implying a comfortable ~31-year domestic reserve life at current rates. Crucially, its FY26 reserve replacement ratio was ~1.02: it found (just) more than it pumped, so the tank isn't draining.

05

The NRL refinery leg — the differentiator

The distinctive downstream engine that sets Oil India apart from pure-upstream ONGC.

Here is Oil India's most unusual feature. A textbook E&P company stops at the wellhead. Oil India doesn't — it owns a 69.63% majority stake in Numaligarh Refinery Ltd (NRL), a refinery in Assam. That gives it a downstream refining-margin leg on top of its upstream barrels — a second, quite different profit engine that ONGC, its bigger upstream rival, largely lacks. And NRL is not a sleepy asset: it had a stellar FY26 and is in the middle of the group's biggest ever expansion.

Because Oil India consolidates NRL (it owns the majority), a big chunk of the group's consolidated profit actually comes from the refinery, not the oilfields. In FY26, NRL's ₹3,057 Cr PAT was a large part of why consolidated PAT (₹7,551 Cr) held up and rose even as the standalone upstream result (₹4,455 Cr) softened on the lower oil price. In other words, NRL's refining strength partly offset the upstream's crude-price weakness — a genuine internal hedge.

Why owning a refinery hedges the upstream

Upstream and downstream often move in opposite directions with the oil price. When crude is high, Oil India's oilfields earn more — but a refiner's margins can get squeezed. When crude is low, the oilfields earn less — but refining margins often widen because cheap crude in, similar fuel prices out. By owning both a producer (the fields) and a refiner (NRL), Oil India smooths its combined result: the two legs partly cancel each other's swings. That's a structural advantage pure-upstream ONGC doesn't enjoy, and it's why the consolidated Oil India is a steadier animal than its raw crude-price exposure suggests.

The 3 → 9 MTPA expansion — the group's biggest bet

NRL is tripling its capacity from 3 MTPA to 9 MTPA — the Numaligarh Refinery Expansion Project (NREP). This is a multi-year, multi-billion-rupee undertaking and the single largest capex-and-value driver in the Oil India group.

  • Scale & cost: the project cost has escalated over the years (roughly from an original ~₹22,000 Cr estimate toward ~₹34,000 Cr by early 2026 as scope and inputs grew) — a reminder that big infrastructure builds carry cost-overrun risk.
  • Progress: by early 2026 the project was ~85% complete, with commissioning of the expanded units expected to begin processing crude in the second half of FY27.
  • The supporting infrastructure: because landlocked Assam can't source enough crude locally for a 9 MTPA refinery, NRL is building a crude-oil import terminal at Paradip port (Odisha) and a ~1,640 km cross-country pipeline to carry imported crude to Numaligarh — a huge logistics project in its own right.
  • Why it matters: a 9 MTPA refinery earns roughly three times the throughput margin of a 3 MTPA one. Once fully ramped, NRL's contribution to group profit steps up materially — turning Oil India, over time, into a more balanced integrated upstream-plus-downstream energy company. NRL was also granted Navratna status, reflecting its scale and autonomy.
The distinction to hold onto: BPCL and HPCL are downstream (they refine and sell fuel); GAIL is midstream (it transmits gas). Oil India is upstream (it produces the crude and gas) — but the NRL stake bolts a downstream refining leg onto that upstream core. It is the only one of these PSUs that both pumps the crude and refines a big slug of it in-house.
06

Pipelines and the new-energy pivot

The transport business — and the long bet on renewables, green hydrogen and CBG.

Beyond wells and the refinery, Oil India runs two more lines of activity: a crude-oil and product pipeline network (its midstream leg) and a growing new-energy portfolio meant to hedge the very long-term decline of fossil fuels. Neither is large today, but both matter to the story.

Pipelines — the quiet, steady leg

  • Oil India operates India's oldest crude-oil pipeline (the historic Naharkatiya–Barauni line) and product pipelines that move crude and fuels across the North-East and beyond — a stable, fee-like business.
  • Expansion is underway: the Numaligarh–Siliguri product pipeline (NSPL) is being expanded (1.72 → 5.5 MTPA), and the group is enlarging its gas-pipeline capacity in the region — infrastructure that supports both its own output and the NRL expansion.
  • Pipelines give Oil India a slice of midstream economics (transport tariffs) to complement its upstream and downstream legs.

The new-energy pivot — insuring against the long decline

Every fossil-fuel producer faces the same existential question: what happens as the world decarbonises over decades? Oil India's answer is a measured diversification, backed by a headline commitment of roughly ₹20,000 Cr toward new energy by 2040.

  • Renewables: a target of 5+ GW of renewable-energy capacity by 2040 (solar and wind), a meaningful build-out from a small base.
  • Green hydrogen: Oil India commissioned India's first AEM-technology green-hydrogen pilot at Jorhat, Assam, and its NRL refinery is procuring green hydrogen (a 10,000 TPA supply contract) and building in-house electrolysis to decarbonise the refinery.
  • Compressed bio-gas (CBG) & ethanol: a plan for ~25 CBG plants by 2030, turning agricultural and municipal waste into gas — aligning with India's biofuel push.
How to read the new-energy push

Be clear-eyed: for the next decade, Oil India's cash and profits will come overwhelmingly from oil, gas and the NRL refinery — not from renewables or hydrogen. The new-energy portfolio is best understood as optionality and insurance: a way to build capability and stay relevant for the day (many years out) when fossil demand plateaus, and to satisfy the government's decarbonisation agenda for its state-owned energy champions. It won't move the earnings needle soon, but ignoring it entirely would be a strategic error a Maharatna can't afford — so a modest, steady spend is the sensible middle path, which is exactly what Oil India is doing.

07

The company and who controls it

From the 1889 Digboi gusher to a 13th Maharatna.

Oil India carries one of the longest pedigrees in Indian industry. Commercial oil was first struck in India at Digboi, Assam, in the 1880s — the North-East is quite literally the birthplace of the country's petroleum industry. Oil India Limited itself was incorporated on 18 February 1959, initially a joint venture between the UK's Burmah Oil Company and the Government of India, and later fully nationalised. It has been headquartered at Duliajan, Assam, at the heart of its fields, ever since.

Ownership & control

  • Government of India ~56.66% — Oil India is a state-controlled Maharatna. The government appoints the board, and its priorities (energy security, consumer-price protection, dividends to the exchequer) shape strategy. This is both a backstop and a constraint.
  • Public & institutions ~43% — the free float held by mutual funds, insurers (LIC), FIIs and retail investors. It is an index constituent and a widely-held PSU.
  • Subsidiaries & JVs: the crown jewel is NRL (69.63%); the group also holds stakes in pipeline and city-gas ventures and various exploration JVs at home and abroad.
  • Balance sheet: Oil India carries some debt (largely tied to the NRL expansion and acquisition), but it remains a cash-generative, dividend-paying business with a comfortable net-worth base (consolidated net worth ~₹53,700 Cr).
The PSU reality: as a government company, Oil India can be asked to serve national objectives that a private producer wouldn't — from selling gas cheaply under APM to occasionally bearing subsidy-sharing burdens when fuel politics demand it. That "controlling-shareholder-with-an-agenda" factor is a permanent feature of every Indian oil PSU, and part of why they trade cheap.
08

The industry and the rivals

Where Oil India sits among India's oil-and-gas champions.

India imports over 85% of the crude oil it consumes — domestic production simply can't keep up with demand. That makes home-grown upstream producers like Oil India strategically important but structurally small relative to the import-dependent downstream giants. The company's peer set spans the whole oil chain, and it's worth placing each rival precisely.

The one comparison that matters — Oil India vs ONGC

The cleanest way to frame Oil India is "the smaller, nimbler ONGC — with a refinery attached." Both are state-owned upstream producers whose profit tracks the oil price and whose gas is APM-priced. ONGC is far bigger (and more offshore-heavy), so it's the benchmark. Oil India's distinguishing features are (1) its onshore, low-cost Assam base, (2) its majority NRL refinery that internally hedges the crude-price swing, and (3) its smaller size, which means a single big project (NRL's tripling) can move the needle more than it would for a giant. If you like the theme "own India's oil price cheaply, with a dividend," Oil India and ONGC are the two vehicles — and Oil India is the one with the extra downstream kicker.

Financials, valuation and the dividend

Cyclical earnings, a cheap multiple, and a fat, reliable payout.

Oil India's financials tell the classic PSU-upstream story: earnings that swing with the oil price, a low valuation that reflects that volatility and government control, and a generous dividend that is a core part of the total-return case. FY26 was a solid but not spectacular year — consolidated profit edged up ~7% as a strong NRL refinery offset a softer, lower-realisation upstream.

Why the dividend is central to the case

Oil India is a high-payout, cash-rich PSU: for FY26 it paid ₹11.50 per share (₹3.50 + ₹7.00 interims + a ₹1.00 final), a yield around 2.7%, and it has returned roughly ₹9,000 Cr over five years. For a state-owned producer this is not incidental — the government, as majority owner, relies on these dividends for its own budget, so the payout is both generous and reliable. That turns Oil India into a hybrid: part leveraged oil-price bet, part income stock. The dividend gives you a steady return while you wait for the cycle (and the NRL ramp-up) to lift the share price. It's the "get paid to hold the oil price" thesis.

The low multiple — ~10× earnings, ~1.2× book — is not an accident or a bargain-in-hiding so much as the market's fair price for the package: volatile, commodity-driven earnings; a controlling shareholder with non-commercial objectives; policy risk (windfall tax, APM); and heavy capex (NRL) that ties up cash. Bulls argue that's too cheap for a low-cost producer with a growing refinery and a fat dividend; bears argue the cheapness is deserved and permanent for a government-run cyclical. Both have a point — which is why the stock rarely re-rates far in either direction without a big move in oil.

The growth runway

More barrels, a bigger refinery, and a green option on top.

Oil India's growth is not about opening new markets — it's about pumping more, refining more, and replacing reserves, with new energy as a long-dated call option. Management has laid out concrete production and investment ambitions for the rest of the decade.

Three engines, stacked

  • Volume — raising oil+gas production toward 10–12 MMTOE via record drilling, enhanced recovery from mature fields, and new exploration acreage. Growth that doesn't need a higher oil price.
  • NRL ramp-up — the tripled refinery coming on-stream from FY27, materially lifting the downstream profit leg.
  • The oil-price cycle — the swing factor. On top of volume and NRL, any upcycle in Brent flows straight through to profit (now un-capped, with the windfall tax gone).

The honest caveat: North-East onshore production has fought decline and disruption for years, so lifting volume by ~50% to 10–12 MMTOE is an ambitious target, not a certainty — it needs the drilling to convert, blockade risk to stay contained, and enhanced-recovery techniques to work on ageing fields. The NRL expansion, likewise, carries cost-overrun and commissioning risk. This is a "show-me" growth story, delivered in barrels and completion percentages each quarter.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, cash-rich oil-price proxy — with a refinery and a dividend attached.

Oil India is, at heart, a low-cost, cash-generative national oil producer whose profit rides the crude-oil cycle, sweetened by a growing refinery leg and a fat dividend, and constrained by government control and policy. The debate isn't whether the business works — it does — but what an inherently cyclical, state-run producer is worth, and whether its growth plans land.

The bull case

  • Cheap on every metric — ~10× earnings, ~1.2× book, ~2.7% yield: a low-cost producer priced like a value trap it may not be.
  • Windfall tax gone — crude upside is un-capped again after December 2024; any oil upcycle now flows through more fully.
  • NRL is a real hedge & growth kicker — the 69.63% refinery had a bumper FY26 and triples to 9 MTPA from FY27, adding a steadier downstream leg.
  • Low-cost onshore base — mature Assam fields keep lifting costs low; it earns even at modest oil prices, and a ~31-year reserve life underpins longevity.
  • Fat, reliable dividend — a government owner that depends on the payout keeps it generous.

The bear case

  • Hostage to the oil price — a Brent downcycle hits realisation and profit almost mechanically; earnings are volatile and hard to forecast.
  • Government control & policy risk — APM gas ceiling caps the gas leg; a windfall tax could return; subsidy-sharing can be imposed.
  • Production & disruption risk — North-East blockades and ageing-field decline threaten the ambitious 10–12 MMTOE volume target.
  • Execution/capex risk — the NRL expansion has seen cost escalation; commissioning and cost overruns could disappoint.
  • Structural energy transition — a very long-dated headwind to fossil demand; the new-energy pivot is small so far.
The bottom line

Oil India is best understood as a cheap, dividend-paying proxy for the oil price — a low-cost national producer with a genuinely differentiating refinery leg (NRL) that both hedges the crude cycle and offers real growth as it triples in size. What you're buying is a volatile, commodity-linked earnings stream at a low multiple, ballasted by regulated gas and a fat payout, and steered by a government whose objectives don't always align with minority shareholders'. Get an oil upcycle plus the NRL ramp plus volume growth, and a 10× stock can re-rate and compound; get a Brent slump, a fresh windfall tax, or an NRL delay, and the cheapness persists for good reason. The two numbers to watch each quarter are simple: the crude realisation (is the oil cycle with you?) and production volume plus NRL progress (is the growth actually arriving?).
