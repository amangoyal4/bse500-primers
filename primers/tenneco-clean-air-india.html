Tenneco Clean Air India, the emission-control name that is half suspension

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Tenneco Clean Air India is the listed Indian business of Tenneco Inc., a US-headquartered global auto-parts major. It is a Tier-1 supplier — meaning it sells finished systems directly to the carmakers and truck-makers themselves (Maruti, Tata, Mahindra, Hyundai, the commercial-vehicle and two-wheeler names), which then bolt those systems into the vehicles you buy. What makes it more interesting than a one-line "emission-control company" is that it runs on two engines, not one, and they face the electric-vehicle future very differently.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is two businesses wearing one name. Roughly 56% of revenue is Clean Air & Powertrain — the systems that scrub harmful gases out of an engine's exhaust (catalytic converters, particulate filters, after-treatment). The other ~44% is Advanced Ride Technologies — shock absorbers, struts and dampers that control how a vehicle rides over bumps and takes corners. The first half is tied to the combustion engine; the second half is needed by every vehicle ever built.
  • The IPO put no money into the company. The November 2025 listing raised roughly ₹3,600 Cr, but it was a 100% offer-for-sale — every rupee went to the selling shareholder, Tenneco's Mauritius holding vehicle, not to Tenneco Clean Air India. Promoter holding stepped down from ~97.25% to about 74.79%. The parent cashed a slice of its stake; the operating company's balance sheet was untouched by the raise.
  • The financials are strong, the listed history is thin. FY26 (approximate) revenue was ~₹5,404 Cr with a ~17% EBITDA margin (~₹925 Cr) and PAT of ~₹604 Cr, on a near debt-free balance sheet, an order book of ~₹12,400 Cr, and eye-catching returns — ROE ~44%, ROCE ~61%. But the shares have traded only since November 2025, so every trend line is short and several figures here are best read as approximate.
In plain terms

Two products sit under one roof. One cleans the dirty gases coming out of a petrol, diesel or CNG engine before they reach the tailpipe — a business that only exists because combustion engines exist. The other keeps the wheels planted and the ride smooth — a business every car, van and truck needs whether it burns fuel or runs on a battery. The market tends to file Tenneco under "emission control" and worry that electric vehicles will slowly melt that away. That worry is real, but it only touches the first product. The second product does not care what powers the wheels. The whole investment argument is a tug-of-war between those two facts.

01

Auto components 101: what a Tier-1 supplier is

No engineering degree needed — just how the money reaches a parts-maker like this.

A finished car is an assembly of thousands of parts, and the carmaker (the "OEM" — original equipment manufacturer) makes surprisingly few of them itself. It designs the vehicle, stamps the body, runs the final assembly line, and buys almost everything else from a pyramid of suppliers. Where you sit in that pyramid decides how you earn.

The supplier pyramid — where Tenneco sits
TierWhat it suppliesWho it sells to
OEMThe finished vehicle — designs it, assembles it, brands it.You, the buyer, and fleets/dealers.
Tier-1Complete, engineered systems — a full exhaust after-treatment line, a corner suspension module. This is Tenneco.Directly to the OEM's assembly line.
Tier-2 / Tier-3Individual components and raw materials that feed the Tier-1 — castings, valves, coated substrates, steel, precious-metal washcoats.To Tier-1 suppliers.

Being a Tier-1 supplier has a particular economic shape worth internalising, because it drives everything that follows. You win a "platform" or "programme" — the right to supply a specific system for a specific vehicle model — and you then supply it for the life of that model, often five to seven years. Revenue is not a single sale; it is a slow annuity that runs as long as the OEM keeps building that vehicle, scaling up and down with how many units roll off the line.

The two numbers that drive a Tier-1's revenue

A parts-maker's top line is the product of just two things: how many vehicles get built (volume) and how much of its product goes into each vehicle (content per vehicle, measured in rupees of parts sold per car). Volume rides the auto cycle — good years and bad. Content-per-vehicle is the quieter, more powerful lever: if regulation or design forces more of your parts into every car, your revenue can grow even when the number of cars is flat. Hold on to that phrase — content per vehicle — because for Tenneco's Clean Air half it is the engine of growth, and for the whole company it is the number that settles the argument.

Why "systems, not parts" matters

  • Stickiness. Once a supplier is designed into a vehicle programme, switching mid-life is expensive and risky for the OEM — the part is validated, tooled and integrated. That gives the incumbent Tier-1 a multi-year, defensible revenue stream per win.
  • Engineering moat. A catalytic converter or a modern damper is not a commodity casting; it is a tuned, tested, warranty-backed system. Deep R&D and validation keep out casual entrants — which is why global Tier-1s and a few scaled Indian names dominate.
  • Programme dependence. The flip side: if you lose the next-generation platform, or the OEM discontinues a popular model, that annuity ends. Winning the replacement programme every cycle is the job that never stops.
02

Engine one: Clean Air & Powertrain (~56%)

The emission-control business — the half everyone means when they say "Tenneco".

When an internal-combustion engine burns petrol, diesel or CNG, the exhaust that comes out is not just harmless steam. It carries genuinely toxic products of combustion — nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide, unburnt hydrocarbons, and (especially in diesel) fine soot particles. Left untreated, that is the smog you can see over a city. The Clean Air business exists to clean that exhaust in the fraction of a second between the engine and the tailpipe, so that what actually exits the vehicle meets the legal limits. It is roughly 56% of Tenneco Clean Air India's revenue.

What is actually in the box

An emission-control system is a chain of specialised components welded into the exhaust line. Each one attacks a different pollutant:

  • Exhaust manifold & pipes — collect the hot gases as they leave each cylinder and channel them into the after-treatment chain.
  • Catalytic converter — the heart of the system. Inside is a honeycomb substrate coated with a thin "washcoat" of precious metals — platinum, palladium and rhodium. As exhaust flows through, chemical reactions on that coating convert toxic gases into far less harmful ones (carbon monoxide to carbon dioxide, nitrogen oxides to nitrogen). The precious metals are why this component is both effective and expensive.
  • Diesel particulate filter (DPF) — in diesels, traps the fine soot particles physically, then periodically burns them off. It is the reason a modern diesel does not belch black smoke.
  • SCR after-treatment (selective catalytic reduction) — injects a urea solution (AdBlue) into the hot exhaust to break nitrogen oxides down into harmless nitrogen and water. Essential for meeting the tightest diesel and commercial-vehicle norms.
  • Mufflers & resonators — the tail end, which controls exhaust noise and back-pressure.
Why tightening rules, not just more cars, grow this business

Here is the mechanism that makes Clean Air more than a bet on the auto cycle. Emission limits in India have ratcheted steadily tighter — from BS-IV to BS-VI, and onward toward real-driving-emission (RDE) testing that measures a vehicle on the actual road, not just on a lab bench. Every ratchet forces more after-treatment hardware into each vehicle: a bigger catalyst, a particulate filter that was not there before, an SCR system a cheaper vehicle used to skip. So the value of Clean Air content bolted into each car climbs with every regulatory step — the business can grow on content per vehicle even if the number of cars sold barely moves. Tightening norms are, counter-intuitively, this segment's best friend.

The customer set for Clean Air is broad — passenger cars, commercial vehicles (trucks and buses), two-wheelers, and off-highway equipment (tractors, construction machines). Each has its own emission regime and its own content curve. Commercial vehicles and off-highway, running big diesels, carry the heaviest after-treatment content; two-wheelers carry the least, but there are tens of millions of them. This spread of end-markets is a hedge in itself: a weak year for trucks can be offset by a strong year for tractors or bikes.

The quiet dependency to keep in view: the catalyst's effectiveness comes from platinum-group metals whose prices swing on global commodity markets. Tenneco largely passes that cost through to OEMs, but the sheer value of precious metal flowing through inventory inflates the working capital the business must carry, and any pass-through lag can pinch margins. The chemistry that makes Clean Air valuable also makes it commodity-exposed.
03

Engine two: Advanced Ride Technologies (~44%)

The suspension business — the half nobody mentions, and the half electric vehicles cannot kill.

The second engine has nothing to do with exhaust. Advanced Ride Technologies makes the parts that control how a vehicle moves over the road: shock absorbers, struts, dampers and ride-control systems. When you drive over a pothole and the car settles instead of bouncing, that is a damper doing its job. When the vehicle stays flat through a corner instead of leaning alarmingly, that is the ride system. It is roughly 44% of revenue — and it is the part of the story the "emission-control company" label completely hides.

What suspension actually does

Every vehicle sits on springs so the wheels can move up and down independently of the body. But a spring on its own would oscillate — bounce, bounce, bounce — for ages after every bump. The damper (shock absorber) is the component that absorbs that energy and calms the motion, turning a jarring, unstable ride into a controlled one. It is a safety component as much as a comfort one: a vehicle whose tyres are skipping off the road because the damping has failed cannot brake or steer properly. Struts combine the damper and a structural mounting into one unit; ride-control systems add electronic or adaptive tuning for premium vehicles.

The single fact that reframes the whole company

Every vehicle needs suspension — petrol, diesel, CNG, hybrid, or fully electric. An electric car has no engine and no exhaust, so it needs zero emission-control hardware; that is the melting-ice-cube fear at the heart of the "EV kills Tenneco" thesis. But an electric car still rolls on four wheels over the same broken roads, so it needs exactly the same dampers and struts — arguably more of them, because a battery pack makes an EV heavy, and a heavier, more premium vehicle demands beefier, better-tuned ride control. So the ~44% of Tenneco's revenue that comes from Advanced Ride Technologies is not merely EV-resistant; it is EV-agnostic, and in places EV-positive. The electrification wave that threatens engine one washes harmlessly past engine two.

This is why treating Tenneco Clean Air India as a pure emission-control play misreads it. Nearly half the business rides on total vehicle production — every car and truck built in India, regardless of what powers it — plus a premiumisation tailwind as Indian buyers move up to vehicles with better ride comfort and, eventually, adaptive suspension. It is the ballast that steadies a company whose other half faces a long-term structural question.

A genuine surprise: the market frames this IPO as an emission-control company staring down the EV threat — a classic melting-ice-cube. Look inside and roughly 44% of revenue comes from suspension and ride systems that an electric vehicle needs just as much as a petrol one. The "EV loser" narrative, examined closely, touches only about half of Tenneco Clean Air India. That is the fact most casual observers miss.

04

The two-engine EV split: the crux of the whole case

The single most important idea in the investment debate — and it is specific to this company's shape.

Put the two engines side by side against the one force everyone worries about — electrification — and the real investment question comes into focus. It is not "will EVs hurt Tenneco?" It is "how much of Tenneco do EVs actually touch, and can the untouched half plus tightening-norm content growth outrun the shrinking half?" The answer lives in the split.

The naive "emission-control = EV loser" read applies the melting-ice-cube to 100% of the company. The reality applies it to roughly 56% — and even that half is softened because hybrids keep needing after-treatment.

Write the company's value as a sum of two streams and the whole debate becomes legible:

  • Clean Air value = Clean Air content-per-vehicle × the number of internal-combustion and hybrid vehicles built. In the near term this stream grows, because tightening norms lift content-per-vehicle faster than EVs are shrinking the ICE build. In the long term it becomes a slowly shrinking terminal tail as pure EVs replace combustion — though hybrids, which still have an engine and an exhaust, keep it alive for longer than the pessimists assume.
  • Advanced Ride value = ride content-per-vehicle × total vehicles built, EVs included. This stream has no combustion dependency at all. It grows with India's overall auto production and with premiumisation, and electrification does not subtract from it.
The device that only fits Tenneco: two tails, opposite slopes

Most auto-component companies you can judge on one axis — are they on the right side of electrification or the wrong side? Tenneco Clean Air India sits on both sides at once, in known proportions. Its value is the sum of a ~56% stream sloping gently downward over decades (ICE-exhaust content, cushioned by hybrids and lifted near-term by norms) and a ~44% stream sloping upward (EV-proof ride content on a rising total-vehicle base). The company is not a melting ice cube and it is not immune — it is a race between two tails. The bet is that content growth on the durable half, plus norm-driven content growth on the ICE half while it lasts, out-earns the eventual electrification erosion. That framing could not be lifted and dropped onto Suzlon, or a pure-EV parts-maker, or a pure exhaust company — it is this company's exact shape.

The honest reading holds both truths without collapsing into either. This is not a doomed exhaust-maker whose revenue evaporates the day EVs win — nearly half of it is untouched, and the exhaust half grows for years yet on tightening norms and a still-overwhelmingly-ICE Indian fleet. But it is also not immune — a real, structural fraction of the business does face a slow, decade-scale decline, and pretending otherwise would be wishful. The pivotal question is one of pace: how fast can Tenneco grow content-per-vehicle and lift the EV-agnostic share of revenue, relative to how fast electrification erodes the exhaust tail.

The company, the parent, and who controls it

A US Tier-1 major, taken private by Apollo, listing its Indian arm — and selling, not raising.

Tenneco Clean Air India is the Indian operating business of Tenneco Inc., one of the world's larger automotive parts groups, with a long global history in exhaust systems and ride control. The ownership chain above the Indian company is where several of the investment nuances live, so it is worth getting straight.

Ownership & the shape of the float

  • Promoter / parent: Tenneco (ultimately controlled by Apollo Global Management) holds about 74.79% post-IPO, via Tenneco Mauritius Holdings. This is a controlled company with a large, professional parent that supplies technology, licences and global platform relationships.
  • The 100%-OFS signal: because the IPO was entirely an offer-for-sale, the money raised went to the selling shareholder, not into Tenneco Clean Air India's own coffers. The company did not de-lever or fund expansion with the raise — it was already near debt-free — but the structure does mean the parent chose to monetise part of its stake rather than inject growth capital. Read it as a partial cash-out, not a capital-raising for the business.
  • Related-party ties: as an arm of a global group, the Indian company sources technology, pays royalties/technical-licence fees, and may transact with parent affiliates. These related-party flows are normal for a multinational subsidiary but deserve scrutiny — they are a channel through which value can move between the listed entity and its parent.
The overhang to name plainly: a private-equity owner sitting on ~75% of a freshly listed company is, by design, a future seller. Apollo bought Tenneco to eventually exit at a profit, and the Indian listing is one route to that. So investors should expect the possibility of further sell-downs over time — an equity overhang that can cap the stock even when the business performs — and should weigh the royalty/licence and related-party terms that govern how much of the Indian profit stays in the listed company versus flowing up to the parent.
06

Content-per-vehicle: the growth lever that isn't volume

Why Tenneco can grow even in a flat car market — and where that runs out.

Return to the phrase from section 01. A Tier-1's revenue is volume × content-per-vehicle, and for Tenneco the second term is doing a lot of the work. Understanding why content rises is the key to why an "emission-control" business can be a near-term grower rather than a melting asset.

What pushes content-per-vehicle up — on each engine
DriverSegmentHow it lifts content per vehicle
Tighter emission norms (BS-VI → RDE and beyond)Clean AirEach step forces more after-treatment hardware into every combustion vehicle — bigger catalysts, added particulate filters, SCR on vehicles that skipped it. More rupees of Clean Air content per car, independent of how many cars sell.
Diesel & commercial-vehicle mixClean AirHeavier vehicles need heavier after-treatment; a shift toward trucks, buses and off-highway raises average content.
Premiumisation of rideAdvanced RideBuyers moving up to SUVs and premium models want better ride comfort — higher-value dampers, struts and, at the top end, adaptive/electronic ride control. More rupees of ride content per vehicle.
Heavier vehicles (incl. EVs)Advanced RideBattery packs make EVs heavy; a heavier vehicle needs sturdier, better-tuned suspension — content that rises in an electrifying fleet.

The counter-intuitive part: hybrids are Tenneco's friend, not its enemy

The instinct is to lump hybrids with EVs as "electrification" and assume both hurt an exhaust company. The opposite is true for the Clean Air half. A hybrid still burns fuel, so it still needs a catalytic converter and after-treatment — Clean Air content survives. And because it also carries a battery, it is heavier, so it needs more suspension — Ride content rises. If India's transition runs through a long hybrid phase (as many expect, given charging-infrastructure gaps), Tenneco's exhaust tail shrinks far more slowly than a straight-to-EV scenario implies, while its ride business grows the whole way. The powertrain that worries the market most on paper is one this company can live with comfortably.

The limit is worth stating plainly: content-per-vehicle on the Clean Air side keeps climbing only while combustion engines are being built at all. Once a vehicle is a pure EV, its emission-control content is zero — no amount of norm-tightening adds hardware to a car that has no exhaust. So the content lever is powerful in the ICE-and-hybrid era and simply switches off, vehicle by vehicle, as pure EVs take share. On the Ride side, there is no such cliff — content-per-vehicle there rises regardless of powertrain. That asymmetry is exactly the two-tails picture from section 04, seen through the content lens.

07

Customers and the order book

A large booked backlog — and the concentration risk that comes with Tier-1 life.

Because Tier-1 revenue is programme-based, a supplier's order book — the value of parts it is contracted to supply across the live programmes it has won — is a real signal of forward revenue. Tenneco Clean Air India carries an order book of roughly ₹12,400 Cr, which against ~₹5,400 Cr of annual revenue represents well over two years of booked work. That backlog is the spine of management's growth confidence and the reason the greenfield capacity at Kharkhoda is being built.

The customer base is the who's-who of vehicle-makers selling in India — passenger-car OEMs, the big commercial-vehicle houses, two-wheeler makers and off-highway/tractor firms. That breadth across vehicle types is a genuine diversifier. But Tier-1 supply is inherently concentrated at the top: a handful of large OEMs account for a big share of any supplier's revenue, and each relationship runs programme-by-programme.

Why customer concentration cuts both ways

Winning a platform with a high-volume OEM is a multi-year annuity — steady, sticky, and profitable while the model sells. But the same concentration is a risk: if a major customer's flagship model sells poorly, discontinues, or moves to a rival supplier for the next generation, a chunk of revenue can step down at once. And OEMs are powerful buyers — they run annual cost-down negotiations, squeezing supplier margins programme after programme. A Tier-1's profitability is a constant defence of price against customers who hold most of the leverage. The order book tells you what is won; it does not remove the pressure to keep winning, and to keep it at a decent margin.

08

The India auto cycle and the Kharkhoda bet

The backdrop both engines share — and the capacity being built to ride it.

Whatever the long-run EV question, in the near and medium term both of Tenneco's engines ride the same wave: how many vehicles India builds. India is now among the world's largest vehicle-production markets, and its fleet remains overwhelmingly powered by combustion engines, with EV penetration still in the low single digits for cars. That combination — rising total production plus a still-ICE-dominated mix — is a favourable backdrop for a company that earns on both exhaust content and ride content.

The concrete expression of this confidence is a greenfield plant at Kharkhoda, Haryana, with start-of-production targeted around Q3 FY27. New capacity is a supplier's way of pre-positioning for programmes it expects to win and volumes it expects to serve — but it is also the moment the story becomes checkable. Capacity built for programmes that do not fully materialise is a drag on returns; capacity that fills up cleanly is what turns an order book into delivered revenue.

The milestone that will speak louder than a quarter's revenue

The Kharkhoda start-of-production around Q3 FY27 is the near-term event to watch, because it is where guidance meets reality. If the plant comes up on schedule and its lines fill with the programmes the order book implies, it validates both the demand and the execution. If it slips, or ramps into softer-than-expected volumes, it is the first hard evidence that the growth runway is narrower than the headline order book suggests. For a company with barely a year of listed history, a clean plant ramp is worth more than any single quarter's print.

The industry and the rivals

A concentrated global Tier-1 arena — with different competitors on each engine.

Emission control and ride control are both scale-and-engineering games dominated by a handful of global Tier-1 majors, with capable Indian specialists strong in particular niches. Tenneco Clean Air India faces a different competitive set on each of its two engines — and, on the Clean Air side, a longer-term substitute in the form of EV powertrains themselves.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, the rivals differ by engine: on Clean Air, Tenneco spars with global exhaust majors (Forvia, BorgWarner, Marelli); on Advanced Ride, it also meets scaled Indian specialists like Gabriel and Endurance who know the domestic OEMs intimately. Winning is a two-front effort. Second, the most important "competitor" on the Clean Air side is not a company at all — it is the EV powertrain itself, which does not out-compete Tenneco's catalyst so much as make it unnecessary. That is the substitution risk that no amount of manufacturing excellence can win; the defence is the durable Ride business and the content growth on the combustion fleet while it lasts.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-return Tier-1 with a durable half and a slowly fading half — asked to prove the race goes its way.

Tenneco Clean Air India debuts as an unusually profitable auto-component supplier with a clean balance sheet, a strong parent's technology, a full order book, and two engines that face electrification in opposite directions. The debate is not whether the business is good today — the returns say it is — but whether the durable and norm-boosted halves can grow fast enough, long enough, to out-earn the eventual erosion of the exhaust tail, all while a PE-owned parent sits on three-quarters of the stock.

The bull case

  • Half the business is EV-proof — ~44% Advanced Ride suspension content is needed by every vehicle, EVs included, and grows with premiumisation and heavier vehicles.
  • Norms drive Clean Air, not just volumes — tightening BS-VI/RDE rules lift content-per-vehicle on the combustion fleet, a growth lever independent of the auto cycle.
  • Hybrids extend the tail — hybrids keep an exhaust (Clean Air survives) and add weight (Ride grows); a hybrid-heavy transition is friendly to the mix.
  • Exceptional returns, clean balance sheet — ROCE ~61%, ROE ~44%, near debt-free, ~₹12,400 Cr order book and a global parent's technology.
  • Rising, still-ICE-dominated India build — near-term, both engines ride growing vehicle production with EV penetration still low.

The bear case

  • Structural EV overhang on ~56% — every pure EV built needs zero emission-control content; the Clean Air half faces a slow, decade-scale decline no execution can reverse.
  • PE parent as future seller — Apollo/Tenneco at ~74.79% is a built-in equity overhang; the 100%-OFS IPO signals the parent took cash rather than funding the company.
  • Customer / OEM concentration & pricing power — a few large automakers, programme-dependent, running annual cost-downs that squeeze supplier margins.
  • Related-party & royalty leakage — technology licences, royalties and parent transactions can move value out of the listed entity.
  • Commodity, cyclical & thin-history risks — platinum-group-metal costs and working-capital swings, auto cyclicality, and a track record barely one year long (figures approximate).
The bottom line

Tenneco Clean Air India is filed under "emission control," and that label frames it as a straightforward casualty of the electric-vehicle era. The closer look is more interesting: roughly 44% of it is suspension hardware that an electric car needs as much as a petrol one, the exhaust half grows for years yet on tightening norms and a hybrid-heavy transition, and the whole thing throws off ROCE north of 60% with almost no debt. The unresolved question is one of pace and ownership — whether content-per-vehicle and the EV-agnostic share of revenue climb fast enough to out-run the fading exhaust tail, and how a private-equity parent's three-quarter stake gets unwound over time. The next concrete test is checkable: the Kharkhoda plant coming to start-of-production around Q3 FY27 and filling with the programmes the order book promises. Watch that ramp, then watch the content-per-vehicle and EV-agnostic-revenue lines each year; together they will tell you which of the two tails is winning.
