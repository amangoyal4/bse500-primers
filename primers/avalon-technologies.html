Avalon Technologies, the Indian EMS that builds in America

A plain-English walk through one of India's few full-stack electronics manufacturers — the company that builds the circuit boards, wiring, magnetics and finished boxes inside other firms' clean-energy inverters, rail signalling, aircraft cabins and factory machines, and does it from ten plants in India and two on American soil. Why "box-build" beats bare board-stuffing, why owning a US factory is both its rarest asset and its heaviest cost, and why the whole story now turns on margin.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Avalon Technologies is an Electronics Manufacturing Services (EMS) company — a contract manufacturer that builds electronics for other companies. When a maker of solar inverters, railway signalling gear, medical devices or aircraft systems wants the physical hardware built but does not want to run its own factories, it hands the design to a firm like Avalon, which sources the components, stuffs the circuit boards, wires the harnesses, bends the sheet metal and assembles the finished, tested product. Avalon is unusual on two counts: it is a "full-stack" builder that does far more of the value chain than a bare board-assembler, and it is genuinely dual-shore — ten plants in India and two in the United States.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It builds whole boxes, not just boards. The easy, low-margin end of EMS is "PCB assembly" — soldering components onto a bare circuit board and shipping it back. Avalon has climbed to box-build: it hands back a complete, working, warranty-ready product — enclosure, wiring, boards, software-loaded and tested. Box-build was ~54% of FY26 revenue, and that mix is the whole margin story: the more of the product Avalon makes, the stickier the customer and the fatter the slice.
  • Its biggest factory floor is in America. Roughly 60% of revenue comes from US customers, served partly from two American plants — a rarity for an Indian-listed manufacturer, and its single most distinctive feature. It is a real edge (proximity to US OEMs, a hedge on tariffs) and a real drag (US manufacturing is expensive and the American operation has historically run at thin-to-negative margins). Which of those wins is the crux.
  • FY26 was an inflection. After a flat, disappointing FY24, revenue jumped ~46% to ₹1,603 Cr in FY26 and net profit rose ~78% to ₹113 Cr, with an order book of ~₹2,196 Cr (about 14 months of visibility). The market has noticed: the stock trades near 100× earnings — a valuation that assumes this ramp keeps compounding and margins keep climbing.
In plain terms

Avalon is the invisible builder behind other companies' machines. You will never see its logo on a shop shelf, because its "product" is someone else's product — the electronics guts inside an EV charger, a train's braking system, a satellite ground station or a hospital scanner. It earns its keep two ways: by doing more of each build (from stuffing a board all the way to shipping a finished, tested box), which raises the price it captures per customer; and by being physically present where its customers are, including on US soil. The bet the market is making is that a mid-sized Indian EMS firm can keep winning bigger, more complex builds — and finally make its American factories earn their cost — fast enough to justify a valuation priced for a much larger, more profitable company.

01

EMS 101: the hidden factory behind the electronics

No hardware background needed — just how the "someone else builds it" business works.

Almost no electronics brand builds its own hardware anymore. The company that designs a solar inverter, a rail controller or an aircraft display usually does not own the factory that physically makes it. Instead it outsources the building to an Electronics Manufacturing Services (EMS) partner — a specialist contract factory that turns a design and a bill-of-materials into finished, tested hardware. Avalon is one of those partners. To follow the business, you need a handful of terms.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
EMSElectronics Manufacturing Services — a company that builds electronics for other firms (the "brands" or OEMs). It does not own the product design or sell under its own name; it manufactures to the customer's spec. Avalon's entire business.
OEMOriginal Equipment Manufacturer — Avalon's customer. The company whose name is on the final product (a clean-energy firm, a rail-systems maker, an aerospace supplier) but which hands the actual manufacturing to Avalon.
PCB / PCBAA Printed Circuit Board is the green board with copper tracks. "PCBA" is that board with all the chips, resistors and connectors soldered on — the electronic heart of any device. Assembling PCBAs is the base layer of EMS.
Box-buildThe full assembly: taking PCBAs, wiring, enclosure, display and everything else and building a complete, working product — tested and ready to ship. The high-value end of EMS, and where Avalon has deliberately moved.
High-mix / low-volumeMaking many different products in small batches (say, a few thousand specialised rail controllers) rather than millions of identical phones. It is harder, but far less price-competitive — Avalon's chosen ground.
The economics that decide everything: where you sit on the value chain

Two EMS firms can build the same gadget and earn wildly different margins depending on how much of it they build. A firm that only stuffs a bare board earns a thin fee on a cheap operation that anyone with a pick-and-place machine can do — it competes on price and loses when a rival quotes a rupee less. A firm that takes raw components and hands back a finished, tested, warranted machine has done design-adjacent, hard-to-replicate work — the customer cannot easily switch, because switching means re-qualifying an entire product. The single most important thing to understand about Avalon is that it is deliberately climbing up that chain, from board-stuffing toward whole-product box-build, because every step up raises both the value it captures per customer and the cost of the customer ever leaving.

Two flavours of EMS — and why they are almost different businesses

  • Consumer / high-volume EMS — building millions of identical, low-cost items (phones, TVs, chargers). It is a scale and price game: razor-thin margins, huge volumes, brutal competition. India's giants like Dixon live largely here.
  • Industrial / high-reliability EMS — building smaller runs of complex, mission-critical hardware for industries where failure is expensive or dangerous: clean energy, rail, aerospace & defence, medical. Fewer units, much more engineering, longer product lives, stickier customers. This is Avalon's world, and it is a structurally better place to compete.
02

What Avalon actually builds: one product, six factory skills

The "full-stack" capability set — read this once and the moat becomes visible.

Avalon's real edge is not any one process — it is owning the whole toolkit under one roof. Most EMS firms do PCB assembly and buy in everything else. Avalon can make the board, wire the harness, wind the magnetics, bend and machine the metal, mould the plastic, and then assemble all of it into a finished box. That vertical breadth is what lets it win the complex builds that a board-only shop cannot bid for.

A board-only EMS firm can bid for Layer 1. Avalon bids for the whole stack — and once it engineers a product with a customer (Layer 0), that customer is designed into Avalon's factories.

The six capabilities, decoded

  • PCB assembly (PCBA) — the electronic core: populating circuit boards with chips and components. The base skill every EMS firm has; Avalon's is the foundation on which the rest is layered.
  • Cable & wire harnesses — the nervous system of a machine: the bundles of wiring that connect everything. Labour-intensive, exacting, and essential to a working box-build.
  • Magnetics — transformers, inductors and coils. Critical in power electronics (think inverters and chargers), where getting the magnetics right is half the product — a genuinely specialised capability few EMS firms carry in-house.
  • Sheet-metal fabrication & precision machining — the enclosures, frames and metal parts. Owning this means Avalon controls the physical body of the product, not just its electronics.
  • Injection-moulded plastics — the moulded housings and plastic components. Another in-house layer that would otherwise be bought from an outside vendor, adding cost and delay.
  • End-to-end box-build — the integration of all of the above into a finished, tested product. This is the prize: the point at which Avalon stops being a parts-maker and becomes the maker of the whole machine.
Why owning every layer is worth explaining

A customer building a complex machine — a solar inverter, a rail controller — has a choice: coordinate a dozen different suppliers (one for boards, one for wiring, one for the metal casing, one for magnetics) and pray they all deliver on spec and on time, or hand the whole thing to a single partner who does all of it. The second is enormously more valuable: fewer interfaces to manage, one throat to hold accountable, one quality system, one delivery schedule. That is what Avalon sells — not a process, but the removal of complexity. The flip side is the same as any integrated model: Avalon also owns every point of failure, and running six different factory disciplines well is far harder than running one. Breadth is a moat only if each layer is genuinely good.

03

The dual-shore model: an Indian firm that manufactures in America

The single most distinctive — and most debated — fact about this company.

Here is the fact that surprises almost everyone who first looks at Avalon: it is an Indian-listed manufacturer whose largest revenue base is the United States, served in part from two American factories. Roughly 60% of revenue comes from US-based customers; roughly 40% from India. Very few Indian manufacturers own and run plants on US soil — most "export" from India. Avalon builds there. That single structural choice shapes the entire investment case, for better and worse.

The US is the bigger book but India is now growing fast too; in FY26 the US business grew ~59% and the India business ~29%. The split is roughly 60/40 in favour of the US — unusual for an Indian-listed EMS name.

Why owning US operations is valuable

  • Proximity to demanding customers. US aerospace, defence and industrial OEMs often want a manufacturing partner on the ground — for design collaboration, fast iteration, quality audits, and in defence, for rules that favour or require domestic build. A plant in America opens doors an India-only exporter cannot.
  • A natural tariff and trade hedge. When the US raises tariffs on imported electronics, a company that already builds inside the US is insulated. Avalon has said its dual-shore setup let it pass on essentially all of the FY26 tariff impact to customers — a live demonstration of the hedge working.
  • Onshore + offshore optionality. Avalon can quote a customer the cheaper India build, the faster US build, or a blend — flexing where each product is made. Rivals with only Indian plants cannot offer the onshore option at all.

Why it is also a burden

  • US manufacturing is expensive. American labour, compliance and overheads are far higher than India's, so the same work earns a thinner margin. The US operations have historically run at low — at times negative — profitability, a drag the cheaper Indian plants have had to carry.
  • Currency exposure. Revenue in dollars, some costs in dollars, reporting in rupees: the group carries genuine foreign-exchange risk that a purely domestic manufacturer does not.
  • Two systems to run well. Managing plants across two continents, cultures and cost structures is operationally hard; a stumble in the US shows up in the consolidated margin.
A genuine surprise: for most of Avalon's listed life, its American factories — the very thing that makes it special — were the loss-making part of the business, while its ten Indian plants did the heavy lifting on profit. So the dual-shore story is really two stories: a distinctive strategic asset, and an operational turnaround that still has to be finished. If the US plants swing from a margin drag to a margin contributor, the whole group's profitability re-rates. That single line item is doing more work in the thesis than almost anything on the top line.
04

The company, the founders, and who controls it

A 25-year-old builder that only reached the public market in 2023.

Avalon is not a start-up — it was incorporated in 1999 and spent two decades quietly building a full-stack EMS operation across India and the US before listing. It came to the public market only in April 2023, which is why many investors are still meeting it for the first time. It is a promoter-founded business, not a professional-manager conglomerate spin-off.

Timeline

  • 1999
    Avalon Technologies incorporated, entering electronics contract manufacturing and, over time, adding capability after capability — boards, harnesses, magnetics, metal, plastics, box-build.
  • 2000s–2010s
    Builds out a dual-shore footprint: manufacturing plants in India plus operations in the United States, deliberately targeting high-mix, high-reliability industries rather than commodity consumer electronics.
  • Apr 2023
    IPO on NSE & BSE at ₹436/share (a ~₹865 Cr issue: fresh equity plus an offer-for-sale). The listing crystallised the founders' decades of building and gave the company capital to grow.
  • FY24
    A soft year — revenue dipped to ~₹867 Cr and margins compressed to ~7% as customer destocking and a weak US patch bit. The reminder that this is a cyclical, customer-dependent business.
  • FY25–FY26
    The recovery. Revenue climbs to ₹1,098 Cr then ₹1,603 Cr; box-build mix rises past half of sales; the US business re-accelerates; order book reaches ~₹2,196 Cr.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the company was founded and is led by promoters Kunhamed Bicha and Bhaskar Srinivasan, who between them built the dual-shore operation over 20-plus years. Both sold part of their stake in the 2023 IPO's offer-for-sale, and promoter holding has drifted down since listing — a pattern worth watching in the shareholding filings, as founder selling can signal either diversification or a view on value.
  • Twelve plants, two countries: Avalon operates roughly 12 manufacturing facilities — about 10 in India and 2 in the United States. The Indian plants (concentrated around Chennai and elsewhere) carry the profit; the US plants provide the onshore capability and the trade hedge.
  • Balance sheet: the business runs with modest borrowings (~₹213 Cr at Mar 2026) against ~₹12,000 Cr of market value — not a highly levered company, and returns have recovered to a ~19–20% RoCE as utilisation improved. The IPO's fresh capital helped fund the growth without straining the balance sheet.
Why the founder-led, recently-listed status matters: a company that built for 24 years before listing carries deep operational know-how and long customer relationships — Avalon's ties to some OEMs go back to product conception, which is exactly the design-in stickiness that protects an EMS firm. But a newly public company also has a short track record as a listed entity: two full years of public results, one of them soft. Investors are underwriting a long private history they largely cannot see, on the strength of two noisy public years.

High-mix, high-reliability: the industries Avalon serves

Why the unglamorous end of electronics is the better business.

Avalon does not build phones or televisions. It builds the electronics inside machines that have to work for years or decades in demanding conditions — a solar inverter baking on a rooftop, a braking controller on a moving train, an avionics box in an aircraft, a monitor in a hospital. This is the high-mix, high-reliability end of EMS: many different products, small batches, exacting standards. It is a deliberately chosen, and deliberately harder, place to compete.

Why "high-reliability" is a better niche than commodity EMS

  • Certification is a moat. To build for aerospace you need AS9100; for medical devices, ISO 13485; for autos, IATF standards. Winning and holding these certifications takes years and audits — and once a customer has qualified Avalon's certified line for a product, moving to a cheaper rival means re-qualifying everything. The paperwork is the barrier.
  • Long product lives, sticky demand. A rail controller or an aerospace box stays in production for many years — often a decade or more. That means the revenue from winning one design keeps flowing long after the win, unlike a consumer gadget replaced every season.
  • Less price war. When failure is dangerous or expensive, customers do not switch suppliers to save a rupee. They value reliability, traceability and trust over the lowest quote — which is precisely why margins here can be defended in a way commodity consumer EMS margins cannot.
  • Diversification by design. Serving six unrelated industries means a downturn in one (say, a soft solar year) can be offset by strength in another (rail, aerospace). It smooths the ride in a business that is otherwise exposed to customer-specific cycles.
The customers tell you the strategy

Look at who Avalon builds for and the positioning becomes concrete: names associated with its book include Collins Aerospace and Meggitt in aerospace, Kyosan in rail signalling, and Zonar Systems in fleet telematics — serious, regulated, long-cycle OEMs, not fast-fashion gadget brands. Avalon says it engages with some of these from the product's conception, doing design-for-manufacturing work before a single unit is built. That is the deepest form of lock-in an EMS firm can have: not "we build your product cheaply," but "your product was designed to be built in our factories." A customer like that does not leave over price; it leaves only if you fail to deliver.

06

Climbing to box-build: where the margin lives

The mix shift that is the single clearest lever on Avalon's profitability.

If there is one number that captures Avalon's strategy in motion, it is the box-build share of revenue. Every point that box-build climbs is a point of the value chain Avalon has taken back from being merely a component supplier — and box-build revenue carries both more value per customer and a deeper hold on that customer. In FY26, box-build reached roughly 54% of revenue, up sharply from the low-to-mid 40s a couple of years earlier.

Box-build crossing the halfway mark is the strategy working: a few years ago it was in the mid-40s. As the mix tilts toward whole-product builds, Avalon captures more value per order and its customers get harder to dislodge.

The mechanism is straightforward but powerful. When Avalon only supplies a PCBA, it earns a fee on one component and competes with every other board shop. When it supplies the whole box, it earns on the board plus the wiring, the magnetics, the metal, the plastic, the assembly and the testing — a far larger slice of the product's value, on work that is much harder for a rival to replicate. And because the customer has now designed and qualified an entire finished product around Avalon's line, the relationship deepens from transactional to structural. Higher revenue per order, better margins, and stickier customers — all from moving one step up the chain.

The device that only fits Avalon: read the box-build mix against the US margin

Avalon has two profitability levers pulling in opposite directions, and the whole thesis is which one wins. Lever one is the box-build mix climbing (~54% and rising) — pure upside, dragging blended margins up. Lever two is the US operations, historically a margin drag, now supposedly turning. Watch them together: if box-build keeps climbing and the American plants stop bleeding, group EBITDA margin can move from ~11% toward the mid-teens and the profit compounds twice over. If box-build stalls or the US stays sub-scale, the margin sits where it is and a ~100× stock has nothing to grow into. The blended EBITDA margin is the one gauge that nets both levers into a single readable number.

07

The tailwinds: China+1, US reshoring, and the EMS boom

Why the whole sector — and Avalon's dual-shore shape in particular — is in the right place.

Avalon sits at the intersection of three big, durable shifts in how the world builds electronics. None is a subsidy fad; each is a structural relocation of manufacturing that plays directly to a dual-shore, high-reliability EMS firm.

The three structural tailwinds
ForceWhat it isWhy it helps Avalon specifically
EMS outsourcing growthBrands worldwide keep handing more of their manufacturing to specialist EMS partners rather than owning factories.Expands the pool of work available to every capable EMS firm — and rewards the full-stack ones that can take on whole products, not just boards.
China+1 diversificationGlobal OEMs, wary of concentrating supply in China, are deliberately building a "plus one" base elsewhere — India high among them.India-based capacity like Avalon's ten plants is exactly the "plus one" that Western customers are shopping for. A tailwind under the Indian half of the business.
US reshoringThe US is pushing to bring electronics manufacturing — especially defence, aerospace and critical-infrastructure hardware — back onto American soil.Avalon already has US plants. Where rivals would have to build American capacity from scratch, Avalon can win onshore work today — its rarest structural advantage.
The insight that separates Avalon's position from the pack

China+1 and US reshoring are usually treated as one story — "manufacturing is moving out of China." But they pull in different directions, and most EMS firms can only ride one. A pure-India exporter benefits from China+1 (work shifting to India) but is on the wrong side of US reshoring (work it wants to keep onshore in America). A pure-US builder is the reverse. Avalon is one of the very few that is structurally positioned to catch both — Indian plants for the China+1 flow, American plants for the reshoring flow. Its dual-shore shape, so often described as a cost burden, is also the only footprint that lets a single company be on the right side of the two biggest forces reshaping electronics manufacturing at once.

None of this removes the cyclicality. EMS demand still swings with customers' own order cycles — the FY24 dip proved that a soft patch at a few big OEMs, or a bout of inventory destocking, can flatten a year. But the direction of the structural forces is unusually favourable, and Avalon's footprint is unusually well shaped to capture them.

The ~₹2,196 Cr order book is real visibility for a company doing ~₹1,600 Cr of annual revenue — a little over a year of work already committed, and up ~25% on the prior year, so the pipeline is filling faster than it is being consumed. Both geographies are growing, and notably it is the US book that grew fastest in FY26 (+59%), which matters because that is where the margin problem — and therefore the margin opportunity — lives.

Why the US turn is the swing factor, not the top line

It is tempting to watch revenue growth as the headline. But Avalon's revenue was already going to grow — the order book and the tailwinds see to that. The variable that actually moves the profit is whether the US operations convert from margin drag to margin contributor as their volumes scale. American factories are expensive to run half-empty; fill them, and fixed costs spread over more output, and a plant that lost money at low utilisation can make money at high utilisation. So the US revenue growing 59% is not just more sales — it is the operational lever that decides whether the two American plants finally earn their keep. A growing US top line that also fixes the US margin is worth far more than the same growth from India, because it repairs the one dent in the group's profitability.

There is a risk buried here too, and it is the flip side of visibility: an order book is a promise from customers, and EMS customers can push out schedules, destock, or delay — as they did in FY24. Concentration matters as well. A high-reliability EMS book is, by nature, built on a smaller number of large, deep customer relationships than a mass-consumer one; that is what makes it sticky, but it also means the loss or slowdown of one major OEM lands harder. Visibility is high, but it is not the same as certainty.

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, fast-growing Indian EMS field — and where Avalon sits in it.

Indian EMS has been one of the hottest listed themes of the decade, and Avalon competes in a field of well-funded, fast-growing rivals. But it is important to see which rivals it actually overlaps with: Avalon plays in high-mix, high-reliability, box-build — not the mass-consumer volume game where the biggest names live.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, the rising tide: Indian EMS is growing fast enough that several of these companies can win at once — this is far less a share-stealing knife-fight than a sector expanding faster than any one player can absorb. Among the high-reliability names it competes with, it is the one that can offer a customer an onshore-American build today. That is what it must lean on, because on pure scale it is outgunned by Kaynes and Syrma. The risk is the mirror image: if the US advantage never translates into US profit, Avalon is just a sub-scale player carrying an expensive footprint its larger rivals do not have to.

So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the blended EBITDA margin, read alongside revenue growth. Revenue growth alone is not enough: FY26's ~46% top-line surge came with an EBITDA margin (~11%) still shy of where it was three years earlier. The bull case demands both — revenue compounding and the margin marching from ~11% toward the mid-teens as box-build mix rises and the US plants fill up. If those two move together, the earnings can catch up to the price. If revenue grows but margin stays stuck near 11% — the US drag unresolved, box-build plateauing — then the profit base is far too small for a ₹12,000 Cr company, and the stock carries risk on the valuation alone. Growth is the easy half of this story; margin is the half that has to be earned.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A distinctive builder in the right place — asked to turn distinctiveness into margin.

Avalon enters the next phase with a genuinely differentiated position: a full-stack, dual-shore, high-reliability EMS firm riding China+1 and US reshoring at once, with a filling order book and a box-build mix past halfway. The real debate is not whether it can grow — the tailwinds and the backlog make growth likely — but whether it can convert a distinctive shape into a distinctive margin fast enough to deserve its price.

The bull case

  • Only-one-of-its-kind footprint — a dual-shore India + US builder positioned to win both China+1 and reshoring work; rivals can ride only one.
  • Box-build mix climbing — past 54% and rising, dragging value-per-customer and margins up as it goes.
  • High-reliability moat — AS9100/ISO 13485 certifications and design-in relationships (Collins, Meggitt, Kyosan) that customers cannot cheaply leave.
  • Order book & recovery — ~₹2,196 Cr backlog, revenue +46% and PAT +78% in FY26, RoCE back to ~20%.
  • US-margin optionality — if the American plants swing from drag to contributor, group margins re-rate on top of the growth.

The bear case

  • Valuation — ~100× earnings on a ~₹113 Cr profit base prices in years of flawless margin expansion; little cushion if either lever stalls.
  • The US drag — the American operations have historically been low-to-negative margin; the turnaround is a hope, not yet a proven result.
  • Cyclicality & concentration — FY24 showed how a customer downturn flattens a year; a high-reliability book leans on a few deep OEM relationships.
  • Margin still sub-FY23 — even after the recovery, EBITDA margin (~11%) is below where it was three years ago; the compounding thesis is unproven.
  • FX & input costs — dollar revenue, cross-border costs and component-price swings all pressure a mid-single-digit-billion-rupee profit.
The bottom line

Avalon has spent 25 years becoming something rare — a full-stack Indian EMS builder that also manufactures on American soil, serving industries where reliability, not price, wins the order. That shape is exactly right for the decade's two biggest manufacturing shifts, and FY26's surge in revenue and profit shows the model working. What is still unproven is the part that always mattered most: turning that distinctive footprint into a durably higher margin — specifically, getting the two US plants to earn their cost while box-build keeps climbing. The next several quarters answer it in one checkable figure: the blended EBITDA margin, and whether it can march from ~11% toward the mid-teens. Watch that line each quarter against the US-segment profitability; it, not the top-line growth, will tell you whether a ~100× valuation was foresight or fantasy.
