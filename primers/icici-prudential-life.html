00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

ICICI Prudential Life Insurance is one of India's largest private life insurers — a joint venture born in 2000 between ICICI Bank, the country's second-largest private bank, and Britain's Prudential plc, a 175-year-old global insurer. For a long stretch it was the largest private life insurer in India, and it remains a top-three player. But its defining trait sets it apart from HDFC Life and SBI Life: ICICI Pru is the most ULIP-heavy of the big private insurers — market-linked savings plans are still nearly half of its new business. That single fact shapes everything: a lower profit margin on new business, but a vast, market-sensitive pool of invested assets and a different risk profile from its peers.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It sells a lot — but at a structurally thinner margin, now rising. In FY26 (year to March 2026) ICICI Pru wrote about ₹10,641 Cr of APE ("annualised premium equivalent", the industry's true sales gauge, +2.2%) and collected ₹53,125 Cr of total premium (+8.5%). The lifetime profit on that new business (the VNB) was ₹2,629 Cr at a 24.7% margin — a margin that has expanded ~190 bps as the company deliberately shifts toward higher-margin protection and non-par. Because so much of the book is low-margin ULIP, that 24.7% still sits a shade below HDFC Life's ~24–25%.
  • The scorecard is embedded value, not accounting profit. Reported net profit jumped to ₹1,607 Cr (+34.6%) — but PAT is a poor gauge for a life insurer, which front-loads its costs (see §03–04). The number analysts track — embedded value, the worth of the whole book — stood at ₹52,989 Cr, growing at an operating return (RoEV) of about 11.9%.
  • The market pays a discount for it. At a market cap around ₹70,000 Cr, ICICI Pru trades at roughly 1.3–1.5× embedded value — a lower multiple than HDFC Life (~2.1×), though richer than SBI Life on some measures. The discount reflects the ULIP-heavy mix (earnings that swing with markets) and a lower RoEV — even as bulls point to a rising margin, a huge AUM and one of the sector's most diversified distribution engines.
In plain terms

Think of ICICI Pru Life as India's biggest market-linked savings machine, wrapped in a life-insurance licence. Where HDFC Life and SBI Life lean on guaranteed and traditional savings, ICICI Pru made its name selling ULIPs — plans where the customer's money is invested in equity and debt funds, the customer keeps the market upside and the market risk, and the insurer mostly earns fees. That means a thinner margin per policy but an enormous pool of assets (₹3.14 lakh Cr of AUM). It also means the company built one of the widest distribution nets in the business — deliberately reducing its dependence on the ICICI Bank branch counter. The stock debate isn't about franchise quality — this is a top-tier insurer — it's whether the market-linked mix and lower returns justify a discount, or whether the rising margin and diversified engine make it the sector's underpriced compounder.

01

The ULIP-heavy book — the margin-vs-AUM trade-off

This is what truly sets ICICI Pru apart from every other large private insurer. Understand this one idea and you understand the whole stock.

Every large private life insurer sells a blend of products, but ICICI Prudential Life is unmistakably the most ULIP-oriented of the big three. In FY26, linked (ULIP) plans were still ~48% of its APE — versus roughly a third at a balanced peer like HDFC Life. A "ULIP" — Unit-Linked Insurance Plan — is really a market-linked savings product with a thin life-cover wrapper: the customer's premium is invested in equity/debt funds, the returns (and the risk) belong to the customer, and the insurer mostly clips fund-management and mortality/administration charges. That design choice cascades into three consequences that define ICICI Pru.

Linked eased to ~48% (from ~49%) as protection rose ~220 bps to ~17.9% of APE. The whole margin story is this slow rotation away from ULIP toward protection, non-par and annuity.

Consequence 1 — a structurally LOWER VNB margin

Because a ULIP passes the investment return (and most of the value) to the customer, the insurer's slice per rupee of premium is thin. The lifetime profit on new business — the VNB margin — is therefore structurally lower for a ULIP-heavy book. ICICI Pru's 24.7% FY26 margin sits below the ~26–28% that a protection- or non-par-heavy insurer can reach. This is not mismanagement — it's the arithmetic of the mix. It is also why, for years, ICICI Pru's margin lagged the peer group, and why the market applied a discount.

Consequence 2 — a huge, market-sensitive AUM

The flip-side is powerful. Because ULIP premiums are invested on behalf of the customer, a ULIP-heavy insurer accumulates one of the largest asset pools in the industry. ICICI Pru manages roughly ₹3.14 lakh Cr of AUM — among the biggest of any private life insurer. That AUM throws off steady fund-management fees, gives the insurer scale and investment heft, and grows with the market. But it cuts both ways: when equity markets fall, ULIP sales slow (customers buy fewer market-linked plans) and the AUM shrinks — so ICICI Pru's new-business growth is more equity-market-sensitive than a guarantee-heavy peer's. Its fortunes are tied, more than any big peer's, to the mood of the Indian stock market.

Consequence 3 — a different persistency & risk profile

ULIP customers behave differently from traditional-savings customers. They can be more market-timing (buying when markets are hot, lapsing when they cool), which can pressure early persistency — but a ULIP carries far less interest-rate/guarantee risk for the insurer than a non-par savings plan, because the insurer isn't promising a fixed return. So ICICI Pru swaps one risk for another: less exposure to falling interest rates (the bogey of guarantee-heavy peers), more exposure to equity-market cycles and customer behaviour.

The trade-off, in one paragraph

A ULIP-heavy book is a genuine double-edged sword, and it is the single most important thing to grasp about ICICI Pru. On the downside: thinner margins (the customer keeps the investment upside, so the insurer earns less per policy) and more market sensitivity (sales and AUM swing with equities). On the upside: a gigantic asset pool that generates fee income and scale, and far less interest-rate/guarantee risk than the non-par-heavy peers who must earn more than they promised for decades. ICICI Pru's whole strategy for the last few years has been to keep the AUM engine humming while grinding the margin higher — pushing protection (+32% APE in FY26) and non-par to lift the blended VNB margin (+190 bps, to 24.7%) without abandoning the ULIP franchise that gives it scale. Watching that rotation — protection up, margin up, while ULIP stays the volume base — is watching the ICICI Pru thesis play out.

HDFC Life sells balance and SBI Life sells reach. ICICI Pru sells the market itself — the biggest market-linked savings book in private India, deliberately steering toward fatter-margin protection without giving up the AUM scale that ULIPs uniquely provide.
02

The diversified distribution engine — de-risked by design

While peers lean hard on a single bank counter, ICICI Pru deliberately built one of the broadest, least-concentrated distribution mixes in the industry. That's a strength, not an accident.

Here is the second thing that sets ICICI Pru apart. Most big private insurers are bancassurance-heavy — SBI Life runs the bulk of its sales through SBI's branches; HDFC Life leans on HDFC Bank. ICICI Pru started the same way, deeply dependent on the ICICI Bank counter. But after a period when that single channel dominated — and slowed — the company made a deliberate strategic pivot to diversify. Today its distribution is among the least concentrated of any large insurer: no single channel is more than about a third of new business.

How the pivot happened — and why it's a moat of a different kind

  • The wake-up call. When ICICI Pru relied heavily on the ICICI Bank branch network, its growth became hostage to that single channel's productivity — and when banca sales softened, the whole company's growth softened with it. Management concluded that over-dependence on one counter was a strategic vulnerability.
  • Building the alternatives. The company scaled a large agency force (~2.4 lakh advisors), expanded a direct/digital channel (including an NRI push via GIFT City), and — crucially — signed a wide web of partnerships: it now has 53 bank partners giving access to 26,000+ branches beyond ICICI Bank, plus ~1,500 non-bank partners. So even its "bancassurance" is itself diversified across dozens of banks, not just its parent.
  • Why diversification is a genuine strength. A single-channel insurer lives and dies by one relationship — a shock to that channel (a bank pushing a rival product, a regulatory change, a productivity dip) hits it hard. A multi-channel insurer can lean on whichever channel is firing. Diversification is de-risking: it smooths growth, reduces bargaining-power leverage over any one partner, and insulates ICICI Pru against the very "open-architecture" regulatory threat that keeps bancassurance-heavy peers awake (see §08).
The distribution edge, explained

An insurance policy is a commodity — the same abstract promise everyone sells. The durable advantage is who can put it in front of the most people, most cheaply, without being over-exposed to any one gatekeeper. HDFC Life and SBI Life win on the reach and cheapness of one premier bank; ICICI Pru wins on breadth and resilience — five real channels, none dominant, so a wobble in any one is absorbed by the others. The trade-off: a diversified model is a bit costlier to run than piggybacking on a single giant bank (you must fund an agency force, digital, and many partnerships), which is one reason ICICI Pru's cost ratios aren't the lowest in the sector. But in a world where regulators periodically threaten to force banks to sell rivals' products, ICICI Pru's de-concentrated engine is arguably the most future-proof distribution model among the big private insurers.

The subtle contrast with the peers: for HDFC Life and SBI Life, "the bank" IS the moat — and also the concentration risk. For ICICI Pru, the moat is the absence of concentration: it turned an early weakness (an over-reliance on ICICI Bank that once capped its growth) into a distinctive strength. When you read the peers' primers, the bancassurance channel is the hero and the vulnerability at once. Here, diversification is both.
03

Life-insurance economics 101: how an insurer earns

No actuarial degree required — just five ideas that drive everything, and one big reason accounting profit misleads.

A life insurer is, at heart, a promise-and-float business. It collects premiums today in exchange for a promise to pay much later — on death, or when a savings/ULIP policy matures — and it earns in two ways: from the margin baked into each policy it sells, and from managing the giant pool of premiums ("the float") while it waits to pay claims. For ICICI Pru, with its ULIP-heavy book, the second engine — the fees on that huge asset pool — is unusually important. Ordinary "profit" barely captures any of this, because a policy sold today produces cash flows for 20–40 years. So the industry built its own vocabulary. Five terms unlock the whole thing — with ICICI Pru's FY26 numbers alongside each.

Why the ₹1,607 Cr "profit" understates the business

Here is the single most important idea for reading any life insurer. When ICICI Pru sells a 20-year policy, it books the cost of writing it — commission, medical checks, setup — almost entirely up front, but the profit dribbles in slowly over the next two decades as the customer keeps paying. Under normal accounting, a fast-growing insurer therefore looks less profitable the harder it sells, because this year's growth loads this year's costs while the reward is deferred. That's why even a 35%-up net profit of ₹1,607 Cr is a poor guide to the real economics. The industry fixes this with two purpose-built numbers that value the future cash flows properly: VNB (the lifetime value created by this year's sales) and embedded value (the value of the whole book). You judge a life insurer on those — not on the accountant's bottom line.

The two profit engines, simply

(1) Underwriting / new-business margin. Every policy is priced to earn a spread — the premium charged exceeds the expected cost of claims plus expenses plus any returns owed to the customer. Bundle a year's sales together and the lifetime profit is the VNB; express it as a percentage of APE and you get the VNB margin (24.7% for ICICI Pru). (2) The float / fee engine. Because claims are paid years later, the insurer invests the premium pool in the meantime. For a traditional insurer it keeps the investment spread; for a ULIP-heavy insurer like ICICI Pru it mostly earns fund-management fees on that pool — which is why its ₹3.14 lakh Cr AUM matters so much. The art is selling more policies, at higher margins, that stay on the books longer, over a bigger asset base. Every section that follows is really about one of those levers.

04

Embedded value & VNB — the real scorecard, and why P/EV

This is how insurers are actually judged — and it's the number the market attaches ICICI Pru's discount to.

If you only watch reported net profit, you'll misread ICICI Pru. The industry uses two purpose-built numbers that capture future cash flows: Embedded Value (EV) — the worth of the whole existing book — and Value of New Business (VNB) with its margin — the worth of this year's sales. Because a life insurer's value is a stack of decades-long promises, the market prices it on a multiple of embedded value (P/EV), not a price-to-earnings ratio. ICICI Pru's lower P/EV versus HDFC Life is the whole valuation debate.

Embedded value & P/EV, explained without jargon

Imagine you could fast-forward and collect every future profit ICICI Pru will earn from policies already sold, discount it back to today's money, and add the company's net assets. That total is the embedded value — ₹52,989 Cr. Each year it should grow by the unwind of that discount (time passing brings future profits closer, so they're worth more today), plus the VNB you bolt on by selling new policies (₹2,629 Cr this year), then move with markets and any change in the actuaries' assumptions. So the health check is simple: is EV compounding at a healthy rate (RoEV), and is each year's new business added at a fat margin? And because the value lives in future promises, the market can't sensibly use a P/E ratio — it uses price ÷ embedded value (P/EV). ICICI Pru trades around 1.3–1.5× EV — the market saying "I'll pay ~₹1.30–1.50 for every ₹1 of embedded value", noticeably less than the ~₹2.10 it pays for HDFC Life. That discount is the ULIP mix and the lower RoEV made visible.

The nuance in FY26: the margin improved (up ~190 bps to 24.7% as protection surged and mix shifted) and VNB grew a healthy ~11% — genuinely good news for a company long criticised for thin margins. But RoEV slipped to ~11.9% (from ~13% the year before), pressured by only 2.2% APE growth, a persistency variance drag on EV, and — importantly — a very high solvency ratio (227%) that means a lot of idle capital diluting the return. The bull case is that margin expansion and the diversified engine keep compounding VNB; the bear case is that RoEV in the low-teens, well below peers, deserves the discount.

05

The company and who controls it

A landmark ICICI Bank–Prudential plc joint venture — India's first private life insurer, and for years its largest.

ICICI Prudential Life began in 2000 as a joint venture: ICICI Bank — India's second-largest private-sector bank — contributed capital, brand and distribution; Britain's Prudential plc, a 175-year-old global insurer, contributed insurance and actuarial expertise. It was among the first private life insurers to launch after the sector opened up, quickly became the largest private insurer, and listed in 2016 — the first Indian life insurer to go public. Today it remains a top-three private player, controlled by ICICI Bank with Prudential plc a major minority partner.

Timeline

  • 2000–01
    Incorporated as a JV of ICICI Bank and Prudential plc — one of India's first private life insurers after the sector was liberalised.
  • 2000s
    Rapidly becomes the largest private life insurer in India, pioneering the ULIP — the market-linked plan that would define its identity and its distinctive, AUM-heavy model.
  • Sep 2016
    IPO on NSE & BSE — the first Indian life insurer to list; a marquee ₹6,000 Cr offering, with ICICI Bank selling down part of its stake.
  • 2018–2022
    A period of ULIP over-dependence and slower growth prompts a strategic pivot: diversify distribution (build agency, direct, partnerships) and lift margins (push protection and non-par).
  • FY23–FY25
    Absorbs a wave of IRDAI reforms — new surrender-value norms, expense-of-management rules — while the diversification and margin-expansion strategy gains traction; protection and non-par grow.
  • FY26
    Total premium ~₹53,125 Cr (+8.5%), EV ₹52,989 Cr (+10.5%); VNB margin expands to 24.7% (+190 bps) as retail protection APE grows ~32%; PAT jumps to ₹1,607 Cr; solvency a very high 227.3%.

Ownership & management

  • The controlling owner: ICICI Bank ≈ 51% (the "promoter"). This anchors ICICI Pru to India's second-largest private bank — the original bancassurance channel — and links its brand and fortunes to the ICICI group.
  • The foreign partner: Prudential plc (via Prudential Corporation Holdings) ≈ 22% — the UK/Asia-focused insurer that brought the actuarial and product expertise, and remains a committed long-term shareholder (unlike some peers' foreign partners who exited).
  • Public & institutional float: the balance (~27%) is a large, liquid free float held by FIIs, domestic mutual funds and insurers, and retail investors.
  • Balance-sheet strength: solvency ratio ~227.3% against a 150% regulatory minimum — one of the most heavily capitalised insurers in the sector. That's a comfort on safety, but the flip-side is over-capitalisation that dilutes return on equity/EV (a recurring bear point). Claim-settlement record is strong (near-99% on individual death claims).
Why the ownership matters: like HDFC Life, ICICI Pru has a private blue-chip bank as promoter — so it avoids the "PSU discount" that weighs on SBI Life's rating. And unlike HDFC Life (whose foreign partner abrdn sold out) or SBI Life (whose BNP Paribas Cardif trimmed down), ICICI Pru's foreign partner Prudential plc has stayed the course at ~22% — a vote of confidence in the franchise. The offset: the very high solvency ratio means a lot of shareholder capital sits idle, holding down RoEV — part of why the stock trades below HDFC Life.
06

The AUM engine, persistency & cost ratios

A ULIP-heavy insurer lives or dies by three things: how big its asset pool is, whether customers keep paying, and how cheaply it runs. Here's the plumbing behind the VNB margin.

ICICI Pru's ULIP heritage gives it one of the largest AUMs among private life insurers — ~₹3.14 lakh Cr. That pool is a real asset: it earns fund-management fees, confers investment scale, and grows (or shrinks) with markets. But an asset pool only compounds if the underlying policies stay alive and the company runs them cheaply. Two unglamorous ratios decide that: persistency (do customers keep paying?) and the cost / expense ratio (how much of each premium is eaten by expenses?).

Why these ratios decide the profit

Persistency matters because an insurer pays most of a policy's acquisition cost up front (big first-year commission and setup) and only recoups it if the customer keeps paying for years. A lapse in year two can turn a profitable-looking sale into a loss — which is why the actuaries' VNB assumes a certain persistency, and why a miss on it directly reduces embedded value (as it did in FY26). For a ULIP-heavy insurer this is doubly important: ULIP customers can be more market-sensitive, buying when equities are hot and lapsing when they cool, so keeping them invested through cycles is a real operational challenge. The cost ratio is the other half: the lower the expense per rupee of premium, the more survives as profit. ICICI Pru's costs aren't the sector's lowest — running five distribution channels (rather than piggybacking on one giant bank) is inherently a bit costlier — but that's the price of its diversified, de-risked engine. Strong persistency plus disciplined costs over a huge AUM is the machinery that turns the ULIP franchise into value.

The AUM, in perspective: ₹3.14 lakh Cr of assets is a genuine strategic asset — it makes ICICI Pru one of India's larger institutional investors and generates steady fee income regardless of new sales. But remember the double edge from §01: because so much of it is ULIP money, a sustained equity-market fall would both shrink the AUM (mark-to-market) and slow new ULIP sales — a one-two punch a guarantee-heavy peer wouldn't feel as sharply. The AUM is the reward for the ULIP model; the market sensitivity is its cost.
07

Market share, penetration & the tax overhang

A top-three private insurer in one of the most under-insured large economies on earth — but with a ULIP-specific tax cloud the peers feel less.

ICICI Pru is a top-three private life insurer, jostling with SBI Life and HDFC Life for the profitable individual customer. The bigger story is the size of the runway: India is one of the most under-insured large economies in the world, so the pie itself is set to grow for decades. But ICICI Pru carries one industry headwind more acutely than its peers — the tax change on high-ticket ULIPs and non-par, precisely the products it is strongest in.

Life penetration of only ~3% of GDP — well under half the global average — is the multi-decade tailwind. ICICI Pru's in-force sum assured grew ~16.9% to ₹46.1 lakh Cr in FY26, a sign the protection push is expanding real cover.

The opportunity — and ICICI Pru's specific angle on it

  • India is dramatically under-insured. Life-insurance penetration (premiums as a % of GDP) is only around 3% — well under half the ~6–7% global average — and Swiss Re has estimated India's mortality protection gap at over US$16 trillion. Closing even a slice of that is decades of protection-premium growth — and protection is ICICI Pru's fastest-growing, highest-margin line (+32% APE in FY26).
  • Financialisation of savings. As Indian households shift from gold and property toward financial assets — insurance, mutual funds, pensions — a rising share of a growing savings pool flows to insurers. Here ICICI Pru's ULIP franchise is a direct beneficiary: it is the natural home for savers who want market exposure inside an insurance wrapper.
  • Protection is the margin-and-mission overlap. The under-insurance gap is largest among younger, salaried, urban Indians — and pure-protection term cover is both the answer to that gap and ICICI Pru's fattest-margin product. Every protection sale simultaneously serves the social need and lifts the blended VNB margin.
  • Regulator tailwind: "Insurance for All by 2047." IRDAI's stated mission is universal insurance by India's centenary. The policy direction is firmly toward more coverage, more distribution, and a bigger addressable market.
The ULIP-specific tax cloud: Budget moves in recent years removed the tax-free maturity benefit on high-premium ULIPs (annual premium above ₹2.5 lakh) and on high-ticket non-par savings (above ₹5 lakh). Because ICICI Pru is the most ULIP-and-savings-oriented of the big insurers, this bites it harder than protection-heavy peers — it dampened the very high-ticket savings sales that once padded its volumes. It's a structural headwind unique to the savings-heavy part of the book, and a real reason ICICI Pru has been pushing so hard toward protection.
Why the opportunity still favours ICICI Pru

Under-insurance is an industry-wide tailwind — every insurer benefits. What makes it ICICI Pru's opportunity is the combination of a huge AUM base, a broad distribution net, and a rising margin. As Indians financialise, the ULIP franchise captures the savings flow; as they seek real protection, ICICI Pru's fast-growing term book captures the highest-margin business and lifts the blended margin. The tax change on high-ticket ULIPs is a genuine drag, but it also accelerates the very mix-shift toward protection that the market wants to see. Combine a structurally growing pie with a top-three brand, a diversified engine and an improving margin, and you have the shape of a multi-decade compounding story — the question is only the price the market pays for it (see §09).

08

IRDAI, regulation & the rules that shape the margin

A heavily-regulated business where a rule change can move margins and mix — and where, for once, ICICI Pru's diversified model is a shield.

Life insurance in India is governed by the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI), which sets the rules on product design, surrender values, expense caps, commissions and how insurers may distribute. Regulation isn't background noise — it is a permanent swing factor. For ICICI Pru, the recent rule changes cut both ways: some pressured margins, but the GST reform and the diversified-distribution model have actually worked in its favour.

The rule changes that matter now

  • Surrender-value norms (effective Oct 2024). IRDAI now requires insurers to pay a higher "special surrender value" to customers who exit early — even after just one year's premium. This reduces the profit an insurer keeps on lapses, pressuring margins on savings products. ICICI Pru absorbed it and still expanded its VNB margin — evidence the mix-shift is more than offsetting the drag.
  • The GST change (effective Sept 2025) — a tailwind here. The government cut GST on individual life products to nil. For most insurers this raised costs (loss of input-tax credit); but ICICI Pru's management flagged it as a tailwind for retail protection — cheaper term cover improved customer traction and awareness, feeding the +32% protection growth that is lifting the margin.
  • Tax on high-ticket ULIPs & non-par (the ICICI-Pru-specific headwind). As noted in §07, the removal of tax-free maturity on high-premium ULIPs (>₹2.5 lakh) and non-par savings (>₹5 lakh) hits ICICI Pru's savings-heavy book harder than protection-led peers — a structural dampener on its highest-ticket volumes.
  • Bancassurance, "open architecture" & FDI. The rules let a bank act as a corporate agent for up to three life insurers, and periodic mis-selling concerns keep alive the debate over forcing full "open architecture". Legislative moves to raise the FDI limit and explore composite licences point to an expansionary, competitive direction of travel.
Why regulation is (mostly) less scary for ICICI Pru

Two threads run through the rulebook. On product economics, regulation has been mixed for ICICI Pru: surrender-value norms and the high-ticket-ULIP tax are headwinds, but the GST change actually helped its protection push, and the net effect has been a rising VNB margin — a good sign of franchise resilience. On the channel, here is where ICICI Pru is structurally advantaged versus its peers. The single biggest regulatory threat to a bancassurance-heavy insurer is any move toward mandatory open architecture — forcing bank branches to sell competing insurers alongside their own. For SBI Life or HDFC Life, whose books lean heavily on one bank, that would be a serious blow. ICICI Pru, whose bancassurance is only ~30% of APE and is itself spread across 53 bank partners, would be far less exposed. Its diversified distribution — built to solve a growth problem — turns out to be its best regulatory hedge.

The rule to watch: for the sector broadly, the biggest structural swing factor is any move toward mandatory open architecture in bancassurance. It is exactly the risk that would matter more to ICICI Pru's peers than to ICICI Pru — one of the few places where its distinctive model is a clear defensive edge rather than a margin cost.
09

Financials & valuation

A rising margin and a huge AUM, trading at a discount to HDFC Life. Is the discount the ULIP mix and low RoEV — or an opportunity?

FY26 was, on balance, an improving year on quality even if headline APE growth was modest: the VNB margin expanded ~190 bps, VNB grew ~11%, and PAT jumped ~35%. Yet ICICI Pru trades at a clear discount to HDFC Life on price-to-embedded-value — the market's way of pricing the ULIP-heavy, market-sensitive mix and the low-teens RoEV. Whether that discount is deserved is the entire debate.

On capital & the RoEV drag: the 227% solvency ratio is double the 150% minimum — reassuring on safety, but it means a lot of shareholder capital is sitting idle rather than working. That idle capital mechanically depresses return on embedded value. A more capital-efficient stance (higher payout, or writing more business per rupee of capital) is one lever that could lift RoEV toward peer levels and help close the valuation gap — something to watch in the years ahead.
10

The industry and the rivals

An under-penetrated market, a dominant state incumbent, and a handful of well-run private challengers — each with a distinct signature. ICICI Pru's is the market-linked mix.

Indian life insurance is a two-tier market: state-owned LIC still writes over half of all premium (dominant in group and rural agency business), while a cluster of well-run private insurers compete hard for the higher-value individual customer. ICICI Pru is one of the top three in that private pack, alongside SBI Life and HDFC Life. What distinguishes the three is instructive.

The three-way contrast, in one paragraph

The big three private insurers are three different bets. SBI Life is the scale-and-cost play — the biggest bancassurance machine, the lowest costs, the highest RoEV — held back only by a state-owned-parent discount. HDFC Life is the quality-and-brand play — the most balanced book, the best product reputation, a premier private-bank parent — for which the market pays the richest multiple. ICICI Pru is the market-linked, diversified play — the biggest ULIP franchise and AUM, the broadest distribution net, and a margin that is climbing off a structurally lower base — trading at a discount to HDFC Life. Put crudely: SBI Life offers the best returns cheaply, HDFC Life the best quality dearly, and ICICI Pru a rising-margin, market-geared franchise at a middling price. Which suits you depends on whether you want cyclicality (ICICI Pru), balance (HDFC Life), or efficiency (SBI Life).

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A market-linked franchise with a rising margin, priced at a discount — betting on RoEV and re-rating.

ICICI Pru is a top-tier private life insurer with two distinctive traits — the biggest ULIP/AUM franchise and the most diversified distribution — and a genuinely improving margin. The debate is whether the market is right to discount it for the ULIP-heavy mix and low RoEV, or whether the margin expansion, huge AUM and de-risked distribution make it the sector's underpriced compounder. The investment question is the mirror image of HDFC Life's: not "is the premium deserved?" but "is the discount too harsh?"

The bull case

  • Rising margin — VNB margin up ~190 bps to 24.7% as protection (+32% APE) and non-par grow; the long-standing "thin margin" knock is fading.
  • The AUM engine — ~₹3.14 lakh Cr, among private India's largest; a fee-generating, scale-conferring asset that compounds with the market and financialisation.
  • Diversified distribution — five real channels, none dominant; a de-risked model that is also the sector's best hedge against open-architecture regulation.
  • Cheap vs HDFC Life — ~1.3–1.5× EV against HDFC Life's ~2.1×; a rising margin plus a lifting RoEV could drive a re-rating toward the premium peer.
  • Committed owners & huge runway — ICICI Bank ~51% and Prudential plc ~22% (a foreign partner who stayed); India ~3% penetration, a >$16tn protection gap, "Insurance for All by 2047".

The bear case

  • Structurally lower margin — the ULIP-heavy mix caps the VNB margin below protection-led peers; the improvement, while real, starts from a lower base.
  • Market-sensitive earnings — ULIP sales and AUM swing with equities; a sustained market fall is a one-two punch (shrinking AUM and slowing sales) peers feel less.
  • Low RoEV — ~11.9%, well below SBI Life's high-teens, dragged by over-capitalisation (227% solvency) and modest APE growth; the discount may be deserved.
  • Persistency wobble & savings-tax overhang — 13th-month persistency dipped to ~84.5%; and the tax on high-ticket ULIPs/non-par bites ICICI Pru harder than peers.
  • Modest top-line growth — APE +2.2% in FY26; the re-rating case needs both faster growth and a higher RoEV to actually materialise.
The bottom line

ICICI Pru is a distinctive franchise trading at a discount to its best-regarded peer. Its two signatures — the biggest market-linked/AUM book and the most diversified distribution — are genuine differentiators, and its long-criticised margin is now climbing. The investment question is whether the market's discount to HDFC Life is justified by the ULIP-heavy, market-sensitive mix and the low-teens RoEV, or whether an improving margin and a de-risked engine make it undervalued. Watch three things each year — the VNB margin (does the protection/non-par mix-shift keep lifting it toward peers?), RoEV (can capital efficiency and growth push it out of the low-teens toward the mid-to-high-teens of HDFC Life and SBI Life?), and equity-market conditions (the ULIP book's sensitivity means a market cycle moves both sales and AUM). Get the first two right and the discount to HDFC Life should narrow; if RoEV stays stuck in the low-teens and growth stays modest, the discount is the market pricing the ULIP model honestly.
