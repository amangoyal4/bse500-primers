Mazagon Dock Shipbuilders, the yard that builds India's submarines

A complete walk through India's premier warship and submarine builder — how a 250-year-old Mumbai dockyard became a near-monopoly on the Navy's most strategic hardware, why a shipbuilder is sitting on more cash than debt many times over, and how a ₹20,000-crore order book (with a ₹70,000-crore submarine deal knocking on the door) turned a sleepy defence PSU into one of India's most re-rated stocks.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Mazagon Dock builds the warships and submarines of the Indian Navy. Out of a historic dockyard on Mumbai's eastern waterfront it constructs guided-missile destroyers (the Kolkata and Visakhapatnam classes), stealth frigates (the Nilgiri class), and — crucially — conventional submarines (the Kalvari/Scorpene class, built under technology transfer from France's Naval Group). It is a Navratna defence public-sector undertaking under the Ministry of Defence, and it holds a distinction no other Indian yard can match: it is the only shipyard in the country that can build destroyers, stealth frigates and conventional submarines all at once. In submarines it is, effectively, India's near-monopoly builder.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A cash-rich, high-return "manufacturer" that looks nothing like one. FY26 revenue was ₹13,006 Cr (+13.8%) and net profit ₹2,578 Cr (+6.8%), on a ~36% return on capital and ~29% return on equity. Yet it carries almost no debt and sits on a mountain of cash — roughly ₹13,000 Cr of cash and deposits — because customers (the Navy / MoD) pay it advances before ships are built.
  • The order book is the whole story. It ended FY26 with a ₹20,535 Cr order book — about 1.6 years of revenue already signed — anchored by Project 17A frigates, remaining Kalvari submarines and the AIP (air-independent propulsion) programme. Behind that sits a pipeline reported north of ₹3 lakh crore: the ~₹70,000 Cr+ P-75(I) submarine deal (with Germany's TKMS, negotiations completed and awaiting final government approval), three additional Scorpene submarines (~₹36,000 Cr) and next-generation destroyers (~₹70,000–80,000 Cr).
  • The valuation is the debate. At ~₹1.03 lakh crore market cap on ₹2,578 Cr of profit, the stock trades near 40× earnings — many times what a "government shipyard" fetched a few years ago. Defence PSUs have been massively re-rated on the indigenisation story. Bulls buy the scarcity and the pipeline; bears point to lumpy execution, one giant customer, and a rich multiple for a business whose reported profit leans partly on interest income from that cash pile.
In plain terms

Think of Mazagon Dock as the country's flagship naval workshop — the place the Indian Navy goes when it needs a new destroyer or, above all, a new submarine. Building a warship is a multi-year, ferociously complex job: welding a 7,500-tonne steel hull, packing it with weapons, radar and reactors' worth of systems, then proving it at sea. Building a submarine is harder still, and India has spent decades and billions letting exactly one yard — this one — learn how. That hard-won, un-copyable capability, plus a Navy that is spending trillions of rupees to modernise and to build at home rather than import, is why a once-dull government dockyard is now treated like a growth stock. The catch: it has one customer, its deliveries come in lumps, and a lot of the excitement is about orders that haven't been signed yet.

01

Warship-building 101: how a shipyard actually earns money

No naval-architecture degree required — just the four ideas that drive the whole business.

A naval shipyard is, financially, a long-cycle project business. It signs a fixed-price (or largely fixed-price) contract to build a specified number of ships or submarines, then spends five to ten years constructing each platform, recognising revenue as the work progresses and getting paid in stages along the way. Four ideas explain almost everything.

The only concepts you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for Mazagon Dock)
Order bookThe value of ships already contracted but not yet delivered — the clearest window into future revenue. Mazagon Dock's was ₹20,535 Cr at Mar 2026. In a business where a single contract runs for years, the order book is the forward P&L.
Stage / progress paymentsThe Navy pays advances and milestone payments as construction hits agreed stages — not at the end. This is the single most important quirk of the model: the customer funds the working capital, which is why the yard is cash-rich (see §07).
Indigenous contentHow much of a ship is designed and made in India versus imported. Higher indigenous content = more value captured domestically, and it is now a government mandate. Rising indigenisation is both a policy tailwind and an execution challenge.
Order inflow vs executionTwo separate levers. Inflow is winning new contracts (lumpy, headline-grabbing). Execution is turning the backlog into delivered ships and recognised revenue (steady, but exposed to delays). A great year for one can be a quiet year for the other.
Why warship-building is a surprisingly good business — for the incumbent

On paper, building complex machines to a fixed price over a decade sounds risky. But for the yard that already can do it, three features make it very attractive. (1) The customer funds you. Because the Navy pays advances and milestones, the yard barely needs its own capital to fund the build — it can run on negative working capital and pile up cash. (2) The barriers are enormous. You need dry docks, cranes, skilled welders, decades of design know-how, security clearances, and a track record the Navy trusts with its most sensitive platforms. That takes generations to build and cannot be bought off a shelf. (3) The demand is strategic and long. A country doesn't stop needing warships; it plans its fleet decades ahead, so the order pipeline stretches out over multiple governments and budgets. Put those together and the incumbent yard earns high returns on very little capital, with visibility most companies can only dream of — provided it can keep delivering.

02

The submarine & warship moat — a strategic asset you can't replicate

The single most important thing to understand about this company — read this once and the whole story clicks.

Mazagon Dock's real franchise is not a product; it is a capability that took India half a century and enormous investment to build, and that no new entrant can copy. It is the only yard in the country that can simultaneously build guided-missile destroyers, stealth frigates and conventional submarines — and in submarines specifically, it is effectively India's near-monopoly builder. For the Indian Navy, this makes the yard less a "supplier" and more a strategic national asset: if you want an indigenous submarine, there is essentially one place to go.

Why submarines are the deepest part of the moat

Surface warships are hard. Submarines are a different order of difficulty — and that difficulty is exactly what protects the incumbent.

  • The physics is unforgiving. A submarine is a steel tube that must survive crushing pressure hundreds of metres down, stay perfectly watertight, run silently so it can't be detected, and keep a crew alive for weeks. The welding, the hull steel, the systems integration and the quality control are far beyond ordinary shipbuilding — a single flaw can be fatal.
  • The know-how is transferred, not taught. India learned submarine construction through technology-transfer partnerships — first the German Type 209 (Shishumar class) in the 1980s, then the French Scorpene/Kalvari class from Naval Group. Mazagon Dock is where that knowledge physically lives: in its dedicated submarine division (set up in 1984), its trained workforce and its facilities. A rival can't download it.
  • The relationships are decades deep. Building the Navy's most sensitive, most classified platforms requires trust, clearances and a track record built over 40+ years. That relationship is itself a barrier no amount of capital can shortcut.
Why this is a genuine moat, not just "a big factory"

The clean way to understand the moat is to ask: if you wanted to compete, what would you need? You'd need deep-water dry docks and cranes on scarce coastal land, a workforce of thousands of specialist welders and engineers, a licensed technology-transfer relationship with a foreign submarine designer, security clearances to handle classified naval systems, and — above all — decades of proven deliveries so the Navy trusts you with a platform on which sailors' lives and national security depend. You cannot assemble that in a few years or with a cheque. India spent 50 years and vast sums building exactly one yard that can do the full job. That's why Mazagon Dock isn't really competing on price with anyone — it's the only credible answer to a question the Navy keeps asking, which is the definition of a strategic-asset moat.

The scarcity, in one line: India runs a two-yard model for many programmes (Mazagon Dock and Kolkata's GRSE split some frigate orders, for example), but for conventional submarines built under transfer of technology, Mazagon Dock has been the effective sole builder. A new dual-coast submarine effort with Hindustan Shipyard is being discussed — but that is years away and, if anything, underlines how rare and hard-won the capability is.
03

The company and who controls it

A 250-year-old dockyard, nationalised, listed, and now a Navratna.

Mazagon Dock is a government-controlled defence PSU with roots that stretch back to the 18th century. The dockyard was registered as a public company in 1934, nationalised in 1960 to become a public-sector shipbuilder, went public with a celebrated IPO in 2020, and was awarded coveted Navratna status in 2024 — a designation that gives it greater financial and operational autonomy. The Government of India owns ~81%; the rest trades on the market.

  • 1960

  • 2005

  • 2026
    India–Germany inter-governmental agreement signed for the P-75(I) submarine programme (Jan 2026); acquires a 51% stake in Colombo Dockyard; ends FY26 with a record ~₹13,000 Cr revenue and a ₹20,535 Cr order book.

Ownership & management

  • Owner: the President of India / Government of India holds ~81.2% through the Ministry of Defence. This is both a strength (the customer is also the owner — deep alignment, guaranteed relevance) and a constraint (the government sets strategy, appoints leadership, and can direct dividends and disinvestment).
  • Leadership: a serving/retired naval officer typically chairs the board — currently Capt. Jagmohan as Chairman & Managing Director (since 2025). Management is professional PSU leadership, not a founder-promoter — decisions run through government processes.
  • Balance sheet: the opposite of most manufacturers — near-zero net debt and a huge cash balance (~₹13,000 Cr of cash and deposits against a few hundred crore of borrowings). A stable, cash-generative, government-backed institution rather than a leveraged growth company.
Why government ownership matters both ways: being an arm of the state means Mazagon Dock is guaranteed a seat at the table for India's biggest naval programmes and can rely on advance funding — a durable advantage. But it also means the customer (the Navy/MoD) is also the owner and the price-setter: order timing, contract terms, dividend policy and any future stake sale are ultimately government decisions, not the company's. The stock's fortunes are tied to the political and budgetary priorities of the Indian state.
04

What Mazagon Dock actually builds

Two engines: surface warships and submarines — the same yard, two very different crafts.

The business splits into two reporting segments: shipbuilding (surface warships and vessels) and submarines & heavy engineering. Both flow from the same dockyard and the same core skill, but they are distinct programmes with distinct customers-within-the-customer and distinct timelines.

The build-to-delivery chain

Every platform moves through the same value chain — and each stage is a place where a defence yard earns its margin and where a delay can bite.

It also means revenue is recognised on progress, and a slip at the trials stage can push revenue (and profit) from one year into the next.

Surface warships — the shipbuilding segment

  • Project 15B — Visakhapatnam-class destroyers. Four ~7,500-tonne guided-missile destroyers, the class completed with INS Surat commissioned in January 2025. These are among the most powerful surface combatants ever built in India.
  • Project 17A — Nilgiri-class stealth frigates. Mazagon Dock builds four of the seven-ship class (Kolkata's GRSE builds the other three). Deliveries are flowing — INS Taragiri and INS Mahendragiri delivered in 2025–26 — and P17A is the single largest slice of the current order book (~₹8,257 Cr balance).
  • Coast Guard & other vessels, plus ONGC offshore work. The segment also builds Coast Guard ships and undertakes heavy-engineering/offshore platform work for the likes of ONGC — a useful, if lower-profile, revenue stream.

Submarines & heavy engineering — the crown jewel

  • Project 75 — Kalvari (Scorpene) class. Six conventional submarines built under transfer of technology from France's Naval Group, the last (INS Vagsheer) commissioned in early 2025. Residual work and the class remain in the order book (~₹1,722 Cr balance).
  • AIP retrofit & upgrades. An air-independent propulsion programme (~₹1,687 Cr balance) to let the Kalvari submarines stay submerged far longer — a meaningful capability upgrade and a live workstream.
  • The pipeline that dwarfs the current book — additional Scorpene submarines and, above all, the P-75(I) programme (covered in §05–06). This is where the multi-decade growth sits.
05

The indigenisation supercycle — why the multiple exploded

The structural tailwind behind every defence-PSU chart of the last few years.

For most of its life Mazagon Dock was valued as what it looked like: a slow, government-owned shipyard. What changed is not the yard — it's the national policy backdrop. India has launched a multi-trillion-rupee push to modernise its Navy and build its weapons at home ("Atmanirbhar Bharat" / Make-in-India), and Mazagon Dock is one of the clearest, most direct beneficiaries. This is the force that re-rated the entire defence-PSU basket.

Three forces pulling in the same direction

  • A naval build-out. India is a peninsula facing an increasingly contested Indian Ocean, watching China expand its fleet. It has committed to a large, long naval-modernisation programme — reported at the scale of ~₹2 lakh crore-plus — to add destroyers, frigates, corvettes and, critically, submarines over the coming decades.
  • A "make it in India" mandate. Policy now actively pushes the Navy to build indigenously rather than import, and to raise the indigenous content of each platform (e.g. the P-75(I) submarines are designed to rise from ~45% to ~60% indigenous content across the class). That channels spending straight to domestic yards.
  • A structural, multi-government horizon. Warship and submarine programmes are planned and funded over 10–20 years, spanning multiple governments and budgets. This isn't a one-year order cycle — it's a supercycle with visibility measured in decades.
Why a rupee of defence-shipbuilding revenue re-rated so hard

The market decided a rupee of Mazagon Dock's future revenue was worth far more than it used to be, for four connected reasons. (1) The runway got long and visible — indigenisation converts a vague "the Navy needs ships" into a concrete, multi-decade, policy-backed spending plan. (2) The competition is capped — only a handful of yards can bid, and for submarines essentially one, so the incumbent captures the growth rather than competing it away. (3) The customer is the sovereign — payment risk is minimal and demand doesn't disappear in a downturn. (4) It became a strategic priority, not a cost centre — governments want to fund indigenous defence, which is very different from squeezing a commodity supplier. Stack those together and you get a business the market suddenly wanted to own for the next decade — which is exactly how a ~10× P/E government shipyard became a ~40× growth stock.

The honest caveat: much of the re-rating is a bet on the future pipeline, not the current P&L. FY26 revenue grew a solid-but-not-explosive ~14% and profit only ~7%. The supercycle is real, but its biggest contracts (P-75(I), next-gen destroyers) are still awaiting signature — so the valuation is paying today for orders that must actually land, and then be delivered, over the years ahead.
06

The order book & pipeline — visibility, and the catch

A ₹20,535 Cr backlog is the base case; a ₹3 lakh crore pipeline is the bull case.

In a multi-year project business, the order book — signed work not yet delivered — is the clearest window into future revenue. Mazagon Dock ended FY26 with a book of ₹20,535 Cr (about 1.6× annual revenue). But the real story is the pipeline behind it: a set of enormous programmes, some with negotiations already complete, that could multiply the backlog several times over if they convert.

In plain terms — why the pipeline is a promise, not a cheque

Picture a contractor whose current signed work covers about a year and a half of revenue — comforting, but not thrilling on its own. The excitement is a whiteboard of much bigger jobs the client keeps talking about: a giant submarine contract where the terms are basically agreed but the client's finance department hasn't signed off, another submarine order where the contractor is the favourite but talks haven't started, and a huge destroyer programme that's "coming". If even one or two of these land, the backlog transforms. But defence procurement is famously slow: the P-75(I) submarine deal has been in the works for the better part of two decades, and its final sign-off still depends on "approval by competent authority". So the pipeline tells you the demand is real and vast — a genuine strength — but the timing of when it becomes a signed order, and then delivered revenue, is exactly the risk. At ~40× earnings, the market has priced in a good chunk of a pipeline that is not yet on the books.

07

The advance-funded, net-cash model — why a shipbuilder is swimming in cash

The financial quirk that makes this "manufacturer" behave like a fortress balance sheet.

The most counter-intuitive thing about Mazagon Dock is its balance sheet. Most heavy manufacturers are capital-hungry and carry debt. Mazagon Dock is the opposite: it sits on roughly ₹13,000 Cr of cash and deposits against only a few hundred crore of borrowings — a huge net-cash position for a company its size. The reason is the customer-advance model, and understanding it explains both the fortress balance sheet and a subtlety in the reported profit.

How the advances work

  • The Navy pays before the ship is finished. Defence contracts come with large upfront advances and milestone payments as construction hits agreed stages. So the cash arrives years before the platform is delivered.
  • The result is negative working capital. Because the yard is holding customer money against work-in-progress, it runs on the customer's cash rather than its own. These advances sit on the balance sheet as "contract liabilities" — money owed as future ships, not as debt.
  • The float earns interest. That cash pile is parked in deposits and earns substantial "other income" — a large, real, but non-operating chunk of the company's profit.
Why the cash pile is a double-edged sword

The good side is obvious: the balance sheet is a fortress, the company funds a heavy modernisation capex programme out of its own (and the Navy's) money, and it can pay a fat dividend. But there are two nuances worth grasping. First, a slice of reported profit is interest income, not shipbuilding. In FY26, operating profit was around ₹2,266 Cr, yet net profit was ₹2,578 Cr — the gap is largely the interest earned on that cash pile. That's real money, but it's lower-quality and won't grow like the core business; strip it out and the "operating" earnings multiple looks even richer. Second, the cash is partly the customer's. Those advances are liabilities that get "consumed" as ships are delivered — so if new orders (and their advances) don't keep replenishing the pot, the cash balance and the negative working capital can unwind, tightening liquidity and pressuring margins. Analysts flagged exactly this for FY27: the model needs fresh order inflow and advances to keep the flywheel spinning.

08

Exports, commercial shipbuilding & capacity expansion

The optionality beyond the Indian Navy — and the docks being built to capture it.

Mazagon Dock's core is the Indian Navy, but management is deliberately opening new fronts: defence and commercial ship exports, large commercial shipbuilding, and ship repair — backed by a multi-thousand-crore capacity expansion. These are early-stage today but represent the yard's growth optionality beyond its single domestic customer.

The expansion push

  • A ~₹5,000 Cr expansion plan to take on larger ships, including India's largest floating dry dock at Nhava Island (able to dock vessels up to ~12,800 tonnes and handle up to eight ships at once), plus a new wet basin with berthing for two frigates and two submarines. More dock space directly lifts how much the yard can build in parallel — a real constraint for a growing order book.
  • Colombo Dockyard acquisition. Mazagon Dock bought a 51% controlling stake in Sri Lanka's largest shipyard (closed by April 2026), adding overseas repair-and-build capacity and a foothold in the Indian Ocean region — a genuinely strategic move on the export/repair front.
  • Commercial & export ambitions. The company is exploring large commercial shipbuilding (potentially with South Korean or Japanese partners) and has signed a Master Ship Repair Agreement with the US Navy — opening the door to servicing foreign fleets and diversifying beyond the domestic order cycle.
Why exports & commercial matter — and why to be sober about them

The single biggest risk to Mazagon Dock (see §12) is that it has essentially one customer: the Indian Navy. Exports, commercial shipbuilding and ship repair are the antidote — extra demand that doesn't depend on India's defence budget cycle, plus a way to keep expensive docks and skilled workers busy between naval orders. The Colombo acquisition and the US Navy repair agreement show real intent. But be sober: these are early and small today relative to the naval core, and commercial shipbuilding is a brutally competitive, thin-margin global business dominated by Korea, China and Japan. The optionality is worth something, and the new docks are needed regardless to build the coming warships — but the investment case still rests overwhelmingly on the Indian Navy, not on exports.

09

The competitive set

Near-monopoly in submarines; a shared field in surface ships.

Mazagon Dock competes in a small, government-shaped field. In submarines it is effectively alone among Indian builders. In surface warships it shares the field with a handful of other defence PSU yards, and the government often deliberately splits orders to keep multiple yards alive and capable.

The competitive tension to watch

Two dynamics matter. In submarines, Mazagon Dock's position is close to unassailable — which is exactly why it commands the premium. In surface warships, the competition is friendlier than it looks: these are fellow government yards, and the state's goal is to keep all of them viable and busy, so it tends to share orders rather than let one win everything (P17A was split between Mazagon Dock and GRSE; a next-gen destroyer or corvette programme could be split too). That caps how much of any single programme Mazagon Dock captures. The real competitive question, then, isn't "will a rival undercut it?" — it's "how much of India's giant naval pipeline does the government route to this yard versus the others", and whether Mazagon Dock can execute fast enough to keep earning the lion's share of the highest-value work.

Financials

Strong growth, elite returns, a fortress balance sheet — with a margin wrinkle to watch.

Mazagon Dock's financial record is that of a high-quality, fast-growing PSU: revenue up from ₹5,733 Cr (FY22) to ₹13,006 Cr (FY26), profit up several-fold, ~36% return on capital, and a net-cash balance sheet. The one wrinkle in FY26 was margin compression — a reminder that this is a project business where the mix of work drives profitability quarter to quarter.

In plain terms — what the numbers say

Financially, Mazagon Dock is a rare thing: a heavy manufacturer that grew revenue ~26% a year for five years, earns ~36% on capital, and carries no net debt — because its customer funds the working capital. But FY26 shows the tension inside a project business. Revenue grew nicely, yet profit lagged because margins compressed (material costs jumped as the work mix changed) and the order book actually shrank (the yard delivered faster than it won new orders, since the giant submarine deals hadn't signed). Neither is alarming on its own — it's the natural rhythm of a business that just finished big destroyer/frigate programmes and is waiting on the next wave of submarine contracts. But it's exactly why the two numbers to watch are order inflow (does the pipeline convert?) and operating margin (does profitability hold as new, early-stage programmes start?). Those, not the cash pile, will decide the next few years.

11

The valuation debate — has the re-rating gone too far?

Defence PSUs went from cheap to expensive. This is the argument about whether that's justified.

Mazagon Dock trades at around 40× trailing earnings — a market cap near ₹1.03 lakh crore on ₹2,578 Cr of FY26 profit, at a share price around ₹2,555, and a price-to-book above 10×. A few years ago, a government shipyard like this fetched a low-teens multiple. The entire defence-PSU basket was re-rated on the indigenisation story, and the question now is whether the price reflects a genuine multi-decade franchise or "defence euphoria" that has run ahead of the numbers.

How to think about the ~40× multiple

A ~40× P/E on a business growing profit ~7% (as in FY26) only makes sense if you believe earnings are about to reaccelerate hard — and that belief rests entirely on the pipeline. The bull maths: if the ₹70,000 Cr+ P-75(I) submarine deal signs, the Scorpene add-on follows, and next-gen destroyers land, the order book could swell from ₹20,535 Cr toward the ₹1 lakh crore mark, giving a decade of high-visibility revenue and a much bigger earnings base — at which point the forward multiple looks far more reasonable. Bulls are paying a scarcity premium for the only near-monopoly submarine builder in a country structurally rearming. The bear case is the mirror image: at 40× on ~7% profit growth, with margins compressing and the order book actually shrinking in FY26, the price already assumes the pipeline converts smoothly and on time — and defence procurement is anything but smooth or timely. Add that a slice of the reported profit is interest income (not shipbuilding), that there's essentially one customer, and that the government can split orders or slow-walk approvals, and a richly-priced stock has real room to de-rate even if the yard keeps performing. Both cases are internally consistent; the tie-breaker is order inflow.

A genuine strategic-asset near-monopoly — priced as if the pipeline behind it is already signed, delivered and paid for.
The honest tension: the business quality here is real — an un-replicable submarine moat, a fortress balance sheet, a decade-long tailwind. But the valuation leaves little margin for the ordinary hazards of defence: an order that slips a year, a margin squeeze on a new programme's early stages, a government decision to route work to a rival yard. The two numbers to track each quarter are order inflow / book-to-bill (is the pipeline actually converting into signed contracts and advances?) and operating margin (is core profitability holding, stripped of interest income?).
12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A strategic national asset — priced as though its future is guaranteed.

Mazagon Dock is one of the most strategically important companies in India: the yard that builds the Navy's destroyers, frigates and — almost uniquely — its submarines, riding a multi-decade indigenisation wave with a fortress balance sheet. The investment debate is almost entirely about order timing and price, not business quality.

The bull case

  • Un-replicable moat — India's near-monopoly conventional-submarine builder and lead warship yard; a genuine strategic-asset franchise.
  • Structural tailwind — a ~₹2 lakh crore+ naval-modernisation and Make-in-India push channels spending straight to the yard.
  • A vast pipeline — ₹3 lakh crore+ addressable, led by P-75(I) (~₹70,000 Cr+, negotiations done), Scorpene add-on and next-gen destroyers.
  • Fortress balance sheet — ~₹13,000 Cr net cash from customer advances, ~36% ROCE, generous ₹18.12/share dividend.
  • Sovereign customer & owner — minimal payment risk, guaranteed relevance, capacity expanding (Nhava dock, Colombo Dockyard).

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~40× earnings and >10× book on ~7% FY26 profit growth leaves little room for error.
  • Pipeline is unsigned — the P-75(I) deal has crawled for years; the order book actually shrank in FY26 as deliveries outran new wins.
  • Execution & lumpiness — deliveries hinge on trials, inspections and government cycles; revenue can slip year to year.
  • Single-customer concentration — the Indian Navy/MoD is effectively the only customer, owner and price-setter.
  • Margin & profit-quality — FY26 margins compressed; a chunk of reported profit is interest income on the cash pile, not shipbuilding.
The bottom line

Mazagon Dock is a strategic-asset near-monopoly executing India's naval build-out from a fortress balance sheet — the yard the country must use to build its submarines, sitting in the middle of a decade-long, policy-backed spending wave. The business quality is not really in doubt. What's in doubt is timing and price: the stock already reflects a pipeline (P-75(I), Scorpene add-ons, next-gen destroyers) that is talked about, negotiated, favoured — but not yet signed, delivered or paid for. FY26 was the awkward in-between year: revenue grew, but profit lagged, margins compressed, and the order book drew down as old programmes finished before new ones began. The next few years will be written in two numbers — order inflow (does the giant submarine pipeline convert into signed contracts and advances?) and operating margin (does core profitability hold as new programmes ramp?). Convert the pipeline on schedule and the yard grows into its rich valuation; stumble on timing, and a ~40× stock built on future orders has a long way to fall.
