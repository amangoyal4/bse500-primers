RailTel, the fibre that runs beside the trains

A complete walk through the telecom arm of Indian Railways — how a company came to own one of the country's most unrepeatable optical-fibre corridors, why that cable was originally laid to move trains and not data, and how a business sitting on a rare national asset ended up earning most of its money installing cameras and office networks for government departments.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

RailTel Corporation of India is a government-owned telecom company that belongs to the Ministry of Railways. Its founding asset is a pan-India optical-fibre network laid alongside the railway tracks — cable strung down the same continuous corridor that carries trains through roughly 6,000 stations and virtually every commercial town in the country. On top of that fibre it runs three businesses: it leases bandwidth and connectivity to telecom operators, ISPs, banks, enterprises and government departments; it executes large IT and telecom projects for the government — station Wi-Fi, video surveillance, e-office rollouts, ministry data networks, rural connectivity; and it operates two Tier-III data centres (Secunderabad and Gurugram) with an empanelled government-cloud offering. It is headquartered in Gurugram, was granted Navratna status in 2024, and listed in February 2021.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The asset is rare; the revenue is not. Nobody can build a second continuous right-of-way through every Indian town — the railway already owns it, and RailTel owns the cable in it. Yet in the March 2026 quarter, that network produced ₹449 Cr of telecom revenue against ₹1,220 Cr from project work. Roughly three of every four rupees now come from executing government IT and telecom contracts that, in principle, any competent systems integrator could bid for.
  • Growth is real, but it is the low-margin kind. FY26 turnover rose 22% to ₹4,328 Cr and PAT rose ~16% to ₹346 Cr — profit growing slower than revenue, which is exactly the arithmetic signature of a mix shifting toward thin-margin project work. Q3 FY26 showed it plainly: revenue +19% year-on-year, but EBITDA margin down to ~14.6% from ~15.8%.
  • The backlog is the growth engine. The order book stood at ₹11,466 Cr as of 30 April 2026, up ~34% in a single quarter and equal to roughly 2.6 years of FY26 revenue. Management guides for ~20% growth in FY27 on ₹300 Cr of capex aimed largely at data centres. The company is effectively debt-free, earns north of 22% on capital employed, and paid ₹3.25 per share in FY26 dividends — yet the stock has fallen ~25% over the past year to about ₹305, or ~27× earnings.
In plain terms

Two very different companies share one balance sheet here. The first is a landlord: it owns cable in a corridor that cannot be duplicated, and it rents capacity on that cable for high margins with almost no incremental cost. The second is a contractor: it wins government tenders to install cameras, Wi-Fi hotspots, office IT and networks, buys the hardware, deploys it, and earns a modest spread on the work. Over the last few years the contractor has grown far faster than the landlord — which is why revenue keeps compounding while margins keep drifting down. The entire investment question is whether the landlord ever gets big enough to matter again.

01

Fibre 101: what a "telecom infrastructure" company actually owns

No engineering background needed — just the handful of ideas that make the business legible.

Every message, video call and UPI payment in India eventually travels as a pulse of light through a strand of glass. Optical fibre is that glass: hair-thin filaments, bundled by the dozen inside an armoured cable, carrying light over hundreds of kilometres with barely any loss. A mobile tower is the last hundred metres of a journey; the fibre is the other thousand kilometres. RailTel does not sell mobile phone connections. It sells the road underneath them.

The economics of fibre are unusual and worth understanding before anything else, because they explain both why RailTel's asset is valuable and why owning it is not automatically profitable.

The five terms that unlock the whole business
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Route km vs fibre kmA single cable trench running 100 km is 100 route km, but if that cable holds 24 fibres it is 2,400 fibre km. The expensive part is the trench and the permission to dig it; adding fibres inside is comparatively cheap. RailTel's advantage is measured in route km — the corridor — not in glass.
Right of way (RoW)The legal permission to lay cable across land you don't own. In India this is the single hardest part of building a network: thousands of landowners, municipalities, forest clearances and road-cutting fees. The railway already holds a continuous, government-owned RoW across the country — this is RailTel's founding advantage.
Dark fibreUnlit spare strands in a cable, leased to a customer who attaches their own equipment at each end. Almost pure margin for the owner — no operating effort, a long contract, and the customer buys their own electronics.
MPLS / VPNA managed private network built over shared infrastructure, so a bank's 3,000 branches or a ministry's offices behave as one closed network. Higher value than raw bandwidth because it bundles service and security; a mainstay of RailTel's enterprise and government business.
System integration (SI)Winning a tender to design, buy, install and commission a complete system — cameras, servers, screens, software, cabling — and hand it over working. Revenue is large but much of it is pass-through hardware, so the margin on each rupee is thin.
Why fibre is a landlord business, not a factory

Once a cable is in the ground, the cost of carrying the ten-thousandth gigabit is close to the cost of carrying the first. That means a fibre network is nearly all fixed cost up front and near-zero marginal cost afterwards — the classic infrastructure shape. Utilisation is everything: a network running at 20% of capacity and one running at 70% cost about the same to operate, but the second one is dramatically more profitable. This is why bandwidth revenue carries far richer margins than installation work, and why an idle strand of fibre is one of the most expensive things a telecom company can own.

Who buys wholesale connectivity in India

  • Telecom operators and ISPs — Jio, Airtel, Vodafone Idea, BSNL and hundreds of regional internet providers, who lease capacity or dark fibre to reach places their own network doesn't go, or to build redundant routes.
  • Banks, financial institutions and large enterprises — buyers of managed MPLS/VPN networks connecting branches, ATMs and offices, where reliability and a service-level guarantee matter more than the headline price.
  • Government — central ministries, state departments, defence, police and public-sector undertakings, who often prefer a government-owned carrier for sensitive traffic. This is RailTel's natural home turf and the reason its two halves are more connected than they look.
  • Content and cloud players — anyone needing long-haul capacity between cities and data centres, a segment growing with India's data consumption.
02

The right-of-way: why the railway owns India's easiest fibre corridor

The founding asset, and the reason it was built in the first place.

Indian Railways runs roughly 68,000 route km of track, and that track is not just steel — it is a continuous ribbon of government-owned land threading through nearly every town of consequence in the country. When you own a corridor like that, laying cable stops being a negotiation with ten thousand strangers and becomes an internal work order. RailTel was created in 2000 to put fibre in that corridor and then to sell what the corridor could carry.

The genuine surprise

The fibre was not laid for the internet. It was laid to run trains. Modern railway signalling, train control, station announcements and the safety systems that keep two trains off the same block of track all need fast, absolutely reliable communication between stations, cabins and control centres — and copper telegraph lines were never going to do it. Indian Railways needed a nationwide, low-latency, redundant data network as an operational safety requirement. RailTel exists because someone realised that the spare capacity on that safety network — glass strands lit for train control, with room to spare — was a saleable national asset. Practically every commercial rupee RailTel earns from bandwidth is a by-product of keeping trains from colliding.

That origin story shapes the business in ways worth pinning down. The network's shape follows the trains, which is both a strength and a limitation. It is unrivalled at connecting town to town along a rail line, and it reaches semi-urban and rural locations that a commercially built network would never have justified — you get the small district town for free because the track already goes there. What it does not automatically give you is the last mile: the fibre passes through a town, but running it from the station to a customer's office park or a home three kilometres away is a separate, expensive, city-by-city problem. RailTel is superb at the long haul and structurally weaker at the last hundred metres — precisely the opposite of a consumer telco.

Step 3 is the underrated one. A railway station is not just a place the cable passes — it is secured land with power and staff, which makes it a ready-made network node in towns where acquiring commercial real estate would be slow and costly.

There is a second, quieter benefit. Because RailTel is a railway company, it is the natural in-house executor for the railway's own digital projects. Station Wi-Fi, video surveillance across platforms, the e-office system that moved railway paperwork online, hospital management systems, ticketing and control-room networks — these are handed to RailTel not through open competition but because it is the ministry's telecom arm. That captive relationship is a moat and a leash at once: it guarantees a base of work, and it ties a large share of the company's fortunes to one customer's annual budget.

A distinction to hold onto: the railway right-of-way is an advantage in building a network, not a monopoly on selling connectivity. Jio and Airtel have spent the last decade laying far larger fibre footprints along highways and into cities, and Power Grid runs its own optical network along transmission towers using exactly the same corridor logic. RailTel's corridor is genuinely rare; it is not exclusive, and it is not the biggest.
03

The three businesses: bandwidth, projects, data centres

Read this once and the income statement stops being confusing.

RailTel reports along two headline segments — Telecom Services and Projects / Project Work Services — with the data-centre and cloud business nested inside the telecom side and disclosed separately in commentary. They have almost nothing in common except the customer list. One is annuity infrastructure, one is contracting, one is a capital-hungry new bet.

1 · Telecom & bandwidth — the annuity

This is the landlord business. RailTel leases bandwidth and dark fibre to operators and ISPs on multi-year contracts, builds managed MPLS/VPN networks for banks, enterprises and government departments, provides national long-distance and internet services under its own licences, and sells tower and infrastructure co-location at its station nodes. Contracts run for years, revenue repeats without re-bidding, and the incremental cost of serving one more customer over installed fibre is small. In the March 2026 quarter this segment produced ₹449 Cr — a strong quarter, up roughly 24% sequentially, but still the smaller half of the company.

2 · Projects & system integration — the growth engine

This is where the money now comes from. RailTel bids for and executes large turnkey IT and telecom projects for the public sector. The catalogue is broader than most people assume: video surveillance systems (CCTV and, increasingly, AI-analytics camera networks across railway stations), public Wi-Fi at stations, e-office deployments that digitise a department's file movement, data networks and command centres for ministries and state governments, rural connectivity work of the BharatNet type, hospital and campus IT, and telecom infrastructure for railway modernisation programmes. In Q4 FY26 this segment delivered ₹1,220 Cr — nearly three times the telecom line in the same quarter.

3 · Data centres & cloud — the option

RailTel operates Tier-III data centres at Secunderabad and Gurugram, offering colocation, managed hosting, disaster recovery, a MeitY-empanelled government community cloud and a Security Operations Centre service. Revenue here reached ₹202 Cr in FY26, up from ₹127 Cr — roughly 59% growth off a small base, and the fastest-growing thing in the company. The bulk of the announced ₹300 Cr FY27 capex is pointed at this business.

Why the same customer buys both — and why that's a trap

A railway zone that wants surveillance cameras on 200 platforms also needs a network to carry the video and somewhere to store it. RailTel can supply all three, and that bundling is a real commercial advantage — the department gets one accountable vendor with security clearance already in place. The trap is in how the revenue is recognised. The cameras, servers and switches are bought at market price and passed through at a modest markup, so a ₹500 Cr surveillance contract might add ₹500 Cr of revenue and a fraction of the margin the same money would earn if it were bandwidth. Every large project win therefore grows the company and shrinks the margin percentage at the same time. Reported growth and reported profitability are pulling in opposite directions by design.

04

The mismatch: a rare asset, an ordinary income statement

The single most important idea in this primer — and one that applies to almost no other listed company.

Most infrastructure companies are valued on the asset they own. Most contractors are valued on the work they win. RailTel is the unusual case where the two live in the same entity and point in opposite directions — and where the asset is the minority shareholder in its own income statement.

You cannot buy a second continuous corridor through every town in India at any price. In the March 2026 quarter, that corridor earned ₹449 Cr. Installing other people's equipment earned ₹1,220 Cr.

Put the two halves side by side and the strangeness is obvious. The telecom half rests on an asset with a genuine, structural, un-buyable advantage: decades of laid fibre along a right-of-way that no private competitor can replicate, feeding ~6,000 station nodes. If a private company wanted to duplicate it, the binding constraint would not be money — it would be that the land is not for sale. The project half rests on winning competitive government tenders to install hardware, a business with no structural barrier whatsoever: the qualification bar is technical and financial, not proprietary, and dozens of Indian firms clear it.

Now look at which half is growing. Total revenue compounded 22% in FY26. Profit grew ~16%. The gap between those two numbers is the mismatch expressed in arithmetic: the company added a great deal of revenue at a margin below its own average, so the blended margin fell even as the business got bigger. Q3 FY26 is the clean illustration — revenue up 19% year-on-year to ₹913.5 Cr, EBITDA up only ~10% to ₹133 Cr, and margin down from ~15.8% to ~14.6%. Growth that dilutes is still growth, but it is not the growth a fibre landlord is supposed to deliver.

The mismatch ratio — one number, tracked quarterly

The most useful thing an investor can do with RailTel is compute a single ratio each quarter: telecom revenue divided by project revenue. It requires no model and no assumptions, and it captures the entire thesis in one figure.

The mismatch, stated as plainly as possible

Imagine a company that owns a toll road nobody else is permitted to build — and that also runs a crew painting road markings on other people's highways for a fee. Both are perfectly legitimate businesses. But if the painting crew grows to three times the size of the toll road, the accounts stop looking like a toll road's. Revenue rises, margins fall, working capital balloons, and the market has to decide whether it is buying the road or the crew. RailTel's reported numbers are currently the crew's numbers. The road is still there, still un-buildable, still under-used — which is either the opportunity or the indictment, depending on whether management can fill it.

Two escape routes exist from the mismatch, and they are the only two worth watching. The first is selling more of the network — more dark fibre, more enterprise VPN, more long-haul capacity to operators and cloud players, more co-location at station nodes. This is pure margin because the cable is already in the ground. The second is converting the network into a higher-value product, which is precisely what the data-centre push is: instead of renting a strand of glass for a few paise, rent a rack of powered, cooled, connected space with a government-cloud certification attached to it. Both routes lift the mismatch ratio. Winning another ₹2,000 Cr of camera-installation work does not.

Why this cannot be said of the other railway PSUs: Ircon and RITES are contractors by design — nobody expects them to own an annuity asset, so their margin profile is judged on execution alone. RailTel is judged differently because it does own one. A contractor earning 5% is a normal contractor. An infrastructure owner earning contractor margins is a monetisation failure, and that is the specific charge the market is levelling here.
05

Inside the order book: how a government tender becomes revenue

₹11,466 Cr sounds enormous. Here is what it actually contains, and how fast it converts.

As of 30 April 2026 RailTel's order book stood at ₹11,466 Cr, having jumped roughly 34% during the March quarter alone. Against FY26 revenue of ₹4,328 Cr, that is about 2.6 years of work already booked — genuine visibility, and the reason management can guide confidently to ~20% growth in FY27. But an order book is a promise, not a bank balance, and the promises inside RailTel's are of several different kinds.

The conversion mechanics — and why Q4 always bulges

Government project revenue is recognised against milestones: supply, installation, commissioning, acceptance. Departments spend against annual budgets that expire on 31 March, which produces one of the most reliable patterns in Indian public-sector contracting — a fourth quarter far larger than the three that precede it. RailTel's FY26 shows it starkly: Q3 operating revenue of ₹913.5 Cr followed by Q4 at ₹1,668.86 Cr, an 83% sequential jump. That is not a demand surge; it is a fiscal-year deadline.

The working-capital cost of a government customer

The order book has a shadow on the balance sheet. To execute a surveillance or network project, RailTel buys the hardware first, deploys it, and then waits for a government department to inspect, accept and release payment — a sequence that routinely takes several quarters and sometimes longer. The company therefore carries substantial receivables and unbilled revenue, and the faster the project segment grows, the more cash gets tied up in it. This is why a business that looks asset-light on the project side is not actually capital-light, and why RailTel's cash conversion is worth checking every year even though the company carries essentially no debt. The debt-free balance sheet is what makes the model survivable; without it, funding a ₹11,466 Cr backlog on government payment terms would be punishing.

One further nuance separates good backlog from filler. Contracts that end in a long-term annual maintenance or managed-service tail — run the surveillance network, operate the department's data network, host the application — are worth materially more than pure supply-and-install work, because they convert a one-off into a recurring stream that quietly migrates revenue from the project column toward the telecom column. Every rupee of backlog with a service tail attached is a rupee working on the right side of the mismatch ratio.

06

RailWire and station Wi-Fi: the consumer business nobody notices

A government carrier quietly running home broadband in small-town India.

Most people who have heard of RailTel have heard of it once, without knowing it: the free Wi-Fi that appears on a phone at an Indian railway station. What almost nobody knows is that the same company also runs a retail home-broadband service — RailWire — which has for years ranked among India's larger wired-broadband providers by subscriber count and has an unusually strong position in rural India.

The model is deliberately capital-light and worth understanding because it is a template for how the fibre corridor gets monetised without RailTel building a last mile itself. RailTel provides the backbone bandwidth and the brand; a network of local franchise partners — small operators who already know the streets, the buildings and the customers in a town — provide the last-mile cable, installation and support. Revenue is shared. RailTel gets subscriber reach into thousands of towns without laying a metre of local cable or hiring a local engineer; the partner gets national backhaul, billing and a credible brand instead of a patchy one-man ISP.

Why the station Wi-Fi footprint matters more than the revenue it earns

Free public Wi-Fi at thousands of stations is not, in itself, a good business — monetising travellers who connect for twenty minutes is hard, and the project has always been more national-mission than profit centre. Its value is different: it forced RailTel to build live, powered, managed network equipment inside thousands of stations across the country. That physical presence — an operating node, with power and access, in a small town where no commercial operator has invested — is the platform on which everything else can be sold: franchise broadband, enterprise last-mile, edge computing, surveillance backhaul, co-location. The Wi-Fi was the excuse; the distributed node footprint is the asset it left behind.

The honest caveat is that retail broadband is a market RailTel does not control. Jio and Airtel have poured capital into fibre-to-the-home with aggressive bundled pricing, and a franchise-based PSU offering is not built to win a price war on service quality or marketing spend. RailWire is best understood as a way of harvesting incremental value from bandwidth that already exists, in places the giants deprioritise — not as a challenge to them. Judge it on incremental margin per subscriber, not on subscriber-share headlines.

07

Data centres and the government-cloud bet

The highest-value possible use of the network — and the most capital-hungry.

If the mismatch is the problem, the data-centre business is management's clearest attempt at an answer. RailTel runs Tier-III facilities at Secunderabad and Gurugram offering colocation, managed hosting, disaster recovery, a MeitY-empanelled community cloud for government workloads, and a Security Operations Centre. Revenue reached ₹202 Cr in FY26 against ₹127 Cr the year before — the fastest growth rate anywhere in the company, and the destination for most of the ₹300 Cr FY27 capex.

Why this is the right shape of bet

  • It uses the asset instead of bypassing it. A data centre's value depends heavily on the connectivity around it. RailTel can offer a customer a rack and the national fibre reaching it, from one supplier, with route diversity along the rail corridor. That is a genuinely integrated proposition rather than a bolt-on.
  • Empanelment is a real barrier. Government departments can only place workloads with cloud providers empanelled by MeitY, under specific security and data-localisation conditions. Being a government-owned, empanelled provider with existing security clearances is a qualification advantage that private hyperscalers cannot simply price their way past for sensitive workloads.
  • The margin profile is right. Once racks are filled, a data centre behaves like the fibre business — high fixed cost, low marginal cost, contracted multi-year revenue. Every rupee that migrates from installation work to hosted services pushes the mismatch ratio the right way.
  • Demand is structurally rising. Data localisation rules, government digitisation, video surveillance archives (RailTel's own projects generate them), and AI-driven compute demand all point one direction.
And why it is the riskiest thing the company is doing: data centres are a capital arms race. AdaniConneX, Airtel's Nxtra, CtrlS, Sify and the global hyperscalers are committing multi-thousand-crore, gigawatt-scale programmes with cheaper capital, deeper technical benches and faster build cycles. RailTel's entire announced FY27 capex of ₹300 Cr is a rounding error next to those budgets. Scale matters enormously in this business — power procurement, cooling efficiency and utilisation all improve with size. RailTel is not going to out-build anyone; it can only win the specific slice of demand where government ownership, security clearance and network integration outrank scale and price. That is a defensible niche, but it is a niche.
The number that will tell you whether this is working

Watch data-centre revenue as a share of the telecom segment, and watch whether it keeps compounding at anything like the ~59% it managed in FY26. At ₹202 Cr it is under 5% of company turnover — too small to change the margin profile no matter how fast it grows in percentage terms. For this to become the answer to the mismatch rather than a pleasant sideline, it needs to reach a scale of several hundred crores of recurring revenue with the racks actually full. Announced capacity is not revenue; a data centre earns nothing until it is occupied, and utilisation is the metric management should be asked about at every call.

08

The company, the government, and who controls it

A Navratna PSU whose majority shareholder is also its biggest customer.

RailTel is a central public-sector enterprise under the Ministry of Railways, incorporated in September 2000 with the specific mandate of building a nationwide broadband and multimedia network along the railway right-of-way and modernising railway train-control and safety communications. The President of India, acting through the Ministry of Railways, holds the controlling stake — about 72.8% as of June 2026, with the balance in public hands since the February 2021 IPO.

  • 2000s

  • 2013–2016

  • 2017–2020

What government ownership actually changes

  • The customer and the owner are the same institution. The Ministry of Railways controls the company and is also, directly and through its zones and PSUs, a very large buyer of its services. This guarantees a base of work and access no private firm can obtain — and it means revenue visibility depends on the railway's annual budget and the government's digitisation spending, not on winning share in an open market.
  • Navratna status is a real operational upgrade. It raises the board's autonomy to invest, form joint ventures and commit capital without seeking approval for each decision — which matters for a company that now needs to move quickly on data-centre capex and technology partnerships.
  • Public-sector constraints are equally real. Tendering rules, pay structures, procurement timelines and the difficulty of hiring and retaining specialist cloud and cyber-security talent against private-sector pay are structural. So is the pull toward accepting national-mission work whose commercial return is secondary to its policy purpose.
  • Dividend discipline is high. As with most CPSEs, a substantial share of profits is returned — ₹2 interim plus ₹1.25 final per share for FY26 — which is attractive for income, and simultaneously a constraint on how much can be retained to fund a data-centre build-out.
  • Divestment overhang. With ~72.8% government holding, any future stake sale or offer-for-sale is a supply event the market prices in advance. It cuts both ways: it caps re-rating enthusiasm, but a larger float would also improve liquidity and index eligibility.
The governance point worth understanding: RailTel's most valuable asset — the right to use the railway right-of-way — is not something it purchased on arm's-length terms from an independent party. It flows from its parent ministry. That is what makes the moat un-buyable by competitors, and it is also what makes it, in the deepest sense, not fully the minority shareholders' to control. The corridor advantage exists because of the ownership structure; it cannot be separated from it.
09

Who it competes with: not quite a telco, not quite an IT firm

RailTel fights two different wars with two different sets of enemies, and it is the largest player in neither.

A useful way to size up RailTel is to notice that it has no true peer. It is not comparable to a telecom operator, because it has no consumer mobile business and a fraction of their network scale. It is not comparable to an IT services company, because it owns physical infrastructure and earns nothing from software exports. It is not comparable to Ircon or RITES, the other listed railway PSUs, because those are engineering and consultancy contractors with no annuity network. Its competitive set has to be assembled war by war.

The uncomfortable read-across

In the half of the business where RailTel has a durable advantage — the fibre corridor — it is competing against firms with vastly larger networks, so the advantage manifests as a niche rather than a dominance. In the half where it has scale and momentum — government projects — it has no advantage at all beyond relationships and clearances, so it must bid on price. Being the fifth-largest player in a business where you have an unrepeatable asset, and the mid-table player in a business where you have none, is an awkward place to stand. It is also exactly why the mix question dominates everything else about this company.

There is one place the two wars reinforce each other, and it deserves credit. When RailTel wins a departmental project, it frequently ends up operating the network it just installed — which converts an integrator relationship into a recurring telecom customer. Similarly, surveillance projects generate video that has to be transported and stored, and RailTel owns both the transport and the storage. Nobody else bidding for a railway or ministry contract can bundle national fibre, empanelled cloud and security clearance in one entity. The bundle is genuine; the question is whether management prices it as a bundle or keeps selling the pieces at tender rates.

10

Financials & valuation

A debt-free, cash-generative, dividend-paying PSU — trading at a multiple that assumes the mix improves.

RailTel's financial profile is unusually clean for a company growing this fast. FY26 turnover of ₹4,328 Cr was up 22%; profit after tax of ₹346 Cr was up about 16%. Return on capital employed is reported above 22%, the company is effectively debt-free with a net-cash position, and it distributed ₹3.25 per share in FY26 dividends. Nothing about the balance sheet is fragile. The debate is entirely about the quality and durability of the earnings it produces.

What ~27× is actually pricing

Twenty-seven times earnings is not extravagant for a debt-free business compounding revenue at 22% with 2.6 years of visible backlog and a dividend. It is, however, roughly double what a pure government-contracting business would ordinarily command — and that premium is the market paying for the fibre network, the data-centre optionality and the digital-India narrative rather than for the project segment doing most of the work. The 25% drawdown over the past year is the market beginning to question exactly that: if a company earns like an integrator, the argument goes, it should be valued like one.

The one metric that settles this thesis

It is not revenue growth, and it is not the order book — both can rise while the investment case deteriorates. It is the telecom segment's share of quarterly turnover, read alongside blended EBITDA margin. In the March 2026 quarter, telecom was ₹449 Cr of ₹1,669 Cr — about 27%, with blended margins around the mid-teens. If that share works its way toward 40% or better while EBITDA margin holds above ~15%, RailTel is monetising an asset nobody else can build, and 27× is cheap. If turnover doubles while telecom stays stuck in the mid-twenties as a percentage and margin drifts toward 12%, then this is a government IT contractor with an under-used fibre network on its books, and the multiple has further to compress regardless of how large the order book gets. Every quarterly result publishes both numbers. Nothing else needs modelling.

Two accounting nuances belong in any valuation. First, the reported ROCE above 22% is partly an artefact of age — much of the fibre backbone was laid years ago and is heavily depreciated, so the capital employed against which returns are measured understates what an equivalent network would cost to build today. It is a real return on the money still in the books; it is not a guide to the return on a new data-centre rupee. Second, working capital is the swing factor in cash flow. With receivables and unbilled revenue rising alongside a fast-growing project segment on government payment terms, reported profit and cash generated can diverge for several quarters at a stretch. Check operating cash flow against PAT in the annual report before treating the earnings as fully banked.

11

Future, risks, and the bull/bear case

What has to happen, what can break, and where the argument actually sits.

The next few years for RailTel are legible. The government is spending on digitisation, surveillance, rural connectivity and railway modernisation; the company has ₹11,466 Cr of that spending already contracted; management guides to ~20% growth in FY27 on ₹300 Cr of capex directed mainly at data centres. Growth is close to a given. What is not given is whether the growth arrives at a margin worth owning.

The tailwinds that are real

  • Railway modernisation is a multi-year capital cycle. Kavach train-collision-avoidance deployment, station redevelopment, platform surveillance and control-centre upgrades all carry a telecom and IT component, and RailTel is the ministry's in-house executor for much of it.
  • Government digitisation keeps widening. e-office, departmental networks, command-and-control centres and public safety systems are being commissioned across central and state governments — RailTel's core project market.
  • Data localisation and government-cloud rules favour an empanelled state-owned provider for sensitive workloads, a niche hyperscalers cannot simply out-price.
  • Data demand is compounding — video surveillance archives, AI workloads and rising consumption all lift demand for long-haul capacity and hosting, the two things RailTel's asset can supply.
  • The balance sheet permits the bet. Debt-free, cash-generative and dividend-paying, RailTel can fund data-centre expansion without leverage or dilution — an advantage most sub-scale challengers do not have.

The risks, set out squarely

  • The margin-mix problem is structural, not cyclical. Project and system-integration work is competitively bid, hardware-heavy and thin. As it grows as a share of revenue, blended margin compresses mechanically — visible already in Q3 FY26's slide from ~15.8% to ~14.6%. This is the core issue and it does not resolve itself with scale.
  • Government-customer dependence cuts several ways. Revenue rides railway and ministry budgets, tender timing is political, execution waits on departmental clearances, and payment terms tie up working capital. A budget reprioritisation is a revenue event RailTel cannot hedge.
  • Competition in both halves, from bigger players. On bandwidth: Jio, Airtel, Tata Communications and Power Grid's telecom arm, all with larger networks. On projects: TCS, Tech Mahindra, L&T, HFCL, ITI and a long tail of integrators. RailTel is not dominant in either arena.
  • Data-centre capex and technology risk. Competing against AdaniConneX, Nxtra, CtrlS and the hyperscalers on ₹300 Cr a year means racks must be filled fast and utilisation must hold; an under-occupied facility is a fixed-cost drag, and equipment generations turn over quickly.
  • PSU operating constraints. Procurement rules, pay bands that make cloud and cyber-security hiring hard, and national-mission work whose commercial return is secondary. Add the divestment overhang on a ~72.8% government stake.
  • Valuation carries an expectation. At ~27× the price embeds an eventual mix improvement. Nothing in the FY26 numbers proves that improvement has started — Q4's strong telecom quarter is one data point, not a trend.

The case for

  • An asset that cannot be rebuilt. A continuous fibre corridor through virtually every Indian town, with ~6,000 station nodes attached. Money cannot buy a second one because the land is not for sale.
  • Visibility most mid-caps lack. ₹11,466 Cr of backlog — about 2.6 years of revenue — plus captive railway work that is not competitively tendered.
  • Financial quality. Debt-free, ROCE above 22%, real dividends, and capex funded from internal accruals.
  • Data centres are compounding fast (₹127 Cr → ₹202 Cr) in exactly the segment that would fix the margin mix, with MeitY empanelment as a qualification barrier.
  • Q4 FY26 showed the telecom line can accelerate — ₹449 Cr, up ~24% sequentially — which is the first thing that would need to happen if the thesis is going to work.

The case against

  • Three of four rupees come from work with no moat. Government installation contracts are won on price and can be lost to any qualified integrator.
  • Profit is growing slower than revenue (+16% vs +22% in FY26) — the mix is diluting margin in real time, not improving.
  • Working capital absorbs the growth. Government receivables and unbilled revenue rise with every project won; cash lags profit.
  • Sub-scale in every market it plays in — a fraction of Jio's or Airtel's fibre, a fraction of TCS's or L&T's integration capability, a fraction of AdaniConneX's data-centre capital.
  • ~27× for an integrator's margin structure, on a stock already down ~25% in a year, with a large government stake overhanging any re-rating.
The bottom line

RailTel is the rare listed company whose best asset is not the one paying most of its bills. Its fibre runs beside the tracks because the railway needed to talk to its own trains, and the commercial network was the leftover — a corridor through every town in India that no competitor can now assemble. Twenty-six years on, that corridor supplies barely a quarter of quarterly turnover while camera installations, e-office rollouts and departmental networks supply the rest. The company is not in trouble: it is debt-free, growing 22%, earning over 22% on capital and carrying 2.6 years of orders. It is simply being paid, at ~27× earnings, as though the corridor were doing the work. The specific milestone that changes the argument is the quarter in which telecom revenue crosses ₹600 Cr with blended EBITDA margin still above 15% — the first hard evidence that the racks in Secunderabad and Gurugram, and the strands beside the track, are finally earning more than the crews installing hardware. Until that print arrives, every ₹1,000 Cr added to the order book makes RailTel larger without making it better.
