00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Eternal Limited is the holding company you used to know as Zomato — it renamed itself "Eternal" in early 2025 to signal that it had grown from a single food-delivery app into a four-business consumer-internet group. The Zomato app you order dinner from still exists; "Eternal" is just the listed parent that now owns four things: (1) Blinkit — 10-minute grocery delivery, the new growth engine; (2) Zomato — the food-delivery business; (3) District — going-out (dining reservations, movies, events); and (4) Hyperpure — B2B supplies to restaurants. In FY26 (year to March 2026) the group did ₹54,364 Cr of revenue and posted a slim ₹366 Cr net profit.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is now "more Blinkit than Zomato." The business that made the name is no longer the growth story. Blinkit's order value grew ~95% year-on-year in the March quarter and now exceeds food delivery; quick-commerce — not restaurant delivery — is what investors are paying for.
  • Profit is real but tiny relative to the price. Food delivery is comfortably profitable (a ~5.5% margin on order value). But Blinkit, District and the cost of land-grabbing in quick-commerce keep group profit thin — ₹366 Cr of net profit on a company worth ~₹2.5 lakh crore. You are buying the future, not today's earnings.
  • There is no promoter. Unusually for an Indian giant, Eternal has no controlling family or parent — it is a professionally managed, widely held company. Founder Deepinder Goyal owns under 4% and stepped down as Group CEO in early 2026; the largest single holder, Info Edge, owns ~12%.
In plain terms

Think of Eternal as a set of digital marketplaces that sit between you and something you want delivered fast — a restaurant meal (Zomato), a bag of groceries in ten minutes (Blinkit), a movie ticket or dinner reservation (District), or raw ingredients for a restaurant kitchen (Hyperpure). The company doesn't usually make the food or the groceries; it owns the app, the logistics network, the rider fleet and — increasingly for Blinkit — the warehouses and the stock inside them. It earns a slice (a "take rate") of everything that flows through. The bet is simple to state and hard to win: get tens of millions of Indians to order everyday things on your apps, build the delivery muscle nobody can copy cheaply, and turn that habit into a permanent toll on urban consumption. Food delivery has proven the model works and makes money. Quick commerce is racing to prove the same — while three rivals burn cash to stop it.

01

Platform economics 101: GOV, take rate, and why "contribution margin" rules everything

No jargon left undefined — five ideas that decode every line of Eternal's results.

Eternal reports its businesses in a vocabulary that confuses newcomers: GOV, NOV, take rate, adjusted EBITDA, contribution margin. Learn these five terms and the rest of this primer — and every quarterly result — becomes readable. They matter because a delivery platform's revenue is not the value of what's ordered; it's only the cut the platform keeps, and whether that cut covers the cost of delivering each order is the whole game.

The five terms that decode a delivery platform
TermWhat it means (in plain English)
GOV / NOVGross / Net Order Value — the total rupee value of everything customers order through the platform (food, groceries, tickets). This is not Eternal's revenue. It's the size of the "pipe." NOV is GOV minus customer discounts. When you hear "Blinkit did ₹14,386 Cr," that's the order value flowing through, not money Eternal keeps.
Take rateThe slice the platform keeps out of GOV — commissions from restaurants/sellers, delivery fees, platform fees, ad revenue. Zomato's effective take rate is ~20–32% of food NOV (commission + fees + ads); a quick-commerce marketplace historically took ~18%. The take rate is the platform's revenue per rupee ordered.
Contribution marginWhat's left from the take rate after the direct cost of fulfilling that one order — paying the rider, the dark-store running cost, packaging. If contribution is positive, every extra order helps; if negative, growth burns cash. This is the single most important number in quick commerce.
Adjusted EBITDAOperating profit after contribution costs and the segment's fixed overheads (tech, marketing, head office), but before depreciation, interest, tax and share-based pay. Eternal's go-to profitability gauge. Often shown as a % of GOV/NOV so you can compare across sizes.
AOVAverage Order Value — the typical basket size. Blinkit's AOV is ~₹525; food delivery's is higher. AOV matters because fixed per-order costs (the rider trip) are easier to cover on a big basket than a small one — so a platform always wants AOV to rise.
The simple profit equation

For one Blinkit order of ₹525: the platform keeps a take rate (say it earns ~₹50–60 of revenue from margin on goods, delivery fee, and ads). From that it must pay the rider (~₹25–35), the dark-store share of rent, electricity and staff per order, and packaging. What survives is contribution. Then the business as a whole must cover fixed costs — the app, central marketing, head office — to reach adjusted EBITDA. The art of quick commerce is pushing contribution per order up (bigger baskets, ads, private labels, denser routes) faster than you add new, loss-making stores. Get there and a colossal order volume drops a thin-but-real margin to the bottom line; fail and you burn cash forever. That tension — scale now, profit later — is the heartbeat of every Eternal quarter.

Why these businesses are so hard — and so valuable if they work

  • Two-sided networks. A delivery app needs both sellers (restaurants, brands) and buyers, plus a rider fleet in the middle. Each side makes the others more valuable, but you must subsidise all three early — which is why these businesses lose money for years before they don't.
  • Thin margins, vast volumes. Like an FMCG company, the per-order profit is tiny; the prize is doing it hundreds of millions of times a month. Blinkit alone did ~274 million orders a month by Q4 FY26.
  • Local density wins. The economics improve dramatically when a neighbourhood has enough orders to keep riders and dark stores busy. That's why incumbents fight so hard for the same dense urban pockets — it's a land grab for density, not geography.

One holding company, four businesses

What "Eternal" actually contains — and why each piece has totally different economics.

Eternal is a house of four operating businesses, each at a different stage of maturity. Food delivery (Zomato) is the mature, profitable original. Blinkit (quick commerce) is the high-growth, near-breakeven future. District (going-out) is an early, loss-making bet. Hyperpure (B2B supplies) is a quietly profitable supply-chain arm. Understanding that they are different businesses with different margins is the key to reading the group.

Why bundle four different businesses?

Because they share the hardest, most expensive thing to build: a delivery network and a relationship with the customer's home screen. The same rider fleet, the same logistics know-how, the same payments and the same app users can be pointed at food, groceries, tickets or restaurant supplies. Blinkit and Zomato even share customers and, increasingly, infrastructure. So Eternal's logic is: own the demand and the delivery muscle once, then sell more and more kinds of "deliver-this-now" through it. The risk is the mirror image — four businesses means four fronts to defend, three of which still need feeding with cash, and a management team stretched across very different operating models.

03

The company and who controls it

A founder-built start-up that became a promoter-less, professionally run public company.

Eternal began life in 2008 as Foodiebay, a Delhi restaurant-menu listings site built by Deepinder Goyal and Pankaj Chaddah; it was renamed Zomato in 2010, pivoted into food delivery in 2015, listed on the exchanges in a landmark 2021 IPO, bought Blinkit in 2022, and renamed the listed parent Eternal Limited in early 2025. Unusually, it has no promoter — no founding family or parent company in control. It is widely held by institutions and the public, and run by professional managers.

Ownership & structure

  • No promoter. Eternal is one of the few large Indian listed companies with no controlling promoter — no family, no parent, no pledged promoter holding. This is a deliberate "professionally managed" structure, more common in US tech than corporate India.
  • Largest holder — Info Edge (~12%). The Naukri.com parent was Zomato's earliest backer and remains the single biggest shareholder, but well short of control.
  • Founder Deepinder Goyal (~3.8%). Goyal owns under 4% and, after stepping down as Group CEO in February 2026, remains on the board as Vice-Chairman. His low stake is itself a quirk — he built the company but does not control it through equity.
  • Institutions dominate the float. Foreign institutions (FIIs) hold roughly 42–44%, domestic institutions (mutual funds, insurers) ~26%, and retail the rest. Eternal is a heavyweight in India's main indices and a core holding for many growth funds.
  • Leadership. Group CEO Albinder Dhindsa (who scaled Blinkit) now runs the group; Aditya Mangla leads the food-delivery business. A professional bench, not a founder fiefdom.
Why "no promoter" matters

In most large Indian companies, a promoter family or parent calls the shots and minority shareholders watch related-party risk. Eternal flips that: power sits with the board and professional management, accountable to a diffuse base of institutional owners. The upside is alignment with all shareholders and a Silicon-Valley-style governance model. The flip side is that there's no deep-pocketed promoter to lean on in a crisis, and big strategic shifts (renaming, the CEO change, the Blinkit restructuring) ride on management judgment and board oversight rather than a controlling owner's conviction.

Blinkit: the 10-minute machine, and the unit economics that decide everything

The value driver. How "dark stores" work, what it costs to deliver one order, and the land grab for density. The single most important section here.

Quick commerce — "q-comm" — is the business of delivering groceries and everyday essentials to your door in about ten minutes. Blinkit does it not from supermarkets but from "dark stores": small (~2,000–4,000 sq ft) neighbourhood warehouses, closed to the public, stocked with a few thousand fast-moving items, each serving a tight radius so a rider can reach you in minutes. In FY26 this became Eternal's growth engine and the reason the stock is priced the way it is.

How the unit economics work — one order at a time

The reason quick commerce is so hard is that the per-order economics are wafer-thin and only work at high density. Walk through a single ₹525 basket:

  • Revenue per order comes from three places: the margin on goods (especially now that Blinkit owns inventory — see §05), a delivery/handling fee the customer pays, and advertising (brands pay to appear in the app). Together these are Blinkit's "take."
  • Cost per order is the rider trip, the dark store's share of rent/electricity/staff, and packaging. The rider cost barely changes whether the basket is ₹300 or ₹800 — so bigger baskets and denser routes are pure margin.
  • Contribution margin = take minus those direct costs. Then the business must still cover central tech, marketing and overhead to reach adjusted EBITDA.
Why a new dark store loses money — then makes it

A brand-new dark store opens into a neighbourhood that doesn't yet have the habit. For months it does too few orders to cover its fixed rent and staff, so it loses money. As locals start ordering daily, order density rises, the same fixed costs spread over more orders, riders do more trips per hour, and the store crosses into profit — its "payback." So Blinkit's reported profit is a blend: mature stores in dense cities are nicely profitable, while a wave of brand-new stores drags the average down. That is exactly why group margins stay thin even as the model is proven: Blinkit keeps deliberately adding loss-making new stores to grab territory before rivals do. Management has said Delhi NCR — its most mature market — already runs at roughly a 3.5% adjusted-EBITDA margin, with Gurgaon and Noida nearer 5%. That is the "end-state" the whole network is climbing toward.

The bet: as more of the 2,243-store base "ages in" to Delhi-NCR-like density, the blended 0.3% climbs toward the mature 3.5–5%+. The risk: new-city expansion keeps adding immature stores that hold the blend down.

The land grab for density

Quick commerce is a winner-takes-density business — whoever has the most orders in a neighbourhood enjoys the best economics, so all players are racing to plant dark stores in the same dense urban areas before rivals lock them up. Blinkit is explicitly choosing growth over near-term margin: it has guided toward roughly 3,000 dark stores, accepting that each new store is a temporary drag. The strategic logic is that being first to density in a locality is close to permanent — habits, rider supply and store economics all compound for the leader — so the time to spend is now, while the category is being decided.

Why watch this every quarter: the two numbers that tell you whether the bet is working are dark-store count (is the land grab still on?) and Blinkit adjusted-EBITDA margin (is density turning into profit faster than new stores drag it down?). When those rise together, the thesis is on track. If store growth slows and margins stall, the land grab is getting expensive without the payoff.

The inventory-model & FDI shift: why Blinkit started owning the stock

A structural change that re-shaped the financials, the margins, and even Eternal's ownership — and confused a lot of readers.

In FY26 Blinkit made a quiet but profound change: it stopped being a pure marketplace (where third-party sellers list goods and Blinkit just takes a commission) and became inventory-led — buying and owning the groceries itself, like a retailer. By Q3 FY26, ~90% of Blinkit's order value ran on its own inventory. This single shift explains the eye-popping "revenue up 196%" headlines, a step-up in margins, and a change in who is allowed to own Eternal.

The accounting illusion (read this before any FY26 number scares you)

When Blinkit owned no goods, it booked only its commission as revenue. Now that it buys and resells the groceries, it books the entire sale value as revenue — and the cost of those goods as an expense. Nothing about how busy Blinkit is changed overnight, but reported revenue exploded (Eternal's Q4 FY26 revenue of ₹17,292 Cr was up ~196% YoY, and FY26 revenue jumped to ₹54,364 Cr from ₹20,243 Cr) purely because of this re-classification. The same shift also moved a chunk of what used to be "Hyperpure" revenue into the Blinkit line — which is why Hyperpure's reported revenue appears to fall ~47% even though its underlying restaurant-supply business grew ~37%. Lesson: in FY26, never compare Eternal's reported revenue growth to a prior year at face value — the definition changed. Order value (GOV/NOV) is the cleaner growth gauge.

The FDI angle — why this changed who can own Eternal

India's foreign-investment (FDI) rules draw a sharp line: foreign-funded e-commerce companies may run a marketplace (connecting buyers and sellers) but may not own inventory in multi-brand retail. To start owning grocery stock, Blinkit therefore had to ensure its parent qualified as "Indian-owned-and-controlled" (IOCC) — broadly, that Indians hold the controlling economic and voting interest. Because Eternal has no foreign promoter and a diffuse shareholding, it was able to structure itself to meet this test (rival Zepto did the same). So the inventory model and the "no-promoter, Indian-controlled" structure are linked: the ownership design is part of what lets Blinkit own stock at all.

Why owning inventory is worth the bother

  • Fatter, more controllable margins. As a retailer, Blinkit captures the full retail margin and can negotiate directly with brands, run private labels, and optimise pricing — management expects roughly a 1 percentage-point uplift to Blinkit's margin from the switch, much of it already captured.
  • Less discount dependence. Controlling buying and pricing reduces reliance on blunt discounting to drive orders — directly attacking the cash-burn problem.
  • Better data and assortment. Owning the stock means owning the demand signal — what to stock, where, and when — which improves availability (the thing q-comm customers care about most).
The trade-off: owning inventory means tying up working capital in stock and bearing wastage/markdown risk on perishables. The model lifts margins but makes Blinkit more capital-intensive and more exposed to inventory mistakes — a retailer's risks layered onto a logistics business.

Zomato food delivery: the profitable near-duopoly

The original business — now mature, profitable, and growing more slowly. A two-horse race with Swiggy.

Food delivery is the business that built the company, and it has reached the stage every loss-making platform dreams of: a profitable near-duopoly. In India, online restaurant delivery is essentially a two-player market — Zomato and Swiggy — splitting it roughly evenly. That structure is the whole reason it now makes money: with no third player burning cash to grab share, both can charge sustainable take rates and dial discounting back.

Why a duopoly turns profitable

For years, Zomato and Swiggy torched cash fighting for customers with deep discounts and rider subsidies — classic land-grab behaviour. Once the market settled into two roughly balanced players, the dynamic flipped:

  • Discounting eased. With no irrational third competitor, neither needs to "buy" every order — both pulled back promotions, and profitability appeared almost immediately.
  • Take rates rose. Platform fees, delivery charges and restaurant commissions edged up; restaurants have few alternatives to reach delivery customers.
  • Advertising became a profit pool. Restaurants pay to rank higher in the app — high-margin revenue that drops largely to the bottom line.
Why "two players" is the magic number

In a delivery market, three or more well-funded players is a recipe for endless cash-burn: each subsidises orders to grab share, and nobody makes money. Two roughly balanced players is the sweet spot — enough competition to keep them honest, but a stable enough structure that both can rationally raise take rates and cut discounts rather than fight to the death. India's food delivery settled into exactly this Zomato-vs-Swiggy equilibrium, which is why a business that lost money for a decade now throws off a 5.5% margin and rising. It's the same lesson as telecom or aviation: industry structure determines profitability as much as any single company's brilliance.

The catch: the growth is slowing

The flip side of maturity is decelerating growth. Management has openly acknowledged that the post-COVID surge has faded and that the long-run pace is settling toward the low-20s% (and dipped well below that during a mid-FY26 urban-demand soft patch). Food delivery is now a cash-cow that funds the group — reliably profitable, growing at a respectable but unspectacular clip — rather than the headline growth engine. That baton has passed to Blinkit. The bull view is that order frequency and new use-cases (Zomato's "Bistro"/instant food, larger cities deepening) keep food growing at ~20%; the bear view is that urban food delivery is structurally maturing and ~15% may be the new normal.

The read-through: a profitable, slower-growing food-delivery duopoly is genuinely valuable — it's the steady cash engine. But it is not what justifies a ₹2.5-lakh-crore price tag. For the valuation to make sense, Blinkit has to become a second profit engine of comparable or larger scale.
07

District & Hyperpure: the new bet and the quiet supply chain

The two smaller businesses — one an early loss-making land grab, one a quietly profitable B2B arm.

Beyond the two giants sit two smaller businesses with opposite profiles. District is Eternal's young, money-losing push into "going-out" — everything you do outside the home: dining reservations, movie tickets, live events, sport. Hyperpure is the unglamorous but quietly profitable B2B arm that supplies restaurants with ingredients and kitchen essentials.

District — the going-out bet

District is Eternal's attempt to own the "going-out" occasion the way Zomato owns the "eating-in" one. Spun out as its own app in 2024, it bundles restaurant dining-out (discovery, reservations, table booking) with movie and event ticketing — a category dominated by BookMyShow. The logic: Eternal already has tens of millions of food-loving app users and knows where they eat; turning that into reservations, tickets and event spend is a natural adjacency with high-margin potential.

  • Scale. Q4 FY26 NOV of ₹2,736 Cr, up ~46% YoY — growing fast off a small base.
  • Still loss-making. Adjusted-EBITDA loss of ₹81 Cr in Q4, though narrowing from ₹121 Cr the prior quarter. It is the group's cash-consuming bet.
  • Lumpy by nature. Going-out spend swings with the calendar — the IPL skews one quarter, big movie releases or concerts another — so any single quarter is a noisy read.

Hyperpure — the supply chain underneath

Hyperpure sells vegetables, groceries, dairy, packaging and kitchen supplies directly to restaurants. It does two jobs at once: it's a real B2B revenue line, and it deepens Eternal's relationship with the restaurants that power Zomato — better supply, more reliable kitchens, stickier partners. After years of losses, it crossed into sustained profitability in FY26 (a thin but positive ~0.5% adjusted-EBITDA margin), with underlying restaurant-supply revenue growing ~37%.

How to think about these two

District is optionality — a cheap-to-test bet that, if it works, gives Eternal a second consumer category (entertainment/experiences) on top of food and groceries; if it doesn't, the losses are small enough to absorb or shut. Hyperpure is plumbing — not exciting, but it strengthens the food-delivery moat by locking in restaurants and now pays for itself. Neither will move the valuation on its own. The reason to track them: District tells you whether Eternal can keep extending its delivery/marketplace muscle into new categories (the long-term "super-app" thesis), and Hyperpure tells you the restaurant ecosystem under Zomato is healthy. (Note: a Hyperpure reporting reshuffle in FY26 moved some revenue into Blinkit's inventory line — see §05 — so its reported revenue understates the underlying business.)

The group inflection: from cash-burn to (thin) profit

How a serial loss-maker reached profitability — and why the profit is still deliberately thin.

For most of its life, Eternal (as Zomato) lost money — the classic consumer-internet pattern of subsidising growth for years. The big structural story of the last two years is the inflection: the group as a whole turned profitable as food delivery matured, even as it chose to keep group profit thin by reinvesting in Blinkit's land grab and District. Understanding why profit is small by design is essential to valuing the company.

"Profitable, but barely" — and why that's the point

The war chest

Crucially, Eternal can afford to play this long game because it is sitting on a large cash pile — about ₹17,972 Cr of treasury at March 2026 (much of it raised in the IPO and a subsequent QIP). That balance sheet means it can fund Blinkit's land grab and District's losses for years without external capital — a decisive advantage in a war where the side that runs out of money first loses.

Why this war is the whole investment question

Blinkit's value rests on quick commerce becoming a profitable, consolidated market it leads — much like food delivery did. The danger is that q-comm stays a multi-player cash-burn war for years because the giants (Reliance's JioMart, Amazon, Flipkart) have effectively unlimited money and strategic reasons to keep grocery delivery competitive even at a loss. In food delivery, the field narrowed to two and profit appeared; in quick commerce, the field could widen as giants pile in, keeping discounting alive and margins suppressed. Blinkit's edge — leadership, the cash war chest, the inventory model, and Eternal's logistics depth — is real and probably decisive against the pure-play start-ups. Against Reliance and Amazon, the contest is genuinely open. The single biggest swing factor for the stock is whether quick commerce settles into a profitable structure Blinkit dominates, or stays a money-pit knife-fight.

How to think about a ~90×+ "P/E"

On today's ₹366 Cr profit, Eternal trades at a P/E in the high double-to-triple digits — a number that is essentially uninformative, because the company is choosing not to earn its full profit right now. Investors instead value it on order value (GOV) and the path to future margins: roughly, total group GOV (food + Blinkit + District) running at well over ₹1 lakh crore a year, multiplied by the steady-state margins each business can reach (food ~5–6%+, Blinkit climbing toward mature-market 3.5–5%+), discounted for the competitive risk. The bull case: Blinkit becomes a second profit engine the size of food delivery or larger, group margins expand as the store base matures, and a ₹2.5-lakh-crore price proves cheap. The bear case: quick-commerce competition keeps margins suppressed, the land grab burns the war chest, and a stock priced for a near-perfect outcome de-rates hard if growth or margins disappoint. You are not buying current earnings; you are buying a wager on how the quick-commerce war ends.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A proven food-delivery cash engine bolted to an unproven, high-stakes quick-commerce bet.

Eternal is two stories in one. The first — a profitable food-delivery duopoly and a quietly working B2B arm — is largely settled and valuable. The second — Blinkit's race to dominate and profit from quick commerce against well-funded rivals — is unsettled and is what the valuation rides on. The investment debate is entirely about that second story: will the land grab end in a profitable, Blinkit-led market, or a permanent cash-burn war?

The bull case

  • Blinkit is winning the category — ~46% share, the most dark stores, first to positive EBITDA; mature markets (Delhi NCR) already at 3.5–5% margins, showing the end-state works.
  • Quick commerce is a structural shift — Indians are forming a 10-minute-delivery habit; the addressable market (groceries + everything) is vast and barely penetrated.
  • The inventory model lifts Blinkit margins ~1pp and adds private-label upside, while the cash war chest (~₹18,000 Cr) funds the land grab without dilution.
  • Food delivery is a reliable cash engine — a profitable duopoly at 5.5% margins and rising, funding everything else.
  • Optionality — District and Hyperpure extend the platform into new categories at low cost; the "deliver anything" super-app thesis.

The bear case

  • The valuation prices perfection — ~₹2.5 lakh Cr on ₹366 Cr of profit; any stumble in Blinkit's margin march can de-rate the stock sharply.
  • The q-comm war may not end — Reliance (JioMart), Amazon and Flipkart have infinite balance sheets and reasons to keep grocery delivery loss-making; margins could stay suppressed for years.
  • Blinkit's blended margin is still ~0.3% — the gap between mature-store economics and the loss-making new stores is wide; new-city expansion keeps dragging the blend.
  • Food delivery is maturing — growth has slowed toward the low-20s% (and dipped below in a soft patch); the cash engine is steady, not accelerating.
  • Execution & leadership change — a new Group CEO, four businesses to run, inventory/working-capital risk, and thin profit leave little margin for error.
The bottom line

Eternal has done the hard part once already — it turned a money-losing food-delivery app into a profitable duopoly, proving its model can reach durable economics in a settled market. The entire bull case is that Blinkit repeats that feat in quick commerce: dominate density, let mature stores' 3.5–5%+ margins spread across a 2,000-plus (and growing) store base, and become a second profit engine larger than food. The entire bear case is that quick commerce is a worse structure than food delivery — more players, deeper-pocketed giants, perishable inventory risk — so the land grab stays expensive and the perfection priced into the stock never arrives. The next few years will be written in four numbers reported every quarter: Blinkit's dark-store count and EBITDA margin (is density turning to profit?), food delivery's growth and margin (is the cash engine holding?), and group cash burn (is the war chest being built or bled?). Get those right and a steep price compounds into something; get them wrong and a stock priced for the perfect ending has a long way to fall.
