00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

EIH Limited is the listed flagship of The Oberoi Group — the company that owns and runs India's most prestigious luxury hotels under the Oberoi name (ultra-luxury) and the Trident name (upper-upscale), plus Maidens Hotel in Delhi, a handful of palaces and resorts, luxury Nile river cruisers in Egypt, and a flight-catering business. Across owned and managed properties the Group runs roughly 30 hotels and ~4,935 rooms across seven countries.

Three facts frame the whole company:

  • A trophy-asset luxury operator at the very top of the market. Oberoi hotels repeatedly rank among the best in the world in guest surveys; EIH's owned hotels ran 76.8% occupancy at a RevPAR of ₹17,400 in FY26 (up ~8.5%), earned a ~38% EBITDA margin, and the company sits on ~₹1,335 Cr of cash with no meaningful debt. FY26 consolidated revenue was ~₹3,106 Cr and profit ~₹657 Cr.
  • It is riding a genuine luxury up-cycle. India has a structural shortage of premium rooms — luxury demand is compounding at ~10–11% a year while new luxury supply grows only ~6% — so occupancy and room rates have risen together, and because a hotel's costs are largely fixed, the extra revenue lands mostly as profit. That is the entire margin story.
  • The share register is unlike any other hotel company's. The Oberoi family holds ~32.9%; Reliance Industries owns ~18.5% (brought in as a "white knight" in 2010); and long-time rival ITC still holds ~16%. Two of India's biggest conglomerates — corporate rivals — sit on one ~₹20,000 Cr hotel company's cap table, which leaves the genuine public float unusually thin.
In plain terms

EIH is best understood as a collection of a few extraordinary buildings run to the highest standard in the country. It does not chase size — Taj (Indian Hotels) is three times its revenue — it chases the top of the pyramid, where a room can sell for tens of thousands of rupees a night and where reputation, not price, wins the booking. When India's affluent travel more, when weddings and global summits fill the ballrooms, and when there simply aren't enough five-star rooms to go round, EIH's largely fixed cost base means the upside pours through to profit. The flip side is the same fixed base working in reverse: when travel stops — as it did in the pandemic — a trophy hotel still costs a fortune to keep the lights on, and the profits vanish fast.

01

Luxury-hotel economics 101: how a five-star earns

No hospitality background needed — just the handful of numbers that run the business.

A hotel is, in financial terms, a fixed box of rooms with a mostly fixed cost base — the building, the lease or the capital tied up in it, the chefs and housekeepers and front desk, the electricity and the upkeep run whether the hotel is full or empty. It earns by filling those rooms with guests who each pay a nightly rate, and by selling them food, drink, weddings and services while they're there. Three numbers describe almost the whole thing.

The three numbers that decide a hotel's fortunes
TermWhat it means (and EIH's owned-hotel figure, FY26)
Occupancy (%)The share of available rooms actually sold on a given night. Higher occupancy spreads the fixed cost over more paying guests. EIH's owned hotels ran ~76.8% — very high for luxury, where the top end deliberately holds some pricing discipline rather than discounting to fill.
ADR / ARR"Average Daily Rate" (also "Average Room Rate") — the average price of a sold room per night. This is where a luxury operator lives: an Oberoi room commands a multiple of an ordinary hotel's rate. EIH's ADR worked out to roughly ₹22,600 in FY26, up around 10%.
RevPAR"Revenue Per Available Room" = occupancy × ADR. It blends how full you are with how much you charge into a single number — the most-watched metric in the whole industry. EIH's owned-hotel RevPAR was ₹17,400 in FY26 (76.8% × ~₹22,600), up ~8.5%.
Why RevPAR is the metric that matters

Two hotels can have the same occupancy and earn wildly different money, because one charges twice the rate. Two hotels can charge the same rate and earn differently, because one is half-empty. RevPAR collapses both into one figure: revenue per room you could have sold. Grow RevPAR and — because the cost base barely moves — you grow profit faster. That is why every hotel earnings call opens with RevPAR, why analysts model it obsessively, and why, later in this primer, RevPAR is the single number that will decide whether EIH's valuation is deserved.

Own the hotel, or just manage it — two very different businesses

Behind every hotel brand is a choice that shapes the whole economics:

  • Owning the hotel — you put up the land and the building (or a long lease and a full fit-out), you carry the cost and the risk, and in return you keep all the room revenue and F&B. This is asset-heavy: capital-intensive, slow to build, but you own the upside of a great location outright. Oberoi's crown jewels — The Oberoi New Delhi, Udaivilas in Udaipur, Amarvilas in Agra — are owned.
  • Managing someone else's hotel — an investor builds and owns the property, and pays you a management fee (typically a slice of revenue plus an incentive on profit) to run it under your brand and standards. This is asset-light: little capital, high-margin fee income, and you can grow the brand fast without funding every building. It is how Marriott and Hyatt became giants.

EIH has historically leaned to owning — that's what gives it world-topping trophy assets and full control of the guest experience — but its growth plan (Section 07) tilts firmly toward the asset-light, managed model to expand the brand without the capital drag.

02

The Oberoi business model: where a rupee actually comes from

A luxury hotel is four businesses under one roof — plus two that surprise people.

People assume a hotel earns from rooms. Rooms are the engine, but at a luxury property they are often less than half the story. EIH earns across four connected streams inside each hotel, and two more outside it that most first-time readers don't expect.

The two streams outside the hotels

  • Flight catering & airport lounges/restaurants. EIH runs flight kitchens that prepare in-flight meals for airlines, and operates airport restaurants and lounges. It's a lower-margin, more industrial business than luxury hotels, but it's steady, cash-generative, and leverages the group's culinary reputation.
  • Luxury Nile cruisers & corporate air charters. The genuinely unusual bit: EIH operates luxury river cruisers on the Nile in Egypt (the Oberoi Zahra and Philae) and a corporate aircraft charter business (Oberoi Aviation). A "hotel" company, in other words, also sells you a floating five-star holiday down the Nile and a private jet to get around India.
The surprise most people miss

The most prestigious hotel brand in India is also, quietly, an airline caterer and a cruise line. That breadth isn't random — it all runs on the same core competence: obsessive service and food at scale. A group that can plate a flawless dinner in a Udaipur palace can also run the kitchen that feeds an aircraft or a cruise cabin. It diversifies the revenue a little, but make no mistake: the owned luxury hotels are where the profit and the valuation live — everything else is a supporting act.

03

Who controls EIH — the ownership saga

The single most interesting thing about this company isn't a hotel. It's the share register.

EIH ("East India Hotels") is the flagship of a family business built over three generations by the Oberois. But its ownership carries a decades-long corporate drama that a generalist should know, because it explains both the company's independence today and a quirk in how its shares trade.

Founders and family

The group traces to Rai Bahadur M.S. Oberoi, who built it from a single Shimla hotel in the 1930s into India's first genuinely international luxury chain — The Oberoi Inter-Continental in New Delhi (1965) was a landmark. His grandson P.R.S. "Biki" Oberoi then turned "Oberoi" into a byword for the world's best service, obsessing over design and detail at properties like Udaivilas. Biki Oberoi passed away in November 2023; the business is now led by the next generation — Arjun Oberoi and Vikram Oberoi (MD & CEO of EIH) — who are steering the shift toward asset-light growth.

The ITC overhang — and the Reliance "white knight"

Here is the part that surprises people. For much of the 2000s and 2010s, EIH lived under a takeover shadow. Cigarettes-to-FMCG giant ITC Limited quietly accumulated EIH shares from around 2000, building a stake that hovered near the ~15% level at which India's rules can trigger a mandatory open offer. ITC framed it as a passive investment; the Oberois treated it as a slow-motion threat to their independence.

In 2010, the family found a defender. Reliance Industries — Mukesh Ambani's group — was brought in as a "white knight", buying roughly 14% of EIH from the Oberoi family and from entities linked to Analjit Singh (the Max Group founder, a family friend), later raising its stake to about 18.5%. That made Reliance the largest single non-promoter shareholder, sitting above ITC and effectively blocking any hostile move. ITC, for its part, never launched a bid — and, tellingly, never sold either.

A correction worth flagging: a common shorthand says "ITC sold its EIH stake to Reliance in 2023." That is not what happened. ITC demerged its own hotels business (ITC Hotels, listed January 2025) in that window, and around the same time it consolidated its EIH holding to ~16.13% — it did not exit. As of early 2026, both Reliance (~18.5%) and ITC (~16%) remain on EIH's register alongside the Oberoi promoters. Treat any figure here as approximate and check the latest exchange filing.

A device you won't find in any other hotel primer

Read EIH's register like a map of corporate India. The "public" bucket shown as ~46% is a mirage: strip out ~18.5% Reliance and ~16% ITC, and the genuine free float — the shares that actually change hands with ordinary investors — is only around 11–12%. So three of India's great business houses (the Oberois, the Ambanis and the ITC group) effectively sit around one luxury-hotel company, two of them rivals who each hold enough to matter but neither in control. It makes EIH's stock thinly traded relative to its ₹20,000 Cr size, and it means the biggest strategic question — would Reliance ever go further, would ITC ever sell — is a boardroom story, not a spreadsheet one.

The brands and the estate

Two brands, a small number of extraordinary buildings, and a seven-country footprint.

EIH deliberately runs a tight, premium portfolio rather than a sprawling one. Its power is concentration and quality — a handful of best-in-class hotels rather than hundreds of average ones. Two brands do the work.

Why "small" is a feature, not a bug

EIH is a fraction of the size of Taj/Indian Hotels by rooms and revenue, and that's by design. Luxury economics reward scarcity and reputation, not room count. A hotel that ranks first in its city for guest experience can charge the highest rate in that market and keep charging it — the "STR benchmark" measures exactly this, and EIH's owned hotels lead or place second in 13 of 15 of their local sets. The moat is not scale; it is the near-impossibility of building another Udaivilas, and the decades of trust behind the Oberoi name.

05

The India luxury up-cycle and the room shortage

The demand force powering EIH's record numbers — and why it may have real legs.

EIH's recent results aren't just good management; they ride a genuine, structural up-cycle in Indian premium travel. Understanding its two halves — booming demand and constrained supply — is understanding why RevPAR and margins have climbed together.

The demand half

  • A richer, more mobile India. A growing affluent class is travelling more, both for leisure and business; domestic tourism has surged, and premium travellers "trade up" to luxury.
  • Weddings and MICE. India's big-ticket weddings and the return of large conferences, corporate offsites and global summits (India has hosted high-profile international events) fill banquet halls and room blocks at premium rates.
  • Inbound luxury travel and a limited set of genuinely world-class properties mean top hotels can hold rates that were unthinkable a decade ago.

The supply half — the part that makes it structural

A luxury hotel takes years and enormous capital to build, needs a rare site, and clears high approval hurdles. So supply responds slowly and reluctantly. Industry estimates put luxury-room demand growing at ~10–11% a year against supply of only ~6% — a persistent gap. When more people chase a slowly growing pool of top rooms, two things happen at once: hotels run fuller and raise rates. Both feed RevPAR.

The gap between the two bars is the up-cycle. As long as it persists, occupancy and ADR can rise together — the ideal condition for RevPAR and margins.
Why the up-cycle shows up as fatter margins, not just more sales

This is the crux for EIH. When RevPAR rises because of higher rates (rather than just more guests), almost none of it costs extra to deliver — the room, the staff and the building are already paid for. So a 10% lift in ADR flows to profit at a far higher rate than a 10% lift in a normal business's sales would. That is why EIH's EBITDA margin has expanded into the high-30s%, and why the same set of hotels can earn dramatically more in a good year than a bad one. The up-cycle is real; the question the valuation asks (Section 09) is how long it lasts.

Operating leverage: why the profit swings so hard

The most important mechanism in the whole investment case, shown on EIH's own numbers.

"Operating leverage" sounds abstract until you watch it happen on one company's real figures. Because a hotel's costs are largely fixed, changes in revenue don't move profit proportionally — they amplify it. EIH offers an unusually clean demonstration, because the pandemic and the recovery ran the same hotels through the full arc.

The mechanism in one line

Revenue went from ~₹497 Cr to ~₹3,106 Cr in five years — a bit over 6× — but profit went from a loss of about ₹94 Cr to a profit of about ₹657 Cr. That's the leverage: the buildings, the brand and much of the staff cost roughly the same at ₹497 Cr of revenue as at ₹3,106 Cr, so once the fixed base is covered, additional room-nights at high ADR drop mostly to the bottom line. 07

The growth runway: going asset-light

How a company that made its name owning trophies plans to grow without buying more of them.

EIH's expansion plan marks a deliberate shift. The historical model — own the great buildings — produced world-beating hotels but is slow and capital-hungry. The new plan keeps the owned crown jewels while adding rooms mostly through management contracts, where an outside investor funds the building and EIH runs it for a fee.

The development pipeline to 2030 (approximate)
ElementScaleWhy it matters
New hotels planned~25Across India and abroad (Bhutan, Egypt, London, Nepal, Saudi Arabia) — Oberoi and Trident, roughly ~2,033 keys added.
Owned / co-invested~8Where EIH still puts capital in — including marquee resorts (e.g. new Oberoi properties, Rishikesh).
Managed (asset-light)~17The bulk of the additions — high-margin fee income, minimal capital, plus Nile cruisers and Dahabeyas under management.

Concrete near-term markers give the plan texture: new Oberoi and Trident resorts announced for Rishikesh; an Oberoi in Hyderabad's Knowledge City (~220 keys); Trident additions near Nandi Hills and Fort Aguada, Goa; a boutique Oberoi near Gir; and EIH Associated Hotels adding luxury tents at Trident Udaipur. International Oberoi properties extend the brand's reach without diluting its scarcity at home.

The strategic trade-off, plainly put

Asset-light growth is how you compound a hotel brand without compounding the capital. Fee income carries very high margins and almost no balance-sheet risk, so a successful managed pipeline can lift returns on capital markedly. The cost is control and economics: on a managed hotel you capture a fee, not the full room revenue, and you depend on the owner's willingness to invest to your standard. For a brand whose entire value is uncompromising quality, the tension is real — Oberoi must scale its fee business without letting the guest experience that justifies the name slip. The pipeline is the test of whether it can.

The industry and the rivals

A consolidating field of listed hotel companies — with EIH sitting at the luxury apex, but not the top of the size table.

Indian hospitality is a spread of business models, from asset-owners to pure brand-managers. EIH competes for the highest-spending guest, but it does so against both larger domestic groups and the global asset-light giants.

The genuine surprise in this table

Periodic political attention to room-tariffs and events exists but is light-touch at the luxury end, whose guests are price-insensitive.

Financials & valuation

A high-margin, cash-rich, cyclical business — priced on a cycle that is currently near its peak.

EIH's FY26 financials show the up-cycle at work: record RevPAR, a high-30s% EBITDA margin, strong cash generation and a debt-light balance sheet. The valuation debate is not about quality; it is about whether today's RevPAR and margins are a new normal or a cyclical high.

How to read the multiple

At ~₹20,300 Cr for ~₹657 Cr of FY26 profit — roughly 31× earnings, ~16× EV/EBITDA — EIH is priced as a high-quality luxury franchise in a strong cycle. That price is defensible if the up-cycle is durable: a structural room shortage, rate power, a net-cash balance sheet and an asset-light pipeline that lifts returns. The bearish reading is simpler — hotel earnings are cyclical, FY26 RevPAR and ~38% margins may be a cyclical peak rather than a floor, and paying a mid-cycle-plus multiple on peak-looking earnings compounds the downside if the cycle rolls over. Both readings turn on the same lever.

The one metric that settles it: owned-hotel RevPAR (occupancy × ADR), and whether the ~38% EBITDA margin holds as new supply arrives. If RevPAR keeps climbing — or even holds above ~₹17,000 while the asset-light pipeline layers on high-margin fee income — the cycle is behaving structurally and the multiple is earned. If RevPAR stalls and rooms have to be discounted to stay full, operating leverage runs in reverse and the same multiple looks expensive. Watch RevPAR and margin together, quarter by quarter; the reported PAT will lie to you (as FY26 showed), but RevPAR won't.
10

Cyclicality and the risks

A great business in a fragile industry — the ways this can go wrong.

The single most important thing to internalise about any hotel company is that it is cyclical and shock-prone. The very operating leverage that makes EIH shine in an up-cycle makes it painful in a downturn — and travel demand is exposed to forces entirely outside the company's control.

  • Demand shocks. Recessions cut corporate and leisure travel; pandemics can shut hotels outright (Covid drove EIH to a loss in FY21); terror attacks and geopolitical flare-ups hit inbound tourism and event bookings hard. High fixed costs mean occupancy can fall much faster than costs can.
  • Capital intensity & long gestation. Owned luxury hotels take years and vast capital to build and can't flex with demand — you can't shrink a palace in a bad year. This is exactly why EIH is tilting asset-light, but the owned base remains capital-heavy.
  • Seasonality. Results swing quarter to quarter — the wedding and winter-travel seasons (Q3/Q4) are strong; the monsoon quarter is soft. A weak monsoon quarter is normal, not a red flag.
  • Competition for guests, talent and sites. Global chains, IHCL's scale, a newly listed ITC Hotels, and premium owner-operators all compete for the same affluent guest and the same rare luxury sites.
  • Peak-cycle risk. The honest bear point: FY26's RevPAR and ~38% margin sit near cyclical highs. If the up-cycle matures and new rooms finally arrive, rate power softens and the leverage reverses.
  • Thin float & ownership dynamics. With Reliance (~18.5%) and ITC (~16%) holding large blocks, the free float is small and the stock can be volatile; any change in those strategic stakes would be a material, unpredictable event.
The scar worth remembering: in FY21 the very same trophy hotels that earned ₹657 Cr in FY26 lost money. Nothing about the buildings, the brand or the management changed — only demand did. That is the permanent nature of the business: extraordinary in good years, unforgiving in bad ones. Position sizing in a hotel stock is really a bet on the cycle, not just the company.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely elite operator, at a genuinely full point in its cycle.

EIH is one of the highest-quality assets in Indian hospitality: an unmatched luxury brand, world-topping owned hotels, a net-cash balance sheet, and a credible plan to grow the brand asset-light. The investment question is almost entirely about where we are in the cycle and what you pay for peak-looking economics.

The bull case

  • Scarcity moat — a tiny set of irreplaceable trophy hotels with the highest rate power in the market; you cannot build another Udaivilas.
  • Structural room shortage — luxury demand outgrowing supply ~10–11% vs ~6%, sustaining occupancy and ADR together.
  • Operating leverage — a fixed cost base means rate-led RevPAR gains flow to profit at high incremental margins.
  • Balance-sheet strength — ~₹1,335 Cr cash, negligible debt, self-funded growth, and an asset-light pipeline that can lift returns on capital.
  • Backing & brand stewardship — Reliance's strategic ~18.5% underpins independence; the next-generation Oberois are executing the growth plan.

The bear case

  • Cyclical peak risk — FY26 RevPAR and ~38% margins may be a high-water mark, not a base.
  • Full valuation — ~31× earnings / ~16× EV-EBITDA on possibly peak profits; a rolling cycle would hit both E and the multiple.
  • Shock exposure — recessions, pandemics and geopolitics can crater occupancy fast, and fixed costs don't flex.
  • Smaller & slower-growing than IHCL by scale; asset-light fee growth is unproven at Oberoi's quality bar.
  • Thin float & strategic overhangs — Reliance and ITC blocks make the stock illiquid and its ownership future a wildcard.
The bottom line

EIH is not a growth-at-any-count story and never has been — it is a bet that the world's most demanding travellers keep wanting the best rooms in India, that those rooms stay scarce, and that Oberoi keeps owning the very top of the market. The next chapter is concrete: the Rishikesh and Knowledge-City Oberois opening, the ~17 managed keys converting brand into fee income, and — above all — whether owned-hotel RevPAR can hold north of ₹17,000 as the cycle matures. Get a real-time reading each quarter from occupancy and ADR, ignore the noise in reported PAT, and remember the FY21 scar: this is a company that can print a record year and a loss with the very same hotels. What settles the case is not the brand's fame but the RevPAR line — the one number that told the truth through both.
