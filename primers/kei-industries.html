00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

KEI Industries makes electrical wires and cables — from the humble house wire behind your walls, through the low- and high-tension power cables that feed factories and buildings, all the way up to extra-high-voltage (EHV) cables that carry electricity across the grid at up to 400,000 volts. It also runs a stainless-steel-wire business and a turnkey electrical-projects (EPC) arm. It is the second-largest branded organised wires-&-cables player in India (roughly 8% market share, behind Polycab), and one of only a few Indian companies technically qualified to make EHV cable — a genuine engineering moat.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A fast, debt-light compounder. Revenue grew ~23% a year over the last five years to ₹11,746 Cr in FY26 (+20.7%); net profit was ₹918 Cr (EPS ~₹96), it earns an ~11.8% EBITDA margin, a ~20% return on capital (ROCE), and carries almost no net debt — recently topped up by a ₹2,000 Cr equity raise.
  • The story is a deliberate move up the value ladder and toward the shelf. Two levers drive the re-rating: (1) a push into the higher-margin, working-capital-light retail (dealer/distributor) channel — now ~54% of sales — and (2) growing the scarce, premium EHV and export business, where EHV domestic revenue jumped ~66% in FY26 and exports more than doubled.
  • A capacity super-cycle meets a competitive scare. KEI has just fired up its ₹2,000 Cr greenfield Sanand (Gujarat) plant to break a capacity ceiling and chase booming demand (housing, grid capex, renewables, data centres, railways, exports). At the same time, deep-pocketed giants Adani, UltraTech and Aditya Birla have announced entry into wires & cables — a threat that spooked the whole sector in 2025 and hangs over the valuation.
In plain terms

Picture KEI as a company that buys copper and aluminium, adds engineering, and sells the result at a mark-up. At the bottom of the ladder it sells simple house wires under a consumer brand you can buy from an electrician; at the top it builds cables so specialised that only a global handful can make them. Its game plan for the last few years has been to sell more of the branded, shelf-facing stuff (which earns fatter, steadier margins and needs less cash tied up) and more of the ultra-premium EHV stuff (which almost nobody can compete on) — while building a giant new factory to have enough to sell. The cloud on the horizon: a couple of India's biggest conglomerates have decided this is a market worth barging into.

01

Wires & cables 101: how a metal-plus-margin business earns

No engineering degree required — just how copper becomes profit.

A wire is a metal conductor. A cable is one or more conductors wrapped in insulation and armour. The business, at heart, is simple to describe and hard to do well: buy a commodity metal (mostly copper and aluminium), draw it into conductors, insulate and armour it, and sell it for more than the metal plus the conversion cost. Two ideas explain almost the whole economics.

Idea 1 — the commodity pass-through

Copper and aluminium are 60–70% of a cable's cost, and their prices swing daily on global exchanges. If KEI simply ate those swings, one bad copper month could wipe out a quarter's profit. Instead the industry works on a pass-through model: when metal prices rise, selling prices rise with them (via price escalation clauses on institutional orders, or simply re-pricing retail stock). What the maker actually earns is the conversion margin / value-add on top of the metal — the spread for turning raw metal into a finished, tested, branded cable.

Why this matters for reading the numbers

Because metal is passed through, revenue growth can be flattered or depressed by copper prices that have nothing to do with the business winning share. That's why analysts watch volume growth (tonnes / kilometres of cable sold) and EBITDA per tonne more than headline rupee revenue. KEI guided to ~17–18% volume growth for the year — the number that tells you the factory is genuinely busier, stripped of copper noise. It also means margins look thin in percentage terms (~10–12% EBITDA) simply because the metal sits in both the numerator's cost and the denominator's revenue; the real value-add margin on the conversion is much healthier.

Idea 2 — value-add rises as you climb the ladder

Not all cable is equal. A basic house wire is close to a commodity — lots of makers, thin margins, price-driven. A 400kV EHV cable is a piece of high-reliability engineering that can bring down a city grid if it fails — few makers, fat margins, trust-driven. The higher up the voltage-and-complexity ladder you go, the more of the price is your engineering rather than the metal — so margins, pricing power and defensibility all improve. KEI's entire strategy is about shifting its mix up that ladder and into the branded channel at the same time.

02

The value-add ladder: house wires to 400kV

Read this once and KEI's whole strategy clicks into place.

KEI's product range is best understood as a ladder of rising value-add. At the bottom, high-volume commodity-ish products sold to millions of small buyers. At the top, low-volume, ultra-engineered products sold to a handful of utilities and contractors. KEI is one of the very few Indian firms that spans the entire ladder — and it is deliberately shifting weight toward the top rungs and the branded channel.

The segments, decoded

  • House wires (retail / B2C) — the flame-retardant wiring inside homes and offices, sold under the KEI brand through dealers and electricians. This is the consumer face of the business: brand, distribution and advertising matter more than tenders. Fatter, steadier margins; low working capital (dealers pay fast).
  • LT & HT power cables — low-tension and high-tension power cables, the workhorse core: they carry power inside and between buildings, factories, and distribution networks. Sold both to dealers (retail) and directly to institutions/contractors (B2B). This is the largest chunk of revenue.
  • EHV cables (extra-high-voltage) — the crown jewel. KEI is among the very few Indian makers qualified to produce EHV cable, and one of a select global set that can make it up to 400kV. These carry bulk power across the transmission grid; a failure is catastrophic, so buyers pay a premium for a proven, qualified supplier. Highest margins, biggest moat.
  • Stainless-steel wire — a smaller, separate business (~₹217 Cr in FY26) — stainless-steel wire for industrial uses. A diversification, not the main event.
  • EPC (turnkey projects) — KEI doesn't just sell cable; it can execute the whole electrical package on a project (design, supply, install, commission). Kept deliberately small (see section 10) because it ties up cash.

03

The company and who controls it

From a 1968 rubber-cable partnership to a ₹50,000-crore listed compounder.

KEI is a family-founded, family-run business. It began in 1968 as Krishna Electrical Industries, a small partnership making rubber house-wiring cables, started by Dayanand Gupta. His son Anil Gupta joined around 1980, corporatised it, and over four decades turned a tiny cable shop into one of India's leading integrated wires-&-cables companies. He remains Chairman & Managing Director; the next generation (Akshit Diviaj Gupta) is now in the business.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Anil Gupta family holds ~35% of the equity (with zero pledged shares) after the November-2024 QIP diluted the stake somewhat. A promoter with skin in the game but no over-leverage.
  • Institutional shareholders: the remaining ~65% is largely with domestic and foreign institutions, mutual funds and the public — a well-owned, well-covered mid/large-cap. The 2024 QIP brought in fresh marquee institutional money.
  • Balance sheet: effectively net cash — gross borrowings of only ~₹253 Cr against ~₹6,600 Cr of reserves and the QIP proceeds. The expansion is being funded from equity and internal cash, not debt.
Why the ownership shape matters

A promoter family that has run the business for two generations brings deep operating knowledge and long-term orientation — useful in a business where customer trust and quality reputation are built over decades (especially in EHV). The relatively modest ~35% promoter stake and clean balance sheet also mean KEI raised growth capital the healthy way — issuing equity to institutions rather than piling on debt — which is exactly what let it fund a ₹2,000 Cr plant without stressing the finances. The flip side: a lower promoter stake gives the family less absolute control and makes the company more answerable to institutional shareholders each quarter.

04

The retail pivot — why the stock re-rated

The single most important strategic shift in the KEI story.

For years KEI was seen as a lumpy, project-and-tender business — big institutional cable orders and EPC jobs that were low-margin, cash-hungry and dependent on winning tenders. The re-rating of the last few years has been driven by a deliberate turn toward the opposite kind of revenue: the branded retail (dealer/distributor) channel, which sells house wires and cables through a dealer network to thousands of small buyers. That shift lifts margins, cuts working capital, and earns a higher valuation multiple. This is KEI's version of "the thing an analyst leads with."

Dealer/distribution contributed ~54% of total sales in FY26 (management disclosure), on a base of ~2,125 active working dealers as of 31 Mar 2026. Institutional (incl. exports) is the balance; EPC is deliberately kept a sliver.

The distribution engine — dealers, depots, and brand

  • Dealer network: ~2,125 active working dealers/distributors as of March 2026 (up steadily year on year), backed by regional depots, marketing offices and a large field sales force. More dealers = more shelf space = more retail pull.
  • Brand-building: KEI has stepped up advertising and brand spend (celebrity endorsements, trade marketing) to turn "a cable" into "a KEI cable" in the electrician's and consumer's mind — the same playbook that made Polycab and Havells household names in wiring.
  • Why it re-rates the stock: as the retail mix rises, blended margins firm up, cash conversion improves, and earnings become steadier — so the market is willing to value KEI more like a branded consumer-electrical company and less like a lumpy project contractor.
The mechanism, in one line

Every point of sales that migrates from a low-margin, cash-hungry tender to a branded product on a dealer's shelf does three good things at once: it earns more, it uses less cash, and it deserves a higher multiple. Stack that shift on top of overall volume growth and you get the compounding — and the re-rating — that has defined KEI as an investment. The catch, covered in section 08, is that this is exactly the profitable retail pool that Adani, UltraTech and Aditya Birla now want a share of.

05

EHV and the export kicker — the scarce, premium end

The technical moat that a new entrant can't buy off a shelf.

If retail is the margin-and-multiple story, EHV and exports are the moat-and-premium story. Extra-high-voltage cable — the kind that carries bulk power across the transmission grid at 66kV, 132kV, 220kV and up to 400kV — is technically brutal to make and even harder to get qualified to sell. KEI is one of only a handful of Indian companies that can do it, and one of a select global group that can reach 400kV. That scarcity is worth a lot.

Why EHV is a genuine moat

  • Technology & testing: EHV cables need extreme insulation purity, specialised manufacturing (clean rooms, vertical extrusion), and pass rigorous type-tests. You can't simply buy a line and start selling.
  • Qualification & track record: utilities won't buy EHV cable from an unproven supplier — a failure can black out a city. Buyers demand years of proven, field-tested performance, so the approved-vendor list is short and slow-moving. This is what a well-funded new entrant cannot shortcut.
  • Premium margins: scarce supply + mission-critical use = pricing power. EHV is among KEI's highest-margin products, so a rising EHV mix lifts the whole company's profitability.

The export kicker

The same EHV/quality credentials open export markets. With global shipping normalising and tariffs easing, KEI has been pushing hard into the US and other developed markets, where cable demand is being driven by grid upgrades, renewables and data centres. Exports of wires and cables in H1 FY26 more than doubled to ~₹757 Cr (from ~₹346 Cr), and management is targeting exports reaching ~20% of total sales. Exports matter for two reasons: they diversify demand beyond India, and developed-market orders typically carry better margins and validate the quality story.

06

The capacity super-cycle — Sanand and the ceiling

You can't sell cable you can't make — so KEI is building a giant new plant.

A cable maker's growth is gated by capacity: however hot demand runs, you can only sell as much as your factories can extrude. Through FY24–25 KEI was pressing against that ceiling — running its plants hard while demand kept climbing. The answer is the largest capital project in its history: a greenfield mega-plant at Sanand, near Ahmedabad in Gujarat, funded largely by the ₹2,000 Cr QIP raised in late 2024.

The Sanand plant, in numbers

  • ~₹2,000 Cr total investment, financed mainly by the November-2024 QIP plus internal accruals — so it doesn't load the balance sheet with debt.
  • Phase-1 began commercial production in December 2025 (LT/HT cables), adding roughly 60,700 km of annual cable capacity — a step-change in what KEI can physically make.
  • EHV production at Sanand is slated for ~Q1 FY27 (Phase-2), expanding the scarce high-value capacity where margins are best.
  • Ongoing capex of roughly ₹600–700 Cr over the next couple of years to complete the ramp — comfortably fundable from cash flow.
Why the timing is the whole bet

Big greenfield plants are a double-edged sword. Get the timing right — demand booming as new capacity switches on — and you capture years of growth you'd otherwise have turned away. Get it wrong — capacity landing into a demand air-pocket or a price war — and you're left with expensive, under-utilised assets dragging on returns. KEI is betting that India's power, housing and industrial capex cycle (section 07) keeps the new Sanand lines busy, and that its retail brand + EHV edge protect pricing even as the giants arrive. The QIP funding is the prudent part: by paying for the plant with equity rather than debt, KEI kept the downside survivable if the ramp is slower than hoped.

07

The demand drivers — why the cable market is booming

A rare case where several structural tailwinds line up at once.

KEI's aggressive capex only makes sense because demand for wires and cables is in a multi-year up-cycle, driven by several independent forces that all happen to need more cable. India's wire & cable market is estimated at ~$21 billion in 2025 and is projected to grow at ~9% a year to ~$33 billion by 2030. Here's what's pulling.

The structural demand tailwinds
DriverWhy it needs more cable
Housing & real estateIndia's property up-cycle means more homes and commercial space — every one wired with house wire and building cable (KEI's retail sweet spot).
Power T&D / grid capexMassive spending to strengthen and expand the transmission & distribution grid — the direct market for HT and EHV cables.
RenewablesSolar and wind farms need vast amounts of cable to connect generation to the grid — a fast-growing, cable-intensive segment.
Data centresIndia's data-centre build-out is extraordinarily power- and cable-hungry — high-quality power cabling per facility.

Industrial & manufacturing capexThe "make in India" factory-building wave wires up new plants, warehouses and industrial parks.

Why "several drivers at once" is the key point

Most cyclical businesses lean on one demand engine. Cables are unusual in that housing, grid capex, renewables, data centres, railways and industrial capex are all expanding at the same time — and each needs cable independently. That diversity makes the up-cycle more durable: even if housing cools, grid and renewables can keep the factories busy. It's the fundamental reason KEI, Polycab and the rest are all racing to add capacity — and, less comfortably, the reason the market looks juicy enough for Adani and UltraTech to want in.

08

The rivals — and the threat that spooked the sector

The competitive question that hangs over every cable stock's valuation.

The organised wires-&-cables market has a clear pecking order, and KEI sits comfortably in the top tier. But in 2025 the whole sector was rattled by news that changed the story: Adani, UltraTech (Aditya Birla) and others announced they were entering wires & cables — deep-pocketed giants moving onto turf that had been unusually profitable. Any honest KEI primer has to confront this head-on.

The incumbents

  • Polycab — the market leader (~18–19% share), the benchmark for brand, distribution and scale. The company KEI is chasing.
  • Havells — a powerhouse consumer-electrical brand with deep retail reach; strong in wires, expanding in cables.
  • RR Kabel & Finolex — well-established branded players, strong in the retail wire market.
  • KEI's differentiation — versus these peers, KEI's edge is its EHV capability (few peers match it) and its improving retail mix; its relative weakness has historically been a smaller retail brand than Polycab/Havells, which is exactly what the brand push (section 04) is trying to close.

The new-entrant threat

In 2025, the sector de-rated sharply on a run of announcements: UltraTech (Aditya Birla's cement giant) said it would enter cables & wires; the Aditya Birla Group approved a ~₹1,800 Cr investment with a Gujarat plant targeted for late 2026; and Adani (via a Kutch Copper joint venture) moved into the space. Cable stocks fell hard on the news. The fear is straightforward: these are conglomerates with enormous balance sheets, cost advantages (Adani has captive copper; UltraTech/Birla have vast building-materials distribution), and the appetite to compete aggressively on price — which could compress the healthy margins incumbents have enjoyed.

How worried should you be? The balanced view

The threat is real but often overstated in the panic. Three things temper it. (1) The market is growing ~9%+ a year — new entrants can add capacity without immediately stealing incumbents' volumes if the pie keeps expanding. (2) The moat is uneven. New money can most easily flood the commodity end (basic wire, LT cable) — but the EHV / high-voltage end, the qualified-vendor lists, and a two-generation dealer network are years-long to replicate. KEI's deliberate climb up the value ladder and into scarce EHV is, in part, a defence against exactly this. (3) Time. New plants take years to build, qualify and scale; the disruption, if it comes, is a multi-year drip, not a sudden flood. The honest bear point stands, though: even the threat of aggressive new competition can cap the sector's valuation multiple and, at the margin, pressure retail pricing — which is why this section, not the financials, is where the real debate on the stock lives.

The one number that captures the whole tension: KEI trades at ~55× earnings because the market has been paying up for a high-growth, up-the-value-ladder, retail-mixing compounder. That premium multiple is precisely what is most exposed if the new giants turn the profitable retail pool into a price war. Watch retail margins and EHV order flow for the first real evidence either way.

How to think about the ~55× multiple

At ~₹50,400 Cr market cap on ₹918 Cr of FY26 profit, KEI trades near 55× trailing earnings — a rich price even by the standards of India's beloved consumer-electrical compounders. That multiple only makes sense if you believe three things keep compounding: (1) the retail mix keeps climbing (fatter, steadier margins), (2) the EHV/export premium keeps growing (scarce, defensible), and (3) the Sanand capacity fills into a strong demand cycle. Bulls argue a debt-free, ~20% ROCE, structurally-growing leader with a real EHV moat deserves a scarcity premium. Bears argue a 55× stock has no margin for error — and that the Adani/UltraTech overhang is a specific, dated reason the multiple could compress. Both can be right at different points; the tell will be in the quarterly margin and volume numbers.

10

The EPC arm — deliberately kept small

A useful pull-through business KEI refuses to let get too big.

KEI also runs an EPC (engineering, procurement & construction) business — turnkey electrical projects where it designs, supplies and installs the whole electrical package (substations, cabling, transmission works). It's a legitimate business that pulls through KEI's own cable, but management is unusually clear that they want to keep it small — around 2–3% of sales.

Why cap a growing business?

  • Working capital. EPC has a long cash cycle — money is tied up in projects for months, with retention and slow payments. That's the opposite of the working-capital-light retail model KEI is prioritising.
  • Margin & risk. Project execution carries cost-overrun and completion risk, and generally thinner, lumpier margins than branded product.
  • Capital allocation. KEI would rather deploy its cash and management attention into higher-return, higher-turn retail and EHV manufacturing than into a cash-hungry contracting book.
The strategic discipline this reveals

A lot of cable companies chase EPC revenue for the top-line — and get burned when projects tie up cash and blow through margins. KEI's deliberate decision to cap EPC at a sliver and feed capital into retail and EHV instead is a tell about management quality: it's optimising for return on capital and cash generation, not headline revenue. It's the same instinct behind the whole up-the-ladder, toward-the-shelf strategy — do more of the high-margin, low-cash-cycle work, less of the low-margin, high-cash-cycle work.

The next chapter of the KEI story is less about winning bigger tenders and more about selling more branded cable off dealers' shelves and more EHV cable that nobody else can make — the high-margin, low-cash-cycle end of everything it does.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely high-quality business — priced for it to keep executing, with a new competitive cloud overhead.

KEI is one of the cleaner compounding stories in Indian capital goods: a debt-light, ~20%-ROCE, fast-growing wires-&-cables leader with a real EHV moat, an improving retail mix, and a fresh capacity runway. The investment debate is almost entirely about valuation and the new-entrant threat, not business quality.

The bull case

  • Up-the-ladder mix shift — rising retail (~54% of sales) and EHV/exports lift margins, cash conversion and multiple simultaneously.
  • Real EHV moat — one of few Indian makers qualified up to 400kV; scarce, high-margin, hard to replicate.
  • Capacity runway — the ₹2,000 Cr Sanand plant breaks the ceiling into a booming demand cycle, EHV capacity from FY27.
  • Broad, durable demand — housing, grid capex, renewables, data centres, railways, industrial capex and exports all pulling at once.
  • Clean, self-funded balance sheet — net cash, ~20% ROCE, QIP-funded growth, aligned two-generation promoters.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~55× earnings leaves little room for error; vulnerable to de-rating on any stumble.
  • Adani / UltraTech / Aditya Birla entry — deep-pocketed giants targeting the profitable retail pool; a genuine multi-year overhang on margins and multiple.
  • Commodity & execution risk — sharp copper/aluminium swings can whipsaw reported numbers; a big new plant carries ramp-up risk.
  • Still #2 on retail brand — trails Polycab/Havells on consumer brand strength, the very asset it's spending to build.
  • Cyclicality — cable demand ultimately rides the capex cycle; a slowdown in housing or grid spend would bite.
The bottom line

KEI is a structurally advantaged, high-quality wires-&-cables franchise executing a smart, coherent strategy — climbing the value ladder toward EHV, tilting toward the branded retail shelf, and adding capacity into a broad demand up-cycle, all on a clean balance sheet. The business risk is low; the valuation risk and the competitive risk are the real ones. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter: retail mix and margins (is the shift up-ladder holding, even as giants enter?), EHV and export order flow (is the moat still widening?), and Sanand utilisation (is the new capacity filling profitably?). Get those right and the compounding justifies the premium; stumble — or let Adani and UltraTech turn retail into a price war — and a 55× multiple has a long way to fall.
