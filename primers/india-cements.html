The India Cements Limited, the laggard UltraTech bought to fix

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

The India Cements Limited makes cement — the grey binding powder that, mixed with sand, stone and water, sets into the concrete of every home, road and dam. It is one of India's oldest cement companies (incorporated 1946), historically a heavyweight in South India, with roughly 14.4 million tonnes a year of capacity spread across plants in Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh and Rajasthan. For most of its modern life it was run by industrialist N. Srinivasan — better known to the wider public as a former BCCI president and the man behind the Chennai Super Kings. In 2024–25 it stopped being his: UltraTech Cement, the Aditya Birla group's cement giant, bought control and made India Cements a subsidiary.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A storied brand that quietly fell behind. While rivals invested and tightened costs, India Cements ran up debt, under-invested in efficiency, and posted margins near the bottom of the industry. In FY26 it earned only ~₹385 of EBITDA per tonne of cement — a fraction of what its new parent earns. That gap is the story.
  • UltraTech now owns and is fixing it. UltraTech assembled a stake through 2024, took majority control in December 2024, and set out to drag India Cements' operations up to its own standard — cheaper fuel, shorter freight, better clinker routing, a stronger brand and dealer network. The stock re-rated on the takeover, not on its own earnings.
  • The turnaround is real but early. FY26 was the first genuinely healthy year in a while — revenue ~₹4,480 Cr (+10%), a return to profit (net profit ~₹65 Cr after a ~₹656 Cr FY25 loss), and EBITDA/tonne rising quarter by quarter to a ~₹497 exit rate in Q4. But it still sits far below the ~₹1,000+ per tonne UltraTech expects to reach here by FY28. Everything hinges on that catch-up.
In plain terms

India Cements is a fixer-upper with a famous address. The bones are valuable — long-held limestone leases, plants sitting on top of their mines, a recognised name across the South — but the house had been let go: too much borrowing, ageing kilns burning expensive fuel, and a management whose attention wandered into cricket and shipping. UltraTech, the best operator in the business, has now bought it cheaply relative to what those plants would cost to build new, and its plan is unglamorous: run the same tonnes far more profitably. If it lifts India Cements' profit per tonne even part-way to its own, the earnings arrive almost mechanically. The wager is entirely on the operator, not on the market suddenly loving cement.

01

Cement economics 101: why geography beats brilliance

No engineering degree required — just the five numbers that govern every cement company's fate.

Cement has an awkward economic personality: it is heavy, cheap by weight, and consumed almost everywhere. A tonne sells for only a few thousand rupees, yet weighs a tonne — so hauling it any real distance quickly costs more than the product is worth. That single fact turns cement into a local business wearing a national logo: no plant, however well run, can rescue a shortage a thousand kilometres away, because the freight would swallow the price. Winning is therefore less about clever chemistry and more about owning the right plants in the right places, and running them cheaply. Five numbers carry almost the whole story.

The profit equation, and why this stock exists

Roughly: Operating profit ≈ Volume × EBITDA-per-tonne. Two companies can grind identical tonnes from identical limestone and yet one earns three times the profit — because it burns cheaper fuel, ships shorter distances, blends more waste into each tonne, and negotiates harder. That per-tonne spread is not luck; it is operating discipline, and it is exactly what India Cements lacked and UltraTech has in abundance. Hold India Cements' volume flat and simply lift its EBITDA/tonne toward its parent's, and profit multiplies without a single new customer. That arithmetic is the entire reason to look at this company.

02

Limestone to a bag: where the cost hides

Five steps — and each one is a place a well-run company saves money and a laggard leaks it.

Making cement is two jobs bolted together. The first is hot, heavy and tied to a mine: bake limestone into a hard nodule called clinker. The second is lighter and mobile: grind that clinker with additives into the fine powder sold in bags. Understanding the split explains where every rupee of cost sits — and why India Cements, an old operator with ageing kilns, leaked so much of it.

The cement chain — cost accumulates and mobility shrinks at every step

~1.5 tonnes of limestone per tonne of cement. Plants sit on the deposit.

Limestone fired at ~1,450°C. Fuel-hungry, CO₂-heavy — the costliest step.
→
Step 3 · Grinding

Clinker + slag/fly ash + gypsum milled to powder. Can sit near cities.
→
Step 4 · Product
Bagged cement
OPC / PPC / PSC grades. A ~₹380 bag — low value per kilo.
→
Step 5 · Delivery
Freight to site
Trucked ~200–400 km. Freight can eat a fifth of the price.
The trick every efficient producer uses: build the expensive kiln once, on the mine, then rail hard clinker to cheaper grinding units near demand — cutting how far the finished cement must travel. Blending industrial waste into each tonne stretches the clinker further, lowering both cost and carbon. A laggard with old kilns and long freight lanes pays more at every arrow.

The three levers that decide who profits

  • Fuel at the kiln. Firing clinker means burning coal or petcoke — often a third of the cost of a tonne. Modern kilns with waste-heat recovery, alternative fuels and better efficiency burn far less. India Cements' plants were behind here, and closing that fuel-cost gap is UltraTech's most immediate lever.
  • Blending ratio. Every tonne of clinker you replace with blast-furnace slag or fly ash is a tonne you never had to bake — cheaper and greener. Pushing more blended grades (PPC/PSC) lifts margin without touching the kiln. This is a cheap, fast win for a new owner.
  • Lead distance. The average kilometres a bag travels to reach the customer. UltraTech's dense national plant map lets it serve a customer from the nearest works, not the only one — so absorbing India Cements' plants can shorten everyone's freight, on both sides of the deal.
Why cement is a patchwork, not a market

Put freight and mines together and you get the defining truth: India is not one cement market but five stitched-together ones — North, South, East, West and Central — each with its own supply, demand and price. India Cements lives almost entirely in the South, historically the country's most oversupplied region: too many plants chasing too few buyers, so prices there have long been soft and fickle. A great operator can still make money in a weak region by being the low-cost supplier — which is precisely the game UltraTech is now playing with these assets.

03

The company, and who runs it now

From a 1946 pioneer to a founding family's fiefdom to an UltraTech subsidiary.

India Cements was incorporated in 1946 by S.N.N. Sankaralinga Iyer and T.S. Narayanaswami, part of the first generation of post-Independence Indian industry; its first plant fired up at Sankaridurg / Thalaiyuthu in Tamil Nadu around 1949. For the last few decades it was synonymous with one man — N. Srinivasan, its long-serving Vice-Chairman & Managing Director, and one of the most powerful figures in world cricket. That single-family control is central to both the company's identity and its decline.

Timeline

  • 1946
    Incorporated by S.N.N. Sankaralinga Iyer and T.S. Narayanaswami; first cement plant commissioned in Tamil Nadu around 1949 — one of independent India's earliest cement makers.
  • 1990s–2000s
    Under N. Srinivasan, grows by acquisition into the dominant South-India cement house — plants across Tamil Nadu, Andhra and Telangana, later a Rajasthan unit via subsidiary Trinetra Cement.
  • 2008
    India Cements founds and owns the Chennai Super Kings IPL franchise — a cement company running a cricket team. Srinivasan's cricket career peaks as BCCI president and later ICC chairman.
  • 2014–15
    CSK is hived off to a separate entity (CSKCL) via demerger; India Cements shareholders receive CSK shares. The cricket asset formally leaves the cement balance sheet — but the reputation for distraction lingers.
  • 2010s–early 2020s
    High debt, weak South pricing and thin efficiency leave India Cements a chronic margin laggard; it repeatedly sells assets and land to pare borrowings. Losses appear.
  • Jul–Dec 2024
    UltraTech takes control. Building on a ~22.8% holding, it agrees to buy a further 32.72% for ~₹3,954 Cr, then completes the acquisition — India Cements becomes an UltraTech subsidiary; UltraTech becomes promoter.

Ownership & control

  • Promoter — now UltraTech Cement. The Aditya Birla group company holds ~75% of India Cements and controls the board. The Srinivasan family, promoters for decades, have stepped back from control; N. Srinivasan and family retain the separate Chennai Super Kings business, which was never part of the UltraTech deal.
  • A subsidiary, not yet a merger. India Cements remains a separately listed company (ticker INDIACEM) with a ~25% public float — a parent-and-listed-subsidiary structure that raises the obvious question of whether UltraTech eventually absorbs it fully (see §10).
  • Balance sheet on the mend. Years of high leverage are being worked down; net debt is modest today but is guided to rise again toward ~₹2,000 Cr as UltraTech spends to modernise the plants — spending into the assets rather than starving them.
04

The years of drift — how a leader became a target

The bespoke heart of this company: debt, distraction, and a cement firm that owned a cricket team.

To understand why UltraTech could buy India Cements — and why it was worth buying — you have to understand how a company that was once the South's dominant cement name slid into being an acquisition target. It was not a single disaster but a slow accumulation of the wrong choices: too much debt, too little reinvestment in efficiency, and a leadership whose energy was visibly elsewhere.

Three ways the company lost its edge

  • Debt that never quite left. India Cements carried heavy borrowings for years, servicing interest that peers had shed. Debt forced it into a cycle of selling assets to survive — land, a stake here, a plant there — rather than investing to compete. A balance sheet under strain cannot afford new kilns or waste-heat plants, so its costs stayed high, which kept margins low, which kept debt hard to repay: a loop that steadily eroded its position.
  • An efficiency gap that widened every year. As UltraTech, Shree and others poured money into fuel efficiency, blending and logistics, India Cements' plants aged. The result showed up starkly in the one number that matters: its EBITDA/tonne drifted to a small fraction of the leaders'. Same limestone, same product — dramatically less profit, because the plants and the cost discipline had fallen behind.
  • The distraction problem. India Cements' promoter group ran a sprawl of non-core interests — a shipping arm, power and coal interests, and, most famously, the Chennai Super Kings. For years the same corporate house that mined limestone in Tamil Nadu also fielded an IPL team. N. Srinivasan's parallel career atop the BCCI and then the ICC made him one of cricket's most powerful men — but a cement company competing against focused, capital-hungry rivals could ill afford a management whose attention was so publicly divided.
The genuine surprise: for a stretch, the very same balance sheet that bought limestone and fired kilns in South India also owned and bankrolled the Chennai Super Kings — one of the most valuable franchises in world cricket. A listed cement maker was, in effect, an IPL owner. The cricket was eventually demerged into a separate company (CSKCL) in 2014–15, and the CSK business stayed with the Srinivasan family and outside the UltraTech deal — but the episode remains the tidiest illustration of a company whose focus had wandered far from cement.
Why "lost its way" made it buyable

Distress and quality are not the same thing. India Cements' assets stayed genuinely good — decades-old limestone leases (the scarcest resource in cement), plants sitting on their mines, and a trusted brand across the South. What had decayed was the operating of them. That combination — sound assets, poor operation, a stretched balance sheet, a promoter ready to exit — is the classic profile of a takeover target. It let UltraTech acquire a large chunk of South-India capacity for less than it would cost to build, and to buy it precisely because the previous owner had squeezed so little profit from it. The under-performance wasn't a warning sign; for the acquirer, it was the opportunity.

05

The UltraTech takeover

How India's No. 1 cement maker quietly assembled control of a Southern rival.

The change of ownership was not a single dramatic bid but a staged accumulation. UltraTech — already India's largest cement company, part of the Aditya Birla group — first became a shareholder, then converted that into control, then complied with the rules that limit how much a controlling owner may hold.

Why UltraTech wanted it

  • Instant Southern scale. The South was the one region where UltraTech was relatively light. Buying ~14 MTPA of ready capacity — with limestone reserves and a distribution presence — is faster and often cheaper than building greenfield plants that take years and face land and permitting hurdles.
  • Consolidating an oversupplied region. Taking a chronic price-cutter out of independent hands helps rationalise the South's notorious oversupply. Fewer desperate sellers can mean steadier regional pricing — good for UltraTech's entire Southern book, not just the acquired plants.
  • A margin opportunity priced in cost, not earnings. Because India Cements earned so little per tonne, UltraTech was effectively buying under-utilised profit potential. Every efficiency it imports — fuel, freight, blending, procurement scale — converts directly into EBITDA the previous owner left on the table.
The bigger board: this deal is one square in a nationwide consolidation. UltraTech (now past ~190–200 MTPA) and the Adani group's Ambuja/ACC (~110 MTPA, heading higher) have spent the last few years absorbing regional players — India Cements, Kesoram, Penna, Sanghi and others — leaving India's cement industry increasingly a two-giant contest with a shrinking fringe of independents. India Cements is both a specific turnaround and a token of that land-grab.
06

The turnaround: closing the per-tonne gap

The single idea the whole investment case turns on — and the one number that will settle it.

Strip away the history and India Cements is a bet on one measurable thing: how far, and how fast, its EBITDA per tonne can climb toward its new parent's. This is not a growth story about selling more cement; it is an efficiency story about earning more on the cement it already sells. The clearest way to see it is to line up the per-tonne profit of the players side by side.

The subtraction that is the entire thesis

Here is the arithmetic in one line. Same ~14.4 MTPA, same limestone leases, the same kilns and workers: in FY26 India Cements earned about ₹385 of EBITDA on each tonne while its new parent UltraTech earned roughly ₹1,150. Multiply that ~₹750-per-tonne gap across ~10 million tonnes of volume and you get on the order of ₹700 Cr of operating profit sitting in plain sight — not from selling more cement, but from running the existing cement to the parent's standard. UltraTech has earmarked roughly ₹1,600 Cr for efficiency upgrades here and guided to above ₹1,000/tonne by FY28. The whole case is that one subtraction, and whether UltraTech collects it.

How the gap gets closed — the unglamorous levers

  • Fuel and power. Modernising kilns, adding waste-heat recovery and cheaper alternative fuels — the biggest single cost line, and where an old operator loses the most.
  • Freight and lead distance. Serving customers from the nearest plant in UltraTech's combined Southern map, cutting the kilometres each bag travels.
  • Blending and grade mix. Pushing more blended cement (slag/fly-ash grades) to stretch each tonne of clinker.
  • Procurement and brand. Buying inputs at UltraTech's national scale, and selling under a stronger brand and dealer network that can command a touch more per bag.

The metric that settles the thesis is therefore not revenue, volume or even the share price — it is EBITDA per tonne, quarter after quarter, and how steadily it walks toward the ~₹1,000+ UltraTech has promised by FY28, alongside the utilisation of the Southern capacity. If that line keeps climbing, the case is working; if it stalls in the ₹400–600 range, the market has paid up front for an improvement that isn't arriving.

07

The footprint and the Southern squeeze

A concentrated Southern base — proven strength, but the country's most crowded region.

India Cements' ~14.4 MTPA sits overwhelmingly in South India: a cluster of integrated plants across Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, a Rajasthan unit through subsidiary Trinetra Cement, and grinding units including one in Maharashtra. That concentration is the reason UltraTech wanted it — and also the reason its earnings have been so cyclical.

The South carries roughly a third of India's cement capacity but has long suffered structural oversupply — more plants than the region's demand can comfortably absorb — which keeps utilisation and prices weaker and more volatile than the North or Central belt. For decades that hurt India Cements badly: a high-cost operator in an oversupplied region is squeezed from both ends. UltraTech's consolidation is, in part, an attempt to heal that oversupply by concentrating capacity in fewer, more disciplined hands.

Concentration cuts both ways

For a standalone India Cements, Southern concentration was a liability — all its eggs in the region most prone to price wars. Inside UltraTech it becomes an asset: it plugs the exact geographic gap in the parent's map, lets the combined group route clinker and cement more efficiently across a denser Southern network, and gives UltraTech more pricing influence in a region where discipline has been scarce. The same plants, in different hands, change from a weakness into a strategic fit.

08

The industry and the rivals

A consolidating market where India Cements now sits inside one of the two giants.

Indian cement is mid-way through a decisive shake-out. Once a fragmented field of dozens of regional players, it is consolidating hard around two national giants — UltraTech and the Adani group's Ambuja/ACC — with a handful of strong regionals below them and a thinning tail of independents. India Cements has crossed the line from independent target to part of the No. 1 giant, which reframes how you read its competitive set.

The competitive tension worth watching

Two dynamics matter here. First, India Cements no longer competes as an underdog — it fights with the balance sheet, procurement scale and brand of the market leader behind it, which is a step-change in competitive strength versus its standalone years. Second, the South is becoming a disciplined duopoly-plus: UltraTech (now including India Cements) and Adani's Ambuja/ACC increasingly set the tone on pricing, with Dalmia and Ramco as the credible independents. If that discipline holds, Southern realisations firm up and India Cements' turnaround gets a tailwind it never had as a lone price-taker. If a fresh capacity war breaks out, even a well-run India Cements feels it.

09

The South cement cycle & demand

Why the same plant can print money one year and lose it the next.

Cement demand tracks the things that get built — housing, roads, irrigation, factories, ports. In a growing, urbanising, infrastructure-hungry India, that long-run demand trend points firmly up. But the near-term ride is a cycle, and in the South it is an especially bumpy one, driven by the collision of steady demand growth with lumpy bursts of new capacity.

What drives the demand side

  • Housing — the biggest single consumer of cement, split between individual rural/semi-urban home-building and organised urban real estate. When home-building is strong, bags move.
  • Government infrastructure — roads, railways, irrigation, metros. Capital-spending pushes and state elections meaningfully swing regional demand.
  • The monsoon and seasonality — construction slows in the rains and around the harvest, so cement demand and prices sag in the wet quarters and rebound in the dry building season. Quarter-to-quarter numbers are noisy by nature.
Why the South is the cyclical extreme

Demand grows fairly steadily; capacity arrives in big lumps. When several producers commission plants in the same region at once — as the South has repeatedly seen — supply overshoots demand, utilisation drops, and everyone discounts to keep their kilns busy. Prices fall, EBITDA/tonne collapses, and even good operators bleed. Then demand catches up, the region tightens, prices snap back, and profit per tonne leaps because so much of the cost is fixed. India Cements spent years on the wrong side of that cycle — a high-cost player in the region most prone to it. The consolidation thesis is that fewer, more disciplined owners smooth the swings; the risk is that a new capacity race re-opens them.

The structural backdrop: India is the world's second-largest cement producer, and per-capita consumption remains far below the global average — the long runway bulls point to. Total industry volume rose to roughly 491 million tonnes in FY26 (up ~8.6%). The demand story is genuinely strong; the argument is only ever about timing and pricing, not whether India keeps building.

Financials & valuation

A real turnaround in the numbers — priced as an asset UltraTech will fix, not on today's earnings.

FY26 was India Cements' first convincingly healthy year in a long while, and the trajectory matters more than the level. How to read an EV/tonne valuation

When a company earns almost nothing per tonne, its P/E is meaningless — so cement is valued on enterprise value per tonne of capacity: what you pay for each tonne of grinding ability, versus what building it new would cost (greenfield replacement runs roughly $85–95, or ~₹7,500–8,500, per tonne). India Cements re-rated sharply on the UltraTech deal to roughly ₹9,500–10,500 of EV per tonne — at or above replacement cost. In plain terms, the market is already pricing these plants as if a great operator will lift their profitability, rather than on the meagre profit they make today. That is why a broker like Motilal Oswal can call the turnaround genuine and still rate the stock a Sell: the good news, in their view, is in the price before the EBITDA/tonne has actually arrived.

The minority-shareholder question: India Cements is a ~75%-owned, still-listed subsidiary of UltraTech. That structure invites two live possibilities. UltraTech could eventually merge India Cements into itself (a share-swap that would end the separate INDIACEM listing), or run it as a listed subsidiary indefinitely. A merger's terms — the swap ratio — would decide how much of the turnaround value accrues to India Cements' minority holders versus UltraTech's. It is the key governance overhang on the stock, and worth watching for any related-party or scheme-of-arrangement announcement.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

Good assets, the best operator in the business, and a price that already believes it.

India Cements is a rare thing on the exchange: a former leader being rebuilt by the industry's benchmark operator. The business question is largely settled — UltraTech will make these plants more profitable. The investor question is narrower and harder: how much of that improvement has the share price already banked, and how cleanly will minorities share in it?

The bull case

  • Best-in-class operator now in charge — UltraTech's cost discipline, procurement scale, brand and logistics applied to under-managed assets.
  • A huge, visible per-tonne gap to close — from ~₹385 toward ₹1,000+, most of it dropping straight to profit on existing volume.
  • Scarce, valuable assets — decades-old limestone leases and mine-adjacent plants that can't be replicated cheaply.
  • Southern consolidation tailwind — fewer independents could mean steadier regional pricing after years of price wars.
  • Merger optionality — eventual absorption into UltraTech could crystallise value for minority holders on a fair swap.

The bear case

  • Priced ahead of delivery — the stock re-rated to at/above replacement value on the takeover, before EBITDA/tonne actually reached parent levels; brokers see limited upside (Motilal Oswal: Sell, ₹350).
  • Turnaround is early and multi-year — fixing years of under-investment takes time and capex; margins still lag badly today.
  • South-India cyclicality — the region's oversupply and soft pricing can blunt even a well-run operator.
  • Minority-shareholder dynamics — a future merger's swap ratio, or a lasting subsidiary structure, determines who captures the gains.
  • Legacy overhangs — residual debt, land and asset-sale history, and integration risk as systems are folded into UltraTech.
The bottom line

India Cements is no longer a story about cricket, debt or a founding family — it is a controlled experiment in whether the country's best cement operator can drag a chronic laggard's plants up to its own standard. The evidence so far is encouraging: a first profit in years, and an EBITDA/tonne that climbed from ~₹333 to ~₹497 across FY26. But the market has already paid, in EV-per-tonne, for a destination the company has not yet reached. The one line to follow is unambiguous — EBITDA per tonne, every quarter, on its walk toward the ~₹1,000+ UltraTech has promised for FY28. Reach that mark and the plants finally earn what their limestone always could; the next real signpost is the day UltraTech decides whether INDIACEM keeps trading on its own — or disappears into the parent that fixed it.
