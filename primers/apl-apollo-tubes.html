APL Apollo Tubes, the steel-tube brand-builder

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

APL Apollo Tubes is India's largest producer of structural steel tubes — the square, rectangular and round hollow steel sections that hold up sheds, warehouses, factories, high-rises, airports, furniture and greenhouses. It buys flat steel (hot-rolled coil), forms it into tubes, and sells them under the Apollo brand through a vast distributor network. With roughly 50% of the country's structural-tube market (and ~55–65% of the organised segment), it is the runaway category leader — big enough that when analysts talk about "steel tubes in India," they largely mean APL Apollo.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a processor, not a steelmaker. APL Apollo doesn't make steel — it converts someone else's steel coil into tubes and earns a "conversion spread" (a processing margin) on each tonne. So the number that matters isn't revenue or the steel price; it's EBITDA per tonne — which reached ₹5,161 in FY26, up 36%, on 3.49 million tonnes of volume. That is the North-Star metric for the whole business.
  • A record FY26 driven by mix, not the market. Revenue grew ~11.5% to ₹23,079 Cr, but profit jumped ~59% to ₹1,203 Cr and EBITDA rose ~48% to ₹1,914 Cr — because a richer product mix and a January-2025 price hike lifted the spread per tonne far faster than volumes grew. The value-added-product (VAP) mix ran ~55% by Q4. The business earned ~31–37% return on capital and sits on net cash (~₹1,500 Cr+), with borrowings of just ~₹498 Cr.
  • The story is volume growth + premiumisation. Installed capacity is ~4.3–4.5 MTPA across 11 plants (plus Dubai); management targets ~8 MTPA by FY28, funded from internal cash. FY27 guidance is 15–20% volume growth and 25–30% PAT growth. The market pays ~41× earnings for a branded-building-material compounder — a rich multiple for what is, underneath, a steel processor.
In plain terms

Think of APL Apollo as the "Asian Paints of steel tubes" — it took a plain industrial commodity and turned it into a branded consumer-facing building material. It buys steel sheet, bends and welds it into hollow tubes of every size, stamps the Apollo name on it, and sells it through thousands of dealers to fabricators and builders — the same way a paint company sells through hardware shops. Crucially, it doesn't bet on the steel price going up (that risk it passes through); it earns a fixed-ish fee for the conversion — the value it adds by shaping, coating and branding the tube. The whole investment case is: can it keep selling more tonnes, and keep making more money per tonne by pushing customers up to fancier, higher-margin tubes — while its factories run cheaper than anyone else's?

01

Hollow sections 101: what APL Apollo actually sells

No engineering degree needed — just what a steel tube is, and why builders want it.

A "structural steel tube" or hollow section is exactly what it sounds like: a length of steel formed into a hollow tube — square, rectangular or round — and used as a load-bearing structural member. It is the modern replacement for the solid steel angles, box-plates, timber, and reinforced concrete that traditionally hold up a building or shed. If you have ever seen the steel skeleton of a warehouse, a metro station roof, a factory shed, a rooftop solar mount, a scaffolding frame, or a steel-frame chair — you have seen APL Apollo's product category.

The vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (in plain English)
Hollow sectionA hollow steel tube used structurally. Comes as SHS (square), RHS (rectangular) or CHS (circular). This is APL Apollo's core product.
ERW tube"Electric Resistance Welded" — the manufacturing method: flat coil is rolled into a tube shape and the seam fused by electric welding. The standard way structural tubes are made.
HR coil (HRC)Hot-rolled steel coil — the flat-steel raw material APL Apollo buys from big steelmakers (JSW, Tata, SAIL, AM/NS) and turns into tubes.
GP / GI / colour-coatedGalvanised (zinc-coated) or polymer/colour-coated tubes — higher-value products that resist rust and look finished, used in fencing, roofing, furniture and façades.
EBITDA / tonneOperating profit divided by tonnes sold — the processor's key margin metric. APL Apollo's FY26: ₹5,161/tonne.
Why a hollow tube beats a solid bar

Here's the engineering intuition that drives the whole "steel replaces concrete" theme. For the same weight of steel, a hollow section is far stiffer and stronger than a solid rod — because strength against bending and twisting depends on how far the material sits from the centre, and a tube pushes all its metal out to the edges. That gives a hollow section a superior strength-to-weight ratio and excellent torsional rigidity. Practically, it means you can build the same structure with less steel, faster, and lighter than with old-style box-plates, angles or concrete — which is exactly why architects and builders keep switching to tubes. APL Apollo's job is to make every size of that tube, cheaply and consistently, at national scale.

Where the tubes go — the end-markets

  • Construction & real estate — the structural frames of buildings, homes, high-rises and interiors. In FY26, housing was ~64% of application volume.
  • Commercial & infrastructure — warehouses, factory sheds, malls, hotels, hospitals, airports, metro/railway structures, data centres. Commercial buildings ~23%, infrastructure ~13% of the FY26 mix.
  • Everyday & industrial uses — steel furniture, fencing and railings, greenhouses and agri-structures, solar-panel mounting, scaffolding, cold storage, and general fabrication.
02

The conversion-spread model: how a processor really earns

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — read this once and APL Apollo clicks.

The biggest mistake a first-time reader makes is treating APL Apollo like a steel company whose fortunes rise and fall with the steel price. It isn't. APL Apollo is a converter: it buys hot-rolled coil, processes it into a finished tube, and sells the tube for a fixed markup — the "conversion spread". If steel coil costs ₹X, the tube sells for roughly ₹X plus a processing charge. That charge — not the steel price — is where its profit lives.

The steel-coil cost flows straight through to the tube price; APL Apollo keeps the spread it adds by forming, coating and branding. The richer the product, the wider the spread — that's the entire game.
Why "spread per tonne" is everything — and steel-price cyclicality is a distraction

Because the coil cost is (mostly) passed through, a rising steel price inflates revenue without adding much profit, and a falling steel price shrinks revenue without destroying it. That's why you cannot judge APL Apollo by its top line. The honest scorecard is tonnes sold × EBITDA per tonne. Grow either lever and profit grows; grow both and profit compounds. In FY26 both moved the right way: volume +11% to 3.49 MT and EBITDA/tonne +36% to ₹5,161 — which is exactly why profit (+59%) ran so far ahead of revenue (+11.5%).

The one real steel-price risk: the pass-through isn't instant or perfect. When coil prices fall sharply and suddenly, APL Apollo can be left holding higher-cost inventory it must sell at lower tube prices — a temporary inventory loss that dents a quarter's spread. Conversely, a steady rising market lets it earn small inventory gains. So the spread is structurally stable but tactically wobbly — smooth over a year, noisy quarter-to-quarter. This is the single biggest source of near-term earnings volatility, and the thing to watch when steel prices swing.
03

The company and who controls it

From a small tube trader to a ₹49,000-crore branded building-material leader — under the Gupta family.

APL Apollo is a founder-promoter-led business built by the Sanjay Gupta family. What began decades ago as a modest steel-tube operation was transformed, plant by plant and acquisition by acquisition, into India's dominant structural-tube brand — and, unusually for a metals-adjacent business, into one valued like a consumer compounder.

Timeline

  • 1980s–90s
    The Gupta family enters steel-tube manufacturing/trading — the seed of what becomes APL Apollo Tubes.
  • 2010s
    Scales aggressively via greenfield plants and acquisitions (including Shri Lakshmi Metal Udyog and Apollo/Best in the galvanised and coated space), building a pan-India footprint and a broad product range.
  • 2016–17
    Commissions India's first-ever Direct Forming Technology (DFT) lines — imported Italian tube-mill technology — at Hosur and a new Raipur greenfield. The technological moat that reshapes the cost curve.
  • 2019–22
    Absorbs Apollo Tricoat (polymer-coated premium tubes) into the group; deepens the value-added product ladder; the "Apollo" brand becomes a category standard.
  • 2022–24
    Commissions the flagship Raipur mega-complex (~1.2 MTPA of DFT capacity); pushes toward ~4–5 MTPA installed capacity; opens a Dubai plant for the Middle East.
  • FY26
    Record year — 3.49 MT volume, ₹23,079 Cr revenue, ₹1,203 Cr PAT (+59%), EBITDA/tonne ₹5,161; net cash; ₹8.5/share dividend; reiterates the ~8 MTPA-by-FY28 capacity roadmap.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Sanjay Gupta family (Sanjay Gupta is Chairman), alongside related family members. Promoter holding is around ~28% — relatively modest, the residue of years of growth funding and periodic stake sales, which means the float is large and institutionally owned.
  • Institutional base: heavy institutional ownership — FIIs ~37% and DIIs ~16% as of recent filings — reflecting the market's treatment of APL Apollo as a high-quality, well-governed compounder rather than a commodity metals stock.
  • Balance sheet: net cash — borrowings of only ~₹498 Cr against far larger cash and strong operating cash flow (~₹2,000 Cr in FY26). The ~8 MTPA expansion is being funded almost entirely from internal accruals, not debt.
A note on the modest promoter stake: at ~28%, the Gupta family's holding is lower than many Indian promoter groups. This cuts both ways — it signals a genuinely public, institution-governed company with a large tradable float, but it also means the family's skin-in-the-game is smaller, and the stock is more exposed to institutional sentiment swings than a tightly-held peer would be.
04

From commodity to brand: the pricing-power story

The defining achievement — turning a boring steel product into a branded building material.

Steel tubes are, on paper, a commodity: any competent mill can bend coil into a pipe, and a fabricator could in principle buy from anyone. APL Apollo's central accomplishment — the thing that justifies its premium valuation — is that it escaped commodity economics. It built the "Apollo" name into something a builder, architect and dealer actively ask for, which lets it charge a small but durable premium and, crucially, set the spread rather than merely accept whatever the market gives. This is the same playbook that turned cement, paint, plywood and pipes into branded businesses in India.

How you brand a steel tube — the four levers

  • Scale & availability. With ~50% market share, 11+ plants and ~800 distributors reaching ~2.5 lakh retailers and fabricators across 300+ towns, APL Apollo is the one supplier that can deliver any size, anywhere, on time, in bulk. For a builder on a deadline, reliability is a premium feature.
  • The widest catalogue. 2,000+ products across four brand families means a dealer can source their whole tube requirement from one name — the "one-stop-shop" that pulls demand toward the leader.
  • Consistent, certified quality. A branded, standards-certified tube de-risks the buyer (a structural failure is catastrophic), so professionals pay up for the trusted name over an unbranded local mill.
  • Marketing & pull demand. APL Apollo advertises to end-users, architects and influencers (it has used high-profile brand ambassadors) — genuinely unusual for a steel product, and it manufactures "pull" so dealers must stock Apollo.
Why the brand shows up in the numbers — the January-2025 price hike

The clearest proof that this is real pricing power, not marketing spin: in January 2025 APL Apollo pushed through a general-segment price increase of roughly ₹1,500/tonne — and it stuck. General-category EBITDA/tonne moved from around ₹2,000 to ₹3,500+, and blended EBITDA/tonne climbed to ₹5,161 for the year. A pure commodity can't raise price and hold it; a category-leading brand with limited credible substitutes can. That is the entire difference between valuing APL Apollo at a steel-stock multiple (~10×) and a branded-consumer multiple (~40×) — and the market has clearly chosen the latter.

The whole thesis in one line: APL Apollo doesn't sell steel — it sells Apollo, and gets paid a brand-and-conversion fee on top of the steel.
05

The DFT moat: why one mill can make everything

The proprietary technology that lets APL Apollo out-cost and out-flex every rival.

The deepest part of APL Apollo's edge is a manufacturing technology called Direct Forming Technology (DFT) — imported Italian tube-mill technology that APL Apollo was the first to bring to India (2016–17) and deploys more extensively than anyone else. To see why it matters, you have to understand the pain it removes.

The old way vs the DFT way

In a conventional ERW tube mill, each tube size needs its own set of forming rolls. To switch from making, say, a 50×50 mm square tube to a 100×100 mm one, you must stop the mill, physically swap out and realign all the rolls, and restart — an expensive, hours-long changeover. So conventional mills run long batches of one size, hold big inventories, and struggle to serve many sizes flexibly.

A DFT mill removes the roll change entirely. One DFT line can produce any size within its range without swapping rolls — square sections from 12×12 mm up to 300×300 mm, rectangles up to 400×200 mm, and rounds across a wide band. That single capability cascades into a chain of advantages:

Why DFT is a genuine moat, not just a nice machine

Three compounding effects. (1) Lower cost: DFT saves an estimated 3–7% of steel per tonne (and up to ~2–10% on some specifications) — and in a thin-spread conversion business, a few percent off the biggest cost line is enormous operating leverage. (2) Flexibility = service: because it can make any size on demand without stopping, APL Apollo can promise "any size, fast," carry the widest range, and turn inventory faster — a service advantage rivals can't match. (3) It enabled the big sizes: DFT is what let APL Apollo produce the heavy, large structural sections that actually replace concrete columns and box-plates in buildings — the highest-value, highest-spread part of the range. A competitor can buy a DFT line too, but replicating APL Apollo's scale of DFT deployment, cost curve and product breadth is a years-and-crores undertaking.

Reality check: DFT is licensed technology (from Italy's Olimpia/Fimi lineage), not a patent APL Apollo owns exclusively — rivals can, in principle, acquire similar mills. APL Apollo's real edge is being first, biggest and most integrated in deploying it, married to the brand and distribution. The moat is the combination, not the machine alone.
06

The product ladder and the VAP mix

The premiumisation engine — how APL Apollo lifts EBITDA/tonne by moving customers up the ladder.

If volume growth is one profit lever, mix is the other — and it's the higher-quality one. APL Apollo groups its 2,000+ products into brand families that climb a ladder from plain black tubes to premium coated and finished products. The strategic goal is to keep raising the share of value-added products (VAP) — the fancier, higher-spread tubes — because each rung up the ladder earns a fatter EBITDA/tonne on the same tonnage.

The four brand families

  • Apollo Structural — plain black square, rectangular and circular hollow sections, including the heavy large-diameter DFT sections that replace concrete and box-plates. The volume backbone.
  • Apollo Z — galvanised and colour-coated tubes that resist corrosion, for fencing, roofing, solar structures and outdoor use. A step up in spread.
  • Apollo Tricoat — three-layer polymer-coated tubes, mainly for residential and interior applications (railings, furniture, gates) where finish matters. A premium, higher-margin line.
  • Apollo Building Products — finished building components: door frames, ceiling and light-gauge sections, decorative tubes — the most value-added, closest-to-consumer end.
Why VAP mix is the number to track

By Q4 FY26 the value-added-product mix reached ~55% of volume, and management is pushing it higher (including a small but fast-growing "SG Premium" range at ~8–9% of volumes). The math is simple and powerful: a plain structural tube might earn a few thousand rupees of EBITDA per tonne, while a coated or finished product earns materially more. So even with flat total volume, shifting the mix toward VAP raises blended EBITDA/tonne — profit growth without needing new furnaces. This is why APL Apollo can credibly target 25–30% profit growth on 15–20% volume growth: the extra ~10 points come from mix and price, not tonnes. The risk is symmetric — if premiumisation stalls or competition commoditises the coated segment, the spread expansion that justifies the multiple slows.

07

The volume engine: Raipur, capacity & the road to 8 MTPA

The second profit lever — doubling tonnes through a giant, low-cost, DFT-driven expansion.

Alongside premiumisation, APL Apollo's other engine is raw volume growth — and here the centrepiece is capacity. The company operates ~4.3–4.5 MTPA of installed capacity across 11 plants (Sikandrabad, Malur, Hosur, Dujana, Raipur ×2, Murbad, Hyderabad and others), plus a Dubai unit for the Middle East. Its stated roadmap is to reach ~8 MTPA by FY28 — effectively doubling from a few years ago — with the flagship being the massive Raipur greenfield complex.

Why Raipur is the flagship

  • Scale. The Raipur complex (across ~190 acres) is APL Apollo's largest single site, with ~1.2 MTPA of saleable capacity — a mega-plant built around DFT lines so it can flex across sizes and skew toward heavy, high-value structural sections.
  • Location logic. Chhattisgarh sits in India's steel belt, close to HR-coil supply and central for freight — lowering the delivered cost of both input steel and finished tubes to key markets.
  • Product upgrade. Raipur is geared to the large-diameter, heavy structural tubes that directly substitute for concrete and box-plate construction — the highest-spread, most differentiated part of the range (see §08).
Why the expansion is (relatively) low-risk — and where the risk sits

Two things make this a more comfortable expansion than a typical heavy-industry capex cycle. First, it's self-funded: the remaining ~1 MT of additions cost roughly ₹1,400–1,500 Cr over ~2–2.5 years — small against ₹2,000 Cr of annual operating cash flow — so APL Apollo can grow without leveraging up or diluting. Second, demand-led: as the category leader in a structurally growing market, APL Apollo is filling capacity it can reasonably expect to sell, not building on speculation. The risk is execution and absorption: new capacity that ramps slower than planned drags utilisation and, briefly, EBITDA/tonne (fixed costs spread over fewer tonnes) — the tube-industry version of a hospital's "occupancy J-curve." Domestic plants ran ~80–85% utilisation in FY26; the Dubai unit lagged at ~40% amid Middle-East disruption, a reminder that not every expansion fills on schedule.

08

Steel-tube construction: the structural demand shift

The multi-decade tailwind unique to this business — steel tubes eating concrete's lunch.

Every building-material company cares about construction demand. But APL Apollo rides an extra, structural tailwind on top of the cyclical one: the slow migration of Indian construction away from reinforced concrete (RCC), red brick and old-style steel box-plates, and toward tubular steel structures. This "steelification" of construction is the demand-side thesis that, if it plays out, keeps APL Apollo's volumes growing faster than GDP or steel for years.

Why tubular construction is winning

  • Speed. A steel-tube frame is fabricated and bolted together far faster than concrete is cast and cured. In a country obsessed with project timelines (warehouses, factories, data centres, airports), time is money, and steel wins.
  • Strength-to-weight & less material. As §01 explained, hollow sections carry more load per kilo, so a tubular structure uses less material and lighter foundations than concrete for the same job.
  • Clean, dry, precise, reusable. Steel construction is less labour-intensive, less weather-dependent, produces less on-site waste, and is recyclable — increasingly valued as India's construction professionalises and "green building" matters more.
  • Enabled by APL Apollo's own products. The heavy, large-diameter DFT sections APL Apollo now makes are precisely what allow tubes to replace load-bearing concrete columns and steel box-plates — not just light frames. The company is, in effect, expanding its own addressable market by making the products that unlock new uses.
Why this is a bigger deal than it sounds

India's steel intensity of construction is low by global standards — the country uses far less structural steel per building than developed economies, leaning heavily on concrete and brick. As India urbanises, industrialises (warehousing, manufacturing, "China+1" factories), and builds infrastructure at scale, that gap is a multi-decade convergence opportunity. If even a slice of concrete/brick construction shifts to tubes, the structural-tube market grows much faster than the overall steel or construction market. Industry estimates put the organised structural-tube market on a path from roughly 9 MT today toward ~17 MT by FY30 — and as the ~50% leader with the widest product range and lowest cost, APL Apollo is the single biggest beneficiary. This is the difference between APL Apollo being a cyclical steel-processor and being a secular growth compounder: the category itself is expanding, not just its share of a static pie.

How to read the tailwind honestly: "steel replaces concrete" is real but gradual — it's a change in building practice, codes, contractor habits and relative costs, not a switch that flips. It gives APL Apollo a durable volume tailwind, but it won't rescue a bad steel-price quarter or a demand downturn. Treat it as the structural reason to expect above-GDP volume growth over a decade, not a guarantee for any single year.
09

The industry and the rivals

A fragmented market consolidating around one dominant brand.

India's steel-tube market is large and historically fragmented — thousands of small, regional, unbranded ERW mills serving local fabricators. The structural shift is toward the organised, branded segment, where scale, product range, cost and distribution win — and APL Apollo dominates that shift with roughly 50% of the overall structural-tube market and ~55–65% of the organised segment. No rival is close on the structural-tube category specifically.

The competitive tension to watch: the backward-integration threat

Here's the subtle risk in APL Apollo's model. It buys its steel from the big integrated steelmakers — Tata, JSW, AM/NS, SAIL — and then earns a spread converting it. Those same steel majors can (and do) look at APL Apollo's fat conversion margin and think: why not make the tubes ourselves? Tata already has Tata Structura; JSW is pushing downstream. If a steel major with captive coil decided to compete aggressively in branded structural tubes, it could pressure spreads. APL Apollo's defence is the combination that's hard to copy fast: DFT-driven low cost, the widest catalogue, a deep distribution moat, and a brand fabricators already ask for by name. So far that combination has kept the leader's spread intact — but the backward-integration threat is the permanent competitive question hanging over the multiple.

The upside of fragmentation: the flip side of that threat is a long runway of consolidation. A huge slice of the market is still unorganised local mills. As GST, quality norms, project-scale demands and branding push buyers toward organised suppliers, the category leader captures share almost mechanically — the "formalisation" tailwind familiar from Indian cement, paints and pipes. APL Apollo has been the prime consolidator of this fragmented pool for a decade.

Financials & valuation

Profit racing ahead of revenue — and a premium multiple to defend.

How to think about the ~41× multiple

At ~₹49,600 Cr market cap on ~₹1,203 Cr of FY26 profit, APL Apollo trades around 41× trailing earnings — and on ~9× book. That is a branded-consumer multiple attached to a business that, at its core, converts steel coil into tubes. It only makes sense if you believe three things compound together: (1) volumes keep growing double-digit as capacity ramps toward 8 MTPA and steel-tube construction spreads; (2) EBITDA/tonne holds or expands as the VAP mix richens and the brand's pricing power persists; and (3) the net-cash, high-ROCE, self-funding profile continues. Stack those and you get the 25–30% profit CAGR the multiple is paying for. Bears counter that the multiple leaves no room for error: a sharp steel-price swing (inventory loss), a demand slowdown, a slower ramp, or a serious backward-integration attack by a steel major, and a ~41× stock can de-rate hard toward the ~15–20× that commodity-processors usually command. Both can be right at different points in the cycle — which is why EBITDA/tonne and volume are the two numbers to track every quarter.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

Where the story goes — and what could break it.

APL Apollo's forward story is a clean two-lever compounding machine: more tonnes (capacity toward 8 MTPA, steel-tube construction spreading) × more profit per tonne (VAP premiumisation, brand pricing power), on a net-cash, high-ROCE base. Management's FY27 guidance — 15–20% volume growth, 20–25% EBITDA growth, 25–30% PAT growth, with EBITDA/tonne held at ₹5,000–5,500 — is essentially that machine, quantified. The question for an investor isn't whether the business is excellent (it plainly is); it's whether the ~41× multiple already prices the excellence in.

Bull case

  • Category leader in a secularly growing market. ~50% share of a structural-tube market projected to roughly double toward ~17 MT by FY30 as steel replaces concrete/brick — share × market both grow.
  • Two independent profit levers. Volume (8 MTPA roadmap) and spread (VAP mix, brand pricing) can each drive growth, and together deliver profit compounding well above revenue.
  • Real, proven pricing power. The January-2025 ₹1,500/tonne hike that stuck shows this is a brand, not a commodity — the basis for the premium multiple.
  • DFT cost moat + distribution. 3–7% steel saving per tonne, widest catalogue, ~800 distributors — a low-cost, hard-to-replicate combination.
  • Pristine, self-funding balance sheet. Net cash, ~31–37% ROCE, expansion funded from internal cash — growth without dilution or leverage. Surplus cash may flow to higher dividends/buybacks.

Bear case

  • Valuation leaves no margin of safety. ~41× earnings / ~9× book for a steel processor; any stumble in volume or spread invites a sharp de-rating toward commodity multiples.
  • Steel-price / inventory volatility. A sudden coil-price fall can inflict inventory losses and dent a quarter's spread — the pass-through is smooth over a year but noisy within it.
  • Backward-integration threat. Steel majors with captive coil (Tata Structura, JSW) could attack the fat conversion spread that APL Apollo enjoys.
  • Execution / absorption risk. The 8 MTPA build must be filled; a demand slowdown or slow ramp (see Dubai at ~40% utilisation) pressures utilisation and EBITDA/tonne.
  • Modest promoter stake (~28%). Lower family skin-in-the-game and a large institutional float make the stock more sentiment-sensitive.
The bottom line

APL Apollo is a genuinely rare thing: a commodity business that escaped commodity economics — a steel processor that turned itself into a branded building material with pricing power, a cost moat, and a secular demand tailwind behind it. The business quality is not really in dispute; the returns on capital, the net-cash sheet, and the record of turning boring tubes into ₹5,161-a-tonne spreads speak for themselves. What's in dispute is the price: at ~41× earnings the market is paying for years of 25–30% profit compounding to arrive on schedule. The next chapters will be written in two numbers watched every quarter — volume growth (does the 8 MTPA capacity fill and steel-tube construction keep spreading?) and EBITDA per tonne (does premiumisation and brand pricing hold the spread near ₹5,000–5,500?). Get both right and the compounding justifies the multiple; wobble on either — a soft steel cycle, a slow ramp, a steel-major counter-attack — and a premium stock has a long way to be re-marked.
