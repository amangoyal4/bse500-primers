Va Tech Wabag, the company that sells clean water to a thirsty world

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Va Tech Wabag builds water treatment plants — the industrial facilities that make dirty or salty water safe to use, and make used water safe to return or reuse. It desalinates seawater into drinking water, cleans a city's sewage, treats a factory's chemical effluent, and increasingly recycles wastewater back into fresh supply. It is India's largest listed pure-play water company, but it is not really a construction firm — it is a technology and engineering house. It owns the process designs, the know-how and the patents; it subcontracts most of the brute civil work. And it operates on two clocks: a lumpy project (EPC) business that builds the plant, and a long, steady O&M business that runs it for years afterward.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The order book is enormous relative to sales. Wabag ended FY26 with a record ~₹17,200 Cr order book against revenue of only ~₹3,944 Cr — more than four years of work already signed. Roughly 62% of that backlog is build-the-plant EPC and ~38% is run-the-plant O&M, and around half of it sits overseas. Demand is not the constraint; converting a huge backlog into billed, collected revenue is.
  • It is asset-light and net-cash. Because Wabag sells engineering rather than owning the plants, it needs relatively little fixed capital. It finished FY26 with roughly ₹950 Cr of net cash — its sixth consecutive net-cash year — and is all but debt-free (borrowings ~₹228 Cr against ~₹412/share of book value). That balance sheet is what lets it bid for giant overseas jobs.
  • Profit is recovering, but its history warns you. FY26 net profit rose ~25% to ~₹370 Cr on ~12% operating margins, and returns are respectable (RoCE ~19–21%, RoE ~16%). Yet just three years earlier, in FY23, profit collapsed 92% to ~₹11 Cr — not because it lost business, but because it had to write off ~₹289 Cr of unpaid bills from two customers. That single event is the whole risk of this business in one number.
In plain terms

Va Tech Wabag is best read as a pure-play bet on treated water. It owns the recipe for turning seawater, sewage and effluent into usable water, and it makes money in two ways from every plant: a one-time engineering-and-construction fee to build it, then a small annual fee — for a decade or two — to operate it. The build business is bumpy and swallows working capital; the operating business is quiet, recurring and sticky. The company's value is essentially the order book, multiplied by the margin it can execute at, plus the growing annuity of plants it already runs — with one permanent caveat: a water plant is only as good as the customer who pays for it, and Wabag has been burned when a client stopped paying.

01

Water treatment 101: why anyone pays to clean water

No chemistry needed — just why a city or a factory hands over money for a plant.

Water looks free, but usable water is not. Rivers are polluted, groundwater is falling, seawater is salty, and a city's used water is a health hazard. Turning any of these into something you can drink, farm with, or run through a boiler takes an engineered plant — pumps, membranes, chemistry, biology and controls. Va Tech Wabag builds those plants. To follow the business you only need to know the four kinds of job it does and why each one gets funded.

The four jobs Wabag gets paid for
Type of plantWhat it does (and why the customer pays)
Drinking waterTakes river or reservoir water and makes it safe to drink for a city. Paid for by municipalities and state water boards that have a legal duty to supply clean water to a growing population.
Sewage & wastewaterCleans the used water a city or factory discharges so it doesn't poison rivers. Driven by pollution law — regulators increasingly forbid dumping untreated effluent, so treatment becomes compulsory, not optional.
DesalinationPushes seawater through membranes at high pressure to strip out the salt, producing fresh water where there is no river. The go-to answer in coastal, arid places — the Middle East, Chennai, North Africa.
Reuse / recycleTreats wastewater to a high enough standard that it can be used again — for industry, irrigation, even topping up drinking supply. The fastest-growing category, because reusing water is cheaper and greener than finding new water.
The one idea that explains the whole industry: water is local and non-substitutable

Unlike oil or coal, you cannot ship water across the world economically, and nothing else does its job — a city with no water cannot swap in something cheaper. So when a place runs short, it has only three options: find new sources (desalinate the sea), stop wasting what it has (treat and reuse), or clean up what it has polluted (sewage treatment). All three are Va Tech Wabag's business. That is why water treatment is one of the rare industrial markets with a demand floor set by physics and law rather than by the economic cycle: population keeps rising, rivers keep getting dirtier, regulators keep tightening — and every one of those trends turns into a plant that somebody has to design and build.

Who buys a water plant

  • Municipalities & water boards — cities and state utilities (Delhi Jal Board, Chennai, Bengaluru) building drinking-water and sewage capacity. The traditional, high-volume buyer, often funded by government or multilateral money.
  • Governments in water-scarce countries — Gulf states, North Africa and others commissioning giant desalination plants because they have coastline but no rivers. Big-ticket, hard-currency contracts.
  • Industry — refineries, power plants, steel and petrochemical complexes (BPCL-type buyers) that need vast amounts of process water and are legally required to treat their effluent. A steady, margin-rich customer class.
02

What Wabag actually sells: recipes, not concrete

The asset-light model — read this once and the whole company makes sense.

The key to understanding Wabag is what it doesn't do. It does not, as a rule, own the water plants or pour most of the concrete itself. It owns the process technology — how to arrange the membranes, the biology and the chemistry so a specific dirty input becomes a specific clean output — and it does the engineering and project management that stitches a plant together. The heavy civil construction is largely subcontracted. That is what "asset-light technology play" means in practice: Wabag sells brains and coordination, and hires muscle. Its revenue splits into two very different engines.

The two revenue engines, decoded

  • EPC (Engineering, Procurement & Construction) — the bulk of revenue and about 62% of the order book. Wabag signs a fixed-price contract to deliver a working plant, buys the specialised equipment, subcontracts the civil build, and is paid in stages as the project progresses. This is where the money and the risk both concentrate: win it well and it prints margin; misjudge costs or hit a delay and a fixed price becomes a trap.
  • O&M (Operations & Maintenance) — the smaller but higher-quality ~38% of the order book. Once a plant is built, the customer often pays Wabag an annual fee to run it — keep the output within spec, service the membranes, guarantee uptime. It is modest per plant, but it recurs for many years and grows every time a new plant is commissioned. This is the ballast under the lumpy EPC.

Wabag executes these under a menu of contract structures, and the jargon matters because it changes who owns the plant and who carries the risk. Pure EPC means Wabag just builds and hands over. O&M means it only operates. DBO (Design-Build-Operate) bundles both — Wabag builds it and runs it, the model it likes best because it captures the annuity. BOOT and HAM (Hybrid Annuity Model) go further, asking the contractor to help finance the plant and get paid back over time — higher return but heavier on capital, which an asset-light company treads carefully around.

Why owning the recipe beats owning the concrete

An asset-heavy water company would build plants, own them, and collect water tariffs for thirty years — lucrative, but it ties up enormous capital and loads the balance sheet with debt. Wabag's choice is the opposite: keep the valuable, hard-to-copy part (the process IP and engineering) in-house, and push the low-margin, capital-heavy civil construction out to subcontractors. The pay-off is a company that can run net-cash while booking a ₹17,000 Cr order book — it isn't funding the concrete, so its capital goes into engineering and bid guarantees, not into owning assets. The trade-off is that its margin lives or dies on engineering judgement: price a fixed contract wrong, or let a subcontractor slip, and there's no plant-ownership income to cushion the miss.

03

The company, its heritage, and who controls it

An Austrian-rooted, professionally-run business — with an unusual ownership twist.

Va Tech Wabag's name is a fossil of its history. "Wabag" comes from the German water-technology business (Wasser- und Abwassertechnik); "VA Tech" from the Austrian engineering group that later owned it. The Indian company that trades today is the survivor of a remarkable role-reversal: the offshoot bought its own parent.

Timeline

  • 1995–2000
    The Wabag water business, with roots in Germany, passes to Austria's VA Technologie AG; the Indian operation becomes a subsidiary and is renamed VA Tech Wabag in 2000.
  • 2005
    Management buyout. A team led by Rajiv Mittal, backed by ICICI Venture, buys the business out of Siemens' hands — turning employees into owners of an independent Indian water company.
  • 2007
    The reverse takeover. The Indian company acquires its erstwhile Austrian parent, VA Tech Wabag GmbH, for roughly $100 million via a Singapore subsidiary — gaining control of the global operations, technology and patent portfolio it had once been a branch of.
  • 2010
    IPO on NSE & BSE; Wabag becomes a listed Indian water pure-play with a genuinely international footprint.
  • FY15
    Executes a large power-sector water project (APGENCO, in consortium) — a job whose unpaid dues would come back to bite years later.
  • FY23
    Profit collapses to ~₹11 Cr as the company writes off ~₹289 Cr of long-stuck receivables (Tecpro, in insolvency, and the delayed APGENCO dues) — a painful balance-sheet clean-up.
  • FY25–FY26
    Order book hits a record ~₹17,200 Cr; wins the 300 MLD Yanbu desalination mega-project in Saudi Arabia and enters the UAE; PAT recovers to ~₹370 Cr; sixth straight net-cash year.

Ownership & management

  • A professionally-managed company, not a family empire. Wabag is led by Rajiv Mittal (Chairman & Managing Director / Group CEO), one of the architects of the 2005 buyout. Unlike most Indian mid-caps, promoter ownership is relatively low — a minority stake rather than a controlling 50–75% block — with the bulk of the company held by institutions and public shareholders. (Treat the exact promoter percentage as held — confirm it in the latest shareholding filing; the point is that it is unusually modest.)
  • What low promoter holding means. It cuts both ways. On one hand, decisions are driven by a professional management team and answerable to a broad institutional base — closer to a Western-style widely-held firm. On the other, there is no dominant family owner with a huge personal stake to defend, which some investors read as weaker "skin in the game" and, in theory, leaves the company more open to shareholder or takeover pressure.
  • The technology dowry. The real inheritance from the Austrian acquisition is a deep bank of process patents and references across desalination, reuse and industrial water. In this industry, a track record of plants that actually hit their guaranteed output is the moat — a Gulf government awarding a billion-litre desalination job wants a builder who has demonstrably done it before, and that reference list is hard for a newcomer to replicate.
A genuine surprise: the Indian Va Tech Wabag you can buy on the NSE is not a licensee or a franchise of a European water major — it owns the European business it descended from. In 2007 the former subsidiary bought its former parent. That is why a mid-cap Chennai-headquartered company holds technology and reference plants across the Middle East, Europe and Africa: it didn't build that footprint from scratch, it acquired the mother ship.
04

The order book: four years of work, already signed

The single most important number for a project company — and how to read it.

For a business that builds things to order, the order book (also called the backlog) is the closest thing to a crystal ball: it is the total value of contracts won but not yet executed. Wabag's is now at a record — around ₹17,200 Cr, up ~26% in a year, against annual revenue of just ~₹3,944 Cr. That ratio, over four times sales, is the headline: the company already knows where roughly four years of revenue will come from. But a backlog is a promise, not cash, so you have to read its composition, not just its size.

The ~38% O&M share is the stickier, recurring part; ~62% EPC is the lumpy build revenue. The roughly even India/overseas split is deliberate — international jobs tend to carry better margins and cleaner payment terms, but add currency and country risk.

The flow of new orders matters as much as the stock. In the first half of FY26, Wabag booked new orders of about ₹3,477 Cr, of which roughly 80% came from overseas — led by a single ~₹2,038 Cr desalination win in Saudi Arabia (the 300 MLD Yanbu seawater-reverse-osmosis plant), plus wastewater work in India and Bahrain. When a company adds nearly a full year's revenue of new orders in six months, and most of it is hard-currency international, the growth engine is clearly running.

The metric to watch inside the backlog: book-to-bill

The order book only stays healthy if new orders come in at least as fast as old ones are executed and billed. That ratio — new orders won divided by revenue booked, or book-to-bill — is the pulse of a project company. Above 1.0, the backlog is growing and future revenue is being secured faster than it's consumed; below 1.0, the company is living off its stored orders and the backlog is shrinking. Wabag's recent book-to-bill has run comfortably above 1 (a ~26% jump in the order book while revenue grew ~20% tells you as much). Watch it each half-year: it is the earliest signal of whether the four-year runway is lengthening or quietly running down.

05

The O&M annuity: the quieter, higher-quality half

The recurring business that grows every time a plant is switched on.

Beneath the lumpy build revenue runs a steadier stream: the fees Wabag earns to operate and maintain plants after they're built. It is roughly 38% of the order book, and it behaves nothing like the EPC business. A build is a one-time event — you win it, execute it over two or three years, and it's gone. An O&M contract on that same plant pays a fee every year for the length of the deal, often a decade or more. So each plant Wabag commissions doesn't just add a one-off; it thickens a permanent annuity.

The mechanism is what makes it valuable. Water plants cannot be switched off — a city's sewage does not stop, a desalination plant's output is a lifeline — so the operator's revenue is about as recession-proof as industrial income gets. The output is contracted, the fees are largely fixed or indexed, and the customer has every incentive to keep paying because the alternative is a public-health or shutdown crisis. As the installed base of Wabag-run plants grows, this stream compounds quietly in the background, uncorrelated with whether the company is winning big new construction jobs this quarter.

Why the annuity is the calmer, more valuable earnings

EPC is feast-or-famine and thin on margin at the point of the build; a fixed-price contract can be squeezed by cost inflation or a slow site. O&M is the opposite: predictable, higher-margin at the operating level, and it keeps paying through good order years and bad. For a water company, a large, growing O&M book is what turns a bumpy contractor into something closer to a utility-services business — and it is why analysts watch O&M as a share of revenue so closely. The higher that share climbs, the more of Wabag's earnings are recurring and visible rather than won-and-gone, and the more the market is willing to pay for each rupee of profit.

The nuance: O&M's quality is real, but it is still a modest slice of revenue today (the order-book share overstates its current earnings weight, because O&M contracts span many years while EPC burns off in two or three). The bull case needs that recurring share to keep rising as more Wabag-built plants roll into their operating phase — a shift you can track directly in the segment disclosures, and one of the cleanest tells of whether the earnings mix is genuinely improving.
06

Going global: half the business is abroad

India + the Middle East + Africa — the growth engine, and the risk stack.

Water scarcity is worst where there is coastline but no river, and that map explains Wabag's footprint. The company operates across roughly 25 countries and 16 Indian states, and about half its order book and revenue is international (exports were ~43% of FY26 revenue). Its heartland abroad is the arid, cash-rich Middle East / MENA — Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, Oman — plus North and sub-Saharan Africa and pockets of Southeast Asia. Going global is the single biggest reason a mid-cap Indian firm can guide to years of growth. It is also where the risks concentrate.

Why "international" is both the upside and the worry

Overseas contracts are attractive for concrete reasons: Gulf desalination jobs are large, priced in dollars, and often carry cleaner payment terms and better margins than Indian municipal work. That is the growth story. But every one of those virtues has a shadow. A book concentrated in the Middle East means a company exposed to the region's politics — a conflict, a sanctions shift, or an oil-price shock that squeezes a government's budget can delay a payment or freeze a tender. Revenue in dollars and dirhams means a currency swing can move reported profit. And a large single client — a national water authority — means real customer-concentration risk. Half the book being abroad is exactly why Wabag can grow and exactly why its earnings can wobble in a way a purely domestic contractor's would not.

07

Working capital and the ₹289 Cr lesson

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it's written in scar tissue.

Here is the truth about a project business that a growing order book hides: you build first and get paid later. Wabag buys equipment, pays subcontractors, and executes a plant over two or three years, billing the customer in stages — and then it waits, sometimes a long time, to actually collect. The gap between money spent and money received is working capital, and in EPC it is enormous. A company can be winning every tender, booking record revenue, and still be quietly bleeding cash into unpaid bills. That is not a hypothetical for Wabag. It is the defining event of its recent history.

The business didn't shrink in FY23 — revenue was roughly flat at ~₹2,960 Cr. Profit fell ~92% because the company wrote off ~₹289 Cr of receivables it had given up hope of collecting: dues stuck in the Tecpro insolvency and delayed payments from the APGENCO power-project consortium.

Read that FY23 bar carefully, because it is the whole risk of this company distilled. In that year Wabag executed jobs, kept the lights on, and stayed cash-positive at the group level — yet its reported profit almost vanished, because it finally accepted that ~₹289 Cr of bills it had already counted as earned would never be paid. One customer (Tecpro) had gone into insolvency; another (an Andhra Pradesh power project from FY15) had dragged on unpaid for years. A plant Wabag built well earned nothing, because the counterparty behind it stopped paying.

The device that only fits Wabag: order book × execution margin × the O&M annuity — divided by who actually pays

Most contractors you value on order book and margin. For Wabag you need a third dimension the others let you ignore: collection. The company can win every plant on earth and still lose the year if a single large client stops paying — FY23 proved it, converting a ~₹132 Cr profit run-rate into ~₹11 Cr with no drop in activity. So the real equation is the size of the backlog, times the margin it can execute at, times the growing annuity of plants it runs — all discounted by the quality of the customers behind those contracts and how fast they pay. This is why the company's push toward hard-currency Middle East work and its obsession with staying net-cash aren't side-notes; they are the direct response to the wound. The order book tells you the opportunity; net working-capital days and receivables tell you whether the opportunity turns into money.

The reassuring flip side is that the scar seems to have changed behaviour. It does not remove the risk (a big overseas client can still delay), but it means the next bad debt is survivable rather than existential.

08

Why the world keeps getting shorter of water

The structural demand driver — a tailwind rooted in physics, not policy.

Behind every Wabag contract sits a simple, relentless trend: the world is running short of usable water while needing ever more of it. This is not a cyclical demand that fades when the economy cools; it is a structural squeeze that tightens every year. Four forces drive it, and each one turns into plants that somebody has to design and build.

What's driving structural water demand
DriverWhat it isWhy it helps Wabag
Scarcity & populationGroundwater is falling and populations and cities keep growing, especially in India and the Global South.Forces new supply — desalination and reuse — where rivers can't stretch further. A demand floor set by demographics.
Tightening regulationGovernments increasingly ban the discharge of untreated sewage and industrial effluent; "zero liquid discharge" mandates spread.Treatment shifts from optional to compulsory — every regulated factory and city becomes a forced buyer of a plant.

The insight that makes water different from other infrastructure

Most infrastructure demand is discretionary at the margin — you can delay a road, defer a factory, postpone a power plant when money is tight. Water is not like that. A city cannot decide to stop treating its sewage for a year, and a coastal region that has run out of groundwater cannot simply wait for rain. The need is non-deferrable, which is why water capex tends to keep flowing even when other infrastructure spending stalls. Layer on a genuinely new demand source — the boom in data centres, which are enormous consumers of cooling water — and the structural pull only strengthens. For a pure-play like Wabag, that is the most valuable thing a market can offer: demand that grows for reasons that have nothing to do with the business cycle.

None of this guarantees smooth sailing. Municipal budgets are political, government funding cycles are erratic, and a big desalination tender can slip a year for reasons far outside Wabag's control. But the direction is not in doubt: more people, dirtier rivers, stricter rules and thirstier industry all point one way — toward more plants. That backdrop is what lets Wabag talk about years of growth rather than one good order season.

09

The industry and the rivals

A specialist niche where scale, references and technology decide the winners.

Water treatment is a game of engineering credibility. A government awarding a billion-litre desalination plant cannot afford a contractor who might miss the guaranteed output — so the field narrows to players with a proven reference list, the technology, and a balance sheet strong enough to post large bid guarantees. Wabag competes against a mix of Indian engineering firms and global water majors, and its edge is being a pure-play with international references most domestic rivals lack.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things define Wabag's position. First, among Indian companies it is unusually pure and international — most domestic rivals treat water as one division and stay largely at home, whereas Wabag is a water company and roughly half its work is abroad, which is exactly the credential a Gulf or African government looks for. Second, in the biggest global tenders it is the value challenger against giants like Veolia and Suez — it wins not by out-spending them but by pairing a proven reference plant with a keener price. Its competitive job is to keep that engineering credibility current while protecting margin against far larger balance sheets. It is a specialist punching above its size, not a scale leader.

Financials & valuation

A steady grower with a scarred past — priced as a quality compounder, not a cheap contractor.

The financial story is one of recovery and consistency: revenue grinding higher, margins in the low double digits, profit rebuilt from the FY23 write-off, and a balance sheet kept deliberately clean. It is not a hyper-growth stock; it is a specialist compounding on a huge backlog. The market prices it accordingly — richer than a typical construction firm, because of the asset-light model, the net cash and the recurring O&M.

The one number that settles the thesis: execution margin and O&M share, read against working-capital days

At ~33× earnings and ~₹12,300 Cr of market value, Wabag is priced as a durable, asset-light compounder rather than a lumpy builder — so the case is won or lost on three linked numbers. First, order inflow: is book-to-bill staying above 1, keeping the four-year runway full? Second, the O&M share of revenue: is the recurring, higher-quality slice rising as built plants roll into operation? Third, and decisively, execution margin held against net working-capital days: is the company converting that backlog into profit and collecting the cash, or is margin flattered by revenue it may struggle to bank? Get inflow, mix and collection moving the right way together and the multiple is defensible. Let receivables balloon again — the FY23 pattern — and no order book, however large, protects the earnings. Collection is the scoreboard; everything else is the runway.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A specialist with a full book and a clean balance sheet — asked to prove it can bank what it builds.

Va Tech Wabag enters the second half of the 2020s in genuinely good shape: a record backlog worth four years of revenue, net cash for the sixth year running, a rising international mix, and a structural demand story that does not depend on the economic cycle. The honest question is not whether the business works — it plainly does — but whether it can execute a swelling order book at a decent margin and collect the money, having failed the last part badly once before.

The bull case

  • Structural, non-cyclical demand — scarcity, tightening pollution law, the reuse imperative and thirsty new industries (data centres) all point to more plants, for years.
  • Record backlog, high visibility — ~₹17,200 Cr order book, over 4× revenue, ~half of it international and better-paying.
  • Asset-light & net-cash — sixth straight net-cash year, near debt-free; earns ~19–21% RoCE without owning the plants, and can self-fund large bids.
  • Growing O&M annuity — ~38% of the book is recurring, sticky operating revenue that compounds as built plants roll into service.
  • Deep reference list & technology — the Austrian inheritance gives a global track record that Indian rivals struggle to match, especially in desalination.

The bear case

  • Payment / receivables risk — the FY23 ~₹289 Cr write-off showed a single non-paying client can wipe out a year's profit; government and overseas clients can pay slowly.
  • Fixed-price execution risk — an EPC-heavy book means cost inflation, delays or a subcontractor slip can turn a fixed price into a loss.
  • Geographic & geopolitical concentration — heavy Middle East exposure adds regional-conflict, single-client and currency risk to reported earnings.
  • Modest, EPC-driven margins — ~12% operating margin leaves limited buffer; the higher-quality O&M is still a small slice of current revenue.
  • Rich rating for a contractor — ~33× earnings prices in continued clean execution; a margin or collection wobble can de-rate it quickly.
The bottom line

Va Tech Wabag is a rare thing on the Indian market: a genuine pure-play on one of the century's most durable needs — usable water — run by a professional team, with the technology, the net cash and a four-year book to back it. Everything the industry offers is working in its favour. What it must still prove, quarter after quarter, is the discipline the FY23 write-off exposed: turning a giant backlog into executed plants at a held margin, and — the part that actually broke it before — collecting the cash on time. The next Yanbu-scale win will grab the headlines, but the number that will quietly decide this story is duller and more telling: net working-capital days, and whether receivables stay in check as revenue scales. Watch the cash conversion, not just the order wins; that is where this company's fortunes are really made.
