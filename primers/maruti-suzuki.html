00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Maruti Suzuki is India's largest passenger-vehicle maker — roughly two out of every five new cars sold in India carry its badge. It is majority-owned (~58%) and technology-fed by Japan's Suzuki Motor Corporation, and it built its empire on small, frugal, reliable cars (Alto, WagonR, Swift, Dzire, Baleno). In FY26 it sold a record 24.2 lakh (2.42 million) vehicles and booked ~₹1.74 lakh crore of net sales.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A cash machine with a growth problem. Maruti is debt-free, throws off enormous cash, earns ~14% on equity, and pays fat dividends — but its domestic market share has fallen to a ~13-year low of about 39%, below the symbolic 40% line, because the small cars it dominates are the part of the market that has stopped growing.
  • The story is a mix shift. India's buyers have moved to SUVs (now ~two-thirds of the market). Maruti was late and under-represented there, so FY26 was about pushing into SUVs and lifting the average selling price — revenue grew ~20% on just ~8% more volume, proof that each car sold is now worth more. Margins, though, fell (EBITDA ~12.3% vs 13.9%) on costlier inputs and a heavier model mix to launch.
  • Exports are the new engine. With the domestic small-car market soft, Maruti leaned on shipping abroad: a record 4.48 lakh vehicles exported (+34%), making it India's #1 car exporter for the fifth straight year (~49% of all of India's car exports), including its first EV, the eVitara, already going to 40-plus countries.
In plain terms

Think of Maruti as the supermarket of Indian cars — it wins on scale, reach, low cost and trust, the way a mass retailer does. For two decades the winning formula was "sell the most cheap cars to the most people". The problem is that the cheap-car shelf has stopped growing while the premium SUV shelf is booming — and Maruti was slow to stock the premium shelf. So the whole investment debate is one question: can the company that owns the value end of the market climb up-market — into SUVs, into exports, into hybrids and EVs — fast enough to keep growing, while defending its giant base? It has the factories, the cash and the parent's technology to try. It is now racing the clock against Tata, Hyundai, Mahundra and Kia, who got there first.

01

Auto-OEM economics 101: how a carmaker earns

No finance degree required — just the five forces that drive every car company's profit.

A car manufacturer (an "OEM" — original equipment manufacturer) is a high-fixed-cost, high-volume business. It sinks enormous money into factories, tooling and design, then earns by selling lots of units at a price comfortably above the cost of the steel, parts, labour and overhead in each one. Five numbers explain almost everything.

The only levers you need
LeverWhat it means (and Maruti's FY26 number)
VolumeHow many vehicles you sell. The base of everything. Maruti: 24.2 lakh units (19.7 lakh domestic + 4.5 lakh export).
ASP / realisation"Average Selling Price" — net revenue per vehicle. A small car earns far less than a loaded SUV. Maruti's net realisation is roughly ₹7.2 lakh/vehicle and rising as the mix richens.
Mix / premiumisationThe blend of cheap vs expensive models. Shifting toward SUVs lifts ASP and (usually) margin — the single biggest swing factor for Maruti right now.
Input / commodity costSteel, aluminium, copper, precious metals, plastics, plus the yen (royalty & imported parts). When these rise, margin falls unless you can raise prices. A FY26 headwind.
Operating leverageFactories are mostly fixed cost. Run them full and each extra car is highly profitable; run them half-empty and you bleed. Maruti is running near 100% utilisation.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Revenue ≈ Volume × ASP, and Profit ≈ Revenue − (variable cost per car × volume) − fixed costs − royalty − tax. So an OEM grows profit three ways — sell more cars (volume), sell dearer cars (ASP/mix), and hold the line on costs (commodities, discounts, efficiency). The catch is that the business is brutally cyclical and capital-hungry: you must spend billions on new factories and new models years before the volume shows up, and a soft year leaves expensive plants under-used. That's why the capex cycle, the model pipeline and the commodity cycle all matter as much as this quarter's sales.

Why margins move so much on small swings

  • Thin per-car margins, huge volume. Mass-market OEMs earn high-single-digit to low-teens operating margins — so a 1–2% move in steel prices or discounts swings profit a lot.
  • Discounts are the silent margin killer. When demand softens, dealers discount to clear stock; that comes straight out of realisation. Maruti's lean ~12-day dealer inventory and ~1.9 lakh pending orders signal disciplined supply, which protects pricing.
  • Mix beats volume for margin. Selling one Grand Vitara can add more profit than several entry hatchbacks — which is exactly why the SUV pivot dominates the story.

The business and the value chain

From Suzuki's design in Japan to a car in a small-town Indian driveway.

Maruti's competitive edge isn't one factory — it's an end-to-end system: parent technology, deep local manufacturing and vendor base, the widest sales-and-service reach in the country, and a financing/used-car ecosystem around it. Understanding the chain explains why the moat is hard to copy even as rivals out-style Maruti on individual models.

The further right you go, the higher the margin and the harder to copy. A new entrant can design a sharp SUV (Stage 1) but cannot quickly build Maruti's nationwide service-and-spares network (Stage 3–4) — the real, durable moat.

The two showroom brands

  • Arena — the original, mass-market network selling the volume cars (Alto, S-Presso, WagonR, Swift, Dzire, Brezza, Ertiga). Vast small-town and rural reach is its superpower.
  • NEXA — the premium channel created in 2015 for higher-end models (Baleno, Grand Vitara, Invicto, Fronx, Jimny, eVitara). This is the deliberate up-market face of the brand — built precisely to carry Maruti into the SUV/premium era.
  • Commercial & Super Carry — light commercial vehicles, a smaller adjacency.
Why distribution is the durable moat

Anyone with capital can build a car plant; very few can build Maruti's reach — thousands of sales and service points stretching deep into small towns and villages where rivals barely operate, backed by the cheapest spare parts, the highest resale values and decades of "it never breaks down" trust. That network does three things: it sells cars where competitors can't economically reach; it earns steady, high-margin service and spares revenue regardless of the new-car cycle; and it gives Maruti a low cost of customer acquisition. The vulnerability: this moat was built for value buyers. In premium SUVs, sold to aspirational urban customers who shop on design and features, Maruti's reach advantage matters less — which is the heart of its current challenge.

03

The company and Suzuki

A 1980s national project that became a Japanese-controlled cash machine.

Maruti began as a government-of-India project to put the country on affordable wheels, in partnership with Suzuki. Over four decades the State exited, Suzuki took control, and the company became the dominant force in Indian motoring — and one of Suzuki's most important profit centres globally.

Timeline

  • 1981–83
    Maruti Udyog founded as a government venture; the Maruti 800 rolls out in 1983 and motorises middle-class India.
  • 1980s–90s
    Suzuki of Japan supplies the technology; the small, frugal, reliable formula creates a near-monopoly in Indian small cars.
  • 2002–07
    Government begins exiting; Suzuki takes majority control. Renamed Maruti Suzuki India Ltd; listed on NSE/BSE.
  • 2015
    Launches the premium NEXA retail channel (Baleno, later Grand Vitara) — the first deliberate move up-market.
  • 2017
    Gujarat (Hansalpur) plant begins production via Suzuki Motor Gujarat, expanding capacity beyond Haryana.
  • FY23–25
    SUV push gathers pace (Brezza, Grand Vitara, Fronx, Jimny, Invicto); exports scale up; ASP and revenue climb even as small-car volume softens.
  • FY26
    Record 24.2 lakh units sold and 4.48 lakh exported; launches first EV, the eVitara; 2nd Kharkhoda plant commissioned; but domestic PV share slips to a ~13-year low near 39%.

Ownership & the Suzuki relationship

  • Promoter: Suzuki Motor Corporation (Japan) owns roughly 58%. The rest is held by Indian and foreign institutions (LIC and other insurers, mutual funds, FIIs) and retail investors.
  • Technology & royalty: Maruti licenses platforms, engines and designs from Suzuki and pays a royalty of ~3.5% of revenue (now largely rupee-denominated, reducing yen-exchange risk). It is a genuine cost — but it buys access to global R&D Maruti doesn't have to fully fund itself.
  • Strategic alignment: India is now Suzuki's single most important market and its global export and EV hub. That alignment is mostly a positive (Maruti gets parent investment and global product), but it also means Suzuki's worldwide priorities — including a cautious, "multi-pathway" stance on EVs — shape Maruti's roadmap.
The minority-shareholder lens: the royalty and the parent's control are perennial governance talking-points — every rupee of royalty is a rupee not retained by Maruti's minority owners. The counter-argument: the technology, scale and export access Suzuki provides are worth far more than the ~3.5% fee, and royalty has trended down as a share of sales over the years.

The model mix and the ASP story

Why revenue grew ~20% on only ~8% more cars — the mix is doing the heavy lifting.

The most important single chart in Maruti's FY26 is the gap between volume growth (~8%) and revenue growth (~20%). That ~12-point wedge is price and mix — Maruti is selling dearer cars (more SUVs, fewer entry hatchbacks) and getting more rupees for each one. It is the up-market shift, made visible in the numbers.

Why ASP and mix matter more than headline volume

A mini hatchback might net Maruti ~₹4–5 lakh; a Grand Vitara or Invicto can net two to three times that. So replacing a soft small-car sale with an SUV sale grows revenue even if total unit count barely moves — which is exactly what happened in FY26. The blended net realisation has climbed toward ~₹7.2 lakh per vehicle. This is the engine of Maruti's near-term growth: with the small-car market flat, ASP/mix — not volume — is doing most of the work. The risk hidden in the same fact: if the SUVs Maruti sells are themselves discounted hard to win share, the ASP gain comes at the cost of margin — which is part of why FY26 margins slipped even as realisation rose.

05

The small-car slowdown vs the SUV pivot

The defining strategic shift — and the reason Maruti's share fell while its revenue rose.

For most of Maruti's life, "India's car market" and "the small-car market" were almost the same thing — and Maruti owned it. That world has changed. The entry small-car segment has stalled (post-pandemic price inflation, tighter safety/emission norms and weak first-time-buyer affordability pushed budget buyers toward two-wheelers or used cars), while SUVs exploded to roughly two-thirds of the market. Maruti was heavily exposed to the shrinking half and under-exposed to the booming half — the root cause of its falling share.

Maruti dominates small cars but holds well below its overall ~39% share inside the UV segment that now drives industry growth — the precise gap it is racing to close.

The response has been a deliberate SUV blitz: Brezza (sub-4m), Fronx (coupe-SUV), Grand Vitara (mid-size, hybrid), Jimny (off-roader), Invicto (premium MPV), the new Victoris, and the eVitara (EV) — with major facelifts of Brezza, Fronx, Grand Vitara and the next-gen Baleno slated through FY27. Management has guided to multiple new SUVs over the coming years to lift its UV share decisively.

The two-sided bet

The SUV pivot is doing two things at once. On the upside: it lifts ASP and revenue (Section 04) and re-attacks the fastest-growing, highest-margin slice of the market. On the downside: Maruti is fighting incumbents (Tata, Mahindra, Hyundai, Kia) who got there first and built strong SUV brands, so winning share often means spending on features and discounts — pressuring the very margins the richer mix should lift. Meanwhile, the company can't abandon small cars: it still has ~1.3 lakh pending small-car orders and a once-in-a-generation chance to re-energise the entry segment if affordability returns (GST cuts, income growth). The elegant version of the strategy: defend the value base, climb the SUV ladder, and let exports absorb the small-car capacity the domestic market no longer wants.

The watch-item: the single cleanest gauge of whether the pivot is working is Maruti's UV/SUV market share rising toward its overall ~39% share. If UV share climbs while overall share stabilises, the strategy is succeeding; if SUV launches only cannibalise discounts without lifting share, the pivot is expensive but not winning.
06

The powertrain bet: CNG & hybrid first, EV deliberately late

Maruti's most contrarian call — and the one the market argues about most.

While Tata and several rivals raced into pure electric cars, Maruti — backed by Suzuki — chose a "multi-pathway" strategy: hedge across CNG, mild- and strong-hybrid, flex-fuel/biogas and EV rather than bet the company on battery-electric alone. In a country where charging infrastructure is thin, electricity is largely coal-fired, and budget buyers won't pay an EV premium, Maruti argues this is the pragmatic path to actually cutting emissions at scale. Critics call it being late to the inevitable.

The pieces of the bet

  • CNG — Maruti is by far India's biggest CNG-car maker. CNG runs far cheaper than petrol, needs no plug, and suits cost-conscious buyers and fleets. Management expects it to be the largest slice of the mix by 2031.
  • Strong hybrid — petrol-electric systems (as in Grand Vitara, Invicto) that cut fuel use without charging infrastructure; being pushed into mid-size and larger SUVs. The pragmatic bridge technology.
  • Battery EV — the eVitara is the first, built in Gujarat with 49 kWh / 61 kWh battery options (up to ~543 km range), priced from ~₹15.99 lakh (or a battery-as-a-service plan from ~₹10.99 lakh). Crucially, it is being made in India for the world — exported before it even scaled at home.
  • Flex-fuel & biogas — ethanol-blend and compressed-biogas options as further low-carbon, India-appropriate paths.
Why "late to EV" might be smart — or might be a trap

The bull reading: India's EV adoption is real but slow outside premium urban niches, charging is patchy, and a coal-heavy grid blunts the green case — so a CNG/hybrid-led portfolio may sell more, emit less and earn more today, while Maruti lets rivals fund the costly early EV market and then enters at scale with the eVitara and a pipeline behind it. The bear reading: technology shifts can tip suddenly; if EV adoption accelerates (on cheaper batteries, policy push, better charging), the company that owns the mass market could find itself structurally behind in the technology that defines the next decade, having handed the early lead to Tata and others. This is the highest-stakes judgement call in the entire Maruti story — and there is no consensus on who's right.

The export engine

The growth offset that turned a soft domestic year into a record one.

When the domestic small-car market went soft, Maruti had a release valve few rivals can match: exports. In FY26 it shipped a record 4,47,774 vehicles abroad — up ~34% — making it India's #1 passenger-vehicle exporter for the fifth year running, responsible for roughly 49% of all of India's car exports. India has become Suzuki's global manufacturing and export hub, and that is a structural advantage, not a one-off.

Exports now equal nearly one in five vehicles Maruti makes — and grew far faster than domestic sales in FY26, cushioning the soft home small-car market.
  • Reach: Maruti exports to 100+ countries across Latin America, Africa, the Middle East, ASEAN and now Europe, with ~18 models in the global line-up.
  • The EV twist: the eVitara, India-built, was exported to 40-plus countries (35,000+ units) before it even scaled domestically — proof India is now Suzuki's EV export base, not just a small-car factory.
  • Why it matters strategically: exports let Maruti keep its near-100%-full factories running through domestic soft patches, support ASP (export models skew richer), and diversify away from a single, maturing home market.
Why exports are more than a sales footnote

For a high-fixed-cost manufacturer, a full factory is everything (Section 01). Exports give Maruti a second demand pool to keep plants running flat-out even when Indian small-car demand wobbles — which protects operating leverage, margins and the case for building more capacity. It also reframes the whole investment story: Maruti is no longer just a bet on the Indian car cycle, but increasingly a bet on India-as-Suzuki's-global-export-base. The risks are real — geopolitics (Middle East/shipping disruptions), currency, and trade policy in destination markets — but a 34% jump in a soft domestic year shows how powerful the offset can be.

The honest caveat: exports carry their own ASP and FX dynamics, and a few large destination markets can swing the number. The trend is clearly up and strategically central — but a single year's growth rate (here, +34%) shouldn't be straight-lined forever.
08

Market-share defence: the fight with Tata, Hyundai, M&M & Kia

Still the clear #1 — but the lead is narrowing, and the battleground has moved to SUVs.

Maruti remains India's largest carmaker by a wide margin, but its domestic PV share has slid for three straight years to a ~13-year low near 39%, briefly dipping below the symbolic 40% line before recovering somewhat in early FY27 on new launches. The slip isn't because Maruti is selling fewer cars — it's selling record numbers — but because the market grew faster in the SUV segments where rivals are stronger.

How worried should you be about a 39% share?

Two readings coexist. The bear view: a multi-year share slide for a company whose entire identity is market leadership is a real warning — it shows Maruti structurally missed the SUV/EV shift and is now playing catch-up against sharper, faster rivals, with the lead steadily eroding. The bull view: ~39% of a growing market still means a record number of cars, the share loss is concentrated in the small-car collapse (not a loss of customers Maruti was winning), the SUV blitz and a possible small-car revival (on GST cuts/affordability) can stabilise share, and a 39%-share, debt-free leader earning fat returns is hardly in crisis. The truth is in between — and the next two years of UV-share data will settle it.

Capacity build-out and the Suzuki royalty

Betting big on factories — and paying the parent for the privilege of using its tech.

Maruti is in the middle of its largest-ever capacity expansion, scaling from roughly 24 lakh units today toward ~4 million (40 lakh) a year by 2030–31 — a clear signal that, despite the soft small-car market, management expects volume (domestic SUVs + exports) to keep climbing. Funding it is easy: Maruti is debt-free and cash-rich, with FY27 capex guided around ₹14,000 crore.

The royalty — a permanent, declining toll

Because Suzuki supplies the platforms, engines and designs, Maruti pays a royalty of roughly 3.5% of revenue to its parent — on ₹1.74 lakh crore of sales, a meaningful sum (order of ₹6,000+ crore). It is now largely rupee-denominated, which removes the old yen-exchange volatility. The rate has trended down over the years as Maruti localises more engineering.

How to think about the capex-and-royalty combo

These two items capture the deal at the heart of Maruti: it gets to ride Suzuki's global R&D and capital without funding it all alone — and pays a toll (royalty + parent control) for that access. The capex build-out is a vote of confidence (you don't add 16 lakh units of capacity if you expect demand to shrink), funded entirely from internal cash — a luxury most carmakers lack. The royalty is the cost side of the bargain and a perennial minority-shareholder grievance, but at ~3.5% and falling, with rupee-denomination removing FX risk, most analysts judge the technology access well worth the fee. Watch capacity utilisation as the new plants come on: full plants validate the spend; under-used new capacity in a soft cycle would be the classic auto-OEM trap.

How to think about the ~30× multiple

At ~₹4.4 lakh crore market cap on ~₹14,500 crore of FY26 profit, Maruti trades around 30× trailing earnings — rich for a mass-market, cyclical carmaker (global peers often trade single-digit to low-teen multiples). The premium rests on three things: (1) a dominant, debt-free, high-return franchise with India's best distribution; (2) structural growth optionality from SUVs, exports and a still-low Indian car-ownership base (cars per 1,000 people remain a fraction of developed markets); and (3) faith that margins recover as the mix matures and commodities ease. The bear pushes back: falling share, flat profit, margin pressure and a contrarian EV stance don't obviously justify a 30× multiple — any of those souring could de-rate the stock. As with all auto OEMs, the multiple is really a bet on where in the cycle you are buying.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A dominant franchise in transition — priced for the transition to work.

Maruti is one of India's great franchises: unmatched scale and reach, a fortress balance sheet, Suzuki's technology, and a record-breaking operational year. But it is a leader in transition — from small cars to SUVs, from a domestic story to an export one, from petrol to a multi-fuel future. The investment debate is almost entirely about whether that transition succeeds fast enough to offset a maturing core.

The bull case

  • Unrivalled moat — #1 share, the deepest sales-and-service network in India, lowest cost of ownership, debt-free balance sheet.
  • ASP/mix tailwind — the SUV pivot is lifting realisation (revenue +20% on volume +8%); premiumisation has years to run.
  • Export engine — record 4.48 lakh units, #1 exporter; India is now Suzuki's global hub, diversifying away from a single market.
  • Pragmatic powertrain — CNG & hybrid leadership sells today; eVitara opens EV optionality without betting the company.
  • Long runway — low Indian car penetration + 40-lakh-unit capacity build = structural volume growth if execution holds.

The bear case

  • Share in decline — three years of slipping PV share to a ~13-year low; structurally weak inside the SUV segment driving the market.
  • Margin pressure — FY26 EBITDA fell to ~12.3%; commodity, discount and launch costs are squeezing profit despite record sales.
  • EV risk — deliberately late to battery-electric; if adoption accelerates, the multi-pathway bet could leave Maruti behind Tata & others.
  • Cyclicality & affordability — small-car buyers are stretched; a soft cycle leaves expensive new capacity under-used.
  • Royalty & parent control — ~3.5% royalty and Suzuki's majority stake are perennial minority-shareholder frictions; rich ~30× multiple leaves little room for error.
The bottom line

Maruti is a structurally dominant, financially pristine carmaker whose old growth engine (small cars) has stalled just as new ones (SUVs, exports, hybrids/EVs) are spinning up. The business risk is modest — this is the market leader with a fortress balance sheet — but the growth-and-margin question is genuinely open. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers: UV/SUV market share (is the pivot winning?), EBITDA margin (does mix beat cost pressure?), and export volume (does the second engine keep firing?). Get those right and a 30× multiple on a growing leader looks fair; stumble, and a cyclical carmaker priced for a smooth transition has room to disappoint.
