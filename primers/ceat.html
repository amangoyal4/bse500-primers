CEAT, a rubber-price bet dressed as a tyre company

A complete walk through one of India's major tyre makers — how the RPG Group builds tyres for scooters, cars, trucks and tractors, why the replacement counter and the humble two-wheeler quietly carry the profit, and why a "tyre company's" quarter is really settled by the price of natural rubber and crude oil weeks before the tyres ever get sold.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

CEAT makes tyres — the rubber rings that carry the weight of every scooter, car, truck and tractor in the country. It is one of India's four large listed tyre makers (alongside MRF, Apollo Tyres and JK Tyre), part of the RPG Group controlled by the Harsh Goenka family. It sells across four vehicle categories — two-wheelers, passenger cars and SUVs, trucks and buses, and off-highway (agri and industrial) tyres — through three channels: the replacement aftermarket (you buy a new tyre when the old one wears out), OEM (fitting tyres onto brand-new vehicles at the factory), and exports. In FY26 it turned over roughly ₹15,678 crore.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The profit lives in the aftermarket, not the assembly line. Roughly half of CEAT's sales come from the replacement market — a driver walking into a tyre shop to replace worn rubber. That channel is brand-driven, higher-margin and recurring; the tyres a vehicle-maker fits at the factory (OEM) are lower-margin volume that mainly buys CEAT the right to be the brand a customer re-buys. The mix between the two is a big part of the margin story.
  • A tyre is mostly rubber and oil, so profit swings with commodity prices. More than 60% of what it costs to make a tyre is raw material — natural rubber, crude-derived synthetic rubber and chemicals, carbon black, and steel wire. When those inputs spike faster than CEAT can raise tyre prices, margins get crushed; when they fall while prices hold, margins bloom. FY26 was the squeeze: EBITDA margin compressed to ~13.2% as natural rubber ran hot, and quarterly profit whipsawed accordingly.
  • It is deliberately moving up-market. Once seen as a value brand, CEAT has spent a decade premiumising — sharper two-wheeler and passenger tyres, motorsport and cricket sponsorships, and a push into higher-margin off-highway tyres (OHT) capped by the 2023 acquisition of Michelin's Camso tracks-and-tyres business. FY26 revenue rose ~18.6% and PAT ~47.7%; the stock trades near 24× earnings — a cyclical on a moderate multiple that re-rates when the mix and the margin move the right way.
In plain terms

CEAT is a consumer brand welded onto a commodity conversion machine. On one side it buys natural rubber and oil-based chemicals at whatever the market charges that month; on the other it sells finished tyres, and — crucially — it can only raise the selling price with a lag, after negotiating with vehicle-makers and nudging the aftermarket. The gap between those two prices, quarter to quarter, is where nearly all the profit variation comes from. Everything else — volumes, market share, the premiumisation push — matters, but it moves the needle slowly; the rubber-versus-price spread moves it violently and immediately. A first-time reader should hold that single idea and the rest of the company falls into place.

01

The tyre business 101: how a rubber ring makes money

No engineering degree needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

A tyre looks simple and is anything but. It is a precisely engineered composite — layers of rubber, fabric and steel cured together — designed to grip the road, carry a load, absorb shock and last tens of thousands of kilometres. But for an investor, the business reduces to a few plain ideas: every vehicle needs tyres, tyres wear out, so demand comes in two waves (first when the vehicle is built, then again and again over its life), and the whole thing is a contest between the cost of the raw material and the price the maker can charge. Get comfortable with five terms and the rest of this primer reads easily.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)

OEM"Original Equipment Manufacturer" — selling tyres to a vehicle-maker (Maruti, Hero, Tata Motors) to fit on new vehicles. High volume, low margin, fiercely negotiated, but it seeds the brand a buyer later re-purchases.

The economics that decide everything: cost-in versus price-out

A tyre maker is, at heart, a spread business. On the cost side sits a basket of commodities — natural rubber, crude-linked synthetic rubber and chemicals, carbon black, steel wire — that together run above 60% of the cost of a tyre and are re-priced by global markets every day. On the revenue side sits a price CEAT sets, but only with friction: OEM prices are locked in contracts and renegotiated periodically, and aftermarket price hikes have to be pushed through thousands of dealers without losing share to MRF or Apollo. So costs move fast and daily; prices move slow and lumpy. The distance between them — and the delay — is the entire margin engine. A tyre company with flat volumes can double its profit or halve it in a year without selling a single extra tyre.

Why the aftermarket is the prize

  • It recurs. A new car buys one set of OEM tyres once. Over its life it will get through several replacement sets — and every one of those is an aftermarket sale to whoever the driver trusts. The installed base of vehicles on Indian roads is a slowly compounding annuity of tyre demand.
  • It is branded, so it prices better. At the OEM counter a vehicle-maker beats the tyre supplier down on price. At the replacement counter the consumer chooses, often on brand and dealer recommendation — which lets a strong brand hold price and margin. This is why a premium image is worth real money in tyres.
  • It is less cyclical. When new-vehicle sales dip, people still replace worn tyres on the cars they already own. The aftermarket softens the swings that batter the OEM order book.
02

Four kinds of tyre — and they are not equal

Two-wheeler, passenger, truck & bus, and off-highway — each a different business.

"CEAT makes tyres" hides a lot. A scooter tyre and a mining-loader tyre are utterly different products with different customers, different competition and — the part that matters — different margins. CEAT plays in four buckets, and understanding which ones earn well is most of the investment case.

The four categories, decoded

  • Two-wheeler (scooter & motorcycle) — CEAT's crown jewel. India runs on two-wheelers, CEAT is a leading brand here, and these tyres punch above their weight on margin: they are relatively small, use less raw material per unit, sell heavily into the branded replacement market, and command genuine consumer preference. This is the quiet, high-return core.
  • Passenger car & SUV — the segment CEAT has fought hardest to premiumise. As India buys bigger cars and SUVs, tyres get larger (higher rim sizes), pricier and better-mix. CEAT has won OEM fitments on new models and pushed its brand up-market to capture the replacement re-buy. Growing share here is central to the "CEAT is no longer a value brand" story.
  • Truck & bus (T&B) — the heavyweight of the tyre market by tonnes of rubber. Commercial-vehicle tyres are a large, essential business tied to freight and the economy, but they are the most commoditised and price-sensitive category, and the one most exposed to cheap Chinese imports. CEAT competes here, but it is a volume game, not a margin haven.
  • Off-highway (OHT — agri & industrial) — the deliberate move up the margin curve. These are the specialist tyres and rubber tracks on tractors, combines, loaders and construction equipment, sold heavily into export markets (Europe, the Americas). They are lower-volume but far higher-value and higher-margin, and — as we'll see with Camso — the area where CEAT has spent real capital to build a global specialty position.
The surprise a newcomer never expects

Ask someone which tyre earns the most and they picture the glamorous, expensive car or SUV tyre. In fact, two of CEAT's best-margin businesses are the least glamorous: the humble two-wheeler tyre on a commuter scooter, and the mud-caked agricultural / off-highway tyre on a tractor. The scooter tyre wins on brand pull and a heavy branded-aftermarket skew; the farm and construction tyre wins on specialisation, export pricing and low competition from mass players. Meanwhile the giant, essential truck & bus segment — the one that moves the most rubber — is often the hardest place to make money, squeezed by imports and haggling fleet owners. In tyres, size of market and size of margin routinely point in opposite directions.

Three ways to sell a tyre: replacement, OEM, export

The channel mix — read this once and CEAT's margin behaviour makes sense.

Cut CEAT a different way — not by what tyre but by who buys it — and you get three channels with very different economics. This split matters more for profitability than the vehicle categories do, because it decides how much pricing power CEAT actually has.

The three channels, and what each one is worth

  • Replacement (~half of sales, most of the profit). This is the branded aftermarket. A consumer or a dealer chooses CEAT over a rival, which means CEAT sets the price and defends the margin. It is recurring — driven by the huge, growing base of vehicles already on the road — and less volatile than new-vehicle demand. When analysts talk about CEAT "improving mix," they usually mean tilting more of the business toward here.
  • OEM (~30%, thin margin, strategic). Selling to Maruti, Hyundai, Hero, Bajaj, Tata Motors and the rest to fit on new vehicles. The vehicle-maker has all the negotiating power, so margins are slim. But OEM is not charity: winning a fitment puts CEAT rubber under a brand-new vehicle, and many drivers re-buy the same brand at replacement time. OEM is a loss-leader that feeds the profitable aftermarket.
  • Exports (~19% standalone, ~a third of the group). Selling into international markets — increasingly the higher-margin off-highway and specialty tyres, turbo-charged by the Camso acquisition. Exports diversify CEAT away from the Indian cycle and lift the mix, but they add currency risk and exposure to global trade friction and tariffs.
Why the channel mix is the real margin dial

The vehicle-category mix tells you where CEAT competes; the channel mix tells you how much it gets to keep. A company selling mostly into OEM is a price-taker running on thin, negotiated margins. A company skewed to branded replacement and specialist exports has genuine pricing power and can hold margin even when a customer pushes back. CEAT's long-run project — premiumise the brand, grow the aftermarket share, scale high-value OHT exports — is, underneath the marketing, a deliberate campaign to shift the channel mix toward the parts of the business where it, not the customer, sets the price.

04

RPG, the Goenkas, and who controls CEAT

A ~70-year-old brand inside one of India's storied business houses.

CEAT is not a start-up story. The brand dates to 1958 — it began as the Indian arm of an Italian tyre maker, Cavi Elettrici e Affini Torino (the "CEAT" acronym), and was acquired by the RPG Group in 1982. Today it sits under the Harsh Goenka side of the RPG empire, a diversified house spanning tyres, infrastructure, pharma, IT services and plantations. The tyre business is run with a clear premiumisation agenda and a family that has publicly backed heavy reinvestment.

Timeline

  • 1982

  • 2000s
    CEAT modernises and expands manufacturing; builds out a nationwide dealer network and a value-brand reputation.
  • 2010s
    The premiumisation era begins — new plants (Halol, Nagpur, Chennai), a sharper brand, motorsport and cricket sponsorships, and a decisive push into two-wheeler and passenger radials.
  • 2023
    CEAT acquires Michelin's Camso off-highway construction-compact and agriculture tyres-and-tracks business (brand and assets), a major step up the specialty/export margin curve.
  • FY24–FY25
    Record revenues on volume growth and premiumisation; margins swing with natural-rubber prices; capacity added across categories.
  • FY26
    Revenue ~₹15,678 Cr (+18.6%); EBITDA ~₹2,063 Cr (~13.2% margin) after a rubber-cost squeeze; PAT ~₹698 Cr (+47.7%); Camso integration progressing; ~₹1,000 Cr capex.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the RPG Group / Goenka family (through entities such as Instant Holdings and other RPG group companies) hold the controlling stake — roughly the high-40s percent (~47% approx.). Harsh Goenka is chairman; Anant Goenka (vice-chairman of the group) has been closely associated with CEAT's premiumisation drive. Track the exact promoter holding and any pledge directly in the quarterly shareholding filings.
  • Institutional & public: the balance is held by domestic and foreign institutions and public shareholders. With ~4.05 crore shares outstanding, CEAT is a mid-cap by Indian standards — smaller than MRF or Apollo by market value.
  • Balance sheet: CEAT carries moderate debt — a Debt/EBITDA of roughly ~1.5× and Debt/Equity around ~0.6× at FY26 — reflecting the heavy, ongoing capex to add capacity and integrate Camso. Net debt is in the order of ~₹3,000 Cr (approx.), comfortably serviced but worth watching through the capex cycle.
Why the ownership matters: a promoter family that thinks in decades has let CEAT spend years — and a lot of capital — dragging the brand up-market and buying a global specialty business, accepting near-term margin and balance-sheet strain for a better long-run mix. That patience is an asset when the strategy works and a risk if capital keeps going in faster than returns come out. The scoreboard is whether the premium mix and OHT/export push actually lift through-cycle margins above where the old value-brand CEAT sat.

What a tyre is actually made of — and why that is the whole story

Follow the raw material and you have found the profit.

To understand CEAT's profit you have to understand its shopping list. A tyre is a bundle of commodities cured into a shape, and the cost of that bundle — not the cleverness of the shape — is what moves earnings from quarter to quarter. Here is what goes in, roughly, and where each ingredient's price is set.

Shares are illustrative, not exact — but the shape is the point: natural rubber is the single biggest input, and much of the rest (synthetic rubber, carbon black) is crude-oil-derived. So a tyre's cost rides on two commodities CEAT cannot control — rubber and crude.

Two independent commodities dominate. Natural rubber, tapped from trees and traded globally, is the largest single input; its price swings on South-East-Asian supply, weather, disease and demand. Crude oil feeds the synthetic rubber, carbon black and many chemicals that make up much of the rest. Add steel wire for the reinforcing beads and belts, and you have a product whose cost is set almost entirely by markets outside the factory gate. When you read that CEAT's margin "expanded" or "compressed," nine times out of ten the sentence really means rubber and crude moved, and CEAT's tyre prices had not yet caught up.

Where the money actually leaks or gushes

Because raw material is above 60% of the cost of a tyre, a modest move in rubber or crude swamps almost everything management can do operationally. Trim factory waste, improve a blend, run the plant a little harder — all real, all worth a fraction of a margin point. A double-digit move in the natural-rubber price, meanwhile, can move gross margin by several points in a single quarter. That asymmetry is why tyre companies are classified as cyclicals: their fortunes are chained to a commodity cycle they can influence only slowly, through the lag with which they raise prices. CEAT's skill is real, but it operates on top of a commodity wave it does not create.

06

The rubber-price spread and the lag

The single most important idea in the case — and the one that is unique to a tyre maker.

Here is the mechanism that decides whether a CEAT quarter is good or grim. Costs move fast: natural rubber and crude re-price daily, and a factory buying rubber this month feels it almost immediately. Selling prices move slow: OEM contracts are renegotiated periodically, and aftermarket hikes have to be pushed through a dealer network without ceding share. So there is always a gap — and a delay — between when input costs change and when CEAT's realisations catch up. That gap, with its lag, is the whole margin story.

The tyre volume barely changes across these states; the profit changes enormously. That is the spread — the gap between input cost and realisation, opening and closing with a delay.

FY26 is the illustration written in CEAT's own numbers. Natural rubber ran hot through much of the year, and CEAT could not raise tyre prices fast enough to keep up. The result: EBITDA margin compressed to about 13.2%, and in the June-2025 quarter net profit collapsed by roughly 96% year-on-year — not because CEAT sold fewer tyres (revenue that quarter actually grew ~22%), but because the spread had slammed shut. Later in the year, as rubber cooled and earlier price hikes stuck, the same business swung the other way: the December-2025 quarter saw profit jump about 60%. Broadly the same volumes, broadly the same factories — and profit that lurched from near-zero growth to a 60% surge, dictated almost entirely by the rubber-versus-price gap.

The device that only fits a tyre maker: profit is a rubber bet settled late

Most companies you can read through revenue growth. CEAT you have to read through a spread. Picture two dials CEAT does not fully control — the rubber-and-crude cost dial (moves daily, set by world markets) and the tyre-price dial (moves in lumpy steps, weeks or months later). When the cost dial jumps and the price dial hasn't yet turned, margins crater; when the cost dial falls and the price dial is still high, margins soar. The lag between them is why a tyre company's quarter is, in effect, a bet on natural rubber placed a quarter or two before the result is known. In FY26 that bet went badly, then well, inside twelve months. This is why volume growth can look healthy while profit halves — and why the very same order book can print a great quarter next time. No amount of good management removes the spread; it only lets CEAT ride it a little better than the next maker.

The takeaway is not that CEAT is uninvestable — it is that its quarterly profit line is the least reliable guide to the underlying business. To judge CEAT you look through the spread to the things that persist: the trajectory of the replacement and export mix, market-share trends by category, and whether through-cycle margins are structurally drifting higher as premiumisation takes hold. The rubber bet is noise around that signal — loud, unavoidable noise, but noise.

07

Moving up-market: premiumisation, OHT and Camso

The long game that is meant to lift through-cycle margins above the old value-brand base.

If the spread is the noise, CEAT's premiumisation is the signal it hopes will slowly win out. For a decade the company has worked to shed its cheap-and-cheerful image and climb the value chain — better products, a stronger brand, and a growing tilt to the highest-margin corners of the tyre world. Three moves define the campaign.

1 · Brand and product premiumisation

CEAT has invested heavily in brand — motorsport associations, cricket sponsorship (the CEAT Cricket Ratings and IPL-era visibility), and a sharper premium-product line in two-wheeler and passenger radials. The aim is straightforward: a consumer who perceives CEAT as a premium tyre pays more and switches less, which lifts realisations and defends the replacement margin. Winning OEM fitments on aspirational new car and SUV models reinforces the halo and seeds future aftermarket re-buys.

2 · Off-highway tyres — the high-margin specialty

OHT (agricultural and industrial tyres) is where CEAT most deliberately chases margin. These are lower-volume, engineered, export-oriented products with far less competition from mass-market players and pricing that reflects specialisation. Scaling OHT lifts the group's overall margin mix and diversifies revenue toward export markets that don't move with the Indian auto cycle.

3 · The Camso acquisition

In 2023 CEAT acquired Michelin's Camso business — a well-regarded brand in off-highway construction-compact and agricultural tyres and rubber tracks, with international reach. This was the boldest move in the premiumisation story: it bought CEAT a global specialty footprint, brand and customer relationships that would have taken years to build organically. Integration is a multi-year job — around USD 30 million earmarked for upstream capabilities (compound mixing, calendering), with completion targeted around the end of FY27 and the bulk of the margin benefit expected to show up from FY28.

Why this is the part that could re-rate the stock

The rubber spread makes CEAT's quarters unpredictable; premiumisation is a bet on its through-cycle average. If the two-wheeler stronghold holds, passenger share keeps climbing, and OHT-plus-Camso grows into a materially larger, higher-margin slice of the group, then CEAT's mid-cycle margin settles structurally higher than the old value-brand CEAT ever managed — and a higher, steadier margin on a bigger revenue base is exactly what turns a cyclical's moderate multiple into a re-rating. The catch is that this is slow, capital-hungry work: Camso's full margin payoff is still a couple of years out, and every rupee of capex has to earn its keep against a balance sheet already carrying ~1.5× Debt/EBITDA. Premiumisation is the reason to own CEAT for years; the rubber spread is the reason its next few quarters are anyone's guess.

08

The industry and the rivals

A four-way domestic contest, a specialist king, and the constant threat of cheap imports.

Indian tyres is a scaled, capital-intensive oligopoly. Building tyres at quality needs big plants, deep R&D and a national dealer network, so a handful of players dominate — but they compete hard, and each has a distinct personality. CEAT is a strong number three or four by size, punching above its weight on brand and mix.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two patterns matter. First, the domestic majors mostly rise and fall together on the rubber cycle — when input costs squeeze one, they squeeze all, so relative performance comes down to mix and brand rather than beating peers on the commodity. Second, the sharpest competition is category-specific: in truck & bus the enemy is cheap Chinese imports and thin pricing; in passenger premium it is the global brands (Michelin, Bridgestone); and in off-highway the benchmark to chase is Balkrishna, which has shown just how profitable a focused export specialist can be. CEAT's strategy — lean into two-wheeler strength, premiumise passenger, and scale OHT toward BKT-like economics — is essentially an attempt to spend more time in the profitable corners of that map and less in the bloody T&B one.

09

What actually drives the volumes

Beneath the rubber spread sits a real, cyclical demand story.

The spread decides margins; demand decides how many tyres cross the counter. And tyre demand, usefully, has two engines that don't move in lockstep — which is part of why the aftermarket cushions the cycle.

The mid-2025 GST rate cuts on vehicles gave CEAT a helpful demand tailwind — cheaper vehicles pull forward sales, which feeds both OEM fitments today and the replacement pool tomorrow. Q1 FY27 (June 2026) revenue of about ₹4,318 crore, up ~22% year-on-year, shows the top line still growing briskly even as margins do their rubber-driven dance.

The two-speed demand engine

New-vehicle sales are the flashy, cyclical engine — they roar in a boom and stall in a downturn, dragging OEM volumes with them. The replacement engine is the quiet, geared-down one: it runs off the millions of vehicles already on Indian roads, which keep wearing out tyres whether or not anyone is buying new cars this year. That is why a tyre maker with a big aftermarket franchise is more resilient than its OEM-heavy peers — and why CEAT's ~half-replacement mix is a genuine structural cushion under the volume line, even before you get to the premium brand's pricing power.

The market prices CEAT as a decent-quality cyclical — not cheap, not expensive — with the upside case resting on the premium mix and OHT/exports lifting through-cycle returns.

The one number that settles the thesis: the gross-margin spread, read with the mix

At ~24× trailing earnings CEAT is priced as a competent premiumising cyclical — richer than a pure commodity tyre-maker, far cheaper than a branded consumer staple. The metric that proves or breaks that pricing is CEAT's gross margin — realisation per tyre minus raw-material cost per kg — read through the rubber cycle rather than in any single quarter, alongside three companions: the replacement + OHT/export share of the mix (is it structurally rising?), volume growth by category (is the two-wheeler stronghold holding and passenger share climbing?), and net debt (is the Camso-and-capex bill staying under control?). If through-cycle gross margin drifts upward as the premium mix takes hold, CEAT earns a re-rating toward the branded end of its peer set. If margins keep simply oscillating around the old level with every rubber swing — growth but no structural improvement — then it stays a moderately-priced cyclical whose good quarters and bad quarters cancel out. The spread is the scoreboard; the mix is what's meant to bend it upward over time.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A premiumising tyre brand riding a commodity wave it cannot control.

CEAT enters FY27 with real momentum — a growing top line, a strengthening premium brand, a scaling off-highway business, and a demand tailwind from cheaper vehicles post-GST. The honest question is whether the premiumisation and OHT push can lift through-cycle margins enough to matter, against the ever-present headwind of a raw-material bill it does not set.

The bull case

  • Premiumisation working — a genuinely stronger brand, rising passenger share and a high-margin two-wheeler stronghold shifting the mix upward over time.
  • OHT + Camso optionality — a scaling, high-margin, export-led specialty business, with Camso's full margin benefit still to arrive (targeted from ~FY28).
  • Replacement-heavy mix — ~half of sales in the stickier, higher-margin aftermarket, cushioning the cycle and giving real pricing power.
  • Demand tailwinds — post-GST vehicle affordability, a growing vehicle parc, and export diversification all feeding volume.
  • Operating leverage on a falling rubber cycle — when input costs ease while prices hold, the same volumes can produce a step-change in profit.

The bear case

  • Raw-material volatility & the pricing lag — the recurring, dominant risk: a natural-rubber or crude spike CEAT can't pass on fast enough crushes margins, as FY26 showed.
  • Intense competition & imports — MRF, Apollo, JK, plus global majors and cheap Chinese truck-bus imports cap pricing, especially in commercial tyres.
  • Cyclicality — auto sales, freight and the replacement cycle all swing with the economy; a downturn hits volumes as well as the spread.
  • Capex & debt — heavy ongoing capacity spend and Camso integration keep net debt around ~1.5× EBITDA; returns must justify the outlay.
  • FX & trade risk — a bigger export/OHT mix brings currency swings and exposure to tariffs and anti-dumping actions abroad.
The bottom line

CEAT is two businesses stacked on one balance sheet: a slow, promising consumer-brand story — premiumise, grow the aftermarket, scale off-highway — sitting on top of a fast, brutal commodity engine that can make any single quarter look brilliant or dreadful regardless of how the brand is doing. The brand story is the reason to own it; the rubber spread is why you must not judge it a quarter at a time. The concrete milestones to watch are specific and dated: Camso's integration completing around end-FY27, with its margin benefit meant to show from FY28; and, every three months, whether CEAT's gross margin holds up as natural-rubber prices move — the single print that reveals whether pricing discipline and mix are finally winning the tug-of-war with the commodity cycle. Watch the spread each quarter and the mix each year; together they tell you whether CEAT is becoming a better tyre company or just a bigger one.
