ABB India, the arms-dealer of the electrification age

A complete walk through the company that quietly powers and automates India's factories, data-centres, metros and grids — the switchgear, motors, drives, control systems and robots that turn electricity into work. How an electrical-equipment business actually earns money, why order intake and backlog matter more than a single quarter's profit, the four engines (Electrification, Motion, Process Automation, Robotics), the capex supercycle it rides, the extraordinary margin re-rating that made it a market darling — and why one of India's most expensive capital-goods stocks trades where it does.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

ABB India makes the electrical and automation equipment that sits between the power socket and the machine — the switchgear and circuit breakers that distribute electricity safely inside a building or plant, the motors and variable-speed drives that spin pumps, fans, conveyors and trains, the control systems that run a refinery or a cement kiln, and the industrial robots that weld and assemble on a factory line. It is the Indian arm of ABB Ltd of Switzerland, a ~$35-billion global electrification-and-automation leader that owns 75% of it.

A note on the calendar first: unlike most Indian companies, ABB India reports on a calendar year (January–December), not an April–March fiscal year. Its latest full year is therefore CY2025 (ended 31 December 2025), and its most recent quarter is Q1 CY2026 (Jan–Mar 2026). All the headline numbers below are on that calendar basis.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A record-orders year, but with margins cooling off a peak. CY2025 delivered its highest-ever orders (₹14,115 Cr, +8%) and revenue (₹13,203 Cr, +8%), yet reported profit actually fell: PAT was ₹1,669 Cr (−11%) as the PBT margin normalised from an exceptional ~20.6% in CY2024 to 16.9%, hit by metal-cost inflation, currency and new labour-code costs. That single divergence — booming demand, softening margin — is the crux of the recent debate.
  • The order book is the forward-looking gauge. ABB India ended CY2025 with a ₹10,471 Cr backlog (+12%), and by Q1 CY2026 that had risen to ₹11,094 Cr (+17%) on 25% order growth. For an equipment-and-projects business, that pipeline — not this quarter's sales — is what tells you how full the factories will be.
  • It's a prime bet on India's electrification & automation "supercycle." Data-centres, railways and metros, renewables, grid modernisation and "China+1" manufacturing are all pouring electrical and automation spend into exactly ABB India's product shelves. That structural tailwind — plus a debt-free, net-cash, high-return balance sheet — is why the market pays a punchy ~75–95× earnings for it, one of the richest multiples in Indian capital goods and the single most contested feature of the stock.
In plain terms

Think of ABB India as the "arms dealer" of the electrification age — it doesn't build the data-centre or run the railway, it sells the essential kit that everyone building those things has to buy. Whenever India adds a factory, a metro line, a data-centre or a solar park, someone needs switchgear to distribute the power, motors and drives to move things, and controls or robots to run the process — and ABB is one of a handful of trusted names for the high-end versions of all of it. The beauty of the model is that it is capital-light: ABB India owns very little debt, generates lots of cash, earns ~30% on its capital, and lets a Swiss parent fund the R&D. The catch is the price — the market has already priced in years of this good behaviour, so the stock leaves little room for disappointment.

01

What ABB India actually sells

No engineering degree required — just follow the electricity from the grid to the machine.

The simplest way to understand ABB is to follow a unit of electricity on its journey inside a factory, a building or an infrastructure asset. At each step, something has to distribute it, convert it into motion, coordinate it, or automate the work it powers — and ABB makes the equipment for every one of those steps.

The journey of a unit of electricity — and what ABB sells at each step
StepThe job to be doneWhat ABB makes for it
1. Distribute it safelyBring grid power into a plant/building and split it up without fires, faults or blackouts.Switchgear, circuit breakers, low- & medium-voltage panels, smart power products — the "electrification" shelf.
2. Turn it into motionSpin pumps, fans, compressors, conveyors, lifts and trains — efficiently and at controllable speed.Electric motors, variable-speed drives, traction converters for rail/metro — the "motion" shelf.
3. Coordinate the processRun a whole refinery, cement kiln, mine or ship as one automated, measured, safe system.Distributed control systems (DCS), instrumentation, measurement & analytics — the "process automation" shelf.

Why this framing matters

Almost everything ABB India makes is a "pick-and-shovel" input — it is essential, technical, and bought by someone else who is doing the visible work (building the data-centre, running the mine, laying the metro). That gives ABB two structural advantages. First, it is diversified across ~23 end-market segments — data-centres, autos, metals, cement, food & beverage, railways, renewables, buildings, chemicals — so no single customer or cycle makes or breaks it. Second, much of the product is "short-cycle": standardised switchgear, motors and drives that a distributor or contractor orders, ABB ships within weeks, and books quickly — as opposed to "long-cycle" project work (a full refinery control system) that takes years. That mix of fast-turning products and slower, chunkier projects is central to how the business behaves, and we'll return to it.

02

The four business areas, broken into parts

ABB India runs as four distinct businesses under one roof — each with its own customers, cycle and margin profile.

ABB India reports as four business areas (which it calls segments): Electrification, Motion, Process Automation, and Robotics & Discrete Automation. They are genuinely different businesses — a switchgear factory and an industrial-robot line share very little except the brand — so it pays to understand each one on its own terms.

Electrification — the biggest engine, and the data-centre story

This is the largest business: low- and medium-voltage products — circuit breakers, switchgear, panels, protection devices, and "smart power" gear that distributes electricity safely inside a building, factory, data-centre or grid substation. In CY2025 it was the growth star: orders up ~43%, with much of that surge driven by data-centres (which need enormous quantities of reliable power-distribution kit), building electrification and infrastructure. Revenue grew a more modest ~6% (orders lead revenue by quarters/years), and it earns the best divisional margin — a ~21% PBIT — because the product is high-mix, premium and increasingly localised. It ended CY2025 with a ~₹3,300 Cr order backlog.

Motion — motors, drives and the railway kicker

Motion makes electric motors and variable-speed drives — the workhorses that turn electricity into controlled mechanical movement in pumps, fans, compressors, conveyors, cranes and lifts — plus traction converters for railways and metros. Energy-efficiency rules and industrial upgrading push customers toward premium, efficient drives, which is good for pricing. In CY2025, Motion won ~25% order growth, boosted by large mobility/transportation (rail & metro) orders, grew revenue ~7%, and carried the biggest divisional backlog at ₹4,200 Cr — including multi-year railway orders. Its PBIT margin sits around ~16.5%. A service business (maintaining the installed base of motors and drives) sits alongside it, providing steadier annuity-like revenue.

Process Automation — the brains of heavy industry

Process Automation sells the control systems, instrumentation and measurement & analytics that run continuous, heavy-process industries — oil & gas, metals, mining, cement, chemicals, pulp & paper, and marine. These are big, engineered, long-cycle projects: an order can take years to design, build and commission. That makes the division lumpier — after several subdued quarters it staged a strong ~34% order recovery in CY2025 as delayed project decisions finally landed. Its PBIT margin is the lowest of the four at ~14.7%, reflecting the competitive, project-tendered nature of the work. This is the classic "long-cycle" counterweight to the fast-turning Electrification and Motion products.

Robotics & Discrete Automation — small, but strategically live

The smallest business: industrial robots and factory-automation cells, sold mainly to automotive OEMs and suppliers, electronics and general manufacturing. In CY2025 it benefited from a large automotive-sector order (quarterly revenue reached ~₹570 Cr in Q4) and rebuilt its backlog. It is a modest slice of ABB India today, but it sits at the centre of a global corporate event — the ABB parent's plan to spin off its Robotics division worldwide — which we unpack in section 08.

03

Orders, backlog and how it earns

The single most important habit for reading a capital-goods company: watch orders, not just sales.

A products-and-projects company like ABB earns in a specific rhythm. First a customer places an order (order intake / bookings). That order enters the backlog (also called order book) — work committed but not yet delivered. Over the following weeks (for short-cycle products) or years (for long-cycle projects), ABB manufactures and delivers it, and only then books it as revenue. So the three numbers describe three different points in time.

The three numbers, and what each one tells you
MetricWhat it isCY2025

Order backlogCommitted work not yet delivered — the revenue-visibility cushion for coming quarters.₹10,471 Cr (+12%); ₹11,094 Cr by Mar '26
RevenueOrders actually delivered & billed this period — the trailing result.₹13,203 Cr (+8%, record)
Why order intake is the number to watch

Revenue tells you what already happened; order intake tells you what's coming. When ABB India books record orders — especially the +52% surge in Q4 CY2025 and +25% again in Q1 CY2026 — it is a sign that customers across data-centres, railways, metals and buildings are actively committing capital right now. Those orders sit in backlog and convert into revenue over the next several quarters, giving the company visibility that a pure trading business never has. The book-to-bill ratio (orders ÷ revenue) running above 1.0 means the pipeline is filling faster than it's being drained — the healthy state for a capital-goods company in an up-cycle. The flip side: a slowdown in order intake is the earliest warning that the cycle is turning, long before it shows in revenue — which is exactly why analysts obsess over the quarterly order line.

04

The company and its parent

A century-old MNC franchise, 75%-owned by a Swiss engineering giant.

ABB India traces its roots to the electrical businesses that have operated in India for over a century, consolidated over time under the global ABB brand (itself formed by the 1988 merger of Sweden's ASEA and Switzerland's Brown, Boveri & Cie). Today it is a listed Indian subsidiary of ABB Ltd, Switzerland — a ~$35-billion revenue global leader in electrification and automation — which holds exactly 75% via ABB Asea Brown Boveri Ltd. That 75% is the maximum a promoter can hold while keeping the mandatory 25% public float, so the parent is effectively "all-in" on the listed entity.

A cleaner, more focused company than a decade ago

An important piece of context: the ABB India you see today is deliberately narrower than it once was. In 2019–2020 the parent globally sold its Power Grids business to Hitachi; in India, that business was carved out and separately listed as Hitachi Energy India (NSE: POWERINDIA). So the giant transformers, HVDC and high-voltage grid equipment now sit in Hitachi Energy India, not ABB India. What remains in ABB India is the leaner, higher-return, faster-turning electrification-and-automation portfolio — which is part of why its margins and returns re-rated so sharply afterwards.

Ownership & balance sheet

  • Promoter: ABB Ltd, Switzerland — 75.0% (via ABB Asea Brown Boveri Ltd). Institutions hold the rest: FIIs ~8%, DIIs ~9%, public ~7.5%.
  • Balance sheet: essentially debt-free / net cash, sitting on ~₹5,694 Cr of cash at end-CY2025 — the expansion, dividends and working capital are all self-funded.
  • Governance nuance: as a 75%-owned MNC subsidiary, ABB India pays royalties/technology fees to the parent for access to global R&D and IP, and the parent appoints board delegates. This is a genuine moat (world-class technology on tap) but also a related-party dynamic minority shareholders should keep in view.
05

The electrification & automation supercycle

The defining structural force — why India's build-out means a multi-year demand wave for exactly what ABB sells.

ABB India's biggest tailwind isn't a single contract — it's a structural shift in where India is spending money. After a decade of muted private investment, India is in the early years of a broad capex revival, and — crucially — the kind of capex being deployed is unusually electrical- and automation-intensive. Each of the drivers below lands directly on ABB's product shelves.

Railways & metrosNationwide metro expansion, rail electrification and rolling-stock upgrades.Traction converters, drives & motors (Motion) — multi-year railway orders in backlog.

The mechanism — why "electrification" is a decade-long demand, not a fad

Here's the chain of logic. Everything modern runs on electricity, and the world is electrifying more of it — transport, heat, industry, computing. Every new electrified thing has to be distributed to safely (switchgear), moved by (motors & drives), and controlled (automation). India, specifically, is doing three of these at once: building brand-new capacity (factories, data-centres, metros), upgrading old capacity to be more efficient (a regulatory and cost push toward premium drives and motors), and adding the clean-power infrastructure to feed it all. Because these projects run for years and roll out region by region, the demand isn't a one-year spike — it's a sustained, multi-year wave. ABB is positioned as a toll-collector on that wave: it doesn't have to pick which data-centre or metro wins, it just has to be the trusted supplier of the electrical guts that all of them need.

Short-cycle vs long-cycle — why the order mix matters

Not all of this demand behaves the same way, and understanding the split is key to reading ABB:

  • Short-cycle (much of Electrification & Motion products) — standardised switchgear, motors, drives ordered through distributors and contractors, shipped in weeks. This is high-velocity, high-margin, book-and-ship business that tracks the broad economy and responds fast to demand. It's the part that scales beautifully and drove the margin re-rating.
  • Long-cycle (Process Automation, large mobility/rail projects) — big engineered systems tendered competitively and delivered over years. Lumpier, lower-margin, but with long backlog visibility. This is the ballast that smooths (and occasionally distorts) the quarterly picture.

The ideal state — and roughly where ABB India sits in this up-cycle — is short-cycle products humming (economy-wide demand strong) while long-cycle projects refill the backlog (as they did with the +34% Process Automation order recovery in CY2025). When both fire together, revenue and margins can rise at the same time.

The cyclicality caveat: a capex supercycle is still a cycle. Private investment can slow, project decisions can be deferred (as they were in Process Automation for several quarters), and a global or domestic downturn would hit short-cycle products fast. The structural story is real and long, but ABB's demand is not immune to the economic weather — a point the valuation section returns to.
06

The margin-transformation story

The reason ABB India re-rated from an ordinary MNC engineering stock into a market darling.

If one chart explains why ABB India's share price multiplied, it's the margin chart. For years ABB India was a decent, slow, single-digit-margin engineering company. Then, from roughly 2020 onward, its operating margin roughly doubled — and profit growth vastly outran revenue growth. That transformation, more than any single order, is what re-rated the stock.

Operating margin roughly doubled from ~12% (2020) to a ~20% peak (2024), then eased in CY2025 on metal-cost inflation, currency and labour-code costs. PBT margin followed the same path (20.6% in CY2024 → 16.9% in CY2025).
Why margins doubled — four mechanisms

(1) Mix & premiumisation. ABB steadily shifted toward higher-value, differentiated products (smart power, premium efficient drives, digital-enabled kit) and away from commoditised low-end work — richer mix means richer margin. (2) Pricing power. In an up-cycle with strong demand and a trusted brand, ABB could price for value rather than volume, and pass through cost inflation. (3) Localisation. With ~85% of what it sells in India now made in India, ABB slashed import costs, forex exposure and lead times — a structural margin gain. (4) Operating leverage from short-cycle velocity. Because factory and overhead costs are largely fixed, once volumes rose the extra sales dropped heavily to profit — and the fast-turning short-cycle products (section 05) amplified this. Stack the four and profit grew far faster than revenue for several years — the definition of a re-rating story.

The honest 2025 wrinkle: the same operating leverage works in reverse. In CY2025, metal-price inflation, an adverse currency move, and new labour-code-related costs compressed margins even as demand boomed — which is why record orders and record revenue still produced an ~11% fall in reported profit. Bulls call this a temporary normalisation off an unsustainable peak; bears note it shows the ~20% margins were partly cyclical, not permanent. Watch margin — not just orders — as the swing factor from here.
07

The parent moat, localisation & the global-sourcing angle

The Swiss parent is simultaneously ABB India's biggest competitive advantage and its trickiest governance question.

Being 75%-owned by a $35-billion global technology leader cuts two ways, and both matter to the investment case. On one side, it is a formidable moat; on the other, it introduces a set of related-party nuances that keep the "MNC subsidiary" debate alive.

The moat side

  • Technology & brand on tap. ABB India gets access to the parent's global R&D, product platforms and the trusted ABB brand — a body of engineering it could never fund alone. In high-reliability equipment (data-centre power, traction, process controls), that brand trust is a real barrier to entry.
  • Localisation ("local-for-local"). ~85% of products sold in India are made in India, and ABB has invested >$230m over a decade in Indian plants, with a fresh ~$75m in 2026 (on top of ~$35m in 2025) expanding Nashik switchgear/circuit-breakers, Bengaluru drives & motors, and new R&D/test labs in Hyderabad and Bengaluru. Localisation lowers cost, cuts forex risk and speeds delivery — directly feeding the margin story.
  • Export / global-sourcing optionality. ABB India increasingly acts as a manufacturing and sourcing hub for the parent's global network — exporting switchgear, motors and components. As "China+1" reshapes global supply chains, this export angle is a growing, higher-utilisation growth lever.

The governance side

  • Royalty / technology fees. ABB India pays the parent for R&D and IP access. It's fair value for real technology, but it's a recurring cash outflow to the majority owner that minorities don't control — worth monitoring as a share of profit.
  • 75% cap & thin float. With the parent at the 75% ceiling, only ~25% floats publicly; combined with the premium multiple, the stock can be volatile and liquidity-sensitive.
  • Parent-driven structural change. Big decisions — the Power Grids/Hitachi carve-out, the coming Robotics spin-off — are made at the Swiss parent level and flow down to the Indian entity, sometimes reshaping what "ABB India" even contains.
In plain terms

The parent relationship is best thought of as a franchise deal. ABB India gets the crown jewels — world-class technology, a globally trusted brand, and now a role as a low-cost export base — without having to invent any of it. In exchange, it pays royalties, shares governance with a controlling owner, and lives with decisions taken in Zurich. For most of ABB India's history the moat has clearly outweighed the friction — the technology access is exactly what lets it charge premium prices and earn 30% on capital. The friction only becomes a live issue at the margin: if royalties creep up, or a parent-level restructuring disadvantages the listed minority. So far, the balance has been strongly favourable to shareholders.

08

The robotics spin-off — what it is, and what it means for ABB India

A global ABB Ltd corporate event that touches ABB India's smallest segment — but is more noise than substance for the Indian entity.

In 2025 the parent ABB Ltd announced plans to spin off its entire global Robotics division into a separately listed company. This is a group-level strategic move, and because ABB India has a (small) Robotics & Discrete Automation business, it's worth understanding — while being clear about how limited the direct impact on the Indian listed company actually is.

The facts

  • What: ABB Ltd plans a 100% spin-off of its Robotics division as a standalone listed company (working name "ABB Robotics"), targeted to begin trading in Q2 2026, subject to its 2026 AGM approval.
  • How: via a share distribution in-kind — ABB Ltd's own shareholders receive shares in the new Robotics entity pro-rata; it is not a cash sale.
  • Scale (global): Robotics had ~$2.3bn revenue in 2024 (~7% of the ABB Group), ~7,000 employees and a ~12% operational EBITA margin.
  • Related re-org: from Q1 2026, the Machine Automation division (which together with Robotics formed the "Robotics & Discrete Automation" business area) is being folded into Process Automation globally.
What it actually means for the Indian listed company

For ABB India shareholders, the near-term reality is modest. The spin-off is a decision about the parent's global structure and the parent's shareholders. ABB India's own Robotics & Discrete Automation segment is a small ~5–7% of revenue, so even a change in how it's owned or reported wouldn't move the Indian entity's overall numbers much. The plausible outcomes range from "ABB India's robotics business is carved out to align with the new global structure" to "it stays within ABB India but with a different supply/technology relationship." Either way, the two big engines that drive ABB India's value — Electrification and Motion — are entirely unaffected. The honest read: this is a headline worth tracking for structure and optics, but it is not a needle-mover for ABB India's earnings power. Watch for the company's own disclosures on exactly how (and whether) its Indian robotics unit is affected.

09

The industry and the rivals

A concentrated, premium end of a huge market — where a few global names dominate the high-value work.

India's electrical-equipment and automation market is enormous and has a long "tail" of local players making commodity switchgear, motors and panels. But the high-value, high-reliability, technology-intensive top end — where ABB competes — is concentrated among a handful of global names plus a few strong Indian challengers. That concentration is part of what protects ABB's pricing.

The competitive dynamic to watch

Two forces matter. First, at the premium end, competition is rational — a few trusted global brands, high engineering barriers, and customers who value reliability over the last rupee of price. That's why ABB can hold ~15–21% divisional margins. Second, at the commodity end (basic motors, standard switchgear), competition is fierce and pricing pressure is real, which caps how far margins can go and why localisation/mix-up-shift matter so much. The strategic contest of the next few years is whether ABB (and Siemens) can keep pulling their mix upward — into smart, digital, premium products — faster than domestic challengers move up from the commodity end. Add capacity influx across the industry riding the same capex wave, and pricing discipline becomes a key thing to monitor.

Record top-line, a margin dip, and a fortress balance sheet.

The business quality is not in question; the year's earnings dip and the price are.

How to read these numbers

Two things to hold in mind. First, the capital quality is genuinely elite: ABB earns ~30% on capital employed, funds a growing business with essentially no debt, sits on ~₹5,700 Cr of cash, and converts profit to cash superbly. This is about as clean as an industrial balance sheet gets. Second, reported profit can be noisy — a large "other income" line (interest on that cash pile, plus any one-offs/exceptional items in some periods) can inflate or distort a given year's bottom line, so it's wiser to judge ABB on operating profit and margins than on a single reported-PAT figure. The CY2025 story, cleanly told, is: demand up, operating margin down off a peak, balance sheet pristine.

The valuation debate — why the market pays up (and the risk in it)

One of the most expensive capital-goods names in India. Understanding why is the crux of the case.

No honest primer on ABB India can dodge the price. At a ~₹1.50 lakh crore market cap on ~₹1,669 Cr of CY2025 profit, the stock trades at roughly 75–95× earnings (the range reflects timing and whether you strip out other income) and around 18–19× book value — comfortably richer than Siemens, and multiples of L&T's ~30×. That is one of the loftiest valuations in Indian capital goods. Why does the market accept it, and where could it go wrong?

Why the market pays such a premium

Four things justify the multiple in bulls' eyes. (1) Structural growth. ABB is a diversified, pure-play proxy on the multi-year electrification/automation capex wave (section 05) — a rare way to own the whole theme, not one project. (2) Capital quality. ~30% ROCE, net cash, ~85% localisation and high cash conversion make it a "compounder" the market pays quality premiums for. (3) Margin runway. Bulls believe the CY2025 margin dip is temporary and that mix, localisation and operating leverage push margins back up — so today's P/E on dented earnings overstates the forward multiple. (4) Scarcity & parent tech. A world-class MNC franchise with a fortress balance sheet and a global technology parent is scarce on the Indian market, and investors pay up for it.

Why the price is also the biggest risk

The flip side is stark. At ~75–95× earnings, the stock is priced for years of flawless execution — continued strong order growth, a margin recovery back toward the peak, and no cyclical stumble. Several things could puncture that: a margin disappointment (CY2025 already showed margins can normalise hard on input costs/currency/labour), a slowdown in order intake if the capex cycle cools or projects defer (as Process Automation orders did for several quarters), the inherent lumpiness of a project business that the market may be extrapolating too smoothly, or simply a rotation away from expensive quality. When a stock this richly valued disappoints, the de-rating can be brutal — the multiple has a long way to compress.

The honest framing: the business quality here is not really in dispute — a debt-free, ~30%-ROCE, technology-leading electrification-and-automation champion riding a structural boom is about as good as industrial businesses get. What's in dispute is valuation and the durability of margins. A buyer at ~80× isn't betting on whether ABB does well; they're betting on whether it does even better, for longer, than an already-optimistic market expects — and that margins climb back to their peak. That's a demanding bar, especially the year after profit just fell.
12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely elite franchise — trading at a price that leaves little room for error.

ABB India is a rare thing: a diversified, high-return, debt-free electrification-and-automation leader positioned squarely in front of a multi-year, structurally-driven demand wave, with a global parent's technology behind it. The investment debate is almost entirely about valuation and the pace of the margin recovery, not business quality.

The bull case

  • Electrification supercycle — data-centres, railways/metros, renewables, grid and "China+1" manufacturing give a long, diversified demand runway across all four segments.
  • Record orders & rising backlog — ₹14,115 Cr CY2025 orders and ₹11,094 Cr backlog (Mar '26, +17%) give strong forward visibility.
  • Margin re-rating intact — mix, premiumisation, localisation (~85%) and operating leverage; CY2025 dip seen as a temporary cost/currency wobble.
  • Fortress balance sheet — net cash, ~₹5,694 Cr, ~30% ROCE, ~109% FCF conversion; self-funds a $75m expansion.
  • Parent moat & export optionality — world-class technology, trusted brand, and a growing role as a global sourcing/export hub.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — ~75–95× earnings, ~18–19× book; priced for perfection, brutal de-rating risk on any stumble.
  • Margins can normalise hard — CY2025 profit fell 11% on metal costs, currency & labour-code costs; the ~20% peak may have been partly cyclical.
  • Cyclicality & lumpiness — capex is a cycle; Process Automation orders can defer for quarters, and project revenue swings quarter-to-quarter.
  • MNC/related-party dynamics — royalties to the parent, a 75%-capped thin float, and parent-level restructurings (Robotics spin-off) shape the entity.
  • Competition & pricing — Siemens/Schneider at the premium end and domestic players climbing up-market; industry-wide capacity riding the same wave could pressure pricing.
The bottom line

ABB India is a best-in-class way to own India's electrification and automation build-out — a diversified, high-return, debt-free champion with genuine technology depth and a full order book. The business risk is modest; the valuation risk is very real, and CY2025 was a useful reminder that even a great franchise can see profit fall when margins normalise. Everything comes down to two numbers going forward: order intake (is the supercycle still converting into wins? — so far, emphatically yes) and margin (does it climb back toward the peak, or was the peak the anomaly?). Get both right and the compounding can grow into even a demanding multiple; stumble on either, and an ~80× stock has a long way to fall.
