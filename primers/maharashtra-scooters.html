00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're actually buying, before we go deep.

Despite the name, Maharashtra Scooters Limited is not really a manufacturing company at all. It is a Pune-based investment holding company inside the Bajaj group, and its worth is almost entirely a portfolio of Bajaj-group shares — stakes in Bajaj Auto, Bajaj Finserv, Bajaj Finance and Bajaj Holdings & Investment. It does still run a tiny legacy die-casting workshop (dies, jigs, fixtures and pressure-die-cast parts for the auto industry), but that operation is a rounding error next to the share portfolio. The "Scooters" in the name is a historical relic from the era when it assembled Bajaj scooters; that business is long gone.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The shares in the vault are worth far more than the company. The Bajaj stakes Maharashtra Scooters owns were valued at roughly ₹33,800 Cr (approx., end-2025), while the company's own stock-market value is only around ₹14,500 Cr. That means the market prices the whole company at little more than half of the shares it holds — a ~55% discount to net asset value (NAV). Owning a rupee of Maharashtra Scooters buys you well over two rupees of underlying Bajaj shares, on paper.
  • Its fortunes are the Bajaj companies' fortunes. Maharashtra Scooters has no independent growth engine. Its NAV rises and falls with the share prices of Bajaj Finance, Bajaj Auto, Bajaj Finserv and Bajaj Holdings, and its income is essentially the dividends those companies pay it. When the Bajaj financials and Bajaj Auto do well, so does Maharashtra Scooters; when they de-rate, its NAV shrinks with them.
  • The whole debate is the discount. The stock is cheap relative to what it owns — that is not in dispute. What is in dispute is whether that discount ever narrows (through bigger or special dividends, a buyback, a re-rating, or a corporate action) or whether it stays stubbornly wide forever. You can be completely right that the Bajaj stakes are worth ₹33,800 Cr and still make little money if the market keeps applying the same ~55% haircut for a decade.
In plain terms

Picture a locked glass cabinet holding ₹33,800 Cr of blue-chip Bajaj shares. The cabinet itself — Maharashtra Scooters — trades on the exchange for about ₹14,500 Cr. Why would anyone sell the cabinet for half of what's visibly inside it? Because you can't smash the glass: you don't control the shares, you can't sell them and pocket the cash (the company would owe tax if it did), the dividends only trickle out to you slowly, and the cabinet's stock barely trades so getting out is hard. The bet here is not "are the Bajaj shares valuable?" — plainly they are. The bet is "will the market ever pay closer to full price for the cabinet?" — and holding-company discounts have a long history of staying shut.

01

The holding-company discount, in plain English

The one idea you must understand before anything else here makes sense.

A holding company (or "holdco") is a business whose main asset is shares of other companies rather than factories, brands or a service it sells. Its underlying worth is simple to compute: add up the market value of all the stakes it owns (plus any cash, minus any debt). That number is its net asset value, or NAV — the "look-through" value of everything it holds. For Maharashtra Scooters, the NAV is essentially the market value of its Bajaj Auto, Bajaj Finserv, Bajaj Finance and Bajaj Holdings shares.

Here is the quirk that defines the entire category: holdcos almost always trade for less than their NAV. The gap between the two is the holding-company discount. If the shares inside are worth ₹100 and the holdco's own stock is worth ₹45, it trades at a 55% discount to NAV. This is not a Maharashtra Scooters oddity — it is a near-universal feature of listed holding companies around the world. Understanding why the discount exists is the whole game.

Why a holdco trades below the value of what it owns
The reasonWhat it means for you as a buyer
No direct accessYou own the holdco, not the underlying shares. You can't reach in and sell the Bajaj Finance stake to bank the gain — only the company's board can, and it generally won't.
A tax layer in betweenIf the company did sell its stakes to unlock cash, it would owe capital-gains tax first. So the "₹33,800 Cr" is a pre-tax, if-sold number; a rational buyer marks it down for the tax that would leak out on any realisation.
Dividends only trickleThe value comes back to you mainly as dividends the holdco chooses to pass on — a slow drip, and at the board's discretion, not the full earnings of the underlying companies.
No control, no sayYou can't force a sale, a merger or a special dividend. A minority holder is a passenger; the promoter drives.
Thin liquidityHoldco shares often trade in tiny volumes (few shares change hands, promoters own most), so large buyers demand a discount for the difficulty of getting in and out.
Complexity & opacityLayers of cross-holdings are harder to analyse than a clean operating business, so the market applies a "hassle" markdown.
The mental model that unlocks the whole primer

An investor's return in a holdco like this comes from three separate things, and it pays to keep them apart: (1) the dividend yield you collect while you wait; (2) the growth in NAV as the underlying Bajaj shares appreciate over time; and (3) any narrowing of the discount — the bonus you earn if the market decides to pay 40% or 30% below NAV instead of 55%. The first two you largely get "for free" by owning great Bajaj compounders at a markdown. The third is the lottery ticket — real if it happens, but entirely outside your control and historically slow to arrive. The risk that mirrors it: the discount can just as easily widen, quietly taxing your return even as the Bajaj shares rise.

So when you read that Maharashtra Scooters is "cheap," be precise about what that means. It is cheap relative to the assets it holds. It is not necessarily cheap as an investment, because you only capture that cheapness if the discount closes — or if you are content to own the Bajaj stakes at a permanent markdown and harvest the dividends. Both framings are legitimate; they are just very different bets, and the rest of this primer is about telling them apart.

02

The scooter company that makes essentially no scooters

Read this once and the misleading name stops mattering.

Maharashtra Scooters was set up in 1975 as a joint venture between the Western Maharashtra Development Corporation (a state body) and Bajaj Auto, to assemble Bajaj scooters — the iconic Chetak-era two-wheelers — for the western region. For years it did exactly that. But as the Indian two-wheeler market shifted from scooters to motorcycles and Bajaj consolidated its own manufacturing, the scooter-assembly business withered. What survived was a small die-casting unit and, far more importantly, a growing pile of Bajaj-group shares the company had accumulated over decades.

Today the "operating" business is almost invisible. Maharashtra Scooters manufactures dies, jigs, fixtures and pressure-die-cast components — mostly aluminium castings for the automobile industry. This generates only a few crore of revenue in a good quarter (revenue from operations was around ₹6.65 Cr in the March 2025 quarter, i.e. a run-rate of roughly ₹25–30 Cr a year). Set that against a share portfolio worth tens of thousands of crore and the point is unmistakable: the factory is a museum piece kept running; the balance sheet is the whole company.

The device that fits only this company

Maharashtra Scooters' die-casting workshop earns on the order of ₹25–30 Cr of revenue a year. The Bajaj shares sitting in its vault are worth roughly ₹33,800 Cr. In other words, the "business" the company is named after — and the only thing it actually makes — accounts for well under one-thousandth of what the enterprise is worth; the other 99.9%-plus is a stock portfolio. No amount of die-casting brilliance would move the value needle; no die-casting collapse would dent it either. When you buy this stock you are, for all practical purposes, buying a slice of Bajaj Finance, Bajaj Auto, Bajaj Finserv and Bajaj Holdings — and paying about 45 paise on the rupee for the privilege.

A genuine surprise: a company on the National Stock Exchange literally called "Maharashtra Scooters" has, in effect, no scooter business and no meaningful manufacturing business — and yet its shares have long been among the highest priced on the exchange (four-figure and, at times, five-figure rupee price tags per share). The market has quietly re-labelled it: it is classified and traded as a financial-services / investment-holding stock, not an auto-component maker. The name is the last thing about it that still says "scooters."

The portfolio is the company

What Maharashtra Scooters actually owns — and why the four names matter.

Regulatorily, Maharashtra Scooters is treated as a Core Investment Company (CIC) — a company that keeps the bulk of its assets (90%+) invested in group companies. Almost all of that is the four Bajaj-group listed stakes below, with the small remainder in debt and cash. To value Maharashtra Scooters, you value these holdings; everything else is a footnote.

The exact percentage stake Maharashtra Scooters owns of each company is modest — low single-digit percentages, in most cases well under a few percent — but because Bajaj Finance, Bajaj Auto and Bajaj Finserv are enormous companies (multi-lakh-crore market caps), even small percentages translate into thousands of crore of value. Two features of the portfolio are worth internalising:

  • Bajaj Finance is usually the swing factor. Over the past decade Bajaj Finance has been one of the great Indian compounders, and its appreciation has done the heavy lifting on Maharashtra Scooters' NAV.   • A holdco inside a holdco. One of the four stakes, Bajaj Holdings & Investment, is itself a holding company trading at its own discount. So part of Maharashtra Scooters' NAV is another discounted portfolio — a discount stacked on a discount, which is one reason some analysts argue the "true" look-through value is even more complex than a single NAV number suggests.

Why the portfolio's shape matters as much as its size

It is tempting to treat the NAV as one big blue-chip number, but its internal mix drives the risk. This is a portfolio tilted hard toward financials (Bajaj Finance + Bajaj Finserv + the finance-heavy Bajaj Holdings), with Bajaj Auto as the cyclical-industrial ballast. That means Maharashtra Scooters' NAV behaves less like "the Bajaj group" in the abstract and more like a leveraged bet on Indian consumer lending — it swells in a credit up-cycle and shrinks when NBFC valuations compress. An investor who wants "cheap Bajaj exposure" should know they are mostly buying discounted lender exposure, not a balanced conglomerate.

04

The group, and who controls it

A Bajaj-family company, controlled through Bajaj Holdings — with a famously thin float.

Maharashtra Scooters sits firmly inside the Bajaj group, controlled by the Bajaj family through the group's investment arm. The controlling shareholder is Bajaj Holdings & Investment Limited (BHIL), which owns 51% of Maharashtra Scooters. That structure — a Bajaj holdco owning a majority of another Bajaj holdco that in turn owns Bajaj operating shares — is exactly the kind of cross-holding web that produces (and perpetuates) holding-company discounts.

How it got here

  • 1975
    Incorporated as a joint venture between the Western Maharashtra Development Corporation (WMDC) and Bajaj Auto, to assemble Bajaj scooters for the region.
  • 1980s–90s
    Scooter assembly runs alongside a die-casting operation; over time the company accumulates Bajaj-group shareholdings that quietly become its most valuable asset.
  • 2000s
    Scooter demand collapses as India shifts to motorcycles; the manufacturing purpose fades while the investment portfolio keeps compounding as Bajaj group companies grow.
  • 2019
    WMDC exits its stake; Bajaj Holdings & Investment increases its holding to 51%, formally becoming the parent. Maharashtra Scooters is now unambiguously a Bajaj-group investment company.
  • 2024
    Recognised by exchanges as an eligible Investment Holding Company (IHC) for special-call auctions — a periodic price-discovery mechanism for very thinly traded holdco stocks.
  • FY25
    Consolidated PAT ~₹214 Cr (up ~7.5%), driven by dividend income from the Bajaj stakes; total dividend of ₹170/share declared (₹110 interim + ₹30 final + ₹30 special).

Ownership & the free-float problem

  • Promoter (Bajaj Holdings): 51%. This block is not going anywhere — it is the group's strategic cross-holding, held for control, not for sale.
  • Institutions, historically LIC-heavy: the Life Insurance Corporation (LIC) has for years been among the largest non-promoter shareholders of Maharashtra Scooters — a legacy institutional block that, like the promoter stake, tends to sit still. (Disclosed institutional holdings shift over time; recent filings should be checked directly, but the structural point holds.) Between a locked promoter half and a long-term insurer block, the genuinely tradeable free float is small.
  • The consequence — illiquidity: because so much of the register is held by parties who don't trade, only a thin slice of shares changes hands day to day. That thinness is itself a reason the discount stays wide: a large investor can't build or exit a position without moving the price, so the market demands a markdown for the difficulty.
Why the structure is the discount: Maharashtra Scooters is a majority-owned subsidiary of another Bajaj holdco, holding minority stakes in Bajaj operating companies, with a large chunk of its float in long-term institutional hands. Every layer of that sentence — subsidiary of a holdco, minority stakes, locked float — is a textbook reason for a persistent NAV discount. The structure that gives the company its blue-chip assets is the same structure that stops the market from paying full price for them.
05

The discount, decoded — price versus NAV

The single most important idea in the entire investment case — and the axis the whole stock turns on.

Forget earnings, forget margins, forget the die-casting unit. For Maharashtra Scooters, there are really only two numbers that matter, and the space between them is the entire investment thesis: the market value of the Bajaj stakes it owns (its NAV, ~₹33,800 Cr) and its own market capitalisation (~₹14,500 Cr). One divided by the other gives you the discount — currently around 55%. Watch how far apart they sit, and which way the gap is moving.

A ~55% discount is steep, even by holdco standards — many holding companies trade at 30–50% discounts, so Maharashtra Scooters sits at the wide end. That width is the bull's headline ("you're buying two rupees of Bajaj for one") and the bear's warning ("the market has kept it this wide for years — why would that change now?"). Both are looking at the same gap and drawing opposite conclusions.

The non-transferable mechanism: your return = yield + NAV growth ± the discount

An investor's total return in Maharashtra Scooters decomposes cleanly into three parts, and it is worth writing them out because the die-casting business appears in none of them. Part one: the dividend yield you pocket while holding (~1–1.5% on the current price). Part two: the compounding of the underlying Bajaj shares — if the portfolio's NAV grows 12% a year, your NAV-linked value grows with it. Part three, the wildcard: the change in the discount. Buy at a 55% discount and see it narrow to 40%, and you earn a one-time bonus of roughly a third on top of everything else. But the arrow runs both ways: if the discount widens from 55% to 65%, that same mechanism silently confiscates a chunk of your NAV gains. Your return is Bajaj's performance, plus the dividend, plus or minus the market's changing mood about the discount — never the factory.

06

What could actually narrow the discount

The catalysts — and why none of them is in a minority holder's hands.

A discount only closes when the market gets a concrete reason to pay more for the wrapper. For a holdco, there is a short, well-known list of such reasons — and the sobering reality is that every item on it is a corporate action controlled by the promoter, not something a minority shareholder can trigger. Here is the menu, from most to least likely.

The discount-narrowing toolkit — and who controls each lever
CatalystHow it narrows the discountRealistic?
Bigger / special dividendsPassing more of the underlying dividend income (and periodic specials) through to shareholders raises the cash you actually receive, lifting the price the market will pay. Maharashtra Scooters already does some of this (₹170/share in FY25, including a ₹30 special).Happens, but modestly — the payout is a fraction of look-through earnings.
Share buybackThe company buys back its own stock below NAV, which is instantly accretive to remaining holders and signals the board thinks the discount is too wide.Possible but rare for CICs; not announced as policy.

The uncomfortable truth about catalysts here

Read that table again and notice what it has in common: a minority investor cannot make any of it happen. You cannot call a buyback, you cannot force a special dividend, you cannot engineer a merger. You are betting that the Bajaj family and Bajaj Holdings — who already control 51% and have shown no urgency to unlock the discount — will one day decide to reward minority holders. They might; groups do occasionally simplify structures or lift payouts. But there is no announced roadmap, and a promoter comfortable with the status quo has little reason to change it. This is the defining tension: the value is real and visible, but the key to releasing it sits in someone else's pocket.

The most tangible, recurring lever is the dividend — and specifically the special dividend, which the company has paid in the past when the underlying stakes threw off large distributions. That is why a serious investor here tracks the payout policy closely: a step-change in dividends is the one discount-narrowing action that arrives regularly and predictably, even if a full re-rating never does.

07

The die-casting business — a rounding error worth understanding

The only thing the company "does," and why it barely registers.

For completeness — because it is technically the company's only operating activity — Maharashtra Scooters still runs a small die-casting and tooling unit. It manufactures dies, jigs, fixtures and pressure-die-cast components, chiefly aluminium castings supplied to the automobile industry (including, historically, Bajaj group requirements). It is a real, functioning workshop with real employees and real customers. It is also, in valuation terms, almost irrelevant.

The numbers make the point starkly. Operating revenue runs at only a few crore a quarter — around ₹6.65 Cr in Q4 FY25, or roughly ₹25–30 Cr annualised. Compare that to the company's total reported income of ~₹183 Cr in FY25, the vast majority of which is dividend and investment income from the Bajaj stakes, not manufacturing. Strip out the investment income and the "business" is a small-cap tooling shop attached to a very large investment portfolio.

08

The holdco-discount peer set

Maharashtra Scooters is one of a family of Indian discount-to-NAV names — comparison sharpens the case.

Maharashtra Scooters is not a one-off curiosity; it belongs to a small, well-defined class of Indian investment holding companies that trade at steep discounts to the value of what they own. Comparing it to its peers is the most useful way to judge whether its discount is unusually wide, fairly typical, or a trap — and to see that "wide discount" is a feature of the whole category, not a Maharashtra Scooters defect.

What the peer set teaches

Two lessons fall straight out of the comparison. First, wide discounts are the norm, not the exception — nearly every name here trades well below NAV, and has for years, which is a caution against assuming Maharashtra Scooters' gap is a temporary anomaly that "must" close. Second, the discount tends to be widest where the structure is most locked and the float thinnest — which is precisely Maharashtra Scooters' situation (51% promoter, big institutional block, tiny trading volume). The most direct rival for your money is arguably Bajaj Holdings itself: it offers overlapping Bajaj exposure at its own (often narrower) discount, with far more liquidity. Anyone drawn to Maharashtra Scooters for "cheap Bajaj" owes it to themselves to check whether the parent does the same job with less friction.

09

Dividends — what you actually earn while you wait

The one part of the return you can collect without the discount ever moving.

Because the discount may never narrow, the dividend is the part of the return you can actually bank in the meantime — the "carry" for holding a discounted portfolio. Maharashtra Scooters passes through a portion of the dividends it receives from the Bajaj stakes, and it has a track record of topping up with special dividends in good years.

In FY25 the company declared a total of ₹170 per share: a ₹110 interim dividend, a ₹30 final dividend, and a ₹30 special dividend. Against a share price in the region of ₹12,000–13,000, that is a dividend yield of only about 1–1.5% — respectable but not, on its own, a compelling income case. The more interesting feature is the special component: it signals that when the underlying Bajaj companies distribute large dividends (as Bajaj Auto in particular tends to), some of that windfall flows through to Maharashtra Scooters holders.

The honest arithmetic of "getting paid to wait"

Here is the sober version. A ~1–1.5% yield does not pay you handsomely to sit through a permanent discount — it is a trickle, not a torrent, precisely one of the reasons the discount exists (the market can't get at the full underlying earnings, only the fraction paid out). So the dividend is best seen as a modest sweetener on top of the real engine, which is NAV growth from the Bajaj compounders. If you are buying this stock for the yield, you have probably misunderstood it; if you are buying the discounted Bajaj exposure and treating the dividend as a small bonus that occasionally spikes with a special, you have it right. The payout is the floor of the return, not the case for it.

For an income-focused reader the practical takeaway is simple: track the payout ratio and any special dividends closely, because a durable increase in what the company distributes is both the most tangible catalyst for the stock and the most reliable cash you will see from it while the discount does whatever it is going to do.

Financials & valuation

A company you value on its balance sheet, not its income statement.

Almost everything about how you'd normally value a company breaks down here. There is no revenue growth to model, no margin trend that matters, no operating leverage. The income statement is essentially a dividend-collection ledger: FY25 total income of ~₹183 Cr and consolidated PAT of ~₹214 Cr are overwhelmingly the dividends the Bajaj stakes paid, not the product of any operating business. To value Maharashtra Scooters you value the balance sheet — the portfolio — and then argue about the discount.

So the valuation exercise is really a debate about the discount, expressed two ways. The bull framing: at ~55% below NAV, you are buying a basket of Bajaj compounders — led by one of India's best consumer lenders — at roughly 45 paise on the rupee, collecting a dividend, and holding a free option on the discount ever narrowing. The bear framing: the discount has been wide for years, no catalyst is scheduled, the float is too thin to arbitrage it, and you may sit for a decade being "right on NAV, wrong on the discount" while more liquid ways to own Bajaj compound faster.

The one number that settles the thesis

For most companies the settling metric is operational — occupancy, order conversion, same-store sales. Here it is unusual and threefold, but it reduces to one axis: the discount-to-NAV and its direction over time, read alongside the dividend/special-dividend payout (the cash you collect while you wait) and the underlying Bajaj companies' own performance (which sets the NAV you're discounting). If the discount is grinding narrower and the payout is rising while Bajaj Finance and Bajaj Auto keep compounding, the thesis is working on all three cylinders. If the Bajaj names de-rate and the discount stays pinned near 55%, you get the worst of both — a shrinking NAV discounted just as harshly as before. Everything else about this company — the name, the factory, the quarterly PAT line — is noise. The scoreboard is: NAV direction, discount direction, dividend paid.

Valuation here is unusually sensitive to the moment you measure it. Both the NAV (~₹33,800 Cr) and the market cap (~₹14,500 Cr), and therefore the ~55% discount, move every trading day with the prices of Bajaj Finance, Bajaj Auto, Bajaj Finserv and Bajaj Holdings. Treat all three figures as approximate snapshots, not fixed truths, and re-derive them from the latest share prices and the company's disclosed holdings before relying on any single number.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

Great assets, a stubborn discount, and a catalyst that isn't yours to pull.

Maharashtra Scooters is a rare thing: a stock where the assets are unambiguously excellent and unambiguously cheap, and yet the investment case is genuinely hard. Owning it is a wager that a deep, visible discount to a portfolio of Bajaj blue-chips will eventually be rewarded — through dividends, a corporate action, or a re-rating — rather than persisting indefinitely. That is a real bet with a real edge and a real, well-documented failure mode.

The risks, laid out plainly

  • The perpetual-discount risk (the defining one). Holding-company discounts are notoriously sticky. You can be exactly right that the Bajaj stakes are worth ₹33,800 Cr and still earn poorly for years if the market never pays more than 45 paise on the rupee. "Right on NAV, wrong on the discount" is the single most likely way this position disappoints.
  • Total dependence on the Bajaj companies. There is no independent growth engine. If Bajaj Finance, Bajaj Auto or Bajaj Finserv de-rate, the NAV falls with them — and the finance-heavy tilt means an NBFC valuation reset hits especially hard.
  • Low liquidity / thin float. With Bajaj Holdings at 51% and long-term institutions (LIC historically among them) holding much of the rest, the tradeable float is small — hard to build a position, harder to exit, and a structural reason the discount stays wide.
  • No control, corporate-action dependence. Only the promoter can trigger the buybacks, special dividends or mergers that would narrow the discount — and there is no announced plan to do so. A minority holder waits on decisions they can't influence.
  • Tax & structure friction. The NAV is a pre-tax, if-sold figure; unlocking it would trigger capital-gains tax, so the "full" value can never quite be realised even in a wind-up — a permanent, rational reason for some discount to exist.

The bull case

  • Bajaj compounders at a markdown — a basket of high-quality Bajaj-group shares for roughly 45 paise on the rupee of NAV.
  • Wide starting discount — at ~55%, near the wide end even for holdcos; more room to compress than most peers if sentiment turns.
  • Dividend carry, with specials — a modest but real yield you collect while waiting, occasionally topped up by special dividends when Bajaj distributes big.
  • NAV growth does the work — even with a static discount, if the underlying Bajaj stakes compound, so does your value.
  • Optionality on a catalyst — any group simplification, buyback or payout step-change is upside you're not paying for.

The bear case

  • The discount may never close — the category's history says wide discounts persist for years; the edge can stay theoretical.
  • Concentrated, financials-heavy NAV — outsized exposure to Bajaj Finance means a lending down-cycle hits the NAV disproportionately.
  • You can't force the value out — no control, no scheduled catalyst; you rely entirely on the promoter's goodwill and timing.
  • Illiquidity trap — thin float makes both entry and exit costly, and deters the very institutions that might re-rate it.
  • A cleaner alternative exists — Bajaj Holdings offers overlapping exposure with more liquidity, and direct ownership of the underlying names avoids the discount entirely.
The bottom line

Maharashtra Scooters is a locked cabinet of Bajaj blue-chips priced at little more than half of what's inside — a proposition that is either a bargain or a value trap depending on one thing the buyer cannot control: whether the glass ever opens. The assets will look after themselves; Bajaj Finance, Bajaj Auto and Bajaj Finserv are among the market's better franchises, and simply owning them at a discount, collecting the dividend, and letting the NAV compound is a defensible, unglamorous plan. What you must not do is buy it expecting the ~55% gap to close on your schedule — it has stayed wide for years and answers to no one but the Bajaj family. The next thing to watch is concrete and checkable: the FY26 dividend and any special declaration, and whether the discount is drifting narrower or wider each time the Bajaj stakes are re-priced. Those two prints, quarter after quarter, tell you whether you own a compounder at a discount or a portfolio quietly frozen behind glass.
