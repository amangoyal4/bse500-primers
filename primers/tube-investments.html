Tube Investments of India, the Murugappa engineering platform

A complete walk through the Murugappa Group's engineering flagship — how a maker of precision steel tubes, chains and metal parts quietly became a "platform" that rescued CG Power out of bankruptcy, bets on electric trucks and tractors, and now trades less like an auto-ancillary and more like a holding company whose crown jewel is worth more than the whole listed price.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Tube Investments of India (TII) is the engineering flagship of the Chennai-based Murugappa Group, one of India's oldest and most respected business houses. At its heart is a boringly excellent, cash-rich manufacturing business: precision steel tubes, metal-formed parts, chains and bicycles that go into cars, trucks, railways, tractors and infrastructure. But TII is no longer just an auto-ancillary — over the last few years it has turned itself into a capital-allocation "platform" that uses the cash from that core to build and buy a portfolio of new growth businesses.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A high-return, cash-generative core. The standalone business (tubes + metal-formed + mobility) did ~₹8,556 Cr of revenue in FY26 at a pre-tax return on invested capital of ~44% and converted essentially 100% of profit into free cash flow. This is the engine that funds everything else.
  • The marquee bet paid off spectacularly. In 2020 TII led the rescue of CG Power & Industrial Solutions out of insolvency. CG Power — electric motors, transformers, railways, and now semiconductor packaging (OSAT) — is today worth ~₹1.5 lakh crore. TII's ~53% stake in it is worth more than TII's own entire market capitalisation. That single fact reframes the whole company as a holding-cum-operating entity.
  • The new bets burn cash today. The platform strategy — TI Clean Mobility (electric 3-wheelers, trucks, tractors under the "Montra" brand), TI Medical, a CDMO venture and more — is loss-making and investment-heavy. The profitable core funds these; the debate is whether they become the next CG Power or a drag on returns.
In plain terms

Think of TII as a two-headed animal. Head one is a real, profitable factory business that makes the tubes and metal parts inside your car and the chains on your bicycle — steady, cash-spinning, high return on capital. Head two is a disciplined investor that takes that cash and plants it into new businesses, hoping to grow the next big thing. Its greatest planting — CG Power, bought for a song out of bankruptcy — turned into a giant. Now it's trying to repeat the trick with electric vehicles and medical devices, which lose money today. So valuing TII isn't about one P/E ratio; it's about adding up the parts: the core factory, plus its slice of CG Power, plus a pile of loss-making-but-optional bets — and deciding what the whole collection is worth.

01

The two things TII really is

Why an analyst never treats this as a plain auto-parts stock.

The single biggest mistake a first-time observer makes with TII is filing it under "auto components" and stopping there. It is an auto-components maker — a very good one — but that describes maybe a third of what drives the share price. To understand TII you have to hold two ideas at once: the operating company and the capital allocator.

Idea one — the operating company (the "core")

This is the historic TII: a manufacturer of precision steel tubes and strips, tubular and metal-formed components, chains and bicycles, sold to automakers, railways, and industrial customers. It is reported as the standalone entity, and it is genuinely excellent — high margins for a metals-bending business, extraordinary returns on capital (~44% pre-tax ROIC), and near-perfect cash conversion. Nothing exotic; it just executes.

Idea two — the capital allocator (the "platform")

Around 2020, under managing director Vellayan Subbiah, TII articulated a strategy sometimes described internally as "TI 2.0" and "TI 3.0": take the surplus cash the core throws off and deploy it, venture-style, into a portfolio of new businesses — some built from scratch, some acquired, some rescued. The results are held in subsidiaries and associates and only show up in the consolidated accounts. CG Power is the famous one; TI Clean Mobility, TI Medical, 3xper Innoventure (CDMO), Lotus Surgicals and others are the newer bets.

Standalone vs consolidated — read this once and the accounts click

Standalone = just the parent factory business (tubes, metal-formed, bicycles). It did ~₹8,556 Cr revenue and ~₹827 Cr net profit in FY26. Consolidated = the parent plus the subsidiaries it controls, added together line-by-line, with CG Power (a majority-owned, listed subsidiary) folded in. That's why consolidated revenue is a much larger ~₹22,847 Cr — most of the difference is CG Power's own sales flowing up. When you read a TII headline, always check which number it is: the core's health lives in standalone; the group's scale and CG Power's contribution live in consolidated. Confusing the two is the classic error.

A quirk worth flagging early: in FY26 the standalone PAT actually fell ~36% to ~₹827 Cr — but not because the factory got worse. It was a non-cash accounting swing: a "fair-value gain" on compulsorily-convertible preference shares (CCPS) TII holds in a subsidiary collapsed from ₹569 Cr (FY25) to just ₹6.8 Cr (FY26). Strip out that one-off and the underlying core actually grew — standalone pre-tax profit before exceptionals rose to ~₹1,099 Cr. This is a textbook example of why headline PAT can mislead on a platform company.
02

The company and who controls it

A 75-year-old franchise, a demerger, and the Murugappa family behind it.

TII's roots go back to 1949 and a joint venture between the Murugappa Group of Chennai and the original Tube Investments Group of the UK. The bicycle came first (the "BSA" and "Hercules" cycles, later the Diamond chain brand), then the steel tubes to make bicycle frames, then the wider engineering business. The company you buy today is the product of a 2017 corporate demerger that separated the manufacturing business into the listed entity.

  • 1955–60

  • 2012

  • 2017
    Demerger (NCLT-sanctioned): the manufacturing business is vested in the present listed company; the investment/finance arm goes to what became Cholamandalam Financial Holdings.
  • Aug–Nov 2020
    The CG Power rescue. TII acquires a controlling ~50.6% stake in the insolvent, fraud-hit CG Power for a modest ~₹700 Cr — the deal that redefines the company.
  • 2022–24
    Launches the "TI 2.0" platform: builds TI Clean Mobility (Montra EVs), enters medical devices (Lotus Surgicals with Premji Invest), MIM/metal injection moulding (Orange Koi), and a CDMO venture (3xper).
  • FY26
    Consolidated revenue crosses ₹22,800 Cr; CG Power's market value passes ₹1.5 lakh crore; the EV business scales (and burns cash) as the platform thesis is tested.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter — the Murugappa family / Ambadi group: the promoter block holds ~44% of TII (Mar 2026). The controlling family is one of India's most reputed industrial dynasties, with a portfolio spanning Carborundum Universal, Coromandel International, EID Parry, Cholamandalam and Wendt, alongside TII and (via TII) CG Power.
  • The architect: Vellayan Subbiah, TII's managing director and now a central Murugappa figure, is widely credited with the platform strategy and the CG Power turnaround. Reports in early 2026 flagged a family ownership realignment — consolidating around CG Power and TII while paring other stakes — a governance factor worth watching.
  • Institutional interest is heavy: FIIs ~21% and DIIs ~22% (Mar 2026) — a well-owned, well-covered large-cap, not a neglected small-cap.
  • Balance sheet: the core is effectively net-cash and self-funding; consolidated borrowings (~₹754 Cr) are modest relative to scale and largely sit in the growth subsidiaries.
03

The core engineering franchise, decoded

What the factory actually makes — and why customers can't easily switch away.

Before the platform story, TII was — and still is — one of India's best precision-engineering franchises. The core turns flat and coiled steel into high-tolerance tubes and metal parts that end up inside vehicles, trains, tractors and buildings. It sounds commoditised; it isn't. The value is in metallurgy, tight tolerances, and being designed into the customer's product.

The product families (jargon translated)

  • Precision steel tubes — the flagship. CDW (cold-drawn welded) and DOM (drawn-over-mandrel) tubes are precision tubes drawn to exact wall thickness and strength; they become propeller shafts, steering columns, shock absorbers, seat frames and other critical car and truck parts. ERW (electric-resistance-welded) tubes serve more general industrial use. This is high-value, spec-driven work — the tube has to survive fatigue and crash loads.
  • Large-diameter & welded tubes — bigger tubes for construction, infrastructure, and general engineering (the more cyclical, commodity end).
  • Tubular & metal-formed components — TII doesn't just sell raw tube; it forms and fabricates it into finished sub-assemblies (e.g. car door-frame profiles, cold-rolled formed sections, fine blanking), moving up the value chain and stickiness ladder.
  • Chains & industrial products — the heritage Diamond chain business: automotive timing chains, industrial chains, and the fine-blanked parts that go with them.
  • Bicycles & fitness (the "Mobility" segment) — the original business: BSA, Hercules and Montra bicycles, e-cycles and fitness equipment. Small in profit terms, iconic in brand terms.
Why a tube maker earns 40%+ returns on capital

Two reasons. First, "designed-in" stickiness: once TII's tube is engineered into a carmaker's steering column or a tractor's axle, switching supplier means re-validating a safety-critical part — expensive and slow. That protects both volume and pricing. Second, asset productivity: TII runs its plants hard and keeps working capital tight, so it earns a lot of profit per rupee of capital tied up. The result is a business that looks humble (bending metal) but throws off cash like a much fancier one — precisely the cash that funds the platform. The vulnerability is that end-demand is cyclical: autos, trucks, tractors and construction all move with the economy, so volumes wobble even if the franchise doesn't.

Crucially, TII has deliberately diversified the demand base away from just passenger cars — into commercial vehicles, railways, tractors, aviation and industrial uses — so no single end-market controls its fate. Railways in particular is a structural tailwind (India's rolling-stock and infrastructure build-out), and it dovetails with CG Power's railway franchise.

The one number that matters in the core: ROIC

Forget the segment percentages for a second. The single most important core metric is return on invested capital, which ran ~44% pre-tax in FY26. That is exceptional for a metals business, and it's why TII can fund a platform: a business earning 44% on capital and converting all its profit to cash generates a large, reliable surplus every year that management can redeploy. If that ROIC ever erodes — via a tube-pricing war, a raw-steel squeeze, or a demand slump — the whole platform loses its fuel. So the core's health, not the flashy EV headlines, is the foundation to watch.

05

The "TI 2.0" platform strategy

How TII turned surplus cash into a portfolio of growth bets — the defining move.

This is the section that separates TII from every other tube-maker. Rather than pay out all its cash or chase incremental tube volume, management chose to become a capital-allocation platform: use the core's cash flow to seed or acquire a diversified portfolio of new businesses, each with the ambition to become large. It is, in spirit, a mini-conglomerate being built in real time — with CG Power as living proof the model can work.

The portfolio, business by business

  • CG Power & Industrial Solutions (~53% owned, listed) — the crown jewel; electricals, motors, transformers, railway traction and semiconductors. Covered in full in the next section.
  • TI Clean Mobility (Montra Electric) — the big new bet: electric 3-wheelers, small commercial vehicles, heavy trucks and e-tractors. High-growth, high-loss; ~₹3,000 Cr raised, cumulative investment building. Covered in section 07.
  • TI Medical / Lotus Surgicals — entry into medical devices (with Premji Invest as a partner), aiming to build an affordable, "Atmanirbhar" med-tech platform; cumulative investment ~₹265 Cr; guided to grow 15–20%/yr; recently added an IV-cannula facility (Medicura, Ambala).
  • 3xper Innoventure (CDMO) — a contract development & manufacturing venture (specialty chemicals); a Naidupet facility in final commissioning as of FY26.
  • Shanthi Gears (~70% owned, listed) — profitable industrial-gearbox maker; a steady, cash-generative bolt-on that pre-dates the platform era.
  • Orange Koi (~87% owned) — entry into metal injection moulding (MIM), a high-precision small-parts technology; a ~₹73 Cr tuck-in that widens the engineering toolkit.
Why do this at all? The capital-allocation logic

A great manufacturing business eventually faces a nice problem: it generates more cash than it can reinvest in its own factories at high returns. Management then has three choices — hoard it, hand it back to shareholders, or redeploy it into new businesses. TII chose the third, betting that its operating discipline and access to cheap distressed assets (like CG Power) let it earn better returns building a portfolio than by paying dividends. When it works — CG Power — the value creation dwarfs anything the core could have done alone. When it doesn't, it destroys returns and turns a clean compounder into a murky holding company. That tension — brilliant capital allocation vs. empire-building — is the central debate on TII, and reasonable investors sit on both sides.

06

CG Power — the crown jewel

The rescue that turned ~₹700 Cr into a stake worth more than TII itself.

In 2020, CG Power & Industrial Solutions was a wreck — a storied electricals company (the old Crompton Greaves industrial arm) hollowed out by accounting fraud and crushing debt, staring at insolvency. TII stepped in, injected capital, and took a controlling ~50.6% stake for roughly ₹700 Cr. What happened next is one of the great Indian turnaround stories — and it is now the biggest single driver of TII's value.

What CG Power does

  • Power Systems — high-voltage transformers and switchgear: core kit for the electricity grid, riding India's power-capex and transmission build-out.
  • Industrial Systems — electric motors and drives, where CG Power is a market leader; demand tracks factory automation and industrial growth.
  • Railways — traction motors, propulsion systems and control electronics for Indian Railways' rolling stock and electrification — a structural, multi-year order pipeline.
  • Semiconductors (OSAT) — the newest leg: CG Power's CG Semi joint venture (with Renesas and Stars Microelectronics) built one of India's first major semiconductor assembly-and-test (OSAT) plants at Sanand, Gujarat — a ~₹7,600 Cr project inaugurated in mid-2026. This is a bet on India's semiconductor localisation push.

Why one number reframes the whole stock

Do the arithmetic. CG Power's market cap is ~₹1.51 lakh crore. TII owns ~53% of it. That stake is therefore worth roughly ₹80,000 Cr at market value. But TII's own total market capitalisation is only ~₹57,600 Cr. In other words, TII's slice of CG Power alone is worth ~40% more than the entire market value of TII — which implies the market is assigning negative value to TII's core factory, its other subsidiaries, and its cash, once you net out CG Power. That is either a screaming anomaly or a rational "holding-company discount" (see section 08). Either way, it explains why no serious analyst looks at TII's P/E in isolation — the P/E is dominated by how CG Power's profits are consolidated, not by the tube business.

Consolidation nuance: because TII owns a majority of CG Power, CG Power is a subsidiary and is fully consolidated — its entire revenue flows into TII's ~₹22,847 Cr consolidated top line, with the ~47% it doesn't own stripped out as "minority interest" below. So consolidated revenue overstates "TII's" economic share of CG Power, while consolidated PAT is shown after removing minorities. This is exactly why the SOTP (market value of the stake) is a cleaner lens than the consolidated P&L for judging what the CG Power holding is worth.
07

The e-mobility bet and its cash burn

TI Clean Mobility — the boldest, riskiest chapter of the platform.

If CG Power is the platform's triumph, TI Clean Mobility is its live experiment. Under the "Montra Electric" brand, TII is trying to build a full-stack commercial-EV company from scratch — electric passenger and cargo 3-wheelers, small commercial vehicles, heavy electric trucks, and e-tractors. The ambition is large (a stated goal of a ~$1 billion revenue business by FY29–30); the near-term reality is heavy losses funded by the core.

Why they're willing to bleed money here

Commercial EVs are a genuine, large, structurally-growing market in India — last-mile 3-wheelers, intra-city cargo, and eventually trucks and tractors all electrifying. But it's a brutal, capital-hungry, low-margin fight in the early years: you must build products, a dealer/service network, financing partnerships and charging access before profits arrive. TII's bet is that its engineering DNA, balance-sheet muscle and patient capital let it win a durable position that a cash-strapped startup can't. The risk is equally clear: EVs could stay loss-making far longer than hoped, the addressable segments could commoditise, and the losses could compound into a real drag on group returns. This is the part of TII that most divides bulls (optionality on the next CG Power) from bears (a cash furnace bolted onto a great tube business).

The honest risk: unlike CG Power — a mature electricals business bought cheap and already profitable — TI Clean Mobility is a greenfield venture in a fiercely competitive category. There is no guarantee it becomes the next CG Power; it could just as easily be a multi-year, multi-thousand-crore investment that never earns its cost of capital. Watch two things each year: the trajectory of losses (are they narrowing toward EBITDA break-even?) and volume ramp (are trucks and tractors actually shipping?).

08

Sum-of-the-parts and the holding-company lens

The one framework you must use to value a platform company.

Because TII is a bundle of very different assets — a cash-machine factory, a majority stake in a listed giant, a majority stake in a listed gearbox company, and a clutch of loss-making ventures — you cannot value it with a single P/E. The right tool is a sum-of-the-parts (SOTP): value each piece the way it deserves, add them up, then apply a "holding-company discount" because owning assets through a listed holdco is worth a bit less than owning them directly.

TI Clean Mobility / Medical / CDMOHard to value — option value, or the capital invested; can be a dragContested; possibly negative near-term

What the SOTP is really telling you

The startling implication from section 06 — that TII's CG Power stake (~₹80,000 Cr) exceeds TII's whole market cap (~₹57,600 Cr) — is the market applying a large holding-company discount and/or discounting CG Power's own valuation. Why do holdcos trade below their asset value? Three reasons: (1) you can't get the underlying shares directly — you're a passenger; (2) there's a tax/leakage cost if the holdco ever sold and distributed; and (3) the market fears the cash will be redeployed into more bets (like EVs) rather than returned. So the bull sees a rupee of CG Power selling for well under a rupee inside TII — a value gap. The bear notes holdco discounts can persist for years, even widen, and that the loss-making ventures justify some of the discount. This gap — not the P/E — is the actual TII investment debate.

Method caveat: the ~₹80,000 Cr figure is simply ~53% of CG Power's market cap on the stated date; it moves every day with CG Power's share price and TII's exact holding (which has drifted down from ~58% since 2020 as CG Power issued shares). Different brokerages use different holdco discounts (20–35%) and different multiples on the core, so published SOTP target prices vary widely. Treat the SOTP here as a framework for thinking, not a precise valuation.

How to think about the ~87× P/E (don't take it at face value)

On a screen, TII shows a trailing P/E near 87× — eye-watering for an "auto-components" company. But that number is almost meaningless here, for two reasons. First, the denominator (consolidated PAT of ~₹1,118 Cr) is depressed by the young ventures' losses and includes only the after-minorities slice of CG Power's profit — it under-counts the economic value TII controls. Second, the value is dominated by the market price of the CG Power and Shanthi Gears stakes, which the P&L captures poorly. That's why analysts value TII on SOTP (section 08), typically ~24–35× on the standalone core plus the market value of listed subsidiaries less a 20–35% holdco discount. The reported P/E tells you TII is expensive; only the SOTP tells you whether it's expensive relative to what it owns.

Dividend: TII pays a modest, growing dividend — a final ₹1.50/share plus interims took FY26 to about ₹3.50/share — a low ~0.1% yield. That is deliberate: a platform company reinvesting into CG Power's expansion and the EV ramp keeps its cash rather than paying it out. Income investors should look elsewhere; TII is a compounding/optionality story, not a yield story.

The structural tailwinds — and why they matter

Several of India's biggest investment themes run through TII at once. Railways & infrastructure capex feeds both the tube core and CG Power's railway/transformer businesses. Power-grid build-out and factory automation feed CG Power's motors and transformers. Semiconductor localisation feeds CG Semi's OSAT plant. Commercial-vehicle electrification is the whole premise of Montra. And the "China+1" / import-substitution theme underpins the med-tech and MIM ventures. Few Indian companies sit at the confluence of this many national tailwinds — which is precisely the bull case for the platform. The flip side: it also means TII is exposed to more places where things can go wrong (a cyclical auto downturn, a power-capex pause, an EV price war, a semis-ramp delay), and it stretches management across a lot of very different fights at once.

Governance & group-structure watch: as a Murugappa promoter entity that both operates factories and holds large stakes in other listed group/associate companies, TII carries the usual holding-company governance questions — capital-allocation discipline, related-party dealings, and the family's periodic ownership realignments (flagged in early 2026). None of this is a red flag today; it is simply the standard scrutiny any platform/holdco deserves, and a reason the market applies a discount.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A superb core wrapped in a contested platform — priced for the platform to keep working.

TII is one of the most interesting compounding stories in Indian industrials precisely because it's two stories: a world-class engineering core, and an ambitious capital-allocation experiment whose first big bet (CG Power) already made shareholders rich. The debate for the next five years is whether the next bets — EVs, medical, semis — extend that record or dilute it, and how much of CG Power's value the market ever lets you keep.

The bull case

  • A cash machine at the core — ~44% ROIC, ~100% FCF conversion, market-leading precision tubes that fund everything else.
  • Proven capital allocation — CG Power turned ~₹700 Cr into an ~₹80,000 Cr stake; management has earned the benefit of the doubt on the platform.
  • SOTP value gap — the CG Power stake alone exceeds TII's whole market cap; you may be buying the core + ventures for less than free.
  • Multiple national tailwinds — railways, power capex, semis, EVs and import-substitution all run through the group.
  • Optionality — any one of Montra, TI Medical or CG Semi could become the next large value-creator.

The bear case

  • Persistent holdco discount — SOTP gaps can stay wide for years; the market may never fully credit the CG Power value.
  • The EV cash furnace — TI Clean Mobility is loss-making with an uncertain path to profit; it could consume capital for years.
  • Cyclicality — the core rides autos, trucks, tractors and construction; a demand downturn hits volumes and the platform's fuel.
  • Concentration in CG Power — most of TII's value is CG Power; a de-rating there (or in semis hype) hits TII hard.
  • Complexity & governance — a sprawling, promoter-controlled platform is harder to analyse; headline PAT is distorted by non-cash items; ownership realignments add uncertainty.
The bottom line

TII is best understood as a great engineering business strapped to a great capital allocator — with a spectacular past win (CG Power) and an unresolved present bet (EVs and the newer ventures). The core's quality is not seriously in doubt; the debate is entirely about the platform: does the market credit the CG Power value, do the new ventures earn their keep, and does management stay disciplined rather than empire-building? Track four numbers over time — core ROIC (is the engine intact?), CG Power's value and TII's stake (the dominant asset), TI Clean Mobility's losses (narrowing or widening?), and the holdco discount (closing or stuck?). Get those right and TII is a rare, multi-engine compounder; get them wrong and it's a distorted holding company where a wonderful tube business is lost in the noise.
