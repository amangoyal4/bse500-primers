00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Shree Renuka Sugars Limited (SRSL) is one of India's largest sugar companies — and, distinctively, the country's biggest sugar refiner and a major exporter. It does three quite different things under one roof: it crushes sugarcane at integrated mills to make sugar; it refines raw sugar at two large port-based refineries for export and domestic sale (a capability almost no Indian miller has at scale); and it runs a big distillery / ethanol business plus co-generation of power from bagasse. Since a debt crisis last decade, it has been controlled by Wilmar International, the Singapore-listed agri-commodities giant, which today owns roughly 62%.

Three facts frame everything:

  • This is a commodity business with thin, policy-shaped, wildly volatile margins. Sugar is one of India's most heavily regulated products: the price a mill pays for cane is fixed by government (the FRP, and higher state SAP in some states), the minimum price at which sugar can be sold is also set by government, exports are switched on and off by quota, and the ethanol the mill diverts sugar into is bought by state oil companies at an administered price. Renuka is a price-taker at almost every point in the chain.
  • The record is honestly poor — six straight years of losses. Even as revenue swung between ~₹5,600 Cr and ~₹11,300 Cr over FY21–FY26, the company reported a net loss every single year. FY26 was the worst of the run: a ~₹792 Cr net loss, operating profit of barely ~₹19 Cr against a ~₹736 Cr interest bill, and a swing to negative net worth. This is not a company having a good stretch; it is one fighting to stop the bleeding.
  • Yet it is worth ~₹5,100 Cr, because the market prices the backstop and the option, not the P&L. Two things keep the equity alive: Wilmar's balance-sheet support (corporate guarantees and letters of comfort that let the auditors sign a going-concern set of accounts despite negative equity), and the ethanol-blending story — the bet that diverting sugar into fuel-grade ethanol can eventually turn a chronic loss-maker into something that earns.
In plain terms

Picture a very large, very old sugar operation with three engines bolted together — cane mills, standalone port refineries, and distilleries — sitting inside a policy cage where the government sets what it pays for cane, what it can charge for sugar, when it may export, and what its ethanol fetches. For years the cane bill and the interest bill together have swallowed everything the sugar earned, so the company has lost money each year and its accumulated losses have now wiped out its equity. What keeps it standing is a deep-pocketed parent in Singapore that guarantees its loans, and a genuine chance that ethanol changes the maths. The whole investment case is whether the second thing arrives before the first runs out of patience.

01

Sugar 101: how a mill actually makes money

No agronomy degree needed — just cane, the by-products, and the prices someone else sets.

A sugar mill is, at its simplest, a machine that turns sugarcane into sugar — but the money is in what happens to everything else that comes out of the cane. When a mill crushes cane, it gets juice (which becomes sugar), bagasse (the fibrous residue, burned to make steam and power), and molasses (the dark syrup left after sugar is extracted, the classic feedstock for alcohol and ethanol). A modern "integrated" mill monetises all three. To follow Renuka you need six terms.

The only sugar terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
TCDTonnes of Cane crushed per Day — the standard measure of a mill's size. Renuka's Indian mills add up to roughly 46,000 TCD, among the largest crushing footprints in the country.
FRP / SAPThe Fair & Remunerative Price the central government fixes for cane, below which a mill cannot legally pay farmers; some states add a higher State Advised Price. Cane is ~75–80% of the cost of making sugar, so this single administered number dominates the mill's economics.
Recovery %How much sugar a mill extracts per tonne of cane (typically ~10–12%). A higher recovery means more sugar from the same FRP-priced cane — a mill's key efficiency lever.
MolassesThe by-product syrup from sugar-making, sent to the distillery to make alcohol and ethanol. As ethanol has become valuable, molasses (and increasingly whole cane juice) has turned from waste into a second revenue stream.
Bagasse / cogenCrushed cane fibre, burned in the mill's own boilers to generate steam and electricity. Surplus power is co-generated and sold to the grid — a third, steadier income line.
Raw vs refinedRaw sugar is unrefined, off-white, traded globally; refined (white) sugar is the finished product. Renuka's port refineries take in raws — imported or domestic — and turn them into refined sugar, a business apart from crushing its own cane.
The dependency that shapes the whole sector: cane is bought at a fixed price, sugar is sold at a capped one

Unlike almost any other manufacturer, a sugar mill controls neither its main input price nor its main output price. It must pay farmers at least the government's FRP/SAP for cane — a number set for political and rural-income reasons, and one that ratchets up almost every season. It then sells sugar into a market where the government also fixes a minimum sale price (to protect mills) and, when it wants to cool food inflation, effectively caps how much can be sold or exported. So the mill is squeezed between a rising, guaranteed cost and a controlled, often stagnant realisation. That squeeze — not weather or management genius — explains most of why Indian sugar is so cyclical and why a giant like Renuka can crush record volumes and still lose money.

Where the cane comes from, and the seasons that govern it

  • The farmer & the command area — each mill draws cane from farmers in a surrounding "command area." Relationships matter: cane must be crushed within hours of cutting, so a mill is physically tied to its local growers and to whatever the state decides they must be paid.
  • The crushing season — sugar is seasonal, concentrated in the winter crushing months (roughly October–April in most of India). Mills run flat out in season and idle much of the rest of the year, which makes fixed-cost absorption and working-capital timing central to profitability.
  • The government — present at every step: it sets the cane price, the minimum sugar price, the export quota and the ethanol procurement price. For a sugar company, "the regulator" is not a background risk; it is the largest single variable in the model.
02

The two-price scissors: the squeeze that defines Indian sugar

Understand this one mechanism and the chronic losses stop being a mystery.

The central tension of Renuka's business is a pair of scissors. One blade is the cost of cane, which the government pushes up almost every year to keep farmers happy. The other is the price of sugar, which the government tends to hold down to keep food inflation and consumers calm. When the two blades open, mills prosper; when they close — which is often — even an efficient mill bleeds. Renuka sits squarely between them.

Neither blade is set by the company. The mill's job is to run efficiently enough, and diversify enough, to survive the seasons when the blades close.

Two policy levers make this worse than an ordinary commodity cycle. First, the cane price is a hard legal floor, not a market — a mill cannot pay less even in a year of collapsing sugar prices, so the cost side has almost no give. Second, when sugar prices do rise enough to help mills, the government frequently steps in with stock-holding limits or export bans to protect consumers, capping the upside. The 2023–24 export restrictions, imposed to secure domestic supply, are a recent example: mills sat on sugar they could have sold profitably abroad. Heads the farmer wins, tails the consumer wins; the mill takes what is left.

Why the same policy that hurts also, occasionally, rescues

The government is not only the squeezer — it is also the pressure valve. When mills accumulate huge cane-payment arrears to farmers (a recurring political embarrassment), Delhi tends to respond with relief: a higher minimum sugar price, generous export quotas, soft loans, or — most importantly this decade — a lucrative ethanol procurement price that lets mills divert sugar into fuel and get paid promptly. So the sector lives on a policy see-saw where the same hand that caps the sugar price then opens the ethanol tap. For Renuka, whose balance sheet has no slack, the timing of those interventions matters enormously: a good ethanol price or export window can be the difference between a survivable year and another deep loss.

03

What Renuka actually is: three businesses, two of them unusual

Mills, port refineries, and distilleries — and why the refineries make Renuka different.

Most listed Indian sugar companies are, at heart, domestic cane millers: they crush their own cane and sell sugar, with ethanol and power as add-ons. Renuka is that, but with two distinctive extra engines that reflect its Wilmar ownership and its own history. Understanding the three-part structure is the key to seeing why it is not just "another Balrampur."

The three engines

  • Integrated cane mills — seven mills, primarily across Karnataka and Maharashtra, with combined crushing capacity around 46,000 TCD. This is the conventional sugar business: buy cane at FRP/SAP, crush it, sell sugar, and monetise molasses (into ethanol) and bagasse (into power). It is the largest revenue block and the most exposed to the scissors of section 02.
  • Port-based sugar refineries — two large standalone refineries at Kandla (Gujarat) and Haldia (West Bengal), together about 1.7 million tonnes a year of refining capacity. Crucially, these do not depend on Renuka's own cane: they take in raw sugar — imported or sourced domestically — and refine it into white sugar for export or the home market. This makes Renuka the country's largest sugar refiner and gives it an export/trading tilt that pure millers lack (section 05).
  • Distillery, ethanol & co-generation — around 1,250 KLPD (kilolitres per day) of distillery/ethanol capacity, producing fuel-grade ethanol from molasses and (increasingly) grain and cane juice for the national blending programme, plus bagasse-based power co-generated and sold to the grid. This is the growth and diversification engine (section 06).

Why the refineries change the character of the company

A pure cane miller lives and dies by one arithmetic: its own recovery, its own FRP bill, its own sugar price. Renuka has that and a second, largely separate business that earns a refining and trading margin on raw sugar flowing through a port — a margin driven by the gap between world raw-sugar prices and refined-sugar prices, and by the logistics edge of a coastal plant. In a good import or export window the refineries can earn when the domestic mills are struggling, and vice versa. It is genuine diversification of a kind most Indian sugar peers simply do not have. The catch, as the financials show, is that neither engine has been reliably profitable enough to cover the group's heavy interest load.

04

The Wilmar question: how a debt crisis handed control to Singapore

The near-death story, the foreign parent, and the negative net worth it now underwrites.

Renuka's most important shareholder fact is also its most important survival fact. The company was a home-grown success that over-reached, nearly collapsed under debt, and was rescued by Wilmar International — a Singapore-listed, Asia-spanning agri-commodities giant that trades and processes sugar, edible oils and grains worldwide. Wilmar did not buy a healthy asset; it recapitalised a distressed one, and it is still holding it up.

Timeline

  • 1998
    Shree Renuka Sugars founded by Narendra Murkumbi, starting from a single mill acquired with the backing of Karnataka cane farmers.
  • 2005–09

  • 2010

  • 2012–13

  • 2014
    Wilmar steps in — buying an initial ~27.5% stake (~₹517 Cr, at ~₹20 a share) via preferential allotment; Renuka raises roughly ₹1,240 Cr to pay down Indian debt.
  • 2018
    Wilmar deepens control through an open offer, lifting its stake past 58%; founder Narendra Murkumbi steps down as Vice-Chairman & MD. Renuka is now a Wilmar subsidiary.
  • FY21–FY25
    A grinding stretch — losses every year despite rising volumes, as cane costs, interest and a stressed balance sheet outweigh sugar and ethanol earnings; Wilmar extends guarantees and support.
  • FY26

Ownership & what the parent provides

  • Promoter: Wilmar International Limited (through its sugar holding entities) owns roughly 62% of Renuka. Wilmar is one of the world's largest agribusiness groups, with vast sugar milling, refining and trading operations across Australia, Asia and beyond — so its interest in an Indian refiner-exporter is strategic, not merely financial.
  • The lifeline is explicit and ongoing: Renuka's accounts rest on a going-concern assumption only because Wilmar provides corporate guarantees for term loans and letters of comfort for working capital. In plain words, a company with negative shareholders' equity keeps borrowing and operating because a AAA-scale parent stands behind its debt. Remove that support and the picture changes overnight.
  • Ownership could yet change hands: there has been periodic press speculation about Wilmar's long-term intentions, including reported interest from other large Indian groups in acquiring Renuka. None of this is confirmed, but it is a reminder that the controlling stake — and the lifeline attached to it — is not a fixed feature of the investment case.
The structure cuts both ways. Wilmar's backing is the single biggest reason Renuka's equity still exists — it converts a technically insolvent balance sheet into a functioning, financeable business, and it plugs Renuka into a global sugar-trading network few Indian peers can match. But minority investors are along for a ride steered by a foreign parent whose own priorities set the terms: how long the guarantees continue, whether fresh equity is injected or debt simply rolled, and at what price control might one day be sold. You are, in effect, investing alongside Wilmar's patience.
05

The port-refinery edge: earning on sugar it never grew

The single most distinctive thing about Renuka — and the clearest expression of the Wilmar link.

Here is the idea that separates Renuka from every pure domestic miller in the peer table. A cane mill can only sell the sugar its own fields and its own FRP bill produced. Renuka's port refineries at Kandla and Haldia can buy raw sugar from anywhere in the world, refine it at the coast, and sell white sugar into export or domestic markets — earning a refining-and-trading margin that has nothing to do with Indian cane prices at all. It is a second way to make money from sugar, running on a different clock from the mills.

The mechanism is an arbitrage. Global markets quote two prices: raw sugar (the traded, unrefined commodity) and refined white sugar (the finished product). The gap between them — the "white premium" — plus the cost of refining, is the refinery's spread. When world raw sugar is cheap relative to refined, or when a country needs imported white sugar, a coastal refiner with deep-water access and cheap logistics can capture that spread at scale. This is precisely the business Wilmar runs globally, and it is why a Singapore sugar trader wanted an Indian port refiner in the first place.

The device that fits Renuka and almost no domestic peer: a margin decoupled from the cane cycle

Test it against a rival: could you write "profits from refining sugar it never grew, on a spread set by world raw-versus-white prices and port logistics" about Balrampur, Dwarikesh or Dalmia Bharat Sugar? You could not — they are cane-anchored millers. Renuka's refineries mean part of its sugar earnings float on (refined price − raw price − refining cost), a number driven by global trade flows and the rupee, not by Karnataka's cane bill. In a year when domestic crushing is squeezed by the scissors, a favourable import or export window can let the refineries earn independently; in a year of export bans, that same engine can idle. The point is not that it is always good — it is that it is different, and it is the one part of Renuka that most looks like its global parent.

The flip side is that a trading-and-refining tilt adds its own risks: exposure to global sugar prices, freight and the rupee, and to India's on-off import/export policy, which can strand the refineries when Delhi closes the border either way. The refinery edge is real and rare, but it is not a steady annuity — it is a cyclical, policy-gated arbitrage that needs Wilmar's trading intelligence and balance sheet to run well. When it works, it is Renuka's most differentiated earnings source; when policy shuts the window, it is idle steel at a port.

06

The ethanol pivot: sugar's most credible way out

Why fuel-grade alcohol is the growth story every Indian sugar company is now telling.

If the two-price scissors is the disease, ethanol is the medicine the whole industry is taking — and Renuka with it. India's fuel-ethanol programme has, in a few years, turned a sugar by-product into a second, government-backed market that pays promptly and lets a mill dodge the very sugar glut that crushes prices. For a chronically loss-making, debt-heavy producer, ethanol is the most credible route back to earning its keep.

The policy backdrop moved fast. India set out to blend 20% ethanol into petrol (E20) and reached that milestone in the 2025 supply year — roughly five years ahead of the original schedule. To feed it, the government did two things that matter to a mill: it set an attractive, administered ethanol procurement price paid by state oil marketing companies, and — critically, from September 2025 — it removed restrictions on diverting cane juice and syrup straight to ethanol, not just molasses. That lets an integrated mill choose, season by season, whether a tonne of cane is worth more as sugar or as fuel.

Why does diversion help a stressed mill so much? Because ethanol's biggest gift is not just a better margin but cash-flow reliability. The oil marketing companies pay on a schedule, at a fixed administered price, and every tonne of cane sent to fuel is a tonne not added to a sugar glut that depresses sugar prices. For a comfortable producer that is a nice-to-have; for one carrying ~₹7,000 Cr of debt and waiting on slow sugar and export cycles, prompt and predictable cash is close to oxygen. That is why Renuka, like every leveraged mill, is pushing as much of its output down the distillery route as policy allows.

What ethanol can and cannot fix for Renuka

Ethanol genuinely improves the shape of the business: it converts a volatile, glut-prone sugar output into a steadier, promptly-paid fuel stream, and it lets Renuka lean on its ~1,250 KLPD of distillery capacity to smooth the sugar cycle. But it is not a magic wand. Ethanol economics themselves depend on an administered price that the government can freeze or trim, on feedstock costs (molasses, grain, cane), and on continued political commitment to blending. And for Renuka specifically, even a healthy ethanol contribution has to be large enough to clear the group's most stubborn obstacle — a ~₹736 Cr annual interest bill. Ethanol is the most plausible path to that, but the arithmetic still has a long way to travel before the distillery alone can carry the company.

07

Six years, six losses: the number that should stop you

The uncomfortable core of the case — read it before any bull argument.

Strip away the story and look at the scoreboard. Across the six financial years FY21 through FY26, Shree Renuka Sugars reported a net loss in every single one. Revenue nearly doubled and then fell back over that span; the bottom line never once turned black. This is the fact a first-time reader most needs to sit with, because it reframes everything the more optimistic sections describe.

The surprise a generalist never expects: negative equity, positive market cap

Here is what stops people short. Renuka's accumulated losses have turned its reserves to roughly −₹2,890 Cr and its net worth negative — on the books, shareholders' equity has been fully eroded. By the ordinary logic of a balance sheet, the shares are worth nothing. And yet the equity trades for around ₹5,138 Cr. The market is not valuing the business's history at all; it is pricing two things the accounts do not capture: Wilmar's guarantee, which keeps the enterprise financeable, and the ethanol option, the chance that future cash flow rewrites the story. Whether that is faith or foresight is the entire debate — but it is why the price and the book are in different universes.

08

The forces that write the year

Four external levers decide Renuka's result — and it controls none of them fully.

A sugar year is not authored in the boardroom; it is authored by policy, weather and world prices. Renuka can crush more efficiently, tilt toward ethanol, and run its refineries cleverly, but the swing between a survivable year and a deep loss is set by forces outside its walls. Naming them plainly is the whole risk map.

The four swing factors
LeverWhat it isHow it hits Renuka
1 · Cane price (FRP / SAP)The government-fixed floor price for cane, raised most seasons.Sets ~75–80% of the cost of sugar and cannot be negotiated down. A rise the sugar price doesn't match compresses the mill margin directly.
2 · Sugar price & export policyThe minimum sale price, plus stock limits and the on-off export quota.Caps the realisation side. Export bans (as in 2023–24) can strand sugar the refineries could have sold profitably; open windows can rescue a year.
3 · Ethanol policy & priceThe administered ethanol procurement price and the rules on cane-juice diversion.The main upside lever. A generous, prompt ethanol price lifts cash flow and drains the sugar surplus; a freeze or cut removes the clearest path to profit.
4 · Monsoon & global pricesRainfall and cane yields at home; world raw/white sugar prices and the rupee abroad.Drive crushing volumes and recovery in the mills, and the refining/trading spread at the ports. A weak monsoon or an adverse global swing hits both engines at once.
The summary a first-timer should internalise

Renuka's task is to run a large, integrated sugar-and-ethanol operation efficiently and then absorb whatever the government, the monsoon and world markets deliver. It has real self-help levers — push ethanol, keep the refineries busy in favourable windows, chip away at cost and debt — but the four forces above set the ceiling on any year, and three of the four are decided in Delhi. That is why the company can do everything operationally right and still post a loss when cane costs outrun sugar prices and the ethanol tap is only half open. The discipline for an investor is to judge the business on the levers it controls while never forgetting how much of the outcome it does not.

The levers also interact in punishing ways. A rising cane price (lever 1) is bearable if the ethanol price (lever 3) is generous and exports (lever 2) are open — the FY-good scenario the industry hopes for. It becomes ruinous when cane costs climb while exports are banned and ethanol is frozen, so every extra tonne crushed loses money. For a company as leveraged as Renuka, where ~₹736 Cr of interest must be paid regardless, the difference between those two alignments is not a good year versus a great one — it is solvency versus another write-down.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, cyclical sector where balance-sheet strength — not scale — separates winners.

Indian sugar is a fragmented industry of hundreds of mills, but the listed conversation centres on a handful of large integrated players. What distinguishes them is less crushing size than financial health and ethanol readiness: in a policy-shaped commodity, the company with low debt and high distillery capacity survives the down-years and compounds in the up-years. On that scorecard, Renuka is the scaled outlier — big and distinctive, but the most financially stressed of the group.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two truths sit side by side. First, Renuka is genuinely differentiated — no listed peer combines port refineries, an export/trading tilt and a global agri parent the way it does; on the axis of "what can this company do that others cannot," it stands out. Second, on the axis that has actually mattered this decade — who makes money through the cycle — it has been the weakest of the big names, because scale and distinctiveness count for little when a ₹7,000 Cr debt load and a ₹736 Cr interest bill sit on top. Balrampur and Triveni have shown that the sector can be reliably profitable; Renuka's challenge is not proving sugar-and-ethanol works, but proving it can work on its balance sheet.

10

Financials & valuation

A ₹5,100 Cr market cap on a negative book — priced for a turnaround, not the record.

There is no ordinary way to value Shree Renuka Sugars, because there are no ordinary earnings to value. The company has lost money for six straight years, its net worth is negative, and FY26 operating profit was a rounding error against its interest bill. A conventional price-to-earnings multiple is meaningless — you cannot put a multiple on a loss. So the ~₹5,138 Cr the market assigns the equity is not a verdict on results; it is a wager on two things the accounts don't show: that Wilmar keeps the enterprise solvent, and that ethanol plus a better cycle eventually produce real cash.

The valuation therefore rests almost entirely off the balance sheet. What an investor is buying is not a stream of profits but a combination of hard assets — ~46,000 TCD of crushing, ~1.7 MTPA of port refining, ~1,250 KLPD of distillery — that would be expensive to rebuild, wrapped in a parent guarantee that keeps them financeable, and pointed at an ethanol tailwind that has yet to reach the bottom line. Price the option too generously and you are paying real money for a company whose book value is negative; price it too harshly and you ignore a scarce, Wilmar-backed asset in a sector that can turn hard. The fair reading is that neither the loss record nor the asset base settles it — only the trajectory of earnings does.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A distinctive, deeply leveraged turnaround bet — asked whether ethanol arrives before patience runs out.

Shree Renuka Sugars enters FY27 as a paradox: a genuinely differentiated sugar company — the country's biggest refiner, an exporter, a Wilmar subsidiary plugged into global trade — that has nonetheless not made a profit in six years and now carries negative equity. The bet is not on what it is; it is on whether the ethanol pivot and a friendlier cycle can finally lift earnings above its interest bill while Wilmar's support holds. Both cases follow from that.

The bull case

  • Ethanol inflection — E20 achieved and cane-juice diversion freed; a rising, promptly-paid ethanol stream from ~1,250 KLPD is the clearest route to covering the interest bill.
  • Distinctive refining/export franchise — India's largest sugar refiner, with port plants that can earn a trading margin decoupled from the domestic cane cycle.
  • Wilmar backstop — a global agri giant guaranteeing the debt keeps the company financeable and connects it to world sugar trade; it has chosen to keep supporting, not abandon.
  • Operating leverage on a turn — with EBITDA near zero, even a modest, sustained margin improvement or debt refinancing could swing the loss dramatically the other way.
  • Scarcity of the asset — large integrated capacity, two coasts of refining, and export reach are hard to rebuild; a cyclical upturn or a change of control could re-rate it.

The bear case

  • Six years of losses and negative net worth — the record is unambiguous; the equity exists on the books only because of a parent's guarantee.
  • Crushing interest load — ~₹7,174 Cr of debt and a ~₹736 Cr interest bill overwhelm a business earning ~₹19 Cr of operating profit; the maths must change by an order of magnitude.
  • Policy dependence, all four ways — cane price, sugar/export rules and the ethanol price are all set in Delhi; an adverse move on any can undo a year.
  • Dependence on Wilmar's continued patience — the going concern rests on ongoing guarantees; the terms, and the ownership itself, are the parent's to change.
  • Cyclical, thin, volatile margins — a commodity converter with global-price and monsoon exposure and little balance-sheet slack to absorb a bad cycle.
The bottom line

Shree Renuka Sugars is a large, unusual, and financially fragile sugar-and-ethanol company — differentiated by its port refineries and its Wilmar parentage, defined by six years of losses and a balance sheet that has run through its own equity. The self-help is real: ethanol diversion is a credible engine, the refineries are a rare asset, and a global parent is choosing to keep the lights on. What no one can promise is that any single year escapes the vice of a government-set cane cost, a policy-gated sugar price, and a ₹736 Cr interest bill. The next test is concrete and datable: whether FY27 finally lifts group EBITDA well clear of that interest line — so the six-year losing streak ends and reserves start climbing back from −₹2,890 Cr rather than sinking further. Clear that bar and the market's wager on Wilmar and ethanol looks vindicated; miss it again, and the equity remains what the book already says it is — a claim kept alive by a parent's signature.
