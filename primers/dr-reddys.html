00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Dr. Reddy's makes medicines — overwhelmingly generics (copies of drugs whose patents have expired), plus a growing consumer-health and biosimilars business — and sells them across four big theatres: the United States, India, Europe, and Emerging Markets (led by Russia). Founded in 1984 in Hyderabad, it is one of India's largest and most respected pharma companies, and one of the earliest to crack the hard US generics market. Its DNA is science-led — it was among the first Indian firms to try (and fail, expensively) to invent new drugs, and that ambition survives today in a big biosimilars push.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A gently growing top line, but a reset year on profit. FY26 (year to March 2026) revenue rose ~3% to ₹33,593 Cr, yet net profit fell ~24% to ₹4,285 Cr (EPS ~₹50) and EBITDA margin slipped to ~23% (reported) — a sub-20% run-rate underneath. Almost the entire swing is one story: a hugely profitable US drug (generic Revlimid, "gRevlimid") is fading, dragging North America down ~22% and taking its outsized margin with it.
  • The old engine is normalising; three new engines are being built. The US generics business — long the growth driver — is in a tough patch: gRevlimid unwinding plus ordinary price erosion. To replace it, Dr. Reddy's is placing three bets: biosimilars (copies of expensive biologic drugs — a multi-billion-dollar prize led by its abatacept programme), consumer health (the £500m Haleon Nicotinell nicotine-gum acquisition), and GLP-1s / semaglutide (the weight-loss wave).
  • Cash-rich and self-funding the transition. Dr. Reddy's runs a net-cash balance sheet (surplus cash ~₹3,271 Cr; reserves ~₹37,800 Cr), which is exactly what lets it pour money into biosimilar R&D and pay for acquisitions without strain. Promoters (the Reddy family) hold ~26.6%; at ~₹1.12 lakh Cr market cap the stock trades on ~27× earnings.
In plain terms

Think of Dr. Reddy's as a company at a hinge point. For years its profits leaned heavily on the US generics market — and, recently, on one golden drug: a generic of the cancer blockbuster Revlimid, which it was allowed to sell with very few competitors, so the margins were extraordinary. That windfall is now shrinking on schedule, which is why FY26 profits fell even though sales grew. The whole investment question is: what replaces it? Management's answer is a deliberate move "beyond generics" into three harder, stickier businesses — biosimilars (copying biologic drugs, which is far harder and far more lucrative than copying pills), consumer health (it bought the maker of Nicotinell nicotine gum to get a steady branded income stream), and the GLP-1 weight-loss wave. It is rich enough in cash to fund all three at once. Whether these mature fast enough to fill the gRevlimid hole is the story of the stock.

01

Generics economics 101: how a drugmaker like Dr. Reddy's earns

No pharmacology degree required — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

A generics company sells the same molecule an innovator invented, once the patent expires. Because anyone can then copy it, price collapses and competition is brutal. So the whole game is which drugs you make, and in which markets: the harder a drug is to copy, and the fewer rivals that show up, the longer the profit lasts. Five ideas explain almost everything Dr. Reddy's does.

The only concepts you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for Dr. Reddy's)
Plain (oral) genericA simple pill copy. Cheap to make, but anyone can, so many players pile in and the price erodes toward cost, year after year. The commodity base of the US business — and its structural headwind.
Complex / injectable genericA copy that is hard to make — injectables, peptides, drug-device combos. Fewer rivals, so pricing holds longer. Higher-value ground Dr. Reddy's wants to occupy (e.g. its GLP-1 injectables).
Limited-competition / "first-to-file"Being first or one of only a few to launch a generic — often after a patent challenge — grants a window of little competition and extraordinary margins. Temporary, and normalises when rivals arrive. This is the gRevlimid story (§04).
BiosimilarA copy of a biologic — a drug made inside living cells, not mixed in a chemistry lab. Far harder, slower and costlier to copy than a pill, so few can — and the prizes are huge (§05). Dr. Reddy's big long-game bet.
Branded / consumer healthIn markets like India and Russia, and in over-the-counter self-care (nicotine gums, etc.), a trusted brand earns a durable premium — the opposite of the US commodity model. The steady ballast Dr. Reddy's is now buying more of (§06).
The one mechanism that explains Dr. Reddy's whole strategy

Easy money attracts a crowd that competes it away; hard-to-make products keep the crowd out. A plain US generic pill is a price-taker — a dozen rivals grind the price down a little more every year (that's "price erosion"). So a smart generics firm climbs the "difficulty curve" toward products where it is one of only a few: limited-competition launches (gRevlimid), complex injectables (GLP-1s), and — the hardest of all — biosimilars. At the same time it buys branded / consumer businesses whose income doesn't depend on winning the next price war. Dr. Reddy's entire FY26-onward story is exactly this migration: away from a US pill business under structural pressure, toward biosimilars, complex generics and steady consumer brands. When you read "beyond generics" or "diversifying the base" in Dr. Reddy's results, that's the code for it.

The three revenue models under one roof

  • US & complex generics: sell copies into a huge but savagely price-competitive market; lumpy, launch-driven, higher-risk/higher-reward. The swing factor.
  • Branded & consumer (India, Russia, NRT): sell trusted brands to doctors and shoppers; steadier, higher-margin, defensive. The ballast.
  • PSAI & services (API + CDMO): make and sell the raw drug ingredient to other manufacturers, and increasingly do contract development/manufacturing (Aurigene). The base load and a new services engine.
02

The company and who controls it

A science-first Hyderabad institution built by a chemist-entrepreneur, still family-anchored.

Dr. Reddy's is one of Indian pharma's genuine pioneers. It was founded in 1984 by Dr. Kallam Anji Reddy, a chemical engineer and scientist who began by making active pharmaceutical ingredients (the raw drug substance) and then pushed the company into finished formulations, the US generics market, and — unusually and boldly — into trying to invent new molecules. That drug-discovery dream mostly didn't pay off commercially, but it stamped Dr. Reddy's with a science-led, R&D-heavy identity that still shows up today in its biosimilars ambition. It was one of the first Indian pharma companies to list on the New York Stock Exchange (2001, ticker RDY).

Ownership & management

  • Promoters — the Reddy family (~26.6%). The founding family retains control at roughly a quarter of the company; the late founder's son G. V. Prasad is Co-Chairman & MD, and the company is run by a professional team led by CEO Erez Israeli. Unlike some peers, there is no persistent "will the family sell?" overhang here — control is stable.
  • Institutions own the majority of the float. Domestic institutions (DIIs ~31%) and foreign investors (FIIs ~21%) together dominate, reflecting Dr. Reddy's blue-chip, index-heavyweight status; a large US shareholder base holds the NYSE-listed ADRs.
  • A dual-listing quirk. Because it trades on both Indian exchanges and the NYSE, Dr. Reddy's reports to US regulators too (Form 20-F / 6-K filings) — which means unusually detailed, timely disclosure, but also direct exposure to US market sentiment.
Why the identity matters to the thesis: Dr. Reddy's was built as a science-and-R&D company, not just a low-cost copier. That heritage is why it is willing to spend years and hundreds of crores building a biosimilars platform (§05) that most pure generics firms would avoid — and why "beyond generics" is a credible strategy for this company specifically, not just a slogan.

04

North America: the complex-generics engine & the gRevlimid cliff

The biggest, most volatile driver — and the single biggest reason FY26 profits fell. Read this to understand the whole "reset."

North America (essentially the US) is Dr. Reddy's largest segment and its greatest volatility rolled into one — roughly 38% of FY26 revenue. It's also where FY26 went wrong: the segment fell ~22% for the year (and a brutal ~51% in the March quarter, ~38% excluding a one-off accounting charge). Two things hit at once — a golden drug fading, and ordinary price erosion — and together they explain the group's entire profit reset.

The gRevlimid one-off — what happened, in plain terms

Revlimid (lenalidomide) is a blockbuster cancer drug. As its patents expired, only a small number of generic makers — Dr. Reddy's among them — were granted limited-competition, volume-capped launches. Being one of only a few sellers of a high-value drug is extraordinarily profitable: gRevlimid earned outsized, high-margin dollars in FY23–FY25, flattering both US revenue and the whole group's margin. But by design these windfalls normalise: as more competitors are allowed in and volume caps loosen toward full generic competition, the extra profit fades. FY26 is that fade — a tough comparison against inflated prior years, not a broken business. On top of that, FY26 carried a ~₹453 Cr "shelf-stock adjustment" (SSA) on lenalidomide — a one-time accounting charge to compensate distributors for price drops on stock they already hold. Same drug, same story.

The extra FY26 blows — impairments & erosion. Beyond gRevlimid, FY26 profit also absorbed a ~₹228 Cr impairment of its CAR-T and Eftilagimod Alfa innovation assets (write-downs on experimental programmes that didn't pan out) and a ~₹114 Cr VAT provision. And the base US generics business faced higher price erosion on key products. So FY26's US weakness was several things at once — the expected gRevlimid fade, one-time charges, and real competitive pressure on the base.

The structural headwind: US price erosion

Even setting the one-off aside, the ordinary US generics market is a grind. Once a drug goes generic, multiple manufacturers compete on price to win business from a handful of giant US buying groups; prices then drift down a few percent every year — "price erosion." A US generics portfolio therefore needs a constant stream of new launches just to stand still. That's why Dr. Reddy's is pushing up the difficulty curve toward complex generics and biosimilars, where erosion is slower and rivals fewer — and why the base US business, on its own, is not a growth story.

How to read the US business going forward

The US is a "replace the windfall, and out-run erosion" story. The bull view: gRevlimid was always temporary, and Dr. Reddy's is building a broader base — complex injectables, GLP-1 semaglutide, and the first big biosimilar (abatacept, §05) — that is steadier and harder to compete away. The bear view: biosimilar and complex launches take time and money, base price erosion keeps biting, and there is no new gRevlimid on the horizon — so US revenue and margin stay under pressure for a while. Either way, the gRevlimid normalisation is the one-off to strip out when judging Dr. Reddy's underlying earnings power; management stressed that the base business (ex-lenalidomide) grew double digits in FY26. The reported decline overstates how much the real business weakened — but it does not erase the genuine erosion underneath.

05

The biosimilars bet: Dr. Reddy's big "beyond generics" prize

The hardest, slowest, most expensive game in generics — and potentially the most rewarding. This is the long game.

If you want to understand where Dr. Reddy's is aiming, understand biosimilars. This is the company's flagship "beyond generics" bet, and it plays directly to its science-led heritage (§02). CEO Erez Israeli has publicly argued that biosimilars could surpass ordinary generics in the US by the early 2030s — and Dr. Reddy's wants to be a real player, not a spectator. But first you need to know why biosimilars are a different, and much bigger, game than pills.

What is a biosimilar — and why is it so hard?

Most old drugs are small molecules: precise chemical compounds you can mix in a lab and copy exactly. A biologic is different — it's a large, complex protein grown inside living cells (engineered bacteria or mammalian cells). You cannot copy a biologic exactly; you can only make one that is highly similar — hence "biosimilar." Doing that requires:

  • Living-cell manufacturing. You must engineer a cell line to produce the protein, then grow, purify and stabilise it at scale — vastly harder and costlier than pressing a pill.
  • Big clinical trials. Because the copy isn't identical, regulators demand expensive human studies to prove it works and is safe like the original — years and hundreds of crores per programme.
  • Regulatory patience. Approvals are slow and inspection-heavy; a plant setback can delay a launch by years (as happened to Dr. Reddy's rituximab in the US).
Why this difficulty is the whole point

Biologics are the most expensive drugs in medicine — think blockbuster cancer, autoimmune and immunology treatments costing tens of thousands of dollars a year per patient — and many are now losing patent protection. That creates an enormous prize for whoever can make an approved copy. But because biosimilars are so hard, very few companies can make them, so the copies don't collapse to near-zero the way pill generics do — a handful of players share a still-large pie. That's the exact opposite of the commodity-pill trap in §01: high barriers to entry mean durable pricing. The catch is the entry cost — you burn R&D for years before a single dollar comes back. Dr. Reddy's is deep enough in cash (§08) and stubborn enough in its science heritage to play that long game.

It's the one thing an analyst leads with when asked "what's Dr. Reddy's next engine?"
06

The NRT deal: buying its way into steady consumer health

A generics company deliberately buying brands — to trade some volatility for predictability.

In September 2024 Dr. Reddy's completed one of its biggest and most strategically telling deals: it paid £500m (~$633m) to acquire Haleon's global nicotine-replacement-therapy (NRT) business — everywhere except the United States. This is not a generics deal at all. It's a deliberate move into branded consumer health, and it tells you exactly how Dr. Reddy's wants to reshape itself.

What it bought

  • Nicotinell — the crown jewel: the world's #2 NRT brand outside the US, sold in 30+ countries across Europe, Asia (incl. Japan) and Latin America. It holds the #1 or #2 position in 14 of the top 17 global NRT markets, and generated ~£217m revenue in 2023.
  • Local champions — Nicabate (Australia), Habitrol and Thrive (Canada, New Zealand). Nicotine gums, patches and lozenges — the products that help people quit smoking.
Why a volatile generics firm wants steady nicotine gum

NRT is the opposite of the US generics business. A brand like Nicotinell earns a durable premium: shoppers buy it by name off a pharmacy shelf, there's no annual price war with a dozen rivals, and demand is steady and global. For a company whose profits just lurched on one drug (§04), a big, predictable, branded consumer-health income stream is ballast — it smooths the ride and lifts the quality of earnings. It also gives Dr. Reddy's a real consumer platform (distribution, brand-building muscle, retail relationships) it can extend into other self-care categories — for example the hormone-replacement brands (Progynova) it has also been picking up. The strategic logic mirrors what peers are doing: use generics cash to buy stickier branded income.

The integration — and the margin question

The deal has landed well operationally. By March 2026, management said the NRT integration was ~95% complete by value, and NRT is now the single biggest reason Europe revenue jumped ~55% in FY26 — the acquired business contributed roughly 48% of European revenue and was still growing (~16% in Q4). So on the top line, NRT has done its job: it added a large, growing, branded chunk of income.

The open question: a £500m acquisition has to earn its keep. The bull case is that NRT is high-quality, steady, brand-led income that diversifies Dr. Reddy's away from US volatility and can be scaled with the company's distribution. The bear case is that consumer-NRT growth is modest (smoking rates fall slowly, and quitting is seasonal), the price paid was full, and integrating a consumer business is a different muscle from making generics — so the return on that £500m, and the margin it ultimately delivers, still has to be proven over several years. Watch whether NRT margins expand as the business fully beds in.

India, Emerging Markets & PSAI: the steadier base

The three engines that grew nicely in FY26 while the US stumbled — the ballast that kept the top line up.

Strip out North America and FY26 looks completely different: every other engine grew, several strongly. This is the whole point of diversification — when the US wobbled, India, Emerging Markets, Europe (via NRT) and a resilient services arm carried the group to a record top line.

India — branded formulations

Dr. Reddy's India business (~19% of group, ~₹6,219 Cr, +16% in FY26) is a branded-prescription franchise — doctors prescribe Dr. Reddy's brands across cardiovascular, gastro, diabetes, oncology and more. Like all branded-India businesses, it's steady, high-margin and defensive: brand loyalty gives pricing power and repeat demand that a commodity generic never gets, and it's largely insulated from the US FDA and patent cliffs. In FY26 it outgrew the domestic market, helped by new launches — including, notably, Obeda, its generic semaglutide (the weight-loss / diabetes molecule) as those patents open up in India.

Emerging Markets — Russia/CIS-led

Emerging Markets (~17% of group) is anchored by a long-standing, powerful Russia / CIS branded business — Russia alone is ~46% of the segment and grew ~28% — plus other developing markets. It's a genuine differentiator: not every Indian peer has a branded franchise this strong outside India, and it grew double digits in FY26 while the US fell.

PSAI & Aurigene — the services engine

PSAI (Pharmaceutical Services & Active Ingredients, ~10% of group) is two things: selling the raw API (drug ingredient) to other manufacturers — low-margin, vertically integrating — and a growing CDMO (contract development & manufacturing) arm under subsidiary Aurigene Pharmaceutical Services, which makes drugs on contract for other companies. API volumes were soft in FY26 (PSAI dipped slightly), but the CDMO/Aurigene piece is a deliberate new growth avenue — turning Dr. Reddy's manufacturing and chemistry skills into a services business, riding the global trend of pharma firms outsourcing production.

Why the "boring" engines matter more than usual right now

In a normal year these steady businesses are background. In FY26 they were the headline: with the US down ~22%, it was India (+16%), Emerging Markets (+29% in Q4), Europe (+55% on NRT) and a resilient services base that still pushed group revenue to a record. That's diversification working exactly as intended — and it's the reason management could credibly say the underlying base business grew double digits even as reported profit fell. The more of the group these steadier engines represent, the less one US drug can swing the whole company.

The net-cash balance sheet & capital-allocation optionality

Why a cash-rich drugmaker in transition is a strategically special animal.

Here is what makes the whole "beyond generics" transition affordable: Dr. Reddy's runs a net-cash balance sheet. Even after a ~£500m acquisition, it reported a surplus cash position of ~₹3,271 Cr (~$349m) at end-FY26 — cash exceeds debt — with reserves of ~₹37,800 Cr. In an industry where growth is bought through R&D, capex and acquisitions, a fortress balance sheet is a strategic weapon.

Why a cash-rich pharma in transition is worth more than the cash

Net cash does three things here. (2) It buys diversification: the £500m Nicotinell deal (§06), the Progynova hormone-therapy brands, GLP-1 investments — all funded from cash, all deliberately steadier than US generics. Watch the return on the NRT deal and the biosimilar spend — that's where this thesis is won or lost.
09

The USFDA overhang: the recurring operational risk

The one risk that can hit US earnings — and biosimilar launches — out of nowhere.

Every India-to-US drugmaker lives under the US Food and Drug Administration. To sell into America, each plant must pass FDA inspections; fail, and the FDA can block new approvals or halt supply from that site. This is a recurring, hard-to-predict risk — and for Dr. Reddy's it bites hardest on the biosimilars plans, where a plant setback can delay a launch by years.

The FDA's grading, decoded

  • Form 483 — a list of observations at the end of an inspection. It flags issues to fix; it is not yet a final verdict. The routine, manageable outcome.
  • VAI (Voluntary Action Indicated) — minor issues; business as usual. The good result.
  • OAI (Official Action Indicated) — serious concerns; the FDA may withhold new approvals from that site until fixed. A real drag on the pipeline.
  • Warning Letter — a formal escalation; can freeze new approvals from the site and take years to clear. The one to fear.
Where Dr. Reddy's stands (as of mid-2026) — and why it matters

The good news: as of mid-2026, Dr. Reddy's carried no active warning letter or OAI on its core network — a materially cleaner position than some peers. But the risk is live and specific to its biologics ambition. Its Bachupally biologics facility in Hyderabad received a Form 483 with 7 observations after a June 2026 inspection, and earlier a Post-Application Action Letter (PAAL) in January 2026 — the same category of issue that had already delayed its US rituximab biosimilar. The point: for a company betting its next decade on biosimilars (§05), the FDA is the gatekeeper on the most important products. A single adverse classification on the biologics plant could push abatacept or the next biosimilar out by quarters or years. It's the key operational overhang to watch — not (for now) a crisis, but the thing most likely to derail the "beyond generics" story.

10

Financials & valuation

A record top line, a one-off-driven profit reset, and a mid-20s pharma multiple.

Dr. Reddy's FY26 numbers tell a two-layer story: a record ₹33,593 Cr revenue on top (+3%, +4.6% ex-lenalidomide), and a ~24% profit drop underneath — almost entirely the gRevlimid normalisation, the SSA, and innovation-asset impairments. The debate is whether that reset is a temporary air-pocket (bulls) or the start of a lower-margin era before biosimilars mature (bears).

How to think about the ~27× multiple

At ~₹1.12 lakh Cr on ₹4,285 Cr of (reset) FY26 profit, Dr. Reddy's trades around 27× trailing earnings — a fairly typical large-cap Indian-pharma multiple, neither cheap nor stretched. That price embeds a bet that FY26 was a trough distorted by one-offs, and that earnings stabilise and then re-accelerate as (a) the base business keeps compounding double digits, (b) gross margin climbs back above 50% on a better mix, and (c) the new engines — biosimilars, NRT, GLP-1s — mature. Bulls point to the net-cash optionality, the biosimilar prize and a genuinely diversified base; bears point to relentless US price erosion, the absence of another gRevlimid, the biologics-plant FDA risk, and the years-long lead time before biosimilars pay. The multiple leaves room for a recovery — but little forgiveness if the US keeps eroding faster than the new engines fill in.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality franchise at a hinge point — priced for a transition it now has to execute.

Dr. Reddy's is a genuinely strong business: a science-led heritage, a diversified four-geography base, a fortress balance sheet, and credible optionality in biosimilars, consumer health and GLP-1s. FY26 was a reset, not a break — but the next chapter is a real transition, and it isn't automatic. The investment debate turns on the US, the FDA, and how fast the new engines fill the gRevlimid hole.

The bull case

  • Diversified base is working — India (+16%), Emerging Markets (+29% Q4), Europe/NRT (+55%) all grew as the US fell; the underlying business grew double digits.
  • Biosimilar optionality — first-mover interchangeable abatacept in the US, a pipeline (rituximab, pembrolizumab/Alvotech), and a CEO who sees biosimilars overtaking generics in the US by the early 2030s.
  • Consumer-health ballast — the £500m Nicotinell/NRT franchise adds steady, branded, global income that de-risks US volatility.
  • GLP-1 upside — first-mover generic semaglutide (Canada, India) into the biggest drug wave in a generation.
  • Net-cash war chest — surplus cash even after M&A; self-funds R&D and further bolt-ons with no leverage.

The bear case

  • No new gRevlimid — the easy limited-competition dollars are gone, and nothing of that scale is visibly queued to replace them.
  • US price erosion — the base US generics business faces relentless, structural price pressure; it needs constant launches just to hold.
  • Biosimilars are slow & costly — years of R&D burn, big trials, and plant-inspection risk before returns; the payoff is long-dated.
  • FDA overhang — Form 483s / PAAL on the Bachupally biologics plant threaten the very launches the future depends on.
  • Execution / M&A return — the £500m NRT deal and heavy R&D must earn their keep; the CAR-T write-down shows not every bet works.
The bottom line

Dr. Reddy's is a structurally sound, cash-rich drugmaker mid-transition. Its FY26 profit fall is mostly a one-off (gRevlimid) unwinding plus accounting charges, not a deterioration of the core — the base business actually grew double digits, carried by India, Emerging Markets and the NRT-boosted Europe. The next few years will be written in four numbers: the US trajectory (does erosion ease and do complex/GLP-1 launches offset the gRevlimid fade?), the gross margin (does mix push it back above 50%?), the biosimilar launches (does abatacept land on time and profitably?), and each FDA inspection (does the biologics plant stay clear?). Get those right and a diversified, debt-free science house compounds through the transition; stumble on the US or the FDA and the "reset" lingers. Unlike some peers, the promoter and balance-sheet questions are settled — here the entire story is execution on the pivot beyond generics.
