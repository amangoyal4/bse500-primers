00

Snapshot

A 1959-vintage Mumbai contractor, the infrastructure flagship of the Shapoorji Pallonji Group, listed for less than two years — and already through its first bad year as a public company.

Afcons Infrastructure Limited builds things that are difficult to build. Not "large" — large is common. Difficult. A breakwater in open sea. A cable-stayed bridge over a gorge. A twin-tube metro tunnel driven under a live city through mixed soil and rock. A hydro powerhouse cavern blasted 300 metres inside a mountain. An undersea rail tunnel. These are the projects where the tender document itself screens out most of the market before a single price is quoted.

That is the whole company in one sentence, and it explains both the appeal and the fragility. Afcons deliberately avoids the commodity end of Indian construction — plain highway packages, buildings, ordinary civil work — where dozens of contractors qualify and the lowest bidder wins at a margin that barely covers the interest bill. Instead it sits in a thinner competitive pool where a handful of firms in India, and sometimes a handful worldwide, can even demonstrate the reference projects, equipment fleet and engineering depth that a client demands.

The reward is a structurally better margin than commodity EPC: FY26's EBITDA margin of 11.7% sits comfortably above what pure road or building contractors typically earn. The cost is that you cannot manufacture demand. When the marquee tenders do not come — because governments slow award cycles, or a client in Bangladesh or Gabon goes quiet — a specialist cannot pivot to bidding ordinary flyovers to keep the machine fed. FY26 is exactly that year: revenue fell, a quarter went into loss, and the order book stopped being topped up.

In plain terms

Two contractors both build roads. One competes on price against forty rivals. The other is one of five firms on earth cleared to bore a tunnel beneath a harbour. The second earns more per rupee of work — but only when someone actually decides to bore a tunnel beneath a harbour. Afcons is the second kind, and FY26 was a year in which not enough people decided.

01

What "complex EPC" actually means

A first-timer's guide to why two construction companies with identical revenue can be completely different businesses.

EPC stands for Engineering, Procurement and Construction. A client — a state government, a metro corporation, a port authority, an oil company — decides it wants an asset. It does not build the asset itself. It writes a contract that hands one firm the job of designing it in detail (engineering), buying the steel, cement, cables and specialised kit (procurement), and physically constructing it (construction), for an agreed price and an agreed date. The contractor takes the execution risk; the client takes the asset.

India has hundreds of EPC contractors. What separates them is not size. It is what the tender lets them bid for.

The two ends of the market

At one end sits commodity EPC. A standard four-lane highway package. A residential tower. A warehouse. The engineering is well understood, the risks are known, the equipment is rentable and thirty firms can produce the credentials. Clients therefore run a straight price auction. Margins compress toward the level at which the marginal bidder is indifferent — often mid-single-digit EBITDA — and the way you win is by being leaner, not better. Working capital is heavy, competition is permanent, and a bad monsoon or a land-acquisition delay wipes out the year.

At the other end sits complex EPC — Afcons's chosen ground. Here the client is not primarily buying price. It is buying the confidence that the thing will actually stand up. Consider what changes:

  • The engineering is bespoke. A 300-metre cable-stayed span, a tunnel-boring drive under a river, a marine jetty in 20 metres of water with tidal currents — each is a one-off design problem, not a repeat of a standard drawing.
  • The equipment is owned, not rented. Tunnel boring machines, jack-up barges, heavy marine plant, specialised piling rigs, large-capacity cranes. These are capital assets a contractor accumulates over decades. You cannot hire them on a week's notice, and their presence on the balance sheet is itself a barrier.
  • The geology is uncertain. You price a tunnel on borehole samples. The rock you actually meet may be softer, wetter or more fractured. Managing that — and claiming for it contractually — is a specialised skill.
  • The client screens before it prices. This is the crucial one, and it gets its own section below.
In plain terms

Commodity EPC is a price auction with a crowd. Complex EPC is a short invitation list where price still matters — but you had to be invited. Afcons has spent sixty years earning invitations.

02

The five verticals

Marine, surface transport, urban, hydro-and-underground, and oil & gas — what each actually involves, and how much of the book it holds.

Afcons organises itself around five business units. They are not marketing labels; they correspond to genuinely different engineering disciplines, different equipment fleets and different client sets. Understanding them is understanding the company.

1 · Marine & industrial

The heritage business, and the one that made Afcons's international name. Ports, container terminals, jetties, breakwaters, LNG receiving structures, offshore platforms, dry docks. Marine construction is a distinct craft: you are building a permanent structure in a medium that moves. Tides, swell, seabed conditions, corrosion, and a working window that the weather dictates rather than the schedule. It requires floating plant — barges, marine cranes, piling spreads — and crews trained to work over water.

Very few Indian contractors have credible deep-marine capability. It is a specialty in which Afcons competes internationally rather than merely domestically, and it is a large part of why the company wins work in Africa and the Middle East. As of 31 March 2026 marine and industrial accounted for roughly ₹4,779 Cr, about 14.7% of the order book.

2 · Surface transport

Highways, expressways, and — more importantly for Afcons — bridges. Ordinary road packages are commodity work; long-span bridges are not. A cable-stayed or arch bridge over a wide river, a deep gorge or an estuary involves foundation engineering in difficult substrate, precision erection of segments hundreds of feet above ground or water, and structural design that leaves no forgiveness. Afcons has been the contractor on some of India's most-photographed bridge projects, including work on the Chenab railway bridge in Jammu & Kashmir — widely described as the world's highest railway arch bridge.

Surface transport is around ₹2,907 Cr or ~9% of the order book — deliberately the smallest of the big four, because the plain-highway portion is exactly the low-margin work Afcons declines.

3 · Urban infrastructure

The largest block. Metro rail — both elevated viaducts and, more distinctively, underground metro built with tunnel boring machines — plus flyovers and elevated corridors. Underground urban tunnelling is arguably the hardest routine work in civil engineering: you are driving a machine through variable ground directly beneath a functioning city, with buildings, sewers, water mains and other metro lines above and around you, and a settlement tolerance measured in millimetres.

Afcons is among India's leading tunnellers, and this shows in the book. Underground and elevated metro work stood at roughly ₹9,279 Cr (~28.6%), and bridges plus elevated corridors reported within urban infrastructure at about ₹7,580 Cr (~23.3%). Together, urban infrastructure is more than half the forward book.

4 · Hydro & underground

Dams, barrages, hydroelectric powerhouses, water-conveyance tunnels, pumped-storage schemes. This is deep-underground work in the Himalaya and other difficult terrain: long headrace tunnels, machine caverns excavated inside mountains, and geology that is genuinely unpredictable. It is also work with real strategic tailwinds — India's pumped-storage and hydro push, driven by the need to firm up intermittent solar and wind, creates a pipeline that only a handful of contractors can execute. Hydro and underground was around ₹7,423 Cr, roughly 22.8% of the book.

5 · Oil & gas

Offshore and onshore hydrocarbon structures, process platforms, LNG storage tanks, pipelines. Historically important to Afcons and technically adjacent to its marine skills, but currently the smallest vertical at about ₹528 Cr — roughly 1.6% of the book. It is optionality rather than a driver today.

03

The pre-qualification moat

The single most important idea in this business: Afcons's margin advantage comes from which tenders it is allowed to enter, not from cutting costs.

Before a client opens price bids on a difficult project, it runs a pre-qualification round. The tender specifies what a bidder must have already done: perhaps three completed marine structures in water deeper than 15 metres, or two TBM drives of at least 2 kilometres in comparable geology, or a bridge with a main span above 200 metres, plus a minimum net worth, a minimum turnover, and evidence of owning the relevant plant. Only firms that clear these gates are permitted to submit a price at all.

This is the mechanism that makes Afcons a different animal from a commodity contractor, and it works in a specific and slightly ruthless way.

Stage 1

A metro corporation, port trust, hydro developer or foreign ministry decides to build something difficult
→

Stage 4

The completed project becomes the credential that qualifies you for the next, harder one
The loop is self-reinforcing: every difficult project completed widens the set of future tenders you can enter, and narrows it for everyone else. It is also self-limiting — the loop only turns when Stage 1 happens.

Note what this implies about where profitability comes from. A commodity contractor improves margin by procuring cheaper, subcontracting harder, and squeezing overhead — competing within a bid. Afcons improves margin primarily by choosing which bids to enter. Its returns are a selection decision made months before a shovel moves, not an operating efficiency wrung out on site.

In plain terms

Most contractors ask "how do I win this job cheaper?" A specialist asks "should I be in this room at all?" Afcons's margin premium over commodity EPC is essentially the value of the rooms it can enter and its rivals cannot — plus the discipline to walk out of rooms that are too crowded.

The device: a moat that is also a funnel

Here is the framing worth carrying away from this primer, because it is specific to Afcons and would not transfer to a road builder or a T&D contractor.

The same wall that keeps competitors out of Afcons's market keeps Afcons inside it. A moat with a narrow mouth is also a funnel — and in FY26 nothing much came down the funnel.

A commodity contractor facing a dry year has a release valve: it can drop its price and win ordinary work, sacrificing margin to keep the fleet and the workforce employed. Afcons structurally cannot do that without abandoning the very positioning that earns it 11.7% margins. If it starts bidding plain highway packages at commodity prices to fill capacity, it becomes a commodity contractor with a specialist's cost base — the worst of both.

So the specialisation cuts both ways with unusual symmetry: it protects pricing when tenders flow, and it removes the fallback when they don't. FY26's order inflow of ₹4,125 Cr against ₹12,322 Cr of revenue executed is what that looks like on paper. The company burned roughly three rupees of backlog for every rupee it replaced.

Why this matters for reading the stock: the correct question is never "is the order book big?" — ₹32,496 Cr is a respectable 2.6 years of work. The question is whether the funnel has re-opened. FY26 answered "no." FY27's ₹30,000 Cr order-booking target is management's assertion that the answer is about to change.
04

The international book

An Indian contractor building ports, bridges and tunnels across Africa, the Middle East and South Asia — and why that is both a strength and a fault line.

Here is the fact that surprises most people who look at Afcons for the first time: a substantial part of this Mumbai company's work is executed outside India. Not exported equipment, not design consultancy — physical construction, on the ground, in Africa, the Middle East and South Asia. As of an earlier disclosed count, Afcons had active projects spread across roughly thirteen countries.

Indian contracting is overwhelmingly domestic. A handful of firms have meaningful overseas execution, and fewer still have it in genuinely difficult categories rather than simple building work. Afcons's international presence rests on its marine and bridge credentials — the same pre-qualification credentials that work at home also travel, because a port authority in West Africa or a ministry in the Gulf runs the same kind of screening round that a port trust in India does.

Why foreign work can be better business

  • Thinner competition. In many of these markets the realistic bidder set is a few international contractors — Chinese, Turkish, European, Indian. There is no crowd of local firms driving price to the floor.
  • Better commercial terms. Multilateral-funded projects (World Bank, African Development Bank, JICA and similar) often carry cleaner payment mechanics, mobilisation advances and dispute frameworks than some domestic government contracts.
  • Diversification of the award cycle. Indian tendering slows around elections and budget cycles. A foreign pipeline is uncorrelated with that.
  • Currency mix. Revenue in hard currency against a partly rupee cost base can help, though it also introduces exposure that must be hedged.

And why it is also the sharpest risk

FY26 made the downside explicit. Management's own account of the year cited geopolitical instability and logistical difficulty in overseas markets including Bangladesh, Gabon and parts of Africa as a direct drag on execution momentum. That is the international risk in one line: a project can be technically fine, fully engineered, adequately staffed — and still stop, because a border closed, a government changed, a currency became unconvertible, or a client stopped paying.

The international book is not a bolt-on — it is part of what makes Afcons unusual, and part of what makes it volatile. The same portfolio that lets the company keep working when India's tender cycle stalls is the portfolio that can freeze because of an event on another continent that no engineering skill can influence.

05

The order book — and the FY26 inflow drought

₹32,496 Cr of contracted work sounds comfortable. The number underneath it does not.

For any contractor, the order book is the closest thing to a forecast the market will ever get. It is work already contracted and not yet turned into revenue — the queue. Afcons closed FY26 with an order book of ₹32,496 Cr, roughly 2.6 times the year's revenue. On its own, that reads as around two and a half years of visibility, which for an EPC firm is adequate.

But an order book is a stock, and stocks deplete. The flow number is order inflow — new work won during the year. FY26's inflow was ₹4,125 Cr. Against ₹12,322 Cr of revenue executed, that is a book-to-bill ratio of roughly 0.33×.

A contractor's order book behaves like a bathtub. Revenue is the drain; order inflow is the tap. In FY26 the drain ran at three times the tap. Run another year like that and the ₹32,496 Cr becomes something closer to a single year of visibility — at which point the company faces a genuinely uncomfortable choice between idle capacity and bidding down.

Why the tap ran dry

  • Elongated award cycles. Management explicitly cited stretched tendering and award timelines. Projects were not cancelled so much as not awarded — decisions kept sliding right.
  • Geopolitical hesitancy overseas. Some international clients deferred; some existing projects in Bangladesh and Gabon faced disruption, which also slows fresh awards from the same client base.
  • Selectivity. Some of the shortfall is by choice. A specialist that bids everything to fill a book is not a specialist for long. Walking away from underpriced work is the correct decision and still shows up as a bad inflow number.
  • A cautious bid stance on working capital. With receivables already stretched, taking on more contracts with weak payment mechanics would have compounded the cash problem rather than solved it.

What management has committed to

At the Q4/FY26 call (19 May 2026) the company set an FY27 order-booking target of ₹30,000 Cr, of which it said roughly ₹15,000 Cr had clear and definitive visibility. It notably declined to give FY27 revenue or EBITDA-margin guidance, citing geopolitical uncertainty and elongated award cycles.

Read that carefully. Guiding hard on order intake while withholding guidance on revenue and margin is an unusual combination. It says: we are reasonably confident about what we can win; we are not yet confident about how fast we can convert it or at what margin. For an investor, that is the honest shape of the uncertainty — and the reason the FY27 inflow number carries more weight than any other line the company will report.
06

How the cash actually moves

Why a profitable contractor can still run out of money — retention, unbilled revenue, claims and the 120-day target.

The most misunderstood thing about construction is that profit and cash are nearly unrelated in the short run. A contractor recognises revenue as work progresses — percentage-of-completion — but gets paid on a different, slower schedule. The gap between the two is where EPC companies live or die.

The four places cash gets stuck

Working capital in an EPC contract
BucketWhat it isHow long it sits
ReceivablesBills raised and certified but not yet paid by the clientWeeks to many months, depending on client budget
Unbilled revenueWork physically done, recognised as revenue, not yet billed — awaiting measurement and certificationOften a quarter or more
Retention moneyA fixed slice (commonly 5–10%) the client withholds until the defect-liability period expiresYears — released after project completion
Claims & arbitrationAmounts owed for scope changes, client-caused delays, unforeseen geology — disputedYears; sometimes a decade

Complex projects make every one of these worse. Bespoke work invites disputes over scope. Difficult geology produces variation claims almost by definition. Long project durations mean retention sits longer. And on international work, add a currency and a foreign ministry to the chain.

Meanwhile the contractor's own outflows are relentless: wages every month, subcontractor bills, steel and cement, fuel, and the finance cost on the debt used to bridge the gap. That is why net debt of ₹2,653 Cr exists at all in a company earning ₹1,439 Cr of EBITDA — the borrowing is not funding factories, it is funding the lag between doing the work and getting paid for it.

In plain terms

Afcons builds first and gets paid later, and "later" is set by clients who are often government bodies with their own budget cycles. The company's debt is essentially a bridge across that delay. Shorten the delay and the debt shrinks without a rupee of extra profit; lengthen it and the debt grows even in a profitable year.

The FY27 cash plan

Management's stated priorities for FY27 are unusually concrete on this front, and they are the operational commitments most worth tracking:

  • Working-capital days to around 120. A specific, measurable target — and the single lever with the biggest effect on debt.
  • Roughly ₹1,000 Cr of receivables to be unlocked by around June (2026), per the FY26 call.
  • A sizeable reduction in debt over the year.
  • Capex of about ₹725 Cr — meaningful for a firm whose owned plant fleet is part of its moat, but not an expansion binge.

Note the internal logic: the ₹725 Cr of capex is defensive of the moat (TBMs, marine plant and heavy equipment are the physical form of pre-qualification), while the working-capital target is what pays for it. If receivables come in, the capex is self-funded and debt still falls. If they don't, both promises compete for the same cash.

The post-IPO context: part of the ₹1,250 Cr of fresh money raised in the November 2024 IPO was earmarked for reducing borrowings and funding working capital. Net debt/equity of 0.49× at end-FY26 is a materially more comfortable place than the pre-IPO balance sheet — but the FY26 cash burn shows the structural pressure has not gone away, only been made survivable.

The pledge — the part that needs stating plainly

This is the governance issue a prospective owner of the stock has to form a view on, and it deserves to be described accurately rather than either dismissed or dramatised.

Afcons is a well-run operating company. Its parent group, Shapoorji Pallonji, has been through a widely reported period of financial stress at the holding-company level, and has raised substantial structured debt against its assets. Afcons shares are among those assets. In May 2026 Axis Trustee Services disclosed the encumbrance of about 9.21 crore Afcons shares — approximately 25.03% of total share capital — pledged by Goswami Infratech in favour of a common security trustee. Screener-sourced data shows roughly 60% of the promoter holding pledged.

What this does and does not mean:

  • It does not impair Afcons's own operations, cash flows or balance sheet. The debt is at the promoter entity, not at Afcons.
  • It does create an overhang. Pledged shares can, in a stress scenario, be invoked and sold — a potential supply of stock unrelated to the company's own performance.
  • It does raise the standing question of whether a stressed parent might, over time, sell down further, or make related-party arrangements worth scrutinising.
  • It also explains the IPO structure: ₹4,180 Cr of the ₹5,430 Cr issue was an offer for sale — money that went to the promoter, not into Afcons. Only ₹1,250 Cr was fresh capital for the company.
In plain terms

Two balance sheets sit behind this ticker. Afcons's own is reasonable — net debt 0.49× equity, deleveraged after the IPO. The promoter's is under pressure, and it has borrowed against its Afcons shares. Nothing about that changes what happens on a construction site, but it does mean the share register carries a risk the operating business did not create.

08

FY26: the year it stalled

Sixteen years without a loss-making quarter, and then one. What actually happened, and how much of it is structural.

FY26 is the most informative year in Afcons's short listed history, precisely because it went badly. Companies reveal their real shape under stress, and this one revealed three things: the margin structure held better than the revenue did, the balance sheet took the strain without breaking, and the order engine — the part that matters most — genuinely stopped.

The sequence

  • Revenue contracted to ₹12,322 Cr for the year, with Q4 revenue of about ₹2,614 Cr — down roughly 19% year-on-year. This was an execution problem, not a demand-at-site problem: projects that were in the book could not be advanced at the planned pace.
  • Execution was blocked in specific places. Management pointed to payment delays from both government and private clients stretching receivable cycles, and to geopolitical and logistical disruption in Bangladesh, Gabon and other African markets.
  • Q4 carried the provisions. The ₹89 Cr net loss in the March quarter was driven by project-specific provisions — the accounting acknowledgement that certain contracts will not deliver what they were priced to deliver.
  • Full-year profitability survived, barely. PAT of ₹251 Cr on EBITDA of ₹1,439 Cr. The gap between the two is the tell: depreciation on a heavy owned-plant fleet and interest on working-capital debt consume a large share of operating profit in this business.
  • The order book did not collapse — the inflow did. ₹32,496 Cr of backlog remains. But at ₹4,125 Cr of new wins, the year did not replace what it consumed.

The genuine surprise in the FY26 numbers

Most people, told that a contractor had its first loss-making quarter in sixteen years, would assume margins broke. They didn't. The full-year EBITDA margin of 11.7% is the kind of number a specialist EPC firm is supposed to earn — it is well above what commodity road and building contractors typically achieve, and it survived a year of disrupted sites, foreign-market seizures and provisions.

What broke was volume and cash conversion. Afcons's problem in FY26 was not that it priced work badly or executed it at a loss in aggregate. It was that it could not get enough work moving, could not get paid promptly for the work it did move, and could not win enough new work to refill the queue. That is a materially different diagnosis from margin erosion — and it points to a materially different fix. Margin damage requires re-learning how to price. A volume-and-cash problem requires the award cycle to reopen and receivables to be collected. One of those is within management's control; the other largely is not.

In plain terms

The machine did not lose its ability to make money per unit of work. It ran out of work to run and out of cash coming back from work already done. Which means the recovery case rests almost entirely on two observable things — order awards and receivable collection — rather than on any hoped-for improvement in how Afcons builds.

09

The industry & rivals

Who Afcons actually competes with — and why the answer changes completely depending on which vertical you ask about.

There is no single "Afcons competitor," because the pre-qualification screen produces a different shortlist for every kind of project. In a marine tender the rivals are marine specialists; in a metro tunnel package they are tunnellers; in an ordinary highway they would be dozens of firms Afcons simply does not meet, because it does not enter that room.

How Afcons differs from the other EPC names an Indian investor sees

It is worth positioning Afcons against the specialist contractors it is most often listed beside, because the businesses are less similar than the shared "EPC" label suggests:

  • Versus a T&D contractor (transmission towers and substations): that business is about repeatable line-kilometres and tower fabrication at scale — volume-driven, with a manufacturing rhythm. Afcons's projects are one-offs; there is no second identical harbour.
  • Versus a railway PSU contractor: a captive government parent supplies work at negotiated terms. Afcons has no captive client — every rupee of its book was won in open competition, domestically or abroad.
  • Versus a power/industrial EPC firm: those follow the private capex cycle and increasingly data centres. Afcons follows public infrastructure decisions — metros, ports, dams — which move on political and multilateral timelines, not corporate ones.
  • Versus an engineering consultancy: consultancies sell hours and carry almost no working capital. Afcons carries plant, crews, geology risk and a receivables book. Far more capital at stake, and correspondingly more that can go wrong.

The demand backdrop

The structural case for the sector is real: India continues to build metro networks across a widening set of cities, is expanding port capacity, has a substantial pumped-storage and hydro pipeline driven by the need to firm up renewable generation, and is pushing on long-span bridges and expressway connectivity. All of that is Afcons's kind of work. Overseas, African and Middle Eastern infrastructure programmes continue, though funding availability varies sharply by country.

The problem is not the pipeline's existence. It is timing. Public infrastructure awards are lumpy and politically scheduled; a single large metro package can be worth several thousand crore and slip by four quarters for reasons entirely unconnected to the contractor. FY26 was a demonstration of what that lumpiness feels like from inside a specialist's order book.

In plain terms

The work Afcons is built for is being planned in large quantities. Whether it gets tendered this year or next is decided in ministries and metro corporations, not in Mumbai. That gap between an announced pipeline and a signed contract is where a specialist contractor's fortunes are made and lost.

Financials & valuation

What the market is paying, on which earnings, and the one number that will settle the argument.

At roughly ₹300 a share and a market capitalisation of about ₹11,024 Cr (mid-July 2026), Afcons trades at around 36.7× trailing earnings and about 2.0× book value (book value per share ~₹148). The 52-week range — approximately ₹479 high to ₹266 low — tells its own story: the stock has given up a large part of its value over the year in which the order inflow dried up.

Reading the multiple properly

The 36.7× headline overstates how expensive the stock is on a normalised basis, and understates the risk. Both at once, and for the same reason: FY26 earnings were abnormally low.

If order inflow recovers toward management's ₹30,000 Cr target, execution normalises and the margin holds near 11.7%, the earnings base rebuilds and the trailing multiple compresses fast. If the inflow drought extends into a second year, the backlog thins, revenue falls again, and the denominator shrinks further — a 36.7× multiple on falling earnings is a very different proposition from 36.7× on a trough.

The EV/EBITDA figure of roughly 9.5× is the more stable lens. It sits in a range that is neither cheap nor demanding for a specialist contractor with a defensible pre-qualification position — which is a fair description of the market's current stance: it is not pricing in a rescue, and it is not pricing in a collapse.

The metric that settles the thesis

Watch FY27 order inflow against ₹30,000 Cr — and specifically the ₹15,000 Cr management called "clear and definitive." Everything else about this company is downstream of that number. Margin has already proved it can hold at ~11.7% through a bad year. The balance sheet has already proved it can carry the load at 0.49× net debt/equity. Neither of those is the open question. The open question is whether the funnel that fed a ₹32,496 Cr book has re-opened. If Afcons books ₹25,000–30,000 Cr in FY27, the backlog roughly doubles, revenue visibility extends past three years, and FY26 reads as a single disrupted year. If it books ₹10,000 Cr, the backlog keeps draining and the argument moves from valuation to viability of the current cost base.

Two secondary gauges support that primary one, and both are reported:

  • Working-capital days against the ~120 target, plus whether the ~₹1,000 Cr of receivables management expected to unlock actually converted. This determines whether debt falls as promised.
  • The international share of new orders. A recovery driven mainly by domestic metro and hydro awards is lower-risk but also loses part of the diversification argument; a recovery that includes healthy foreign wins restores the full thesis but carries the country risks described in section 04.
In plain terms

Do not spend much time debating whether 36.7× is the right multiple for this company. It is a multiple computed on a year that included a loss-making quarter, and it will move sharply on its own. Spend the time on the order-intake announcements, which arrive continuously through the year and are the actual input to every other number here.

11

Future, risks, bull & bear

The case on both sides, stated without hedging, and what to watch through FY27.

The bull case

  • A moat you can point to. Pre-qualification in deep marine, long-span bridges, urban TBM tunnelling and hydro caverns is earned over decades and cannot be bought quickly. The competitive pool in Afcons's chosen tenders is measured in single digits.
  • Margins proved themselves under stress. 11.7% EBITDA through a disrupted year with provisions is evidence the pricing discipline is real, not a cycle artefact.
  • The demand pipeline is genuinely large. Metro expansion across new cities, port capacity, pumped-storage hydro to firm up renewables, and major bridges — all squarely in Afcons's verticals.
  • Genuine international capability. Few Indian contractors execute complex work across Africa, the Middle East and South Asia. It diversifies away from India's tender cycle.
  • A survivable balance sheet. Net debt 0.49× equity post-IPO, with an explicit deleveraging and 120-day working-capital plan for FY27.
  • A trough base. The trailing P/E is computed on earnings that included a loss-making quarter — normalisation alone changes the arithmetic.

The bear case

  • The order engine stopped. ₹4,125 Cr of inflow against ₹12,322 Cr of revenue is a 0.33× book-to-bill. A second such year turns a comfortable backlog into a thin one.
  • The specialisation has no release valve. When marquee tenders don't come, Afcons cannot fill capacity with commodity work without destroying the positioning that earns its margin.
  • Execution risk is concentrated. Big complex projects mean big single-project exposures — geology surprises, delays, cost overruns, and claims that take years to settle. Q4 FY26 showed how fast one project's provisions can take a quarter into loss.
  • Working capital is structurally heavy and dependent on government and client payment behaviour that management does not control.
  • International exposure carries political, currency and payment risk. Bangladesh and Gabon disrupted FY26 directly.
  • Promoter overhang. Shapoorji Pallonji's financing stress, ~60% of the promoter stake pledged, and ~25% of total share capital encumbered as of May 2026.
  • L&T. A larger, better-capitalised, more diversified rival that can qualify for essentially everything Afcons can.

What to watch, in order of importance

  • Quarterly order-win announcements against the ₹30,000 Cr FY27 target. These come through the year as exchange filings and are the earliest, cleanest signal.
  • Working-capital days and net debt at each result. Did the ~₹1,000 Cr of receivables actually convert? Is the 120-day figure being approached?
  • EBITDA margin holding near 11.7% — and, critically, the absence of further project-specific provisions.
  • Whether management restores revenue and margin guidance. The withdrawal in May 2026 was itself the signal; its return would be another.
  • Any change in the promoter pledge — additional encumbrance, release, or stake sale.
  • Status of the disrupted international projects in Bangladesh, Gabon and elsewhere in Africa.
In plain terms

This is not a company whose fate turns on efficiency or on cost control. It turns on whether the specific, lumpy, politically scheduled tenders that only a few firms can bid for come to market in FY27 — and on whether the clients who already owe Afcons money pay it.

The bottom line

Afcons is one of the few Indian contractors whose competitive position can be described without reference to price. It has spent six decades assembling the reference projects, the plant fleet and the engineering bench that let it stand in tender rooms most of its industry never enters — in marine works, in urban tunnelling, in long-span bridges, in mountain hydro, at home and across thirteen-odd countries. That is a real asset, and FY26 did not damage it: the margin held at 11.7% through disruption, provisions and a loss-making quarter.

What FY26 exposed is the shape of the dependency underneath. A specialist's order book fills in large, infrequent lumps decided by ministries, metro corporations and port authorities on their own schedule — and in one year, those decisions did not arrive. The company consumed roughly three rupees of backlog for every rupee it replaced, and a balance sheet that had just been deleveraged by an IPO went back to funding the gap between building things and being paid for them.

The next chapter is unusually well-defined, and it does not require any judgement about multiples to follow. Between now and March 2027, Afcons has told the market it intends to book ₹30,000 Cr of new orders, with ₹15,000 Cr of that already in clear sight, and to bring working capital to about 120 days. Those two commitments will be marked to reality quarter by quarter, in filings anyone can read. Watch the award announcements land — a metro package here, a hydro contract there, a port abroad — and count. That running total, not the P/E, is the story of this stock in FY27.
