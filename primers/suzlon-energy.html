00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Suzlon Energy is India's largest wind-turbine manufacturer — it designs, builds, installs and then maintains the giant windmills (wind turbine generators, or "WTGs") that convert wind into electricity. It is also one of the great corporate turnaround stories in Indian markets: a company that a decade ago was drowning in over ₹13,000 crore of debt and staring at bankruptcy, and which in FY26 (year ended March 2026) posted record revenue of ₹16,679 crore (+54%), net profit of ₹3,163 crore, and — crucially — a net-cash balance sheet, having wiped out the debt that nearly killed it.

Three facts frame the whole story:

  • It went from near-bankruptcy to net cash — this is THE defining arc. Suzlon over-expanded in the 2000s (above all the disastrous €1.3bn buy of Germany's REpower/Senvion), got crushed by debt when the 2008 crisis hit, spent a decade in painful restructuring, rights issues and QIPs, and emerged debt-free with ₹2,384 crore of net cash by FY26. A clean balance sheet is now the foundation that lets it grow again.
  • It sits on a record order book at the start of an India wind supercycle. India added a record ~6 GW of wind in FY26 and targets 100 GW by 2030. Suzlon's order book has climbed to a record ~6.4 GW — roughly ₹40,000 crore+ of contracted future work, ~66% from creditworthy PSU and corporate (C&I) buyers — giving it multi-year forward visibility rare for a manufacturer.
  • A high-margin service business quietly stabilises it. Suzlon operates & maintains 15+ GW of installed wind fleet — a recurring, sticky, annuity-like O&M business that earned a ~30% segment margin in FY26 and cushions the inherently lumpy turbine-sales cycle. The bull case is a debt-free re-rated growth machine; the bear case is a richly-valued (~25× earnings, ~13× book) stock that must execute a huge order book flawlessly.
In plain terms

Think of Suzlon as a company that makes windmills, plants them in the ground, and then looks after them for decades. It nearly died once — it borrowed far too much money in a global buying spree, and when the world stopped ordering turbines in 2008, it couldn't pay the loans back. It took more than ten years of cost-cutting, asset sales and raising fresh money from shareholders to dig out. Today the debt is gone, there's cash in the bank, and India is in the middle of a huge push to build wind farms — so Suzlon has more orders on its books than ever. The catch: the stock has already soared on this recovery, so a lot of good news is baked into the price, and now the company has to deliver on that giant order book.

01

What Suzlon actually sells — the three engines

No engineering degree required — just the three very different ways a wind company earns.

"Suzlon makes windmills" is true but incomplete. The business is really three distinct engines stacked on top of each other, and understanding how they differ — especially how lumpy one is and how steady another is — is the single most important lens for the whole primer.

The one idea that unlocks Suzlon: lumpy turbines, steady service

A turbine sale is a one-off, big-ticket, project-shaped event: an order is won, then months later megawatts are delivered and revenue lands in a lump. Miss a quarter's delivery target — because land isn't ready, or the grid connection slips — and the reported numbers wobble, even though the long-term story is intact. The O&M business is the opposite: once a turbine is spinning, Suzlon collects a service fee year after year for 5–20 years, whatever the order cycle is doing. So the more turbines Suzlon installs, the bigger its annuity base grows — a flywheel where the lumpy business feeds the steady one. Keep this "lumpy vs annuity" split in mind; it explains why analysts obsess over both the order book (future lumps) and the installed fleet (the annuity).

02

The near-death-to-net-cash turnaround — the defining story

The single thing an analyst leads with: how Suzlon nearly went bankrupt, and how a clean balance sheet became the launchpad.

You cannot understand Suzlon without understanding how close it came to the grave. This is not decoration — it is the central fact that makes today's numbers meaningful. A company that once owed banks and bondholders over ₹13,000 crore and defaulted on its debt now sits on net cash. Here is, honestly, how that happened — and why a clean balance sheet is the whole growth thesis's foundation.

How it nearly collapsed

  • Over-expansion, funded by debt. In the 2000s boom, founder Tulsi Tanti expanded aggressively — culminating in the 2007 acquisition of Germany's REpower (later Senvion), partly financed with ~US$760m of foreign-currency convertible bonds (FCCBs) and heavy borrowing. Suzlon took on far more leverage than a cyclical manufacturer could safely carry.
  • The 2008 crash pulled the rug. When the Global Financial Crisis hit, orders from Europe and the US — the biggest wind markets — froze. Revenue collapsed just as the debt bill came due. Quality issues (blade cracks) in the US hurt too. The classic trap: fixed debt costs meeting collapsing cyclical revenue.
  • Default and restructuring. In 2012 Suzlon defaulted on ~US$221m of FCCBs — one of India's largest such defaults. In 2013 a 19-bank consortium led by SBI restructured roughly ₹9,500 crore of loans (a "corporate debt restructuring", or CDR). Debt peaked well above ₹13,000 crore.

How it dug out

  • Sold the crown-jewel mistake. Suzlon divested its entire REpower/Senvion stake to Centerbridge Partners for ~€1 billion (~₹7,200 crore) — using the proceeds to cut debt. Selling the very acquisition that broke it was the hard, necessary reset.
  • Years of equity infusions. Through the late 2010s and early 2020s Suzlon repeatedly raised fresh equity — a one-time settlement with lenders, then a ₹1,200 crore rights issue and a QIP where shareholders put in real cash — to clear debt for good. This dilutes existing owners (there are far more shares now) but it was the price of survival.
  • Net debt-free by late 2023, net cash by FY26. The restructuring finished the job: gross borrowings are down to ~₹556 crore against a much larger cash pile, leaving ~₹2,384 crore of net cash at March 2026. The debt that nearly killed it is gone.

Why a clean balance sheet is the whole thesis, not a footnote

For a cyclical manufacturer, debt is the thing that turns a bad year into a fatal one. When you owe billions and demand suddenly drops, you still have to pay interest — so a downturn that a debt-free company would simply ride out can instead push a levered one into default. That is exactly what happened to Suzlon in 2008. Being net-cash now flips the risk profile completely: Suzlon can invest in new capacity, bid for large orders (customers trust a financially-sound supplier), fund working capital for big projects, and survive the next industry wobble — all without the sword of debt over its head. The turnaround isn't just a nice story about redemption; the clean balance sheet is the enabler that makes every other part of the growth case (the order book, the capacity ramp, the O&M expansion) fundable and credible. That's why it leads the primer.

A reader's caution on the turnaround: two nuances keep it honest. First, survival came at the cost of enormous dilution — the share count ballooned during the rescue, so per-share value was rebuilt from a low base. Second, some of FY26's headline PAT is flattered by a ₹742 crore deferred-tax credit (a non-cash accounting item from recognising past losses as future tax shields) — the underlying operating story is strong, but the reported profit growth is not entirely "clean" cash earnings. Judge the business on operating metrics (deliveries, order book, EBITDA, cash), not just the bottom line.
03

The company and who controls it

A promoter-founded champion — now with a much-reduced founding-family stake.

Suzlon was built by the Tanti family, founded by the late Tulsi Tanti in 1995. Today it is run by the next generation and a professional team, but one striking feature sets it apart from most Indian promoter companies: after the years of debt-driven dilution and restructuring, the founding family's stake is now unusually low.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter — the Tanti family & trusts: ~11.7%. A far cry from the majority stake founders usually hold. Two forces shrank it: (1) the dilution from years of rescue equity raises, and (2) block-deal sales — including a ~₹1,295 crore promoter block sale in June 2025 — which further reduced the family holding. Vinod Tanti (Tulsi's brother) chaired the recovery; the family remains at the helm but with a minority economic interest.
  • A largely public / institutional register. With promoters at ~11.7%, the rest is broadly held: FIIs ~23.8%, DIIs ~9.2%, and public/retail ~55%. A huge, enthusiastic retail following has made Suzlon one of India's most-traded, most-discussed stocks — which cuts both ways (deep liquidity, but also sentiment-driven volatility).
  • Leadership: a professional management team (with the Tantis in key board/leadership roles) executing the growth ramp. Governance and disclosure have improved through the recovery, though the low promoter stake and history mean investors watch it closely.
  • Balance sheet: the defining strength — net cash of ~₹2,384 Cr, gross borrowings ~₹556 Cr, ROE ~40% and ROCE ~35% in FY26 (see §09).
Why the low promoter stake matters — and why it's double-edged

In India, a high promoter stake usually signals "skin in the game" — the founding family's wealth rises and falls with yours. Suzlon is unusual: the family owns only ~12%, mostly because survival required issuing shares to raise cash. The upside is a widely-held, liquid, institution-followed company that isn't run purely for one family's benefit. The flip side: a small promoter holding means less concentrated commitment and, historically, some pledged shares and block sales that make the market nervous. It also makes Suzlon more of a "float" stock driven by broad sentiment. Neither good nor bad on its own — but it's a genuinely different ownership shape from a typical family-controlled Indian champion, and worth understanding.

04

The India wind-revival supercycle — the growth engine

The tailwind an analyst leads with after the turnaround: India is building wind farms again, at record pace.

Suzlon's recovery is not happening in a vacuum — it is riding a genuine revival in Indian wind energy. After years in the shadow of ultra-cheap solar, wind is back, and building fast: India added a record ~6.05 GW of wind capacity in FY26 (up ~46% year-on-year, the highest annual addition ever), crossing 56 GW of total installed wind. The government targets 100 GW of wind by 2030 and 156 GW by 2036. For India's largest turbine maker, this is the demand wave the whole growth thesis rides on.

Why wind is reviving — the four drivers

  • Round-the-clock & hybrid demand. Solar is cheap but only works by day; wind often blows hardest in the evening and monsoon. Grid planners increasingly want wind + solar hybrids and firm, round-the-clock renewable power — which needs wind in the mix. That structurally lifts wind demand after a decade of pure-solar dominance.
  • The C&I and captive boom. Around 75% of FY26 wind additions came from commercial & industrial (C&I) and captive buyers — factories, data centres and corporates going green via "open access" rules. This is high-quality, price-insensitive demand, and it's exactly where Suzlon's order book is concentrated.
  • PSU and IPP mega-orders. State utilities, PSUs (like NTPC, SJVN, ONGC) and independent power producers are placing large multi-hundred-MW wind orders under competitive auctions — big, creditworthy, visible demand.
  • Policy support. Concessional customs duty on turbine components, waiver of inter-state transmission charges (till 2028), competitive bidding and renewable-purchase obligations all tilt the field toward wind — and toward domestic manufacturers with local content.
How Suzlon differs from Waaree (solar) and the power developers: Waaree Energies manufactures solar panels; JSW Energy and Adani Green own and operate power plants and sell electricity. Suzlon does neither — it is a wind-turbine capital-goods maker: it sells the machines (and services them), earning on equipment and O&M rather than on selling power. It's the "picks and shovels" of the wind boom. Its fortunes track how many megawatts India installs, not power tariffs — a different, order-driven business model.
05

The order book — the forward-visibility metric that matters most

For a project-based manufacturer, the order book is the single best guide to the next two-plus years.

If the O&M fleet is the annuity, the order book is the growth — and for a company that earns in lumps, it is the most important forward number Suzlon reports. It has climbed to a record ~6.4 GW (from ~5.9 GW at the FY26 close), worth roughly ₹40,000 crore+ of contracted future revenue — several years of work at current delivery rates. That backlog is what converts "India is building wind" from a hope into booked, contracted visibility.

Why this order book is high-quality, not just big

  • The customer mix is strong. Around 66% is PSU and C&I — government-backed utilities and large corporates, not thinly-capitalised speculators. That reduces the risk of orders being cancelled or payments delayed. Marquee wins span Tata Power Renewables, NTPC, Jindal Renewables, Juniper Green, Sunsure and others.
  • It's overwhelmingly the new S144 platform. The vast majority of the backlog is for the modern S144 3.x MW turbine — cumulative S144 order intake has crossed ~9 GW. Newer, larger turbines mean more megawatts (and more revenue) per unit installed.
  • It underwrites the capacity ramp. A firm backlog gives Suzlon the confidence (and the cash, thanks to the clean balance sheet) to expand manufacturing to 4.5 GW/year and beyond. Order book and capacity feed each other.
Why an order book is worth more than a single quarter's revenue

A quarter's revenue tells you what Suzlon delivered in the past three months — and for a lumpy business, that can be noisy (a slipped grid connection can dent it). The order book tells you what's coming: contracts already signed, waiting to be built. A backlog of ~6.4 GW against ~2.5 GW of annual deliveries means Suzlon has more than two years of work already booked — so even a soft quarter doesn't threaten the medium-term trajectory. That's why the market reacts more to order wins and book size than to any single quarter's print. The risk flips accordingly: it's no longer "will there be demand?" but "can Suzlon execute — build, deliver and connect — fast enough to turn this backlog into revenue on schedule?" Execution, not demand, is now the swing factor.

06

The O&M installed-base annuity — the hidden gem

The quiet, high-margin service business that de-risks the lumpy turbine cycle.

This is the part of Suzlon most casual observers miss — and it may be the highest-quality piece of the whole business. Suzlon doesn't just sell turbines; it operates and maintains a 15+ GW installed fleet — the largest wind O&M business in India — earning a recurring, sticky, high-margin service fee year after year. In FY26 the O&M segment made ~₹759 crore of profit on ~₹2,484 crore of revenue — a ~30% margin, roughly double the WTG segment's margin.

Why the O&M annuity is such a stable, sticky, high-margin cash engine

Here's the mechanism. Every turbine Suzlon installs spins for 20–25 years — and over that life it needs constant care: servicing, spare parts, blade repairs, gearbox overhauls, software (SCADA) monitoring. Suzlon signs long O&M contracts (5–20 years) to do exactly that, often with performance guarantees. Three things make this golden:

1. It's recurring and predictable. Unlike a one-off turbine sale, the service fee arrives every single year regardless of the order cycle — a bond-like annuity. 2. It's sticky. The manufacturer that built the turbine is the natural, cheapest, most-trusted party to maintain it — switching is disruptive, so customers rarely leave. 3. It's high-margin. Once the fleet exists, servicing it is a light-asset, spares-and-labour business earning ~30% margins — far above the capital-heavy manufacturing side. And it compounds: every new gigawatt Suzlon installs permanently enlarges the annuity base. So the lumpy turbine business is quietly building a growing, steady, cash-rich service annuity underneath it — the definition of a hidden gem.

Suzlon has doubled down here by acquiring a 51% stake in Renom Energy Services (Sept 2024) — India's largest multi-brand O&M company, with assets under management crossing 3 GW. "Multi-brand" is the key word: Renom services turbines made by other manufacturers too, so Suzlon can now earn O&M income across the whole installed base of Indian wind — not just its own machines. That widens the annuity moat well beyond Suzlon's own fleet.

Selling a turbine is a transaction. Maintaining it for 25 years is a relationship — and the relationship is where the steady, high-margin money is.
07

The S144 turbine & vertical integration — the product moat

The flagship machine, the manufacturing base, and why making the parts in-house matters.

A wind company lives or dies on its turbine. Suzlon's growth is built on the S144 — a 3.x MW machine (extendable to ~3.15 MW) with a 144-metre rotor and hub heights of 140–160 metres. It is one of the largest turbines built in India, designed to squeeze more energy out of India's low-to-medium wind sites — the taller and wider a turbine, the more wind it catches and the more power it makes per unit.

Why the S144 is the right machine at the right time

  • More megawatts per turbine. Moving from older ~2 MW machines to the 3.x MW S144 means each installed turbine produces far more power — better project economics for customers and more revenue per unit for Suzlon. It's why the S144 dominates the order book (cumulative intake ~9 GW).
  • Built for Indian wind. The big rotor and tall towers are tuned for India's specific, often modest wind speeds — a genuine engineering fit that international designs (built for windier Europe) don't always match.
  • Certified and scaling. The S144 3.x MW series has the required domestic certification (MNRE RLMM listing), and Suzlon has added production lines and expanded nacelle capacity at Daman and Pondicherry to build it at volume.

Vertical integration — the cost-and-quality moat

Suzlon is unusually vertically integrated for a turbine maker: it manufactures nacelles and blades in-house across ~14 facilities in India, makes tubular towers, and runs R&D centres in Germany, the Netherlands, Denmark and India. Its manufacturing capacity is ~4.5 GW/year and expanding. Making the key parts itself gives Suzlon three edges: cost control (a 20–25% cost advantage from local sourcing and lower logistics), quality control (critical after the historical blade issues), and a dense domestic supply chain that international rivals struggle to replicate. The Renom acquisition (§06) extends this integration downstream into multi-brand O&M.

08

The competitive set — who else builds India's wind turbines

A concentrated market where domestic scale and local content are winning.

India's wind-turbine market is concentrated among a handful of players, and the competitive dynamics have recently shifted in favour of domestic manufacturers like Suzlon. Together, Suzlon and Inox Wind captured roughly 70% of 2025 turbine contracts, aided by a 20–25% cost advantage from local sourcing.

Why the field currently tilts Suzlon's way

Two structural shifts help Suzlon right now. First, international OEMs are retreating from India's onshore wind market — Siemens Gamesa selling down, Vestas and GE trimming exposure under import-cost and GST pressures — which removes competition and hands share to domestic players. Second, local content is a real cost advantage: Suzlon's Indian supply chain gives it a 20–25% edge over import-reliant rivals, and policy (customs duty, RLMM local-certification rules) rewards domestic manufacturing. The main watch-item is Envision and other Chinese players, who bring formidable scale and could compete hard on price and technology. But for now, Suzlon's combination of scale, integration, a clean balance sheet and the O&M moat leaves it the strongest-positioned domestic name.

09

Underneath the headline, profit before tax rose 67% to ₹2,422 crore — and the balance sheet, once the company's death sentence, is now its greatest asset.

10

Valuation — the rich-multiple debate

A re-rated turnaround priced for the order book to keep delivering.

Suzlon's stock has been one of India's great re-rating stories — up many-fold from its penny-stock depths as the turnaround played out. At a share price around ₹57 and a market cap of ~₹79,000 crore (July 2026), it trades on a trailing P/E of ~25× and a striking price-to-book of ~13×. For a capital-goods manufacturer, those are high multiples — the market is paying up for the growth, the order book and the clean balance sheet, and the debate is whether that premium is deserved.

Why a manufacturer trades at ~25× / ~13× book — and why bears push back

Ordinarily a cyclical capital-goods maker trades on a modest multiple, because its earnings swing with the cycle. Suzlon is priced like a structural growth story instead: the market is looking past today's numbers to a company with a record and growing order book, riding a multi-year India wind supercycle, with a debt-free balance sheet and a compounding high-margin O&M annuity. If deliveries scale toward the company's ambition (it has talked of a path to ~10 GW of annual renewable sales and a 15 GW order book by FY31), today's earnings badly understate the future — and the rich multiple is on tomorrow's profit. That's the bull maths.

The bears counter with three points: (1) it's still a cyclical, project-based manufacturer — turbine cycles turn, and one soft year could compress the multiple hard; (2) reported earnings and ROE are flattered by the non-cash deferred-tax credit, so the "real" P/E is higher than it looks; and (3) at ~25× earnings and ~13× book, there is little room for a delivery slip, a margin wobble, or a demand pause — Suzlon is a high-beta, sentiment-driven stock, and any stumble in the order-book execution could trigger a sharp de-rating. Both sides stare at the same order book and disagree on the price.

The market isn't pricing Suzlon for what it earns today — it's pricing it for the wind supercycle it's betting Suzlon will ride for years.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine turnaround riding a real tailwind — priced for the execution to arrive.

Suzlon is one of the most complete turnaround-plus-growth stories in Indian markets: a company that survived near-death, cleaned its balance sheet, and now sits on a record order book at the start of an India wind supercycle, with a high-margin service annuity underneath. The debate is not whether the recovery is real — it plainly is — but about execution, cyclicality, and the price you pay for it.

The bull case

  • Turnaround complete — from ₹13,000 Cr+ debt to ₹2,384 Cr net cash; a clean balance sheet that funds growth and survives cycles.
  • Record order book — ~6.4 GW (~₹40k Cr+), 66% PSU/C&I, 2.5×+ forward coverage: booked, high-quality, multi-year visibility.
  • India wind supercycle — record ~6 GW added in FY26, 100 GW target by 2030; Suzlon is the largest domestic OEM to serve it.
  • O&M annuity moat — 15+ GW serviced fleet at ~30% margin, extended multi-brand via Renom; recurring, sticky, compounding.
  • Structural share gains — foreign OEMs retreating; local content gives a 20–25% cost edge; the S144 is the right machine for India.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~25× earnings and ~13× book leave little margin for error; a high-beta, sentiment-driven stock prone to sharp swings.
  • Earnings quality — FY26 PAT and ROE are flattered by a ₹742 Cr non-cash deferred-tax credit; the "clean" P/E is higher than it looks.
  • Cyclical & lumpy — a project-based manufacturer whose quarters swing on land, grid and approval timing; execution of the big order book is the risk.
  • Competition — Inox Wind at home and Chinese scale (Envision) could pressure share and pricing over time.
  • Ownership optics — a low ~11.7% promoter stake, past block sales and a chequered governance history keep some investors cautious.
The bottom line

Suzlon is a genuine, hard-won turnaround — a company that clawed back from the edge of bankruptcy, wiped out its debt, and now rides one of the strongest structural tailwinds in Indian energy with a record order book and a high-quality service annuity. The business is in the best shape of its life. What dominates the investment case is no longer survival or demand — it's execution and price. The story over the next few years will be told in a handful of numbers: the pace of WTG deliveries (can it convert the ~6.4 GW backlog on time?), the order-book trajectory (does the supercycle keep feeding it?), and the O&M fleet growth (does the annuity keep compounding?). Deliver those, and a premium multiple can be defended; stumble on execution with a ~25× / ~13×-book stock, and there is a long way to fall. A remarkable comeback — now priced to keep performing.
