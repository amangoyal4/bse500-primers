Aarti Drugs, India's bulk-drug volume machine

A complete walk through one of India's broadest active-pharmaceutical-ingredient makers — the company that quietly produces more of certain antibiotic and anti-protozoal molecules than anyone else on Earth — how a bulk-drug business actually earns its keep, why its profit lives in a thin spread that widens and narrows with input chemicals it imports, and why a falling revenue line can be good news rather than bad.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Aarti Drugs Limited (ADL) makes the raw drug itself — the actual chemical that does the healing inside a tablet — for the world's generic-medicine industry. These are active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), also called bulk drugs: the fine white powders that a formulations company presses into a Metformin tablet or a Ciprofloxacin capsule. Aarti Drugs is not a brand you see at the chemist; it is the supplier two steps behind the brand. It runs one of India's broadest API portfolios — 50-plus molecules across antibiotics, anti-diabetics, anti-inflammatories, anti-protozoals and antifungals — plus smaller businesses in pharma intermediates, specialty chemicals and finished formulations. It is part of the Mumbai-based Aarti group, and it exports to 100-plus countries.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a genuine world leader — in molecules you have never heard of. A ~₹3,900 Cr company by market value, Aarti Drugs is nonetheless the largest manufacturer on the planet of several specific bulk drugs — the fluoroquinolone antibiotics (ciprofloxacin, ofloxacin, norfloxacin and cousins), plus Metronidazole Benzoate, Tinidazole, Ketoconazole, Celecoxib and Nimesulide — and a top-tier global maker of Metformin (the anti-diabetic). Leadership here is measured in tonnes of a molecule, not in shelf-brand recognition.
  • The profit is a thin, cyclical spread — not the sticker price. Aarti Drugs buys imported and domestic key starting materials (KSMs) and intermediates, converts them into API, and sells at prices set by a competitive global generics market. In FY26 it turned ~₹2,565 Cr of revenue into just ~₹309 Cr of EBITDA — a ~12% operating margin. That thin band, not the top line, is the whole game, and it swings with input costs and Chinese pricing.
  • FY26 was a recovery year after a hard stretch. After two years of squeezed margins (FY23–FY25), revenue rose ~7% and net profit ~16% to ~₹195 Cr, helped by fast-growing formulations and specialty chemicals and a firmer pricing environment. The stock trades around 20× earnings — a mid-cycle multiple for a diversified but competitive bulk-drug maker climbing back toward its better margins.
In plain terms

Picture the pharmacy supply chain in reverse. A patient buys a branded tablet; the brand-owner bought the pressed formulation; the formulator bought the pure drug powder — the API — from a company like Aarti Drugs; and Aarti Drugs bought the chemical building blocks (the KSMs and intermediates) that it cooked into that powder. Aarti Drugs sits at the least glamorous, most competitive link: it is a high-volume chemical converter that happens to make medicine. Its edge is not one blockbuster molecule but breadth — dozens of bulk drugs, made cheaply and at scale, with world-leading share in a handful of them. Its profit is the gap between what it pays for input chemicals and what a crowded generics market will pay for the finished API. FY26 was a year that gap widened again after a bruising couple of years when it did not.

01

Bulk drugs 101: what an API actually is

No chemistry degree needed — just the powder, the chain above it, and why margins are thin.

Every medicine is two things stuck together: the active ingredient that treats you, and the inactive stuff (fillers, binders, coating) that turns it into a swallowable tablet. The active ingredient is the API — the "bulk drug." Making it is a chemistry business, not a branding one: you take cheap starting chemicals and run them through a sequence of reactions until you have a pure, regulator-approved drug substance. Aarti Drugs is one of India's larger, broader players in exactly this. To follow the company you need five terms.

The only bulk-drug terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
API / bulk drugThe active pharmaceutical ingredient — the pure chemical that treats the illness (e.g. ciprofloxacin, metformin). Aarti Drugs' core product. Sold by the kilogram/tonne to formulators, not to patients.
KSM & intermediateKey Starting Material and the half-built chemicals between it and the finished API. The input bill. India (and Aarti Drugs) still imports many KSMs — heavily from China — so their price is the biggest swing in the cost base.
FormulationThe finished dosage form — the actual tablet, capsule or syrup. A step downstream of the API. Aarti Drugs does some of this too, via its Pinnacle subsidiary, but it is mostly an API supplier to other formulators.
Regulated vs semi-regulated market"Regulated" = strict agencies (US FDA, EU EDQM) with high entry barriers and steadier prices; "semi-regulated" = the rest. A higher regulated-market share means stickier, better-quality revenue.
The spreadWhat Aarti Drugs keeps: API selling price minus KSM/intermediate cost minus conversion. A few rupees per gram, in effect. Widen or narrow it a little and the whole year's profit moves — the crux of section 05.
The one fact that shapes the whole sector: the API business is a commodity converter's game

Unlike a patented drug — where one molecule can earn a fortune for years — a generic API is a commodity. Once a drug is off-patent, many companies can and do make its API, in India and China, and buyers shop on price and reliability. So a bulk-drug maker rarely gets rich on any single molecule; it competes on cost, scale, quality-compliance and breadth. That is why margins across the sector sit in a modest band, why input-cost swings pass through so violently, and why the two structural escapes — going upstream into your own intermediates (backward integration) and downstream into formulations (branded value-add) — are the only ways to earn a durably better spread. Everything Aarti Drugs does strategically is some version of those two moves.

Who buys, and how the powder reaches a patient

  • Generic formulators — Indian and global drug companies that buy API by the tonne and press it into tablets. They are Aarti Drugs' direct customers, at home and across 100-plus export markets.
  • Other API/intermediate makers — some of Aarti Drugs' output is intermediates sold to fellow manufacturers, and some specialty chemicals go to non-pharma industries entirely.
  • Its own formulations arm — a slice of demand is captive: Aarti Drugs feeds some of its API into Pinnacle Life Science, its formulations subsidiary, capturing the downstream margin itself (section 08).
02

The molecule leadership: small company, world-scale share

The surprising heart of the story — a mid-cap that is No. 1 on Earth in several drugs.

The most counter-intuitive thing about Aarti Drugs is the mismatch between its modest size and its outsized position in specific molecules. This is not a company that is vaguely present across pharma; in a defined handful of bulk drugs it is the single largest producer in the world. Understanding which molecules, and in which therapy areas, is understanding what actually earns the money.

The genuine surprise: a ₹3,900 Cr company that is No. 1 on Earth in its niches

Investors instinctively map "world's largest producer" onto a giant like Divi's or a multinational. Aarti Drugs upends that. It is a mid-cap by market value, yet it out-produces everyone — including Chinese and Western majors — in fluoroquinolones, metronidazole benzoate, tinidazole, ketoconazole, celecoxib and nimesulide. The reason is that these are unglamorous, off-patent, high-tonnage molecules where the prize goes to whoever can make them cheapest and most reliably at scale, year after year, with the compliance record to sell into regulated markets. Aarti Drugs chose exactly those battles and won them through sheer manufacturing depth rather than blockbuster luck. It is leadership by grind, not by patent — and that is a more durable, if lower-margin, kind of moat.

What Aarti Drugs actually sells: four businesses in one

A big API core, plus three deliberately-grown satellites with different economics.

Aarti Drugs is really four businesses stacked around a single chemistry competence. The API core is by far the largest and defines the company; the other three — pharma intermediates, specialty chemicals and formulations — are smaller but faster-growing, and each exists to earn a better or steadier margin than plain bulk-drug conversion. Why the three satellites matter more than their size suggests

FY26's ~33–37% growth in formulations and specialty chemicals — against ~2% in API — is not noise; it is the company slowly rebuilding its mix toward earnings that don't live and die by a Chinese price list.

04

The company, the group, and the confusing cousins

A four-decade-old Aarti-group company — and emphatically not the other two "Aarti" stocks.

Aarti Drugs is one of three separately-listed companies that share the "Aarti" name and a common founding lineage — a fact that trips up first-time investors constantly. Before anything else, it is worth being clear about who is who, because the three are distinct businesses with distinct economics, and only one of them is the subject of this primer.

Do not confuse the three "Aarti" stocks. Aarti Drugs (this company, NSE: AARTIDRUGS) is the broad-portfolio bulk-drug/API maker. Aarti Pharmalabs (AARTIPHARM) is a separate listed pharma/intermediates company demerged from Aarti Industries. Aarti Industries (AARTIIND) is the large specialty-chemicals company. All three trace to the same Gogri-family entrepreneurial house, but they are independent listed entities with their own boards, balance sheets and share prices. This primer is about Aarti Drugs — the API/bulk-drug business — only.

Timeline

  • 1990s–2000s

  • 2014

  • ~2021

  • FY23–FY25

  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters (the Gogri / Aarti-group founding family and associates) hold roughly 54% (approx.) — a controlling, aligned, entrepreneur-led stake. Confirm the exact figure in the latest shareholding filing.
  • Institutions: domestic institutions (DIIs) hold a meaningful ~10%, with foreign holding low (~1.5%); the balance is public float. It is a promoter-and-domestic-owned mid-cap, lightly held by foreign money.
  • Footprint: around 15 manufacturing facilities — the bulk in Maharashtra (Tarapur), several in Gujarat (including the Saykha specialty-chem site), and one in Himachal Pradesh (Baddi) for formulations. Several sites carry US FDA and other regulatory approvals, enabling regulated-market API exports (e.g. ciprofloxacin HCl, zolpidem tartrate to the US).
Why the ownership shape is both a comfort and a caution

A founder-family that has run this business for four decades and still owns a majority is, on balance, a reassurance: management is invested, patient, and knows the chemistry cold — exactly what a long-cycle, compliance-heavy manufacturing business needs. The flip side of a closely-held mid-cap is the usual one — minority investors depend on the promoter's capital-allocation judgement and on related-party dealings across the wider Aarti house staying clean and arm's-length. There is no dominant foreign or institutional check on the register. The record here has been that of a steady, unflashy compounder rather than a value-destroyer; the discipline to watch is that expansions and any inter-group transactions keep serving the listed company's minorities first.

05

The spread, not the sticker: how a bulk-drug maker earns

The single most important idea in the case — and it explains why falling revenue can be fine.

The intuitive way to read Aarti Drugs — watch its revenue — is precisely the wrong way. A bulk-drug maker does not keep the price of its API; it keeps the spread between that price and the cost of the input chemicals it converts. And because API selling prices and KSM input costs tend to move together, revenue can rise or fall for reasons that say almost nothing about profit. The whole company is best read through one figure: EBITDA margin — the spread as a percentage.

Of every ₹100 collected, roughly ₹88 goes straight back out — overwhelmingly to raw material — leaving ~₹12 of EBITDA. The business is the ~12% slice on the right; the big bar on the left is mostly a pass-through of chemical costs.

Now watch why the top line misleads. When global KSM prices fall — as they did through FY25 as Chinese oversupply pushed input chemicals down — Aarti Drugs passes much of that saving on, so its API selling prices fall too, and reported revenue drops. That is exactly what happened in FY25: revenue fell ~5%, yet the EBITDA margin held around 12.6% because the spread was intact. Conversely, when input costs spike, revenue can balloon while the spread gets crushed in the lag before higher API prices stick. A rising revenue line is not automatically good news, and a falling one is not automatically bad. Only the margin tells you which world the company is in.

The device that fits only a broad-portfolio bulk-drug maker: 50 molecules, one weighted spread

Here is the construct that makes Aarti Drugs specifically Aarti Drugs. No single one of its 50-plus molecules is the company — each is a small, competitive, commodity market on its own. What Aarti Drugs really sells is a weighted-average spread across a basket of bulk drugs: dozens of thin, independent margins, blended into one number. That is its hidden diversification. When ciprofloxacin pricing is ugly, metformin or metronidazole may be firm; when antibiotics soften, anti-inflammatories may hold. The portfolio is a shock-absorber — the reason a company at the mercy of commodity chemistry still posts a fairly steady ~12–14% margin rather than lurching molecule by molecule. The number to track is not any product; it is the blended EBITDA margin, and whether the mix shift and integration can push it back toward the mid-teens the company earned in better years.

This is why comparing Aarti Drugs to a branded-formulations pharma company is a category error. A branded drugmaker sells a marketed product at a marketed price and keeps 20–30% margins on brand and patent. Aarti Drugs sells a commodity chemical into a global auction and keeps ~12%. It is closer to a fine-chemicals converter than to a consumer-facing pharma name — and it should be valued, and its quarters read, on that basis: tonnes shipped and the spread per tonne, not a sticker price.

06

The China question: the dependency and the tailwind

The one external force that both threatens the margin and could re-rate the whole sector.

No force shapes an Indian bulk-drug maker more than China — and it cuts both ways at once. China is simultaneously Aarti Drugs' biggest input supplier, its fiercest price competitor, and — through India's push to break free of it — the source of its most important structural tailwind. A first-time investor cannot understand the margin cycle without holding all three at once.

The two faces of the China dependency

  • China sets Aarti Drugs' input costs. India still imports a large share of its KSMs and intermediates from China. When Chinese chemical prices move — on energy costs, environmental crackdowns, or deliberate over-production — Aarti Drugs' cost base moves with them. It does not fully control its own inputs.
  • China sets Aarti Drugs' selling prices too. Chinese API makers compete head-on in the same molecules. When China floods the market with cheap fluoroquinolone or metformin — as in FY23–FY25 — global API prices fall and the spread compresses. The FY23–FY25 margin squeeze was, in large part, a Chinese-oversupply story.
The structural counter-force: India is trying to un-depend on China

The pandemic exposed how dangerously reliant the world — and India itself — had become on Chinese APIs and KSMs for essential medicines. The policy response is real and durable: India's Production-Linked Incentive (PLI) scheme for bulk drugs, dedicated bulk-drug parks (in Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh and Andhra Pradesh), and a broader "China+1" instinct among global buyers who want a second, non-Chinese source. For an established, broad-portfolio, regulated-market-approved API maker like Aarti Drugs, this is a rare structural positive: it widens the customer base, supports domestic KSM production that could ease the import dependency over time, and rewards exactly the scaled, compliant Indian producers that already exist. It will not repeal the commodity cycle — but it tilts the long-run field toward Indian bulk drugs.

There is a quiet quality upgrade buried in the market mix. Aarti Drugs' regulated-market contribution rose from roughly 66% to ~73% of the relevant revenue between FY25 and FY26 — a meaningful shift, because regulated markets (US FDA, EU) are harder to enter, stickier, and priced more steadily than the semi-regulated tail. On a standalone basis the split is about 63% domestic and 37% exports across 100-plus countries. A China+1 world rewards exactly this profile: a scaled Indian maker with the compliance record to sell into the demanding markets that global buyers are actively trying to source outside China.

Why this reframes the risk: the China dependency is the reason Aarti Drugs' margin is cyclical and only partly in its own hands — a Chinese price war can compress the spread regardless of how well the company runs its plants. But the same China problem, seen from India's policy chair, is Aarti Drugs' long-term friend: every rupee of PLI support, every bulk-drug park, every global buyer seeking a non-Chinese source pulls in the direction of the scaled Indian API maker. Read the two together — the cyclical Chinese threat to the spread, and the structural Indian tailwind behind the demand — and the investment debate comes into focus.
07

The margin cycle: a hard stretch, then a turn

FY23–FY25 was the squeeze; FY26 into FY27 is the recovery — shown without flattering it.

It would be misleading to present Aarti Drugs as a smooth grower. Bulk drugs are cyclical, and the last few years have shown both edges of that. A candid look at the recent path — the input-cost-and-oversupply squeeze, and the subsequent recovery — is the fairest way to judge what a normal year actually looks like.

The honest reading is this. FY23 through FY25 was a genuinely difficult period: Chinese oversupply dragged API prices down, input costs stayed stubborn, and the spread narrowed — a reminder that this is a cyclical, price-taking business, not a steady-eddy grower. But the company held its volumes and cost discipline through it, and FY26 showed the turn: revenue back to growth, profit up ~16%, and — crucially — the fastest growth coming from the higher-value formulations and specialty-chemical lines. Recent quarters have extended the recovery, with EBITDA growth outpacing revenue on firmer pricing. The cycle has not been abolished; it has simply turned favourable again.

The uncomfortable truth to internalise about the cycle

A bulk-drug maker's job is to run tight, compliant, low-cost plants and then survive the price cycle that global chemistry hands it. Aarti Drugs can improve its odds — broaden the portfolio, integrate backward, grow formulations, chase regulated markets — but it cannot promise any single year's spread, because the biggest swing factor, Chinese API pricing, is set outside its walls. FY26 was a good year because the cycle cooperated and the self-help compounded on top. A disciplined investor prices the recovery knowing the model does not control whether the next Chinese price war arrives in FY28 or FY31 — only that one eventually will, and that breadth and integration are what carry the company through it.

08

Climbing off pure API: the two escapes from commodity

Backward into your own inputs, forward into finished formulations — the whole self-help strategy.

If the API core is a thin, cyclical spread, the strategic project is to depend on it a little less each year. Aarti Drugs is pursuing the only two durable escapes a bulk-drug maker has: go upstream to own more of its input chemistry, and go downstream to sell finished, branded dosage forms. Both widen the blended spread; both are visible in the FY26 numbers.

Escape 1 — backward integration and specialty chemicals

The further up its own supply chain Aarti Drugs climbs, the less of its cost is set by an imported KSM price list. Each rung — buying KSMs, making its own intermediates, scaling specialty chemicals, then selling finished formulations — converts a commodity, price-taking activity into one with more of its own pricing power. It makes a growing share of its own intermediates, and it is scaling a specialty-chemicals business — a new greenfield plant at Saykha, Gujarat — that both diversifies revenue into non-pharma fine chemicals and deepens the in-house chemistry. Specialty chemicals grew ~37% in FY26 off a small base, and the Saykha ramp is meant to lift both capacity utilisation and gross margin as it fills. (The value chain in section 03 traces the same climb from imported input to branded dose.)

Escape 2 — formulations via Pinnacle

The downstream prize is Pinnacle Life Science, Aarti Drugs' formulations subsidiary — finished tablets and capsules rather than raw powder. Formulations grew ~33% in FY26 to ~₹331 Cr, and exports now make up the majority of that (around two-thirds of Q4 FY26 formulation sales), which is the higher-quality kind of growth: branded, regulated-market, value-added revenue that captures the margin a formulator earns on top of the API. Some of Pinnacle's API is supplied captively by Aarti Drugs itself, so the group keeps margin at both links of the chain.

Why both escapes are worth the capital even in a cyclical business

You might ask why a company would keep investing to escape a business it already leads. Because leadership in a commodity earns only a commodity margin. Every tonne of intermediate Aarti Drugs makes instead of imports, and every Pinnacle tablet it sells instead of raw API, lifts the blended spread and — just as importantly — makes it steadier through the Chinese price cycle that periodically batters pure bulk drugs. Over a full cycle, a group that is one-quarter formulations-and-specialty and climbing should earn a higher, less volatile margin than one that is pure API. The self-help won't rescue a bad quarter for ciprofloxacin, but it slowly changes what a "normal" year looks like — and that, not any single molecule, is the multi-year thesis.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, cost-driven API field with a clear quality leader and several specialists.

Indian bulk drugs is a competitive, fragmented business where the winners are those with the deepest chemistry, the tightest costs and the cleanest regulatory records. Aarti Drugs is one of the broader, more established players by molecule count and world-scale share, but it is not the sector's most profitable name — that reputation belongs to the more specialised, higher-margin CDMO and niche-API houses.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, this is a cost-and-compliance contest, not a branding one — in commodity APIs the lowest-cost, most reliable, best-integrated producer wins the widest spread on the same molecule, which is why Aarti Drugs' decades of manufacturing depth and its backward integration matter more than any marketing. Second, the listed Indian peers have split into two camps: the premium CDMO/complex-API names (Divi's, Neuland, Laurus) that earn richer margins on harder chemistry, and the scale-generic names (Aarti Drugs, Granules) that earn thinner margins on high tonnage. Aarti Drugs sits firmly in the second camp and is judged accordingly — cheaper on multiple, more cyclical, with its re-rating case resting on climbing up-value rather than on already being a premium franchise.

A recovering cyclical on a mid-cycle multiple — the question is the margin, not the growth rate.

FY26 was a recovery: revenue up ~7%, profit up ~16%, with the diversification arms carrying the growth. Aarti Drugs is not priced as a story stock — it trades around a low-twenties earnings multiple, roughly in line with a diversified but cyclical bulk-drug maker. The debate is not whether the price embeds heroic growth; it is whether the ~12% margin is a floor from which mix and integration lift it, or a ceiling the Chinese cycle keeps pressing down.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A world-scale commodity leader climbing up-value — asked whether it can lift the margin.

Aarti Drugs enters FY27 in improving shape: a recovery year behind it, world-leading share in several molecules, fast-growing formulations and specialty chemicals, fresh capacity at Saykha, and a China+1 policy wind at its back. What no bulk-drug maker can promise is the one thing that decides the year — that the spread between imported input chemistry and a Chinese-influenced API price stays kind. That tension frames both cases.

The bull case

  • World-scale molecule leadership — No. 1 globally in fluoroquinolones, metronidazole benzoate, tinidazole and more; a manufacturing moat built over four decades.
  • Mix shift up-value — formulations (+33%) and specialty chemicals (+37%) are growing far faster than API, structurally lifting and steadying the blended margin.
  • China+1 tailwind — PLI, bulk-drug parks and global buyers seeking a non-Chinese source play directly to a scaled, compliant Indian API maker.
  • Structural demand — antibiotics, anti-diabetics and anti-infectives are essential, non-discretionary medicines with rising global volumes (diabetes, infection load).
  • Cheap-ish for the quality — a ~20× multiple on a recovering, diversifying, world-leading manufacturer with room for margins to rise toward the mid-teens.

The bear case

  • Commodity margin, Chinese pricing — the spread is thin (~12%) and set partly in China; a fresh oversupply wave can compress it fast, as FY23–FY25 proved.
  • Input-cost & import dependency — heavy reliance on imported KSMs (much from China) means the cost base is only partly in the company's control.
  • Range-bound top line — revenue has hovered around ₹2,400–2,565 Cr for years; growth has been mix and margin, not volume breakout.
  • Capital intensity & working capital — ~15 plants, ongoing capex and a wide inventory tie up cash; returns are decent but not exceptional.
  • Premium peers set the bar — Divi's, Laurus and Neuland earn richer margins on harder chemistry; Aarti Drugs must keep climbing up-value to close a persistent quality gap.
The bottom line

Aarti Drugs is a four-decade-old chemistry house that quietly makes more of certain essential medicines than anyone on Earth, earns a thin commodity spread for doing it, and is slowly rebuilding its mix toward the higher-value formulations and specialty chemicals that don't live at the mercy of a Chinese price list. The self-help is genuine and visible in the FY26 numbers; the cyclicality is equally genuine and visible in FY23–FY25. The next test is concrete and datable: whether the blended EBITDA margin can push from ~12% back toward the mid-teens through FY27 as Saykha specialty-chem capacity fills, formulations exports scale and API pricing holds firm. Reclaim that margin and a world-leading manufacturer is being underrated on its commodity label; lose it to the next oversupply wave, and FY26 was simply the good year the portfolio is built to ride out until the cycle turns again.
