00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Endurance Technologies makes the metal parts that go inside two-wheelers, three-wheelers and cars — aluminium castings, front forks and shock absorbers, disc brakes and clutches. It is one of India's largest auto-component suppliers, built around the two-wheeler, headquartered in Aurangabad (now Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar), Maharashtra, and it also runs a sizeable European casting business with nine plants in Italy and Germany.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A four-product, one-vehicle supplier. Endurance clusters four businesses — aluminium die-casting (the biggest, ~42% of revenue), suspension (~26%), braking (disc brakes ~12%), and transmission (~4%) — plus alloy wheels and an aftermarket book. The unifying logic is content on a single scooter or motorcycle: on some Bajaj and Royal Enfield models it supplies parts worth 12–15% of the bike's ex-showroom price.
  • India + Europe, two very different engines. Roughly three-quarters of revenue is India (higher-margin, 2W-driven) and ~24% is Europe (lower-margin, scale die-casting for European carmakers). FY26 revenue was ~₹14,596 Cr (consolidated total income ~₹14,720 Cr, +26%), with a ~14.2% EBITDA margin and ₹952 Cr net profit (+14%).
  • The EV question is a content story, not a death sentence. An electric scooter still needs suspension, brakes, castings and — now — a battery-management system and motor-control electronics. Endurance has been buying its way into EV electronics (the Maxwell Energy BMS acquisition) and racking up EV-specific orders worth over ₹1,300 Cr/year. The debate is whether that new content offsets the parts EVs delete, at a stock trading around 38–39× earnings.
In plain terms

Think of Endurance as the parts-bin of the Indian two-wheeler. When Bajaj, Royal Enfield, Honda or TVS designs a new bike, Endurance wants to supply the engine casing, the front forks, the brakes and the clutch — as much of the bike as it can win. It earns money not just when more bikes are sold, but when it puts more of its own parts on each bike. On top of that domestic core sits a big European casting arm that supplies car-makers there — lower margin, but it adds scale and technology. The whole company is a geared bet on India's two-wheeler recovery, on richer, safer, higher-value bikes, and on whether it can keep winning content as scooters go electric.

01

Auto-component economics 101: how a Tier-1 supplier earns

No engineering degree required — just the handful of ideas that drive a parts business.

An auto-component maker sits one step upstream of the vehicle brand. It doesn't sell to the public; it sells to the OEM (Original Equipment Manufacturer — Bajaj, Honda, a European carmaker). Its revenue is, roughly, the number of vehicles the OEM builds × the value of Endurance's parts on each one. Understand those two levers and you understand the business.

The core ideas of a parts supplier

The two ways a parts maker grows

(1) The market grows — its OEMs build more vehicles, so Endurance ships more parts. This is the cyclical lever, tied to the India two-wheeler cycle and European auto demand. (2) It wins more content — it adds a new product (say, brakes on a bike where it only supplied suspension), or moves to a higher-value version (an ABS brake instead of a drum brake, an alloy wheel instead of steel). This second lever is the durable one: it lets Endurance grow faster than its end-markets, and it's exactly what the "order book" and "content per vehicle" numbers measure. A great component company is one that keeps stealing more of each vehicle for itself.

Why the two-wheeler is the right thing to be built around

  • Volume. India is the world's largest two-wheeler market by units — the scooters and motorcycles that move a billion-plus people. That gives a 2W-focused supplier enormous, recurring volume.
  • Under-served by global giants. The world's biggest component companies (Bosch, ZF, Schaeffler) are built mostly for cars. India's 2W-specific needs — cost, ruggedness, local design — favour a focused domestic specialist like Endurance.
  • Aftermarket tail. Bikes wear out forks, brake pads and clutches; a base of vehicles on the road creates a recurring replacement-parts business (~5% of revenue) at healthy margins.

The four product lines, broken into parts

Die-casting is the giant; the other three are where the pricing power and safety-content live.

Endurance is really four businesses stapled to the same vehicle. The largest by far is aluminium die-casting; the other three — suspension, braking, transmission — are smaller but carry more engineering value, more safety content and, often, better margins. What each line actually is (jargon decoded)

  • Aluminium die-casting — melting aluminium and forcing it into moulds at high pressure to make engine cases, crankcases, cylinder heads, housings and structural parts. It is capital-intensive and scale-driven: the winner is whoever runs the biggest, most efficient casting machines at the highest utilisation. Margins are thinner here, but scale is the moat. This is also where the European business sits.
  • Suspension — front forks and shock absorbers: the parts that soak up the bumps. Endurance is a market leader in 2W suspension in India. This is a higher-value, ride-and-handling component with strong OEM stickiness.
  • Braking — disc brakes, drum brakes and complete brake assemblies, increasingly with ABS (anti-lock) and CBS (combined braking) content. This is the fastest-premiumising line: safety regulation is forcing more braking content onto every bike, which lifts the value Endurance can win per vehicle.
  • Transmission — clutches and continuously-variable transmission (CVT) parts: how power gets from engine to wheel. Smaller today, but a growing capability, especially for scooters.
  • Alloy wheels & aftermarket — cast-aluminium wheels (replacing steel — a premiumisation win) for both 2W and cars, plus a recurring replacement-parts book sold through distributors.
Why owning four lines is the whole strategy

A single-product supplier lives and dies by one part on one bike. Endurance's edge is that it can walk into an OEM and offer to supply the casing AND the forks AND the brakes AND the clutch on the same model. Each win it lands raises its content per vehicle — and because it already has the relationship, the tooling and the plant nearby, adding a second or third product to an existing model is high-return, low-risk growth. That is why the company obsesses over "cross-selling" more product lines into existing customers: it's the cheapest revenue it can buy.

03

The company and who controls it

A first-generation Aurangabad entrepreneur who bought out a Bajaj-family joint venture.

Endurance is a promoter-built, promoter-run company. Founder Anurang Jain — a first-generation entrepreneur — started the business in the mid-1980s in Aurangabad, grew it in the orbit of the Bajaj group (his family and the Bajaj family were long connected), and turned it into a global auto-component group before listing it in 2016.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter group: the Anurang Jain promoter family holds a controlling ~75% of the company — an unusually high, high-conviction promoter stake. (Within that, Anurang Jain personally is reported at ~31%.) A near-75% promoter holding means the founder's interests are heavily aligned with minority shareholders, but also means limited free float.
  • Anurang Jain is Managing Director and the driving force; the company remains a founder-led operation rather than a professionally-diffused one.
  • Institutional base: foreign institutions hold ~12%, mutual funds ~6%; the Government of Singapore is among the larger public holders — a respectable institutional following for a mid-cap.
  • Balance sheet: modest net borrowings (~₹1,300 Cr) against ~₹6,700 Cr of reserves — a comfortably-financed, cash-generative business that self-funds most of its capex and acquisitions.
The Bajaj connection: Endurance's history is intertwined with the Bajaj family, and Bajaj Auto remains its single largest and most strategically important customer. This is both its greatest asset — a deep, decades-long relationship on India's most successful bike franchise — and its most-cited risk (see §04). The relationship gives Endurance privileged access to Bajaj's models, including the Chetak EV.

The mechanism that lets Endurance out-grow the two-wheeler market — and the concentration risk that comes with it.

The single most important idea in the Endurance story is content per vehicle: how many rupees of Endurance parts sit on one bike. Because it supplies four product lines, it can keep raising that number on a model it already serves — the cheapest, highest-return growth in the business. But the flip side is customer concentration: a handful of OEMs, led by Bajaj, drive the bulk of demand.

The customer roster

  • Honda (HMSI) — India's scooter king; Endurance has strong suspension and casting content here, with headroom in brakes and transmission — a clear cross-sell target.
  • Royal Enfield — premium, higher-priced motorcycles where Endurance's wide content bundle earns more absolute rupees per bike.

Why "business won" is the number to watch

Each quarter Endurance reports how much new business it has won — future, contracted content on models yet to ramp. In FY26 its India business wins ran to roughly ₹1,596 Cr (almost all genuinely new business), spread across TVS, Royal Enfield, Honda, Suzuki, Hero and Mahindra. That order book is the leading indicator: it tells you how much Endurance's content-per-vehicle is set to rise before the volumes actually show up in revenue. A rising order book with a broadening customer list is the healthiest possible signal — growth and diversification at once.

The concentration question: a large share of demand traces back to Bajaj. If Bajaj's volumes stumble — a weak festive season, a share-loss to a rival, a strategy shift — Endurance feels it directly. The multi-year answer is exactly the widening customer base above: winning meaningful content at TVS, Honda, Suzuki, Hero and in Europe swaps single-customer risk for a more balanced book. Watch the non-Bajaj share of order wins as the de-risking gauge.
05

The European business

A second, very different company hiding inside the first — nine plants, lower margins, real scale.

Roughly a quarter of Endurance's revenue comes from Europe — a die-casting operation supplying car makers (not two-wheelers) from nine plants: six in Italy and three in Germany. It's a structurally different business from the Indian core: bigger volumes of aluminium castings, but thinner margins, a different customer set, and a growth story driven as much by acquisition as by organic wins.

Why keep a lower-margin European arm at all?

  • Scale in aluminium. Die-casting rewards volume; the European plants add serious tonnage and machine capability, sharpening Endurance's edge in a scale-driven craft.
  • Technology transfer. Supplying demanding European car OEMs forces higher engineering and quality standards that flow back into the Indian business — useful as Indian bikes premiumise.
  • Diversification. Europe's auto cycle is not synchronised with India's two-wheeler cycle; the two geographies partly smooth each other out.
  • Acquisition-led growth. Europe is where Endurance deploys M&A — the Italian Fonpresmetal Gap (2019) and, most recently, Stöferle, which drove roughly 29% European revenue growth in FY26. Buying established casters is faster than building greenfield plants in Europe.
The margin trade-off, made concrete

The Indian business earns high-teens-plus EBITDA margins; the European casting business earns much less. So a bigger Europe dilutes the blended group margin even as it adds revenue and profit in absolute rupees. That's why you'll sometimes see revenue jump sharply (Europe growing fast, helped by an acquisition) while the group EBITDA margin stays flat or edges down — it's the arithmetic of a lower-margin business becoming a larger slice of the mix, not a deterioration in the underlying Indian franchise. The bull reading: Europe is scale and optionality. The bear reading: it caps how high group margins can go.

06

The India two-wheeler cycle

Endurance is a geared play on Indian bikes recovering — and premiumising.

Because the Indian business is the profit engine and is overwhelmingly tied to two-wheelers, Endurance's near-term fortunes rise and fall with the India 2W cycle. That cycle has two moving parts that both favour Endurance right now: a volume recovery after several soft years, and a premiumisation shift toward bigger, better-equipped bikes that carry more Endurance content.

Volume: the cyclical lever

  • The Indian two-wheeler market went through a multi-year slump (demonetisation, a cost-raising emissions/insurance step-up, then the pandemic) that compressed unit sales well below the prior peak. A recovery in bike volumes lifts every Endurance product line at once.
  • Endurance's revenue is geared to this: when OEMs raise production, Endurance ships more castings, forks, brakes and clutches with high operating leverage on its fixed plant base.

Premiumisation: the durable lever

  • Bigger bikes, richer specs. Buyers are trading up to higher-cc motorcycles and better-equipped scooters. A premium bike carries more Endurance rupees — larger castings, upside-down forks, disc (not drum) brakes, alloy (not steel) wheels.
  • Safety regulation. Rules pushing ABS/CBS braking onto more of the fleet directly grow Endurance's braking content — the regulator is, in effect, mandating more of its product onto every bike.
  • Alloy over steel wheels. The shift to cast-aluminium alloy wheels is a pure content-up-sell that lands squarely in Endurance's casting wheelhouse.
Why premiumisation matters more than raw volume

Unit growth is cyclical — it comes and goes with the economy. Premiumisation is structural — it's a one-way ratchet upward in content per vehicle that continues even in a flat-volume year. That's the more valuable of the two levers, because it lets Endurance grow its Indian revenue faster than bike unit sales. Stack a cyclical volume recovery on top of a structural premiumisation trend, both landing on the same fixed plant base, and you get the operating leverage that a bull is paying up for. The catch: premiumisation also raises the stakes on winning the right content — miss the shift to a new brake or suspension technology and a rival captures the up-sell instead.

Margins and the aluminium question

Where the profit actually comes from — and why the group margin looks lower than the Indian business.

Endurance's group EBITDA margin runs around 14% — respectable for an auto-component maker, but a blended number that hides a sharp split: a high-teens-plus Indian business averaged down by a lower-margin European casting arm and the pass-through mechanics of aluminium. Understanding the margin is understanding three forces.

The three forces on the margin

  • Geography mix. India earns much more per rupee of sales than Europe. When Europe grows fast (as with the Stöferle acquisition), it pulls the blended margin down even though absolute profit rises. A flat group margin during a Europe-led revenue surge is the mix at work, not a problem.
  • Aluminium pass-through. Aluminium is the dominant raw material. Contracts let Endurance pass price changes to OEMs, but with a lag — so a sharp aluminium spike squeezes margin for a quarter or two before the pass-through catches up (and the reverse when aluminium falls). This is timing noise, not a structural margin issue.
  • Premiumisation & mix within India. Higher-value braking (ABS/CBS), alloy wheels and richer castings carry better margins than commodity parts — so a richer product mix lifts the Indian margin over time, partly offsetting the European drag.
What to watch: because the pass-through lag and geography mix both move the margin around, don't over-read a single quarter. The signal is the trend in the standalone (Indian) margin and whether premiumisation is lifting it — that's the real gauge of pricing power, separate from the mechanical drag of a growing, lower-margin Europe.
08

The EV transition: threat or a bigger parts bin?

The structural question every 2W-component maker must answer — and Endurance's answer is "buy the new content."

The scariest sentence for any engine-adjacent auto supplier is "the vehicle is going electric." For Endurance the honest answer is nuanced, not catastrophic: an electric two-wheeler deletes some of its content (the internal-combustion engine castings, the clutch/CVT) but keeps most of it (suspension, brakes, structural castings) and adds new content that didn't exist before (battery-management systems, motor controllers, DC-DC converters, EV-specific housings). Whether EV is a net threat or a net opportunity turns entirely on that arithmetic.

How Endurance is playing it — buy the electrons

  • Maxwell Energy Systems (BMS). Endurance acquired this battery-management-systems and power-electronics business (~₹308 Cr / ~$40M) to gain the "brains" of an EV — the smart controller that keeps a battery pack safe and efficient. It's winning fresh EV orders (new BMS variants, a DC-DC converter, an e-2W battery pack).
  • EV-specific casting and braking. The core skills transfer: EV motor housings and battery enclosures are die-castings; e-scooters still need Endurance brakes and forks.
  • A real, growing EV order book. Endurance's EV-related India order wins run to over ₹1,300 Cr/year (including Bajaj's EV business) — concrete evidence that electrification is adding to its book, not subtracting from it. Its Bajaj relationship gives it privileged content on the Chetak, one of India's best-selling electric scooters.
The honest read on EV

The bear worry — "EVs kill the transmission business and shrink engine castings" — is partly true: some legacy content does disappear. But the majority of Endurance's content (suspension, braking, structural casting) is powertrain-agnostic, and the company is deliberately buying its way into the new content EVs create. The strategic prize is that an early, credible EV-electronics capability lets Endurance raise content per vehicle on electric bikes even as ICE-specific parts fade. The risk is execution and competition: EV electronics is a crowded, fast-moving field where specialists and OEMs' own in-house teams compete hard, and it's a small slice of revenue today. Net: EV looks like a manageable transition and a content opportunity for a 2W-heavy supplier — provided the new-content wins keep outpacing the deleted parts.

The tension to hold: for a supplier this close to the two-wheeler powertrain, EV is simultaneously the biggest structural risk and the biggest new addressable market. The single best gauge is the EV order book: if EV-specific wins keep compounding faster than legacy transmission content erodes, the transition is being managed. If they stall, the bear case gains ground.

The industry and the rivals

A fragmented, consolidating auto-ancillary sector where focus is the edge.

India's auto-component industry is large, globally competitive and fragmenting into specialists. Endurance competes not against one rival but against a different set in each of its four product lines — and its edge is being a broad-bundle, 2W-focused supplier where most rivals are narrower or car-focused.

Where Endurance actually wins

No single rival matches Endurance across all four product lines on the two-wheeler. Gabriel is strong in suspension but not casting or braking; Sona BLW is an EV-driveline specialist mostly for cars; the global majors are car-centric and expensive for 2W. Endurance's defensible position is being the one-stop, cost-competitive, 2W-native supplier that can bundle castings, forks, brakes and clutches — and increasingly EV electronics — onto the same bike. The competitive risk isn't being out-flanked in one product; it's the slow grind of OEMs multi-sourcing, insourcing, or a focused rival winning the next-generation content (say, a new braking or EV architecture) before Endurance does.

The structural tailwind: two industry forces favour organised, scaled suppliers like Endurance — localisation (OEMs and the government pushing to make more of the vehicle in India) and premiumisation/safety content (each raising the value available to win per vehicle). Both expand Endurance's addressable content faster than raw 2W unit growth would suggest.

How to think about the ~38× multiple

At roughly ₹37,000–37,400 Cr market cap on ~₹952 Cr of FY26 profit, Endurance trades around 38–39× trailing earnings — a full price for an auto-component maker, though not the ~50×+ of the market's most-loved compounders. That multiple assumes the levers keep working: content-per-vehicle gains, the India 2W recovery and premiumisation, a growing EV order book, and Europe adding scale. Bulls argue a founder-led, four-line, cash-generative franchise with structural content tailwinds and EV optionality deserves a premium. Bears argue the group margin is mid-teens (capped by Europe), the growth is steady rather than explosive, and ~38× leaves little room if the 2W cycle wobbles or EV content wins disappoint. The dividend (~₹11.50/share for FY26, ~0.4% yield) signals a company returning cash while still funding growth — a compounder's posture, priced as one.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality, founder-led supplier — priced to keep executing on content and EV.

Endurance is one of the cleaner franchises in Indian auto components: a broad-bundle, 2W-native supplier with real scale in aluminium, a genuine European arm, a growing EV order book and a founder who owns three-quarters of it. The investment debate is about the durability of content growth and the EV transition, set against a full-but-not-extreme valuation.

The bull case

  • Content-per-vehicle machine — four product lines let it keep raising rupees per bike, out-growing the 2W market.
  • Twin tailwinds — a cyclical India 2W recovery plus structural premiumisation (ABS/CBS, alloy wheels, bigger bikes).
  • EV is additive — most content is powertrain-agnostic; Maxwell + a ₹1,300 Cr/yr EV order book turn EV into new content.
  • Europe adds scale & tech — nine plants, acquisition-led growth (Stöferle), diversification away from India-only risk.
  • Aligned owner, solid returns — ~75% founder holding, ~18% RoCE, modest debt, self-funded growth and M&A.

The bear case

  • Full valuation — ~38× earnings for a mid-teens-margin, steadily-growing supplier leaves little margin for error.
  • Customer concentration — heavy reliance on Bajaj; a stumble there flows straight through.
  • Margin cap from Europe — a growing, lower-margin European arm dilutes the blended group margin.
  • EV execution & deletion — transmission content is at risk; EV electronics is crowded and small today.
  • Cyclicality & aluminium — tied to the 2W cycle and to aluminium pass-through timing; quarters can be lumpy.
The bottom line

Endurance is a structurally advantaged, founder-led auto-component leader geared to the Indian two-wheeler and its premiumisation, with a European casting arm for scale and an early EV-electronics foothold for optionality. The business quality is high and the balance sheet is sound; the questions are about price and transition. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter — India business won / content per vehicle (is it out-growing the market?), the EV order book (is new content outpacing deleted parts?), and the standalone margin (is premiumisation lifting Indian profitability under the European drag?). Get those right and a ~38× multiple compounds; stumble on the 2W cycle or the EV pivot and it has room to de-rate.
