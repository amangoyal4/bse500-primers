Kotak Mahindra Bank, the conservative compounder in transition

A complete walk through India's fourth-largest private bank — the one a founder built from a ₹30-lakh bill-discounting shop into a ~₹4-lakh-crore financial empire. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Kotak Mahindra Bank is the fourth-largest Indian private-sector bank and the anchor of a full-service financial group spanning lending, stockbroking, asset management and life insurance. It was built — uniquely among big Indian banks — by one founder, Uday Kotak, who turned a 1985 finance startup into the first non-bank lender India ever allowed to become a bank, then compounded it into a ~₹4-lakh-crore institution famous for conservative underwriting and a premium valuation. As of March 2026 it serves 5.2 crore customers through roughly 2,400 branches.

Three facts frame everything:

  • High quality on every traditional metric. FY26 delivered a 1.97% standalone return on assets (2.06% consolidated — among the best of any large bank), the sector's highest net interest margin at 4.60%, gross bad loans of just 1.20% (net 0.25%), and a fortress 22.4% capital ratio. Standalone profit was ₹14,008 Cr; consolidated — adding the subsidiaries — ₹19,288 Cr, of which the subsidiaries contributed roughly ₹5,280 Cr.
  • But returns-on-equity are held back by its own caution. Kotak runs a core-equity (CET1) capital ratio of 21.3% — roughly double what it needs — so its return on equity (11.08%) trails ICICI and HDFC even though its return on assets leads. It is a bank that has long chosen safety, optionality and a war-chest over squeezing the last rupee of return out of its balance sheet.
  • The live story is leadership, not the loan book. Founder Uday Kotak stepped down in September 2023; his successor Ashok Vaswani took charge in January 2024 — and in June 2026 announced he will leave by 31 December 2026, citing personal reasons. That makes three CEO transitions in three years, layered on top of a 2024–25 RBI ban that froze digital growth. The bank is healthy; the open question is who steers it next, and how hard the bank now pushes for growth.
In plain terms

Think of Kotak as the careful, cash-rich member of India's private-banking big four. For four decades it earned a premium price tag by lending cautiously, keeping bad loans tiny and never running short of capital — the bank you'd trust to still be standing after a downturn. The flip side of that caution is that it has under-used its balance sheet, so its profit-per-rupee-of-equity lags flashier rivals. With the founder now gone, a regulator's growth-freeze just lifted, and a second CEO already on the way out, the live debate is simple: can a famously conservative bank, sitting on a pile of spare capital and a clutch of valuable subsidiaries, finally convert that caution into faster — but still safe — growth, and who will lead it there?

01

Bank economics 101: how a bank actually earns

No finance degree required — just six terms that explain almost everything.

A bank is, at heart, a spread business. It borrows money cheaply — mostly from depositors — and lends it out at a higher rate, pocketing the difference. From that gross spread it subtracts the cost of running branches and technology, and the cost of loans that go bad; on top of it, it adds fees for services. Six numbers tell you almost the whole story of any bank, and Kotak scores well on most of them.

The only banking terms you need (and Kotak's FY26 number)
TermWhat it means
NIINet Interest Income — interest earned on loans and investments, minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings. This is the core profit engine. Kotak: ₹30,010 Cr in FY26 (+6%).
NIMNet Interest Margin — NII expressed as a percentage of the bank's interest-earning assets; the "spread", standardised. Higher = more profit per rupee lent. Kotak's 4.60% is the highest among large Indian banks.
CASACurrent & Savings Account deposits — the cheapest money a bank can get, because it pays little or no interest on it. A high CASA ratio (Kotak 43.3%) directly underpins that fat margin.
GNPA / NNPAGross / Net Non-Performing Assets — the share of loans that have gone bad (borrowers not paying), before and after the bank sets aside money against them. Lower = better underwriting. Kotak: 1.20% gross, 0.25% net — very clean.
ROA / ROEReturn on Assets / Equity — profit per rupee of total assets, and per rupee of shareholders' money. Kotak: 1.97% ROA standalone, 2.06% consolidated (excellent), but only 11.08% ROE — held down by surplus capital (see §09).
CAR / CET1Capital Adequacy Ratio / Common Equity Tier-1 — how much loss-absorbing capital the bank holds against its risk-weighted loans. The safety cushion. Kotak: 22.4% CAR, 21.3% CET1 — roughly double the regulatory floor.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. So a bank gets richer four ways — lend more (grow advances), fund cheaper (more CASA, lower deposit cost), lose less (low NPAs), and earn fees (cards, broking, mutual-fund distribution, insurance). The art is doing all four without reaching for risky borrowers to juice growth. Kotak's four-decade reputation is built on refusing that temptation: its bad-loan ratio has stayed near 1% through every credit cycle India has thrown at it.

Why "cheap funding" is the whole game

  • CASA is gold. A current account pays 0% and a savings account ~3–3.5%; a fixed deposit pays 6–7%. The more of your funding that sits in CASA, the lower your cost of money — and the wider your margin on the very same loan. A bank with lots of sticky CASA earns a structurally fatter spread than one funded by expensive term deposits.
  • Deposits are sticky but increasingly scarce. Indian household savings are migrating to mutual funds and equities chasing higher returns, so every bank is fighting harder for the same deposits. Winning deposits cheaply is the hardest, most valuable skill in banking.
  • Fee income smooths the ride. Beyond interest, Kotak earns fees on cards, payments, wealth management and — through its subsidiaries — broking, fund management and insurance. This income doesn't consume bank capital and cushions margin swings when the lending cycle turns.
02

One customer, many products: the group funnel

Kotak isn't just a bank — it's a group engineered to keep a customer for life.

What separates Kotak from a plain lender is that it sits at the centre of a full financial-services group. The strategy is simple to state and hard to execute: acquire a customer through a bank account, then cross-sell them everything else they will ever need over a lifetime — a loan, a credit card, a trading account, a mutual fund, a life policy — and capture the profit at every stage inside the same group. Each product deepens the relationship and raises the cost (and pain) of leaving.

Why the funnel is worth more than the sum of its parts

Acquiring a new customer is expensive — advertising, onboarding, KYC. If that customer only ever holds a savings account, the bank may barely recover the cost. But if the same customer also takes a home loan, runs a Kotak Securities trading account, holds a Kotak mutual fund and buys a Kotak Life policy, the bank earns four or five income streams from a single acquisition cost. That lifts the lifetime value of each relationship and the return on every marketing rupee. It is also defensive: a customer with five Kotak products is far less likely to switch banks than one with a lone account. This "capture the whole wallet" logic is why Kotak built — and kept — a federation of subsidiaries rather than just being a bank.

03

The company and its founder

India's most personally-built big bank — and a founder who still owns a quarter of it.

Kotak's history is, unusually, the biography of one man. Uday Kotak started a bill-discounting business in 1985 with seed capital of around ₹30 lakh, backed in part by the Mahindra family (which is why "Mahindra" sits in the name). Over nearly four decades he compounded that into one of India's most valuable banks — and made himself one of the country's richest self-made bankers. The defining feature of the institution is that its risk culture, its capital conservatism and its "never blow up" reputation were set, deliberately and personally, by its founder.

04

The promoter-stake saga: a founder vs the regulator

A years-long tug-of-war over how much of his own bank Uday Kotak was allowed to own.

One of the most distinctive chapters in modern Indian banking is the multi-year standoff between Uday Kotak and the Reserve Bank of India over a single question: how much of the bank a founder is permitted to own. Indian banking rules push promoters to dilute their stakes over time — the philosophy being that no single owner should dominate a deposit-taking institution that holds the public's money. Kotak began as a near-majority owner, and the RBI wanted that brought down sharply.

Why regulators force promoters to dilute

A bank is not a normal company — it runs on other people's money (depositors'). If one person or family controls it outright, the regulator worries about concentration of power, related-party risk, and a founder running the bank for personal rather than depositor interest. So the RBI's licensing conditions require promoters to taper their voting rights and shareholding over a long schedule. The tension with Kotak was that the founder believed his large, aligned ownership was a strength — skin in the game that kept the bank conservative — while the regulator wanted the textbook dilution. The dispute even reached the courts before being settled.

The eventual resolution was a bespoke arrangement: rather than the steep single-digit ownership the rules might literally imply, a compromise capped Kotak's promoter voting rights at a low level (around 15–20%) while allowing the promoter group to retain an economic shareholding in the mid-20s percent. As of FY26 the promoter group holds roughly 25.9%. This matters for an investor in three ways:

  • Deep alignment. A founder with a quarter of the company has enormous skin in the game — his wealth rises and falls with minority shareholders'. That alignment is part of why the market trusts Kotak's risk discipline.
  • A latent overhang. Whenever the promoter sells down further (to meet remaining dilution expectations or for personal reasons), large blocks can hit the market and weigh on the price in the short term. Investors watch promoter stake changes closely.
  • A precedent. The bespoke deal showed the RBI will negotiate individualised arrangements with a systemically important, well-run bank — but also that even a founder of Kotak's stature operates firmly within the regulator's reach. That theme returns, sharply, in the 2024 tech embargo (§06).
The throughline: the promoter-dilution saga and the conservative-bank identity are two sides of the same coin. Uday Kotak's argument was always that a heavily-invested, hands-on founder is precisely what keeps a bank from blowing up — and Kotak's pristine asset quality is his exhibit A. The regulator's job is to make sure no bank depends on one person. The bank's next chapter (§05) tests exactly that: can the conservatism outlive the conservative founder?
05

The post-Uday transition

The single biggest open question hanging over the stock right now.

For 38 years Kotak was Uday Kotak — his caution, his capital discipline, his relationships were the franchise. The defining challenge of this era is whether that founder-built culture survives the handover to professional management. And the handover has been bumpy: in three years the bank has had a founder-CEO, a bridge interim CEO, an external CEO — and, as of June 2026, is hunting for the next one.

Why a CEO change matters more here than at most banks

At a typical large bank, leadership is institutionalised and a CEO change is a non-event. Kotak is different because so much of its identity — the risk culture, the capital conservatism, the "never blow up" reputation that earns its premium multiple — was set by the founder personally. Each transition therefore reopens three questions at once. (1) Continuity of risk culture: does the next leader keep underwriting as tightly? (2) Growth ambition: will new management finally deploy the surplus capital and push harder, or stay cautious? (3) Stability: a second CEO leaving inside three years invites questions about the board, the bench and the succession process itself. None of these is a balance-sheet problem; all of them feed directly into the valuation the market is willing to pay.

The reassurance, in fairness: the founder still owns ~26% and remains a powerful presence in spirit; the bank just posted clean, growing FY26 numbers; and the candidates being discussed are seasoned internal leaders. A smooth, RBI-approved internal appointment that signals "more growth, same risk discipline" could turn the overhang into a positive catalyst. The transition is a question mark, not a crack.
06

The 2024 RBI tech embargo & the digital catch-up

A regulator-imposed growth freeze — and the FY26 bounce-back that followed.

In April 2024 the Reserve Bank took the rare and pointed step of barring Kotak from onboarding new customers through its online and mobile channels and from issuing fresh credit cards. The cited reason was serious: repeated deficiencies in the bank's IT systems and information-security governance — frequent outages in the core banking system and digital channels that the RBI judged Kotak had failed to fix over two years of supervisory engagement. For a bank whose growth engine was digital acquisition (811) and a fast-growing card book, the order hit the highest-margin, fastest-growing parts of the business.

During the ban, fresh card issuance and digital acquisition stopped, the card base shrank, and the unsecured-retail share of the book contracted. Once the RBI lifted the curbs (12 Feb 2025), customer acquisition and the loan book re-accelerated through FY26.

How Kotak got the ban lifted

Lifting an RBI business restriction is not automatic. Kotak undertook a programme of IT remediation — strengthening its core banking resilience, data-centre redundancy, and information-security governance — and, crucially, commissioned an external audit to demonstrate to the regulator that the deficiencies had been fixed. Only after the RBI was satisfied with that independent verification did it lift the embargo in February 2025, about ten months after imposing it. The episode put technology investment squarely at the top of the new CEO's agenda and remains a standing item the regulator will monitor.

Why this episode still matters after it's "over"

The curbs were lifted in February 2025 and FY26 shows the franchise back to mid-teens growth — so the operational damage is largely behind it. But the embargo leaves two lasting marks. First, a credibility cost: a top-tier bank publicly sanctioned for tech and risk-control failures dents the very "best-run bank" reputation that justifies its premium multiple. Second, a lost-ground cost: roughly a year of frozen digital acquisition and a shrinking card base handed share to rivals in exactly the high-margin unsecured segments Kotak most wanted to grow. The recovery is real, but the bank is partly playing catch-up — and it must now keep investing heavily in technology, both to compete and to stay on the right side of a regulator that has shown it will not hesitate to act.

07

Deposits and the high-NIM engine

Why Kotak earns the sector's fattest margin — and what could erode it.

Kotak's 4.60% net interest margin is the highest of any large Indian bank, comfortably ahead of HDFC's ~3.4% and the ~3.5% of most peers. That gap is not luck; it is the result of two deliberate choices on each side of the balance sheet. Understanding why Kotak runs a high NIM — and why it's under pressure — is the heart of understanding the bank's earnings.

The two levers behind a high margin

  • The funding side — historically cheap deposits. Kotak built its brand on gathering low-cost CASA. When it launched the high-interest 811 digital savings account in 2017, the goal was to acquire customers and cheap savings money at scale. A high CASA mix (43.3% in FY26) means a lower average cost of funds, which directly widens the spread.
  • The lending side — a higher-yielding asset mix. Kotak deliberately tilts toward higher-yielding loans — unsecured retail, consumer finance, business banking and, increasingly, microfinance — rather than only low-yield mortgages and large-corporate loans. Higher loan yields on one side and low funding cost on the other produce a structurally fat margin.

Why the margin is sliding — and why it matters

FY26 NIM fell from FY25's ~4.96% to 4.60%, and that drift tells the central tension of the moment. Two forces are squeezing it. First, CASA has drifted down from the mid-50s a few years ago to ~43% as savers chase higher fixed-deposit and mutual-fund returns — so more of Kotak's funding now comes from pricier term deposits, raising the cost side. Second, the whole sector is in a deposit war: credit demand is outpacing deposit growth across India, so every bank is straining to fund loans, bidding up deposit rates. Because Kotak's premium valuation rests heavily on its margin leadership, even a modest, steady erosion in NIM is something the market watches with unusual intensity. Defending CASA — and the brand that gathers it cheaply — is the single most important thing Kotak must do to protect its earnings identity.

Asset quality and the conservative DNA

The thing Kotak has never gotten wrong — and the real reason for the premium.

If there is one number that defines Kotak, it is the quality of its loan book. Through every Indian credit cycle, Kotak has kept bad loans among the lowest in the system — a reflection of deliberately cautious underwriting, a willingness to walk away from risky growth, and a balance sheet always carrying more capital than required. Asset quality is the foundation on which the premium valuation rests, because it tells investors the reported profits are real — not borrowed from a future of write-offs.

What "net NPA 0.25%" really tells you

Gross NPA (1.20%) is the share of loans that have soured; net NPA (0.25%) is what's left after the bank has set aside money (provisions) against those bad loans. The huge gap between the two means Kotak has provisioned heavily — it has already absorbed most of the expected loss into past profits. A net NPA of a quarter of one percent is about as clean as a large bank's book gets in India. It means a downturn would have to be severe before it dented capital, and it's a big part of why depositors and rating agencies treat Kotak as a safe haven.

The one watch-item: to lift its margin, Kotak has been pushing (cautiously) into higher-yielding unsecured retail and microfinance lending. India saw sector-wide stress in microfinance and unsecured retail through 2024–25, and credit costs ticked up across the industry. Asset quality at Kotak remains excellent overall, but this is precisely the segment where a conservative bank's discipline gets tested — the thing to watch is slippages and credit costs in the unsecured book, not the reassuring headline GNPA.
09

The over-capitalisation paradox

Why the safest big bank also has the most frustrating returns — and the biggest hidden lever.

Here is the puzzle at the centre of every Kotak debate. It earns the best return on assets of any large Indian bank (1.97% standalone, 2.06% consolidated) — yet its return on equity, at 11.08%, trails ICICI and HDFC, which sit in the mid-to-high teens. How can a bank be more profitable per rupee of assets but less profitable per rupee of shareholder capital? The answer is a single, deliberate choice: Kotak holds far more capital than it needs.

The mechanism, in one idea

Return on equity = return on assets × leverage (how many rupees of assets you support per rupee of equity). Most banks run "tighter", supporting more assets on each rupee of capital, which multiplies a good ROA into a high ROE. Kotak runs a CET1 ratio of 21.3% — roughly double the regulatory minimum — so it supports fewer assets per rupee of equity. That surplus capital sits relatively idle, dragging ROE down even while every loan it does make is highly profitable. In plain terms: Kotak has a powerful engine but deliberately drives in a low gear. The capital isn't lost — it's dry powder.

This is simultaneously the bear's frustration and the bull's opportunity. Bears see a bank that under-earns its potential and has been slow to put capital to work — value trapped on the balance sheet, justifying a lower multiple. Bulls see the inverse: a bank that can grow its loan book at a fast clip for years without raising a single rupee, or fund a transformational acquisition outright, all while remaining the safest lender in the system. Which view wins depends almost entirely on what the next CEO chooses to do with that capital — which loops straight back to the leadership question in §05.

The surplus capital is the whole investment case in miniature: it's either lazy money holding returns down, or a coiled spring for safe, self-funded growth — and the CEO decides which.

The subsidiaries, one by one

More than a quarter of group profit comes from things that aren't the bank — and most of them are gems.

Valuing Kotak purely as a bank understates it. It is a diversified financial-services group, and its non-bank subsidiaries contributed roughly ₹5,280 Cr — about 27% of FY26 consolidated profit of ₹19,288 Cr. Several are fee-and-float businesses that earn money without taking balance-sheet credit risk, which is why analysts value Kotak on a sum-of-the-parts basis rather than as a single bank. Kotak Securities — the broking franchise (FY26 PAT ₹1,642 Cr)

Kotak Securities is one of India's largest full-service stockbrokers, serving both retail investors and institutions. It earns brokerage and fees on the trades and the assets it executes and custodies — income that consumes almost no capital and carries no credit risk. Its profit rides the Indian retail-investing boom: as tens of millions of new demat accounts have opened over the past decade, trading volumes (and broking revenue) have surged. The risk is the flip side of that same coin — broking is cyclical and price-competitive, squeezed by discount brokers and falling when markets turn quiet. Still, at ₹1,642 Cr of profit, it is the single largest subsidiary contributor, and a powerful cross-sell channel: a Kotak Securities client is a natural buyer of Kotak mutual funds and a Kotak bank account.

Kotak Mahindra AMC — the mutual-fund machine (FY26 PAT ₹1,082 Cr)

This is, for many analysts, the crown jewel. Kotak's asset-management arm runs one of India's larger mutual-fund houses, with assets under management of roughly ₹5.99 lakh crore across its funds. An AMC is the textbook capital-light fee annuity: it earns a small percentage (the expense ratio) every year on the money it manages, regardless of whether it lends or takes risk on its own balance sheet. As AUM compounds — driven by India's structural shift of household savings into mutual funds via SIPs (systematic investment plans) — that fee base grows almost automatically, and because the cost of managing ₹6 lakh crore is not much higher than managing ₹4 lakh crore, profits grow faster than revenue. At ₹1,082 Cr and rising, it's a high-return, scalable business that a standalone listing would likely value richly.

Kotak Mahindra Prime — the auto-finance NBFC (FY26 PAT ₹1,008 Cr)

Kotak Mahindra Prime is a non-bank lender focused mainly on car finance (and some other secured lending). Unlike the AMC and broking arms, Prime does use its own balance sheet and take credit risk — it borrows and lends, much like the bank, but specialised. It is a long-established, steadily profitable book that lets the group capture auto-loan customers (and cross-sell them) outside the bank's own balance sheet. At ₹1,008 Cr of profit it's a meaningful, dependable earner — less glamorous than the AMC but a solid, well-seasoned lending franchise.

Kotak Mahindra Life Insurance — the protection & savings arm (FY26 PAT ₹628 Cr)

Kotak Life is a mid-sized, profitable life insurer offering protection (term cover) and long-duration savings products, with AUM of roughly ₹92,000 crore. Life insurance is a float-and-fee business: premiums come in years before claims go out, so the insurer manages a large pool of long-term money and earns on both the protection margin and the investment spread. It is valued separately from the bank (on an embedded-value basis) and is a natural cross-sell to bank customers — the "protect" stage of the group funnel. FY26 PAT was ₹628 Cr (down from ₹769 Cr in FY25, reflecting product-mix and accounting swings common in life insurance).

Kotak Mahindra Capital — the investment bank (FY26 PAT ₹350 Cr)

Kotak Mahindra Capital is the group's investment bank — advising on mergers, managing IPOs and raising capital for corporates. It is a high-prestige, relationship-driven, fee business that has long been a top-ranked arranger of Indian equity issues. Its profits are lumpy (they depend on deal flow and market conditions) but high-margin, and it cements Kotak's relationships at the top of corporate India. In FY26 it also recorded a one-off gain on divesting a stake in associate Infina Finance.

Why the conglomerate structure is a real edge

Three reasons this matters. First, diversification of profit: when lending margins are squeezed, the AMC and broking arms (which earn fees on assets and trades) can be booming — they smooth the cycle. Second, capital-light growth: a mutual-fund or broking business scales profit without consuming scarce bank capital, lifting overall group returns. Third, cross-sell economics: acquiring a customer once and selling them four or five products spreads the acquisition cost and raises lifetime value (the funnel in §02). The catch is that a financial conglomerate can trade at a holding-company discount and is harder to value than a clean, single-product bank — but for Kotak, the profitable, fast-growing subsidiaries are a genuine and often under-appreciated chunk of the worth, and a potential source of value if any were ever listed or partly sold (as Kotak General Insurance was, to Zurich, in FY25).

11

The industry and the rivals

The smallest — but historically highest-quality — of the private big four.

India's private-banking premier league is a four-horse race. Kotak is the smallest of the four by assets, but has long carried the highest-quality reputation and a rich valuation. The structural backdrop is favourable: private banks keep taking deposit and loan share from public-sector banks, and India's credit-to-GDP ratio is still low by global standards, leaving a long runway for growth. The pressure point is competition — for deposits above all, but also for the best borrowers and the best digital customers.

12

Financials & valuation

Steady, high-quality compounding — at a price that demands it.

Kotak's financials read like a high-quality bank that grew through a disrupted couple of years: profit growth was held back by the RBI ban and a tough base, then the balance sheet re-accelerated in FY26. There is an important accounting nuance to understand before reading the headline growth.

The "+2% profit growth" is misleading — here's why

Standalone PAT rose just 2% in FY26, to ₹14,008 Cr from ₹13,720 Cr. But the FY25 base was inflated by a one-off gain of ~₹3,013 Cr from selling 70% of Kotak General Insurance to Zurich. Strip that one-time gain out, and the FY25 core profit was materially lower — which means Kotak's underlying, recurring profit grew considerably faster than 2%. The 2% headline compares this year's clean profit against last year's profit puffed up by an asset sale. This is a classic case where reading the headline growth number alone gives exactly the wrong impression of the business's momentum.

How to think about the valuation

Banks are valued mainly on price-to-book against return-on-equity: a bank earning a higher ROE deserves a higher multiple of its net worth. Kotak's puzzle is that it has historically commanded a premium price-to-book (well above 3× in its heyday, ~2.6× now) despite a lower ROE than ICICI or HDFC — the market paid up for its safety, its franchise, the subsidiary value, and the optionality of all that surplus capital. The bull case is that this premium is deserved and that deploying the dry powder will lift ROE to justify the multiple. The bear case is that the premium is a relic — that a slower-growing, lower-ROE bank in CEO transition should trade closer to peers, leaving the stock exposed to a de-rating. The valuation, in short, is a referendum on whether Kotak's conservatism is a feature or a drag.

13

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A fortress bank whose next chapter depends on leadership and ambition.

Kotak is one of the safest, best-capitalised banks in India, anchored by a clean book, the sector's best margin, and a diversified group of profitable subsidiaries. The investment debate isn't really about quality — it's about ambition, returns and leadership: will a famously cautious bank, mid-transition, finally convert its strengths into faster, higher-return growth?

The bull case

  • Fortress quality — top-tier ROA, sector-best NIM (4.60%), GNPA 1.20%, and 21.3% CET1: a bank built to never blow up.
  • Dry powder — surplus capital can fund years of self-financed growth or a transformational acquisition, lifting ROE.
  • Conglomerate kicker — fast-growing AMC, broking, life and auto-finance arms add ~27% of profit, capital-light and cycle-smoothing.
  • Post-ban re-acceleration — advances +16%, deposits +15% in FY26 as digital onboarding resumed; underlying profit growing faster than the headline +2%.
  • Aligned founder — Uday Kotak's ~26% stake keeps interests squarely with minority holders.

The bear case

  • Leadership churn — three CEO transitions in three years; the second non-founder CEO already departing, succession unresolved.
  • Lower ROE — under-deployed capital means returns (11.08%) lag peers; the premium multiple may de-rate if growth disappoints.
  • Margin pressure — NIM sliding (4.96% → 4.60%) and CASA drifting down amid a sector-wide deposit war.
  • Reputation dent — the RBI tech/risk-control sanction bruised the "best-run bank" halo and raised the IT-spend bar permanently.
  • Unsecured stress — pushing into microfinance/unsecured for yield, just as that segment showed system-wide strain in 2024–25.
The bottom line

Kotak Mahindra Bank is a genuinely high-quality, fortress-capitalised franchise — arguably the safest seat in Indian private banking — trading at a premium the market has long granted to its caution, its subsidiary value and its optionality. The business risk is low; the strategic risk is real. The next few years turn on three linked questions: who leads it (does the succession land cleanly and signal continuity?), what they do with the surplus capital (deploy it for growth, or keep sitting on it?), and whether the margin and digital momentum hold against fierce deposit competition. Resolve those well and a cautious bank becomes a safe compounder that finally earns its multiple; resolve them poorly and the premium has room to fade.
