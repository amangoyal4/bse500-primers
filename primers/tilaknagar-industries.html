Tilaknagar Industries, India's brandy king

A complete walk through the company behind Mansion House — one of the world's biggest-selling brandy brands — and the ₹3,400-crore bet that just turned a Southern brandy specialist into a national spirits house: how India's state-controlled liquor business actually works, why brandy is a category almost no one else fought for, how the company clawed its way back from near-bankruptcy to net-debt-free, and why it then deliberately re-levered to buy a whisky brand bigger than itself.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Tilaknagar Industries Limited (TI) makes the spirits people actually drink in South India — above all brandy, where its flagship Mansion House is the country's largest prestige-and-above brand and one of the biggest-selling brandy brands on Earth by volume. For most of its life it was a focused, regional distiller: strong in the five southern states, built around a category that India's big whisky houses never bothered to fight for. In FY26 it stopped being only that. It bought the Imperial Blue whisky brand from Pernod Ricard for ~₹3,442 Cr, and in one move became a genuinely national IMFL company with a whisky franchise larger, by volume, than its own historic base.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It owns a category, not just a brand. Brandy is a heavily Southern taste — Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Puducherry drink the overwhelming majority of India's brandy. The national giants chase whisky; Tilaknagar built the leading brandy franchise in a market they largely ignored. Mansion House brandy crossed 10 million cases in FY26 (from 8.7 million), which is what category leadership in a big, overlooked niche looks like.
  • It sells into ~30 separate regulated markets, not one. Alcohol in India is a State subject: every state sets its own excise duty, its own retail price, its own licensing and often its own distribution monopoly. There is no single national liquor market — there are dozens, each with its own rules. That is why the meaningful revenue line is net revenue (after the enormous state excise a distiller collects and passes on), and why the whole business is as much about navigating governments as about making spirit.
  • FY26 was a hinge year — bigger, more national, and re-levered. Net revenue jumped ~70% to ~₹2,346 Cr and EBITDA rose ~56% to ~₹352 Cr, flattered in the last quarter by the newly-added Imperial Blue. But to buy that brand, a company that had fought its way to net-debt-free in September 2024 put ~₹1,911 Cr of net debt back on the balance sheet. The investment case is now a race: can premiumisation and integration lift margins fast enough to pay that debt down before the cycle turns?
In plain terms

Here is Tilaknagar in one breath. A distiller that dominates brandy — a category most Indian drinks companies treat as an afterthought — used the steady cash from that Southern stronghold to make the boldest acquisition of its life: an established national whisky brand from a global major. It had spent nearly a decade climbing out of a debt hole that once threatened its survival, reached zero net debt, and then chose to borrow again — heavily — to double its scale and go national. So the reader is looking at two companies stitched together: a mature, cash-generative brandy leader, and a newly-bought whisky business three-quarters bigger by volume that must now be absorbed, re-margined and paid for. The reward is a national spirits house; the risk is doing all of that with borrowed money while every state government still sets your prices.

01

How the Indian spirits business actually works

No jargon needed — just excise, cases, and why "net revenue" is the number that matters.

Before Tilaknagar's story makes sense, you need to understand the strangest thing about selling liquor in India: it is one of the most heavily taxed, most tightly controlled, and most fragmented consumer businesses in the country. Unlike toothpaste or biscuits, a bottle of spirits is not sold into a single national market at a price the maker sets. It is sold into roughly thirty separate markets, each governed by a state that treats liquor as a prized source of revenue and a matter of social policy at the same time.

The only spirits terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
IMFLIndian-Made Foreign Liquor — the local term for factory-made spirits in the "Western" styles: whisky, brandy, rum, gin, vodka. Tilaknagar's entire business sits here. (Country liquor and beer are separate categories.)
State exciseThe duty each state levies on every bottle. It is often the single largest component of the shelf price — bigger than the cost of the liquid itself. Because rates differ wildly by state, the same bottle can cost very different amounts across a border.
Net revenueSales after stripping out that excise (and, in some states, the value the government's own corporation captures). This is the money the company actually keeps to run on — which is why analysts track net revenue, not the much larger gross figure.
CaseThe industry's unit of volume — a standard 9-litre case (e.g. twelve 750ml bottles). Companies report sales in "million cases," and Tilaknagar sold ~20 million in FY26.
Prestige-and-aboveThe better-priced, better-margin tiers of IMFL (prestige, premium, luxury) as opposed to the cheap "popular" segment. Moving the mix up this ladder — premiumisation — is the industry's main profit lever.
The one structural fact that shapes the whole sector: the state owns the rules

Alcohol is on the State List of the Indian constitution, so Delhi barely touches it — each state runs its own regime. A state decides the excise duty, approves (or freezes) the retail price, hands out the manufacturing and retail licences, and in several states is the distributor: Tamil Nadu sells almost all its liquor through the government-owned TASMAC, Kerala through Bevco, and a handful of states (Gujarat, Bihar, Nagaland, parts of Manipur) ban it outright. For a company like Tilaknagar this has three consequences. First, it must win business state by state, dealing with dozens of separate excise departments and price-approval cycles. Second, it cannot freely raise prices to recover cost inflation — a price hike needs the state's blessing, which can lag for years. Third, a single state's policy change — a duty hike, a new tender, a distribution overhaul — can swing a whole region's profitability overnight. The liquor is the easy part; the regulation is the business.

Where the money in a bottle goes

  • The state — takes the largest slice through excise duty and, where it runs the shops, a retail margin too. On many bottles the government's cut exceeds the manufacturer's entire realisation.
  • The distiller — Tilaknagar keeps only the net revenue after excise, out of which it pays for raw spirit, glass, packaging, marketing and overheads before any profit. The margins in this section — the mid-teens EBITDA the company earns — are calculated on that net figure, not the gross shelf price.
  • Raw materials — chiefly Extra Neutral Alcohol (ENA), the base spirit distilled from grain or molasses, plus glass bottles, and for the growing premium range, imported Scotch and malt. ENA and glass prices are the input costs that squeeze margins when a company can't raise its state-approved price to match.
02

The brandy nobody else wanted — and why that is a moat

The defining fact about Tilaknagar: it leads a big category the giants left alone.

If you asked a casual observer to name India's drink, they would say whisky — and they would be right about the national picture, where whisky dwarfs everything. That is exactly why Tilaknagar's position is so interesting. It built its franchise not in whisky but in brandy, a category that is enormous in the South and modest almost everywhere else, and which the big whisky-obsessed houses historically underinvested in. Owning the leadership of an overlooked-but-large category is a quieter, more durable advantage than fighting for share in the crowded one.

What brandy is, and why the South drinks it

Brandy is a spirit historically distilled from grapes or fruit; in the Indian mass market it is typically an ENA base flavoured and coloured to a brandy profile. Its consumption is strikingly regional. The five southern states and Puducherry account for the overwhelming majority of India's brandy volumes — it is woven into local drinking culture in a way it simply isn't in the North or West, where whisky and (increasingly) beer dominate. That regional concentration is a double-edged thing, and both edges matter for Tilaknagar: it is the source of the company's leadership and cash, and it is also the geographic concentration risk we return to in section 08.

The genuine surprise: a "small-cap" makes a global top-selling brand

Here is something that catches most people off guard. A mid-sized Indian company that a few years ago was fighting for survival owns, in Mansion House, a brandy that ranks among the largest-selling brandy brands anywhere in the world by volume — bigger, in cases, than many globally famous French names most drinkers would recognise. That sounds implausible until you remember two things: brandy volumes are heavily concentrated in a handful of huge-population countries (India prominent among them), and Mansion House owns the leading position in India's biggest brandy region. Category leadership in a large, structurally overlooked segment can quietly build a brand of world scale without ever making it a household name outside its home turf.

The brand stable

  • Mansion House — the flagship: India's largest prestige-and-above brandy and the volume-and-cash engine of the historic business. Also extended into whisky and other formats.
  • Courrier Napoleon — a premium brandy, positioned above the mass tier; part of the deliberate move up the price ladder.
  • Madira, Blue Lagoon and others — rum, gin and additional IMFL lines that round out the portfolio across categories and price points.
  • Seven Islands — a pure-malt whisky push at the premium/luxury end, one of the vehicles for taking the company upmarket using the distribution muscle it now has.
  • Imperial Blue — the newly-acquired national whisky brand (section 05): a different category, a different geography, and by volume the largest single thing the company now sells.
Why category leadership is the real moat: in a business where the state fixes prices and everyone buys similar ENA and glass, you cannot easily out-price rivals. What you can own is a brand that a region asks for by name and a distribution network that gets it onto every shelf and into every state corporation's tender. Mansion House's decades-long grip on Southern brandy is exactly that kind of asset — hard to dislodge, expensive to replicate, and the reason Tilaknagar had the cash flow and credibility to attempt a national acquisition at all.
03

From near-death to debt-free: the turnaround that made the bet possible

You cannot understand the Imperial Blue gamble without the decade of deleveraging behind it.

The boldest thing about Tilaknagar's 2025 acquisition is not its size — it is that the same company nearly went under a decade earlier under a pile of debt. The story of the past ten years is a hard, unglamorous deleveraging: restructure the borrowings, sweat the brandy franchise for cash, raise a little equity, sign a helpful manufacturing deal, and grind the balance sheet back to zero. Only a company that had proved it could carry and then extinguish debt could credibly borrow again on this scale.

The arc, in sequence

  • 1933
    Deep roots — the group traces to The Maharashtra Sugar Mills, later building out distilling and IMFL under the Tilaknagar name. Decades of manufacturing heritage in Maharashtra.
  • 2000s
    Mansion House is scaled into India's leading prestige brandy, entrenching the company's grip on the Southern market and making brandy its identity.
  • ~2013–2016
    An aggressive, debt-funded expansion phase turns sour; borrowings become unsustainable, and by 2016 lenders are weighing a strategic debt restructuring. The company is, in effect, fighting for survival.
  • 2020
    Debt restructuring with Edelweiss ARC — part of the debt is converted to equity; the balance sheet begins to be repaired rather than merely rolled over.
  • 2021 onward
    Multiple capital raises (over ₹310 Cr cumulatively) and a 10-year manufacturing agreement with Pernod Ricard in Maharashtra add cash and utilise capacity — a quiet but important cash-flow prop.
  • Sep 2024
    Net-debt-free. After nearly a decade of grind, the company clears its net borrowings — a genuine milestone and the platform for what comes next.
  • Jul–Dec 2025
    Agrees, then completes, the ₹3,442 Cr Imperial Blue acquisition from Pernod Ricard — and deliberately takes debt back on to fund it.
  • FY26
    Record year: net revenue ~₹2,346 Cr (+70%), Mansion House past 10 million cases; net debt back to ~₹1,911 Cr as the acquisition lands.
Why the deleveraging history is the key to reading today's balance sheet

It would be easy to look at Tilaknagar's FY26 balance sheet — net debt leaping from essentially nil to ~₹1,911 Cr — and see a company back in trouble. That reading misses the point. This is not debt that crept up because the business was failing; it is debt taken on from a standing start of zero, by choice, to buy a large, cash-generative brand. The distinction matters enormously. A decade ago the leverage was the symptom of an over-reach that nearly killed the company. This time the same management has demonstrated it can service and then eliminate borrowings, and has re-borrowed against a specific, revenue-producing asset with a stated repayment plan. The risk is real — leverage is always risk — but it is a very different kind of risk from 2016, and the market is pricing the turnaround pedigree, not just the debt figure.

04

The company, the family, and who runs it

A promoter-led distiller with a Maharashtra manufacturing base and a Southern market.

Tilaknagar Industries is a promoter-family-controlled company, led by Amit Dahanukar (Chairman & Managing Director, reappointed in FY26) alongside the founding family's long involvement in the business. Unlike a fertiliser or bank primer, there is no exotic ownership structure to untangle here — the story is about a single controlling family that took the company into a debt crisis, steered it out, and has now bet its recovered credibility on going national. Understanding the promoter's role matters because a levered acquisition of this size is, ultimately, a management judgement call the family has staked its reputation on.

The shape of the company

  • Control: the Dahanukar promoter group retains the controlling stake; the balance is public float. The promoter participated directly in the FY26 fundraising for the acquisition (see section 06), signalling alignment — but investors should confirm the exact post-issue promoter percentage in the latest shareholding filing, since the equity-and-warrant raise changed the cap table.
  • Manufacturing base: rooted in Maharashtra, with owned and tie-up capacity across states (IMFL is often made close to the market to manage inter-state excise). The 10-year contract to manufacture Pernod Ricard's brands in Maharashtra both utilises capacity and, notably, gave Tilaknagar a working relationship with the very company it later bought Imperial Blue from.
  • Market base: historically South-India-weighted through brandy, now materially broadened by Imperial Blue's national whisky footprint — the single biggest change to the company's geographic profile in its history.
The governance lens to hold: a founder-led company executing a transformational, debt-funded acquisition concentrates a great deal in the quality of one management team's judgement and capital allocation. The bull reading is that this is the same team that delivered the deleveraging, now backing itself; the bear reading is that a promoter who once over-extended the balance sheet is doing something structurally similar again, only bigger. Both readings watch the same thing from here: whether the debt comes down on schedule and the acquired margins actually materialise.
05

The Imperial Blue bet: buying a whisky brand bigger than yourself

The single most important event in the company's modern history — and the crux of the case.

In July 2025 Tilaknagar agreed to buy the Imperial Blue whisky business from Pernod Ricard India. It is the move that redefines the company, and the reason its story is no longer just about brandy. The logic is a specific, non-obvious one: use the cash and credibility of a Southern brandy stronghold to acquire an established national whisky brand — diversifying out of a regional category into India's largest spirits category in a single, decisive stroke rather than trying to build a whisky franchise organically over decades.

The deal, in numbers

  • What was bought: the Imperial Blue business division — India's third-largest whisky brand by volume, selling roughly 22.4 million 9-litre cases in the year to March 2025, with brand revenue of about ₹3,067 Cr on a trailing basis. Crucially, that is more volume than Tilaknagar's entire historic business — this is a reverse-scale acquisition, the minnow swallowing something bigger than itself.
  • Price: a lump-sum consideration of ~₹3,442 Cr (about €412.6m / US$485m), plus a deferred €28m payable four years after closing.
  • Timeline: announced late July 2025; cleared by the Competition Commission of India in October 2025; completed in December 2025. So only a partial year of Imperial Blue sits in FY26's reported numbers — which is exactly why the fourth quarter's revenue leapt ~148%.
  • Seller's motive: Pernod Ricard, a global major, chose to shed a large but lower-margin mass-market whisky to focus on its premium portfolio — the kind of asset a hungry domestic player can run harder than a multinational optimising for global margins.

The device that fits only Tilaknagar: a brandy king buying a bigger whisky crown

Strip the deal to its essence and it is a construction you would not find in any other Indian drinks company. A regional brandy leader — cash-rich from a category the giants ignore, but boxed into the South — spends its hard-won balance-sheet freedom to buy a national whisky brand that outsells its own entire portfolio, from a global major that no longer wanted it. In one transaction Tilaknagar addresses its two biggest structural limits at once: it diversifies out of a single regional category, and it acquires a genuinely pan-India distribution reach it could never have built organically. The bet is that a domestic owner, unburdened by a multinational's margin hurdles and armed with its own low-cost operating culture, can wring more profit out of Imperial Blue than Pernod Ricard chose to. If that is right, Tilaknagar bought a national franchise at a mass-market price. If the integration falters, it has bolted a large, unfamiliar, lower-margin whisky operation onto a company that just cleared its debts.

06

Re-leveraging: the debt that came back on to fund the deal

How a ₹3,442 Cr cheque was written — and the balance-sheet reality it created.

A company that had just reached net-debt-free could not simply reach into its pocket for ₹3,442 Cr. Funding an acquisition of this size meant a deliberate return to leverage, financed through a carefully balanced mix of fresh equity, borrowed money and internal cash. The result is a balance sheet that looks, at a glance, alarming — net debt from essentially nil to ~₹1,911 Cr in a single year — but is better understood as a planned, asset-backed re-leveraging with a clear paydown target.

The equity half is important. Rather than loading the entire price onto loans, Tilaknagar raised about ₹2,093 Cr through a preferential issue of shares and warrants to marquee investors and its own promoter group — a vote of confidence from insiders and institutions that also limited how much debt the balance sheet had to absorb. The remaining roughly ₹2,100 Cr came as term loans, with internal accruals covering the rest. That is why net debt landed near ₹1,911 Cr rather than the full ticket size.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with

Tilaknagar posted a record year and re-loaded its balance sheet with debt at the same time — and both are deliberate, not contradictory. The record revenue reflects the brandy franchise firing plus a slice of the new whisky brand; the debt reflects a considered decision to buy scale with borrowed money rather than wait a decade to build it. So the health of this company from here is not captured by the profit number alone. You have to ask a second question every quarter: is the net debt actually coming down, and is the acquired margin actually rising? Management has put concrete numbers on both — net debt toward ~₹1,700 Cr by March 2027, net-debt/EBITDA below 1x, and consolidated margins climbing into the high-teens. The primer's whole thesis, section 10 will argue, is simply whether those two lines cross on time.

07

Premiumisation: the lever that pays for the debt

Why mix, not volume, is where the margin expansion has to come from.

If the debt is to come down, EBITDA has to go up — and in a business where the state fixes your prices, you can't simply raise them. The lever that is available is mix: selling proportionally more of the higher-priced, higher-margin tiers, and squeezing more profit out of the newly-acquired brand. This is premiumisation, and it is the single most important operational story of the next three years.

The two engines of margin

  • Mix up the price ladder. Post-acquisition, Tilaknagar reports that over 90% of its volumes now come from prestige-and-above categories — a portfolio deliberately tilted away from cheap "popular" spirits toward tiers that carry better margins and more pricing resilience. Premium launches like Seven Islands pure-malt whisky, and the extension of Mansion House and Courrier Napoleon upmarket, ride the very distribution networks the company already owns.
  • Re-margin Imperial Blue. Management expects 250–400 basis points of margin expansion from integrating the acquired brand — running it on Tilaknagar's leaner cost base, taking direct operational control (already the majority of Imperial Blue's volumes), optimising packaging and supply chain, and capturing India-UK trade-deal benefits on Scotch inputs used in the blend.

Why premiumisation is the only margin lever that really works here

In a state-controlled market, the usual profit levers are blocked. You cannot freely raise shelf prices — a state has to approve them, often years late. You cannot easily cut input costs — ENA and glass are market-priced, and everyone pays similar rates. What you can do is change what sits in the bottle and on the shelf: sell more prestige-and-above, extend trusted brands into premium tiers, and run an acquired mass brand more efficiently than its multinational parent did. That is why "over 90% prestige-and-above" and "250–400 bps of integration upside" are not marketing lines — they are, quite literally, the mechanism by which the company intends to lift EBITDA enough to pay down the acquisition debt. If premiumisation delivers, the debt melts; if it stalls, the debt just sits there.

08

The risk stack: six things that can go wrong

A regional turnaround executing a leveraged national acquisition carries a specific set of risks.

Tilaknagar's transformation is exciting precisely because it is not safe. Strip the story down and there are six distinct risks stacked on top of one another — some shared by every spirits company, some peculiar to this one at this moment. A clear-eyed reader holds all six at once.

How the risks interact — the summary a first-timer should internalise

These six are not independent — they compound. The acquisition (risk 4) was funded with leverage (risk 5), and both are being repaid out of a margin-expansion plan that a state-price-controlled, input-cost-exposed business (risks 1 and 2) must deliver against larger rivals (risk 6). The one genuinely reducing risk is regional concentration: Imperial Blue makes the company less South-and-brandy-dependent. But it does so by adding integration and funding risk in exchange. That trade — swapping concentration risk for execution-and-leverage risk — is the honest heart of the investment decision. An investor who is comfortable that this management can integrate and deleverage on schedule is buying a national spirits house at an early stage; one who isn't is watching a fresh-from-recovery company take a very large swing.

09

The industry and the rivals

A consolidating, premiumising IMFL market with three larger players Tilaknagar now competes with head-on.

By buying a national whisky brand, Tilaknagar has stepped out of its comfortable brandy niche and into direct competition with India's IMFL heavyweights. It remains the smaller company by revenue, but the acquisition changes the conversation: it is now a national spirits player judged against the sector's leaders, all of whom are riding the same premiumisation wave.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, this is a premiumisation race, not a price war — with states fixing shelf prices, the winners are those who shift mix upmarket fastest and market premium brands hardest. Radico's re-rating shows what the market pays for that; Tilaknagar is explicitly trying to walk the same road with Imperial Blue and its own premium launches. Second, scale matters more now than it did when Tilaknagar was a brandy specialist: going national means competing for the same premium consumer and the same state tenders as far larger, cash-rich rivals. Its edge is a genuinely dominant position in one big category (Southern brandy) and a lean, hungry cost culture; its disadvantage is that it is doing this smaller, and with more debt, than the incumbents it now sits beside.

10

Financials & valuation

A transformational year on a rich multiple — the price already assumes the plan works.

FY26 was a record and a transformation at once: net revenue up ~70%, EBITDA up ~56%, on a doubling of volume that mixes a strong organic brandy year with a partial-year of Imperial Blue. But unlike a cheap cyclical, Tilaknagar does not trade as a bargain — the market has already awarded it a growth multiple that bakes in successful integration and margin expansion. The question here is therefore not whether the stock is cheap; it is whether the ambitious plan the price assumes actually gets delivered.

On valuation, the numbers demand candour about what is being paid. At a market cap near ₹11,055 Cr against adjusted FY26 earnings of ~₹232 Cr, the trailing multiple sits in the high-40s times earnings, and enterprise value (adding ~₹1,911 Cr of net debt) against ~₹352 Cr of EBITDA is roughly the high-30s times EBITDA — rich, comparable to the premiumisation darlings of the sector rather than to a value cyclical. That is the market saying it believes the integration-and-premiumisation plan. It is not a stock where the good news still has to be discovered; it is one where the good news has to be delivered.

The one number that settles the thesis: consolidated EBITDA margin, read against the debt clock

For a state-price-controlled distiller carrying acquisition debt, neither the top line nor a single quarter's profit tells you much — revenue jumps on volume and mix, and reported PAT is distorted by amortisation. The metric that actually proves or breaks this case is consolidated EBITDA margin — the ~15–15.5% of today against the 16–18% management has promised — tracked quarter by quarter and read alongside one companion line: net debt (and net-debt/EBITDA). If margin climbs toward the high-teens as Imperial Blue is re-margined and the mix richens, EBITDA rises, net debt falls toward the ~₹1,700 Cr target and below 1x, and the rich multiple is justified by a genuine national franchise compounding. If margin stalls near 15% while integration drags, the deleveraging slows, the amortisation-heavy reported profit stays ugly, and a demanding valuation meets a company still digesting a brand bigger than itself. Margin up, debt down — the two lines have to cross, and roughly on the schedule the price already assumes.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A regional champion mid-leap to national scale — asked whether it can land it while carrying debt.

Tilaknagar enters FY27 mid-transformation: a dominant brandy franchise throwing off cash, a large new national whisky brand to absorb, a premiumisation plan to execute, and a debt pile to work down — all at once. What the company cannot promise is the one thing that decides it: that integration and margin expansion arrive fast enough to deleverage on schedule. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Category ownership — an entrenched, world-scale brandy leader (Mansion House past 10m cases) throwing off reliable Southern cash.
  • Transformational acquisition — Imperial Blue turns a regional brandy house into a national spirits company overnight, at a mass-market price for a top-three whisky brand.
  • Clear margin path — 90%+ prestige-and-above mix plus 250–400 bps of integration upside targets a 16–18% EBITDA margin.
  • Proven deleveraging — the same team that went from a debt crisis to net-debt-free is running the paydown, with an explicit sub-1x target.
  • Premiumisation tailwind — India's IMFL market is trading up structurally; a richer mix lifts the whole portfolio's economics.

The bear case

  • Integration risk — absorbing a brand bigger than itself, from a multinational, is hard; the margin thesis fails if it slips.
  • Leverage, again — a company fresh out of a debt crisis is levered to ~₹1,911 Cr net debt; disappointing EBITDA stalls the paydown.
  • State-excise squeeze — frozen retail prices against rising ENA/glass/Scotch costs can compress margins the plan is counting on.
  • Rich valuation — high-40s P/E leaves little room if integration, margins or deleveraging run behind schedule.
  • Bigger, richer rivals — United Spirits, Radico and ABD can outspend on premium; the national arena is far more contested than Southern brandy.
The bottom line

Tilaknagar Industries is a genuine rarity: a company that turned a decade-long survival fight into the credibility to make a transformational bet, and is now spending that credibility on the biggest leap of its life. The brandy franchise is real, dominant and cash-generative; the Imperial Blue acquisition is a shrewd, non-obvious way to become national in one move; and the margin plan, if delivered, converts a regional specialist into a scaled spirits house. But it is all being attempted with borrowed money, against price-setting state governments, and at a valuation that already assumes success. The next test is concrete and datable: whether consolidated EBITDA margin climbs from ~15% toward the promised 16–18% while net debt falls to the ~₹1,700 Cr March-2027 target. Deliver both, and the market's rich verdict on a new national franchise looks earned; miss them, and a company that only just cleared its debts will be judged for taking on more before it was ready.
