00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're actually looking at, before we go deep.

Cohance Lifesciences is a contract drug-ingredient manufacturer — the kind of company that never puts a pill in a chemist's shop under its own name, but makes the active ingredients, complex intermediates and specialty chemistry that other pharmaceutical and agro-chemical companies sell. It is, in plain fact, the renamed Suven Pharmaceuticals: in May 2025 the listed Suven Pharma absorbed a private chemistry platform called Cohance and took its name. The controlling owner throughout is the global private-equity firm Advent International.

Three facts frame the whole story:

  • It is a merger, wearing a new name. Advent already controlled a cluster of API/CDMO businesses (branded "Cohance" in 2022) and separately bought control of listed Suven Pharma from its founding Jasti family. It then fused the two. The result is a single, larger, more integrated CDMO spanning small-molecule APIs, complex intermediates, antibody-drug-conjugate (ADC) chemistry and captive specialty chemicals — bigger than either piece alone.
  • FY26 was a deliberately ugly year. Revenue was ~₹2,269 Cr but fell ~13% on a like-for-like basis; net profit dropped to ~₹150 Cr from ~₹265 Cr; consolidated adjusted EBITDA margin slid to ~21% from the low-30s the standalone business once earned. Two big CDMO molecules went through customer destocking, a key API site (Nacharam) had disruptions, and merger integration ate into operating leverage. Management calls FY26 the "bottoming-out" year.
  • The premium never left. Even on that depressed profit, the stock trades near 85× earnings at a ~₹16,900 Cr market cap. The market is not paying for FY26; it is paying for the ADC niche, the China+1 outsourcing wave, and the 10 Phase-III molecules in the CDMO pipeline that are meant to turn into commercial supply.
In plain terms

A drug company that has invented a molecule faces a choice: build its own chemical plant to make the active ingredient, or hand the recipe to a specialist who already has the reactors, the regulatory approvals and the safety systems. Cohance is that specialist. It takes an innovator's molecule — or the tricky payload that arms a cancer antibody — and figures out how to make it at gram, then kilo, then tonne scale, cleanly and repeatably, inside plants that the US FDA has inspected. It earns a development fee while the drug is in trials, then years of manufacturing revenue if the drug reaches the market. The catch, which FY26 laid bare: that revenue arrives in lumps, a single customer's stocking decision can swing a quarter, and stitching two companies together takes longer than a spreadsheet suggests.

01

What a pharma CDMO actually does

No chemistry degree required — just four words and why each one matters.

To follow Cohance you need four plain ideas. Get these and the rest of the primer reads easily.

The four words that unlock the business
TermWhat it means (and where Cohance sits)
API"Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient" — the actual drug molecule inside a tablet (the paracetamol in a Crocin). Everything else in the pill is filler. Making API cleanly and consistently is hard chemistry; Cohance is among India's larger merchant API makers.
IntermediateA half-built molecule — a chemical step on the way to the API. Innovators often outsource the messy early steps and keep the final one in-house. Complex intermediates are a high-skill, high-margin niche and a Cohance specialty.
CDMO"Contract Development and Manufacturing Organisation." The "D" is the lab work to invent a scalable process; the "M" is running it at commercial volume. A CDMO does both for a fee — it does not own the drug.
HPAPI / ADC"High-Potency API" — molecules so toxic (think cancer payloads) they must be handled in sealed, negative-pressure suites. When such a payload is chemically linked to an antibody, you get an "Antibody-Drug Conjugate." This is the scarce, high-value frontier Cohance is chasing.
Why anyone outsources this at all

A new drug's chemistry is expensive, specialised and regulator-policed. Building a dedicated plant for one molecule — with the right reactors, effluent treatment, and FDA-grade quality systems — can cost more and take longer than a biotech has. Outsourcing converts that fixed cost into a variable fee and hands the problem to someone who runs a dozen such processes at once. For the CDMO, the reward is stickiness: once your process is written into a drug's regulatory filing, switching suppliers means re-filing with the FDA — slow, risky and rarely worth it. That is the moat. The flip side is concentration: a CDMO's revenue leans on a handful of molecules and customers, and if one of them destocks or a drug stalls, the hit is immediate. FY26 was a live demonstration.

Where the money comes from — and when

  • Development revenue (early, small, sticky). Fees for figuring out how to make an innovator's molecule while it is still in clinical trials. Low volume, but it plants the seed — win the process now and you often win the commercial supply later.
  • Commercial revenue (late, large, lumpy). Once a drug is approved and selling, the CDMO manufactures the API/intermediate at scale for years. This is the bulk of the money — but it rises and falls with the customer's own sales, inventory policy and life-cycle.
  • Merchant / generic API (steady, competitive). Off-patent active ingredients sold to many formulators. More competitive and lower-margin than innovator CDMO work, but it keeps plants busy between commercial launches.

03

The merger and who controls it

How a private-equity firm assembled a CDMO out of five companies and one listed shell.

Cohance is, at heart, an Advent International construction project. The US private-equity firm spent four years buying chemistry businesses, branded them, then reverse-merged the private cluster into a public company it also controlled. Understanding that sequence explains the ownership, the ~57% promoter stake, and the eventual overhang the market keeps one eye on.

Timeline — the assembly

  • Oct 2018
    The CRAMS (contract research & manufacturing) arm is demerged out of Suven Life Sciences to form Suven Pharmaceuticals, sharpening it into a pure CDMO. Founded and run by the Jasti family.
  • 2020
    Suven Pharmaceuticals lists on NSE & BSE (scrip 543064) — a high-margin, cash-rich CDMO darling.
  • 2020–22
    Advent quietly builds an API platform: control of RA Chem Pharma (Oct 2020), ZCL Chemicals (Mar 2021) and Avra Laboratories (Apr 2022).
  • Nov 2022
    Advent launches the "Cohance Lifesciences" brand over those three companies — its stated aim: become a top-3 merchant API + CDMO player in India.
  • Dec 2022
    Advent agrees to buy 50.1% of listed Suven Pharma from the Jasti family (~US$762m), plus a mandatory 26% open offer to public shareholders. The founders exit control.
  • 2023
    Acquisition completes; Advent now controls both the private Cohance platform and listed Suven Pharma.
  • Feb 2024
    Boards approve the merger of Cohance into Suven Pharma — a court-sanctioned scheme, share swap of 11 Suven shares for every 295 Cohance shares.
  • May 2025
    Merger effective (1 May); Suven Pharma is renamed Cohance Lifesciences Limited (name change 7 May; trading in the new name from 19 May). A ~US$3bn combined entity.
  • FY26
    First full year as the merged company — a trough of destocking and integration, with the ADC build-out running underneath.

Ownership & control

  • Promoter — Advent International (~57–58%), held through investment vehicles Berhyanda Limited (~33%) and Jusmiral Holdings (~21%). Advent is the classic private-equity owner: it bought to improve, scale and eventually sell.
  • Public float ~42%, split between retail, FIIs (~6%) and domestic institutions (~20%+) — the latter having stepped up around the merger.
  • Leadership: a professional management team runs the combined platform; the Jasti founders have exited control, so this is a PE-governed company, not a promoter-family one.
The Advent overhang: private-equity owners do not hold forever. At some point Advent will sell down — via block deals, an offer-for-sale, or a strategic exit. That is a normal, healthy endgame, but a large promoter selldown can cap the share price and creates a persistent "when, and to whom" question hanging over the stock. It is the ownership-side risk to keep on the radar.

This is the classic innovator business: developing and manufacturing APIs and complex intermediates for global drug companies, from clinical scale through commercial supply. The captive/merchant chemistry arm, making specialty intermediates for agrochemicals and electronic materials. Management has flagged FY27 as a key "qualification year" — the period when new customers validate the plants before volumes scale.

How the pieces connect

05

The ADC and high-potency niche — the real reason for the multiple

The scarce, complex, high-margin frontier that separates Cohance from a commodity API maker.

Strip away the merger noise and the single most important thing about Cohance is a capability most Indian pharma companies simply do not have: making the armed warhead of a cancer antibody. Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are the fastest-growing modality in oncology, and the chemistry to build them is genuinely hard. This is the part of Cohance the ~85× multiple is really underwriting.

What an ADC is, in one breath

An ADC is a guided missile. An antibody (the guidance system) finds a cancer cell; a linker (the tether) holds a payload (a highly toxic small molecule) until it reaches the target, then releases it right inside the tumour. The point is precision: a poison too dangerous to inject freely becomes usable when it is delivered only where it's needed. Building one needs three scarce skills — designing the toxic payload, engineering the linker, and the bioconjugation step that chemically welds payload-linker to antibody without wrecking either.

Cohance's ADC stack — what it can actually do
CapabilityWhat Cohance offers
PayloadsA global frontrunner in high-potency payloads — deep expertise in camptothecin (CPT) derivatives and a proprietary s-Trione scaffold. These are the cytotoxic warheads at the heart of many modern ADCs.
LinkersIndustry-standard and proprietary linkers — maleimide, MC-VC-PAB, SMCC, hydrazone, PEG8-DBCO and more — the chemistry that controls when and where the payload releases.
BioconjugationVia US subsidiary NJ Bio (Princeton, New Jersey) — a US$10m expansion built a cGMP bioconjugation suite that can make up to ~2 kg of ADCs and supply clinical programmes through Phase 2b. Cohance reported 5 GMP bioconjugation batches completed in FY26.
High-potency handlingSealed, negative-pressure containment for ultra-toxic compounds — the safety engineering that keeps operators alive and lets Cohance handle molecules most plants cannot touch.
Adjacent modalitiesA cGMP block for oligonucleotides, and a collaboration with Ajinomoto for its AJICAP site-specific conjugation technology — widening the platform beyond classic ADCs.
Why this niche is worth so much

Three things make ADC/high-potency chemistry a rare kind of business. (1) Scarcity of capability. Very few companies worldwide can handle payloads this toxic at GMP scale — the containment, the analytics and the regulatory history take years and serious capital to build, which keeps competition thin and pricing firm. (2) Demand is exploding. ADCs are among oncology's hottest modalities; big-pharma deal-making around them has run into the tens of billions of dollars, and every one of those drugs needs someone to make the payload and do the conjugation. (3) It sits upstream of the antibody. Cohance doesn't need to make the (much more competitive) antibody itself; it owns the specialised chemistry — payload, linker, conjugation — where the value and the scarcity concentrate. Owning the CPT-derivative and s-Trione payload franchise plus a US GMP conjugation site is a genuinely differentiated position for an Indian CDMO.

The honest caveat: today the ADC business is small in rupee terms — a handful of GMP batches, not a commercial cash cow. It is optionality, not yet an earnings pillar. The bet is that clinical-stage ADC programmes Cohance supplies now graduate to commercial volumes over the next several years. If they do, this niche re-rates the whole company; if ADC development slows or a key customer's drug fails, the frontier stays a promise rather than a P&L line.
06

China+1 and BIOSECURE — the tailwind at the platform's back

Why Western drug companies are actively looking for a supplier exactly like Cohance.

Cohance's timing is not an accident. A structural shift in global pharma supply chains — away from over-reliance on China — is precisely the wave a scaled, FDA-audited Indian CDMO is built to ride. Understanding it explains why the market pays up despite a weak FY26.

  • China+1. For two decades, Western pharma leaned heavily on Chinese suppliers for APIs and intermediates. Pandemic shocks, geopolitics and tariff risk have made that concentration uncomfortable. "China+1" is the corporate response: keep China, but add a second, non-China source. India — English-speaking, FDA-experienced, with deep chemistry talent — is the obvious "+1", and Cohance is one of the larger organised platforms on offer.
  • BIOSECURE. Proposed US legislation aimed at curbing federal business with certain Chinese biotech/CDMO firms (WuXi names among them) has pushed Western customers to qualify alternative suppliers now, even before any law passes. Qualification is slow — so the scramble to line up non-China capacity is a multi-year tailwind for Indian CDMOs, whether or not the bill is enacted.
  • The catch — qualification is not instant revenue. Being a preferred "+1" gets you into evaluations; it does not put volume in your plants overnight. Customers must validate sites, file processes with regulators and de-risk switching. The tailwind is real but slow-moving, which is exactly why a strong structural story can coexist with a soft near-term year like FY26.
The subtlety a generalist misses

China+1 is often pitched as a switch that has already flipped in India's favour. It hasn't — it's a decade-long migration, not a single event. The winners will be the platforms that spent the qualification years building scale, regulatory track record and hard-to-copy capabilities (like ADC chemistry) so that when a customer does move a molecule, there is a validated plant ready. Cohance's merger is, in part, a bet on being big and broad enough to be that ready platform. The risk is that the migration stays gradual while the stock is priced for it to accelerate.

FY26 — anatomy of a trough year

The year the numbers went backwards, and exactly why.

FY26 (year ended March 2026) was the first full year of the merged company — and it was a deliberate, and disclosed, low point. Reading what broke is the fastest way to understand what has to mend for the thesis to work.

Consolidated revenue fell ~13% on a like-for-like basis to ~₹2,269 Cr — the combined weight of destocking, a site disruption, and specialty-chemical timing, layered on merger-integration friction.
  • Destocking of two large CDMO molecules. After the post-pandemic inventory bubble, several customers ran down stock rather than reorder. When two big molecules destock at once, a CDMO's revenue drops even though nothing is wrong with the underlying demand — the pipeline just refills later.
  • Nacharam site disruption. Shipment delays and temporary disruptions at a key API+ manufacturing site knocked that segment down ~8%. A single-site wobble in a plant-heavy business feeds straight to the top line.
  • Margin compression. Consolidated adjusted EBITDA margin fell to ~21% (the reported operating margin ~19%, versus ~31% for the standalone business the prior year). Lower volumes over a fixed cost base, plus the drag of consolidating lower-margin subsidiaries, squeezed profitability. The standalone adjusted EBITDA margin held up better at ~24.6%, hinting at the core's resilience.
  • Profit halved. Net profit fell to ~₹150 Cr from ~₹265 Cr; Q3 FY26 profit alone dropped ~76% year-on-year, and Q4 net profit was a thin ~₹8 Cr on ~₹619 Cr of revenue — the visible bottom of the cycle.
The non-transferable measure of this business

Here is the arithmetic that captures Cohance's whole character. The same chemistry platform, the same plants and the same people earned a low-30s EBITDA margin as standalone Suven and roughly a 21% consolidated margin in FY26 — and net profit swung from ~₹265 Cr to ~₹150 Cr — without any collapse in end-demand. Two customers' inventory decisions and one site's shipment delays did most of that. In a CDMO, the gap between a good year and a bad one is measured in a handful of molecules and a plant or two — which is why a single "bottoming-out" year can look alarming and still leave the franchise intact. The tell to watch is whether that ~21% consolidated margin climbs back toward the mid-20s as volumes normalise.

Where Cohance can actually win

Against Divi's on cost and scale, Cohance loses — Divi's is bigger and more profitable. The place Cohance can carve a defensible position is complexity: high-potency payloads, ADC linkers and bioconjugation, plus niche APIs where only a few suppliers qualify. That is deliberately the harder, thinner end of the market, where a WuXi under a regulatory cloud is a customer Cohance can inherit and where a generic-API price war doesn't reach. The strategic question for the next few years is whether the merged platform can convert that capability edge into commercial volumes faster than larger, better-capitalised rivals build the same skills.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A capable platform in a great structural spot — asking to be trusted through a soft patch at a high price.

Cohance is a genuinely differentiated Indian CDMO — merged into scale, armed with a scarce ADC franchise, riding a real China+1 tailwind, and backed by a serious private-equity owner. It is also, on FY26 numbers, an expensive stock with falling profits, a plant disruption, and integration still in progress. The debate is whether the frontier and the recovery justify paying up through the trough.

The bull case

  • Scarce ADC/high-potency capability — payloads (CPT, s-Trione), linkers and US GMP bioconjugation via NJ Bio: a differentiated, high-margin niche few can enter.
  • Structural China+1 / BIOSECURE tailwind — Western customers actively qualifying non-China suppliers; Cohance is a scaled, FDA-experienced option.
  • Merger scale & breadth — a fuller capability ladder, from agro intermediates to clinical ADCs, under one quality umbrella.
  • Visible pipeline — 10 Phase-III molecules plus a datable 2HFY27 inflection; a clear recovery shape off a trough base.
  • Serious owner — Advent's operational track record in building and scaling healthcare platforms.

The bear case

  • Very high multiple on trough earnings — ~85× P/E, ~7% ROE; the price assumes a strong recovery that hasn't yet shown up in results.
  • Lumpy, concentrated revenue — two molecules destocking and one site's delays halved profit; CDMO income is inherently customer- and molecule-concentrated and biotech-funding-cyclical.
  • Integration & execution risk — merging five-plus businesses and lifting consolidated margin back up is not automatic.
  • ADC is still optionality — small in rupees today; commercial conversion is a multi-year, drug-approval-dependent bet.
  • Advent selldown overhang & forex — an eventual PE exit can cap the price; export earnings carry currency risk; USFDA/site compliance is a permanent watch-item.
The bottom line

Cohance is a capable, differentiated chemistry platform caught between two clocks — a slow structural tailwind (China+1, ADCs) that could take a decade to fully arrive, and a fast market clock that has already priced much of it in. FY26 was the low point by design; the recovery has a name and a date on it. What turns the thesis from hope into proof is narrow and specific: Pharma CDMO revenue growth reaccelerating and the consolidated EBITDA margin climbing back toward the mid-20s as the 10 Phase-III molecules convert and the NJ Bio bioconjugation line scales. The milestone to circle is the company's own: the second half of FY27, when management says growth resumes. If that quarter arrives on schedule with margin following, the premium starts to make sense; if 2HFY27 slips, an 85× stock on 7% returns has to answer for itself.
