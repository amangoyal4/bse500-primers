JSW Dulux, the Dulux paint brand under a steel-house's new colours

A full walk through the company that was Akzo Nobel India until March 2026 — the maker of premium Dulux wall paints — now controlled by Sajjan Jindal's JSW Group. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep — starting with a name that changed.

JSW Dulux Paints makes paint — mostly the wall paints, emulsions, enamels, primers, wood finishes and waterproofing that go on Indian homes, sold under the premium Dulux brand — plus a sizeable industrial and specialty coatings business (marine, powder, protective, packaging and vehicle-refinish coatings). Until 11 March 2026 this exact company was called Akzo Nobel India Limited, the Indian arm of the Dutch paints multinational AkzoNobel. In December 2025 India's JSW Group — Sajjan Jindal's steel-energy-cement-infrastructure conglomerate — bought a 60.76% controlling stake and renamed it. Same factories, same Dulux cans, same dealers; a completely different owner and ambition.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • It is the premium #4 in a big, cosy paints market. India's decorative-paints industry has for decades been a comfortable near-oligopoly led by Asian Paints (the runaway giant), then Berger and Kansai Nerolac, with Akzo/Dulux as the smaller, premium-skewed number four — roughly a 6–7% share (approx.) of decorative paints. It is a high-return, steady-growth, brand-driven business: FY25 revenue was ~₹4,069 Cr, EBITDA margin ~16%, PAT ~₹430 Cr, on a balance sheet that is essentially debt-free.
  • The owner just changed, and the strategy is unwritten. JSW already ran its own young paints venture, JSW Paints (started 2019). Buying Akzo's India business hands JSW the established Dulux brand and a national dealer network overnight, and the group's stated intent is to fuse the two into a far bigger paints challenger. How exactly JSW Paints and Akzo/Dulux combine — brands, dealers, plants, positioning — is the central open question, and it is genuinely unproven.
  • The whole industry is being disrupted at once. Just as Akzo was handing Dulux to JSW, the Aditya Birla group's Grasim launched Birla Opus — a ~₹10,000 Cr assault on paints with aggressive pricing and dealer incentives. So a decades-stable club now faces two well-capitalised invaders (Birla Opus and an enlarged JSW paints business) at the same time. That is a share-and-discount war that can compress margins for everyone — including this company.
In plain terms

Strip away the corporate drama and this is a branded consumer-goods business that happens to sell colour. A household repaints every few years; it walks into a paint shop, the shopkeeper mixes the exact shade on a machine while they wait, and a painter applies it. The brand that wins is the one that is on that shelf, in that machine, and trusted by that painter — distribution and trust, far more than chemistry. Dulux is a strong, premium version of exactly that. The twist is ownership: this branded paints jewel now sits inside a steel-and-energy conglomerate that wants to scale it hard, right as two giants start a price war for the same shelves.

01

Paints 101: how a decorative-paint company earns its keep

No chemistry needed — just the handful of ideas that decide who wins.

A can of wall paint looks like a commodity. It is not, and understanding why is the whole game. Two cans of similar white emulsion can sell at very different prices and margins depending on the brand on the label, whether the shop can tint it to any colour on the spot, and whether the painter recommends it. Decorative paint is a low-cost, high-frequency, emotionally-loaded purchase — you live with the result on your walls for years — and that turns it into one of India's better consumer-goods categories. Here are the ideas that matter.

The concepts that run the paints business
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Decorative paintsThe house-and-building segment — emulsions (wall paints), enamels, distempers, primers, putties, wood finishes, waterproofing, texture coatings. The big, branded, ~three-quarters of the Indian paint market. Dulux's home turf.
Industrial / coatingsPaint for things other than homes — cars (refinish), ships (marine), steel structures (protective), appliances and pipes (powder), packaging. More business-to-business, lumpier, and where Akzo's global technology historically gave this company an edge.
Tinting machineThe dispenser on the dealer's counter that mixes a base paint with colourants to make any of thousands of shades on demand. It slashes the shop's inventory (no need to stock every colour) and quietly locks the dealer to one brand — the machine, software and colourants are the maker's. The single most important piece of hardware in the industry.
Dealer networkThe paint shops that actually sell to households and painters. Paint is bought locally and often on the painter's advice, so reach — how many shops stock you and push you — is the core moat. This is a distribution business dressed up as a chemicals one.
Titanium dioxide (TiO₂)The white pigment that is the biggest single raw material in paint, plus crude-oil-linked monomers and solvents. When crude and TiO₂ prices move, so do paint-makers' gross margins — the input side of the story.
The moat that surprises people

Ask where a paint company's advantage lives and the intuitive answer is "the formula." It mostly isn't. Rivals can match a formula; TiO₂ and acrylic monomers are bought on the open market. The durable edge is the tinting machine on the counter and the relationship around it — because once a dealer has your machine, your colour fan-deck and your painter-loyalty scheme, switching brands means re-learning shades, re-training staff and disappointing repeat customers. Multiply that stickiness across tens of thousands of shops and you get the real barrier to entry. Chemistry gets you a product; a hundred thousand loyal counters get you a franchise.

Why the economics are attractive

  • Recurring, non-discretionary-ish demand. Walls get dirty; monsoons peel paint; homes get repainted on a cycle. Roughly two-thirds of decorative demand is repainting, not new construction — a steadier, replacement-driven base than most building materials.
  • Pricing power through the brand. A trusted brand and a helpful painter let a maker charge a premium for what is, chemically, not that different — which is why the leaders earn fat margins and high returns on capital.
  • Asset-light-ish, cash-generative. Paint plants are not cheap, but the business throws off cash, needs modest incremental capital, and historically pays most of its profit out as dividends. Akzo/Dulux India ran on a near debt-free balance sheet and a very high payout.
02

What JSW Dulux actually sells: two businesses in one company

A premium decorative brand riding on top of a technical coatings business.

This company is really two paint businesses stapled together. The larger, consumer-facing one is decorative paints under the Dulux brand — what you see in a paint shop. The smaller, quieter one is industrial and specialty coatings — the technical paints that protect ships, steel, cars and appliances, an area where the departed parent AkzoNobel was a global leader. They share factories and a name, but they behave very differently.

The decorative range, decoded

  • Interior & exterior emulsions — water-based wall paints, the volume heart of the business, sold across a good-better-best ladder from economy to super-premium (Dulux Velvet Touch and the like). Premium tiers carry the best margins.
  • Enamels, primers, putties & distempers — the workhorse coatings for wood, metal and wall preparation; lower glamour, steady demand.
  • Wood finishes & waterproofing — higher-value adjacencies (wood coatings, exterior waterproofing) that the whole industry is pushing because they grow faster and price higher than plain wall paint.

The coatings range, decoded

  • Marine & protective — high-performance coatings for ships, ports, bridges and industrial steel; technical, specification-driven, tied to infrastructure and marine cycles.
  • Powder & industrial — coatings for appliances, furniture, pipes and general industry; and vehicle refinishes for the car-repair aftermarket, a branded niche where Akzo's technology was strong.
  • Packaging & specialty — coatings for cans and packaging plus other specialty lines; smaller, but sticky and B2B.
Why the two halves matter differently

The decorative half is the brand asset — it is what makes this a consumer franchise, and it is what JSW paid up for. The coatings half is the technology asset — less visible, more B2B, and historically leaning on the global AkzoNobel parent for formulations, marine approvals and know-how. That second point carries a catch worth holding onto: when the Dutch parent exits, some of the technical umbilical to a world-leading coatings company goes with it. The decorative brand travels well under a new owner; a globally-specified marine or powder-coating capability is harder to replicate from a standing start.

03

The ownership earthquake: from Akzo Nobel to the House of Jindal

The single event that redefines this company — a Dutch paints multinational hands its India crown jewel to an Indian steel group.

For decades this was Akzo Nobel India — the listed Indian subsidiary of AkzoNobel, the Amsterdam-headquartered paints-and-coatings giant, whose Indian roots run back through ICI India (Imperial Chemical Industries), which AkzoNobel absorbed globally in 2008. Then, in 2025, the parent decided to simplify its global portfolio and sell India. The buyer was not another paint company. It was the JSW Group.

Who is JSW — and why does a steel group want a paint brand?

The JSW Group, led by Sajjan Jindal, is one of India's largest conglomerates: JSW Steel (a top-tier global steelmaker), JSW Energy, JSW Cement, JSW Infrastructure (ports), and — since 2019 — JSW Paints, a from-scratch paints venture that had been clawing its way up as a small challenger. Paints is attractive to an industrials group for a simple reason: it is a consumer-branded, high-margin, high-return, cash-generative business that diversifies away from cyclical, capital-heavy steel and cement. Buying Akzo/Dulux vaulted JSW's paints ambition forward by years — acquiring an established brand, distribution and manufacturing that would have taken a decade to build organically.

The genuine surprise, stated plainly

A brand that has painted Indian living rooms for generations — Dulux, with its heritage in ICI and then a Dutch coatings multinational — is now owned by the same house that makes structural steel and runs power plants and ports. There is no obvious industrial logic linking rolling mills to emulsion paint; the logic is portfolio strategy. JSW wants a branded consumer cash-compounder, and paint is one of the best in India. So the counter-intuitive fact to sit with is that the future of the Dulux brand in India now depends less on paint chemistry and more on how a steel-and-energy conglomerate chooses to wield distribution muscle in a category it is still learning.

Ownership & balance sheet after the deal

  • Controlling shareholder: JSW Paints / JSW Group holds ~60.76%. The residual public float and institutions hold the rest; the erstwhile foreign parent AkzoNobel has exited control.
  • Balance sheet: essentially debt-free — borrowings of only ~₹62 Cr against a comfortable cash-and-investments cushion — a legacy of the disciplined, high-payout Akzo era. ROCE ~22.7% and ROE ~19.1% point to a genuinely high-quality underlying business.
  • Dividend history: the company historically paid out most of its profit (FY25 payout ~106%). Under a growth-hungry new owner intent on scaling and investing, that generous-payout posture may well change in favour of reinvestment — a watch item for income-minded holders.
04

The Dulux brand and the premium tilt

Why a smaller player can still be a valuable one — it sits up-market.

Number four by volume does not mean number four by quality of business. Akzo/Dulux India has long punched at the premium end of decorative paint, where shades are richer, warranties longer, and prices — and margins — higher. Dulux is a globally recognised masterbrand; in India it competes on colour, finish and aspiration rather than on being the cheapest can on the shelf. That premium tilt is the source of its respectable margins despite a modest market share.

The strategic logic is straightforward. In a market the giant (Asian Paints) dominates on sheer reach, a smaller player is wise not to fight a price war it will lose, but to own the up-market shopper — the urban homeowner willing to pay for a better finish and a trusted name. That is where Dulux has focused: premium interior emulsions, exterior weather-shield ranges, designer colour, wood finishes. It is a smaller pond, but a deeper one.

Why premium is both the strength and the exposure

Selling up-market is what lets a #4 earn double-digit margins and high returns — the shopper who pays for Dulux is less price-sensitive than the one buying the cheapest distemper. But a premium tilt is also thinner cover in a price war. When a deep-pocketed newcomer floods the market with sharp discounts and dealer sweeteners, the value end feels it first, yet the aspirational buyer is not immune to a good deal either. The premium position softens the blow of the coming competitive storm; it does not make this company immune to it. Holding the premium buyer while two rivals spray discounts across the market is exactly the tightrope of the next few years.

05

The crux: dealers × tinting-machine throughput

The single most useful way to think about — and score — a paints challenger. It fits this company and almost no other.

Everything the market is trying to figure out about JSW Dulux collapses into one arithmetic. A paint brand's decorative revenue is, near enough, the product of four things: how many shops actively stock it, how many of those have its tinting machine, how much paint each of those counters throughputs, and the premium mix of what they sell. Win the category and you are winning it store-by-store, machine-by-machine. That is why paint is a distribution-and-logistics game far more than a chemistry one.

Decorative revenue ≈ (active dealers) × (tinting machines placed) × (throughput per counter) × (premium mix). A challenger's entire job is to lift the first two faster than a price war erodes the last two.

Read against that formula, the incumbent gap is stark. Asian Paints reportedly touches on the order of 150,000+ retail points; a premium #4 like Dulux operates a far smaller network — of the order of ~18,000–19,000 touchpoints (approx.). So on raw shelf presence the challenger is outnumbered many times over. This is precisely the gap JSW's capital and distribution ambition is meant to close: add dealers, place machines, widen reach — convert a steel group's balance sheet into paint-shop counters.

The device that only fits this company: dealers added vs share bled

Score JSW Dulux each year on a single race. On one side, net dealer additions and tinting machines placed — the reach JSW's capital can buy. On the other, share and margin bled to the price war as Birla Opus and an enlarged JSW paints business fight for the same counters. If the network expands faster than discounting erodes the economics — more machines, holding throughput, holding premium mix — the challenger thesis is working. If reach comes only by paying dealers ever-fatter margins and slashing prices, the company is buying volume with profit, and the reach on paper flatters a franchise quietly getting worse. The order book, so to speak, is the dealer count; the profit is whether those counters throughput at a healthy margin.

Why this device is non-transferable: it only makes sense for a paint brand, because paint is uniquely won at the point of sale by a machine and a painter's word. You cannot lift this framing onto a steelmaker, a bank or a hospital — there is no tinting machine on anyone else's counter. That is the test the anti-boilerplate rule demands: swap the company name in and the sentence stops meaning anything.
06

A comfortable oligopoly — and its two invaders

The industry backdrop that turns a steady business into a contested one.

For roughly two decades, Indian decorative paints was one of the most pleasant industries in the country to be in: a handful of scaled players, high barriers (that dealer-and-machine moat), pricing discipline, and Asian Paints setting the tone as an unusually dominant leader. Everyone made good money. Then, almost at once, two of India's richest industrial houses decided they wanted in — and the cosy equilibrium cracked.

The disruption, in two arrivals
EntrantWho / how bigWhy it changes the game
Birla Opus (Grasim / Aditya Birla)~₹10,000 Cr investment, multiple large plants, national ambition; launched Feb 2024.The most aggressive greenfield entry Indian paints has seen — buying reach with heavy dealer incentives and sharp introductory pricing, explicitly targeting the #2 slot. A direct margin threat to every incumbent, top-down.
JSW paints (incl. this company)JSW Paints (from 2019) now combined with the acquired Akzo/Dulux business.Turns a small challenger into a serious contender overnight by bolting an established premium brand and network onto a conglomerate's balance sheet — a second well-funded aggressor pushing for share.

Two deep-pocketed entrants arriving within roughly a year of each other is not a normal competitive nudge; it is a structural shock to an industry that had grown used to rational pricing. New capacity has to be filled, and the fastest way to fill a paint plant is to buy dealers and undercut on price — precisely the moves that compress margins across the board. Even the mighty leader has felt the pressure on growth and pricing. For a #4, the environment is simultaneously an opportunity (JSW's capital finally lets it play offence) and a threat (it is now fighting on two fronts, including against a sister-strategy that shares its own new owner's ambition).

The surprise inside the disruption

Here is the twist a generalist misses. The comfortable four-firm club was not disrupted despite stability — it was disrupted because of it. Paints looked, from the outside, like an irresistible prize: high margins, high returns, dependable growth, a fragmented-consumer end. That very attractiveness is what drew two of India's largest conglomerates to storm it at the same moment — and Akzo's decision to sell Dulux to one of them handed an invader a ready-made beachhead. So the same qualities that made this a wonderful business to own are the qualities now inviting the war that could compress it. An industry gets disrupted precisely where it has been too good for too long.

07

The coatings half: the technical, less-watched business

The B2B paint that never touches a home — and its dependence on a departing parent.

While the market obsesses over the Dulux brand and the paint war, roughly a third of this company's revenue comes from a quieter place: industrial and specialty coatings. This is paint as engineering — protecting steel from corrosion, ships' hulls from the sea, appliances from wear, cars from the elements after a repair. It is sold to businesses on specification and performance, not to households on colour and aspiration, and it behaves like a different company.

  • Marine & protective coatings — tied to shipping, ports, infrastructure and heavy industry; specification-driven, globally approved, and cyclical with capex and trade.
  • Powder & industrial coatings — for appliances, furniture, architectural aluminium, pipes and general manufacturing; grows with India's industrial and consumer-durables output.
  • Vehicle refinishes — the branded aftermarket for car body-shop repairs, a niche where Akzo's global technology and colour-matching systems were a real edge.
  • Packaging & specialty — coatings for cans and packaging plus assorted specialty lines; sticky, technical, B2B.
The hidden dependency to watch

The coatings business leaned, invisibly, on being part of a world-leading coatings multinational — for formulations, global marine and protective approvals, cross-border technical support and R&D that a standalone Indian company would struggle to fund alone. With AkzoNobel exiting control, the question is how much of that technical umbilical stays connected, and on what terms. The decorative brand can flourish under any capable owner; a globally-specified marine or refinish capability is far harder to sustain once the parent that supplied the science walks away. JSW gains a strong consumer brand cleanly — but on the coatings side it inherits a business whose competitive edge partly lived in Amsterdam.

Why the split matters for valuation: the two halves deserve different multiples. The decorative franchise is a branded-consumer compounder that markets pay premium multiples for; the industrial/coatings arm is a solid but more cyclical, more B2B, technology-dependent business worth rather less per rupee of profit. When you value JSW Dulux, resist pricing the whole thing as if it were all Dulux emulsion.
08

Why India's paint demand keeps growing

The structural tailwind that makes the fight worth having.

The reason two conglomerates are willing to spend billions to enter is simple: the pie is growing, and it should keep growing for a long time. Indian per-capita paint consumption is a fraction of developed-market levels, and several durable forces push it up — which is why paint has compounded ahead of GDP for years and why incumbents and entrants alike are betting on volume, not just share.

The demand truth behind the noise

It is tempting to read the price war as a sign the category is in trouble. It is the opposite. Two giants are spending billions because they believe the market will be much larger in a decade — you do not invest ₹10,000 Cr to fight over a shrinking pie. The near-term reality is margin pressure as everyone chases share; the long-term reality is a structurally growing, under-penetrated category with a durable repainting base. The tension between those two timelines — painful competition now, a bigger prize later — is the honest heart of the investment debate here.

None of this makes demand immune to shocks. Paint volumes soften when construction slows, when a monsoon is poor, or when household budgets tighten; and input costs (crude-linked monomers, TiO₂) can squeeze margins regardless of how many litres sell. But the multi-year direction of Indian paint consumption is up, and that is the backdrop that makes even a bruising competitive phase worth enduring for a well-branded player.

10

The integration question: two paint businesses, one owner

The unproven strategic puzzle at the centre of the story.

JSW now owns two paint businesses: JSW Paints, the venture it built from 2019, and the acquired Akzo/Dulux business that is now JSW Dulux. How it stitches them together — brands, dealers, plants, positioning, capital — is the strategic question the market cannot yet answer, because it has not been done. This is a fresh acquisition under a first-time paints conglomerate; the playbook is being written in real time.

The open threads are concrete. Brands: does Dulux become the group's premium spearhead while JSW Paints covers other tiers, or do the portfolios overlap and cannibalise? Distribution: can the two dealer networks be merged and widened without alienating shopkeepers who fear channel conflict? Capital & payout: will the historically high dividend give way to heavy reinvestment in capacity and reach? Culture & talent: can a steel-and-energy group run a fast-moving consumer brand, and retain the people who know how? None of these has a proven answer, and each can add or destroy value.

Why integration is the swing factor, not a footnote

Acquisitions of strong consumer brands succeed or fail on execution far more than on the price paid. Get the integration right — clear brand architecture, a widened dealer network, sensible reinvestment, retained talent — and JSW turns a #4 into a genuine #2–#3 challenger, exactly the prize it paid up for. Get it wrong — overlapping brands, unsettled dealers, lost technical people, a distracted management — and a high-quality franchise erodes while a price war rages around it. The acquisition bought JSW a strong hand; whether it plays that hand well is the variable no spreadsheet can pre-compute, and it is why "unproven" is the fairest one-word verdict on the strategy today.

11

Financials & valuation

A high-quality, slow-growing franchise — priced richly, into a competitive storm.

The numbers describe a good business growing modestly: high returns on capital, fat-ish margins for a #4, a debt-free balance sheet, and — until now — a very high dividend. What the market is really pricing is not the sedate recent record but the JSW-scaling story layered on top of it, and the multiple leaves little slack if that story disappoints amid a price war.

The one number that settles the thesis: decorative share trend, read against gross margin

At ~38× earnings, JSW Dulux is priced as a business that will gain share and scale under JSW without wrecking its economics — not as a sleepy #4 growing at 8%. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the trend in decorative market share, read alongside gross and EBITDA margin: is the company adding dealers, machines and volume and holding its margins, or is share coming only by discounting and paying dealers more? Rising volume with stable margin means JSW's distribution muscle is compounding a premium franchise — the multiple is earned. Rising volume with sliding margin means it is buying share with profit while the price war bites — and a 38× stock re-rates hard. The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality premium franchise, a deep-pocketed new owner, and a war it did not start.

JSW Dulux enters its new life with real assets — a trusted premium brand, a debt-free balance sheet, high returns, and an owner with the capital and appetite to scale it. It also enters a fight on two fronts, with an integration nobody has attempted before and a valuation that assumes success. The debate is not whether the business is good; it plainly is. It is whether a #4 premium brand, freshly changing hands, can gain ground rather than lose it while two giants wage a price war for the same shelves.

The bull case

  • A conglomerate's firepower behind a premium brand — JSW's balance sheet and distribution ambition can finally scale Dulux's reach: more dealers, more tinting machines, more capacity.
  • High-quality economics — ROCE ~22.7%, near debt-free, respectable margins; a genuinely good franchise, not a turnaround.
  • Premium positioning — Dulux sits up-market, partly insulated from the sharpest price competition at the value end.
  • Structural demand — under-penetrated, growing category with a durable repainting base and premiumisation tailwind.
  • Strategic optionality — combining JSW Paints and Dulux could vault the group to a credible #2–#3, worth far more than a standalone #4.

The bear case

  • A two-front price war — Birla Opus and JSW's own aggression can compress margins across the sector; the #4's economics are exactly what gets tested.
  • Integration is unproven — a steel-and-energy group merging two paint brands and networks for the first time; brand overlap, dealer conflict and talent loss are real risks.
  • Rich valuation — ~38× earnings on ~8% historic growth prices in a scaling story that has not yet begun.
  • Lost parent, lost tech — AkzoNobel's global technology and coatings support exit with control, most exposed on the industrial side.
  • Input & demand cyclicality — crude-linked monomers and TiO₂ pressure margins; monsoons and housing swings pressure volumes; the once-generous dividend may be cut for reinvestment.
The bottom line

JSW Dulux is a genuinely good paints franchise handed to an owner determined to make it bigger, at the exact moment the industry it lives in stopped being comfortable. The Dulux brand and the debt-free, high-return balance sheet are real; so are the two well-funded rivals now fighting for every dealer's counter, and so is the fact that no conglomerate has yet proven it can run and integrate a consumer paints business of this kind. The next stretch is not decided by chemistry or by the brand's heritage — it is decided at the shop level, one machine at a time. Watch a single, checkable thing each quarter: whether JSW Dulux's decorative volumes and dealer count keep rising while its gross margin holds. If both move up together, the steel house is compounding a premium franchise. If reach climbs only as margin slips, it is buying a paint war it will struggle to make pay.
