SBFC Finance, the lender that refused to pick a home state

A complete walk through India's fastest-scaling secured-MSME lender — how you lend ₹10 lakh to a shopkeeper who has no audited accounts and still lose less than 1.5% a year, why a gold-loan book quietly grew to a fifth of the balance sheet, and why the single most interesting thing about SBFC is the thing it deliberately did not do: concentrate.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

SBFC Finance is a non-bank lender to very small businesses. Its main product is a loan of roughly ₹10 lakh to a shopkeeper, trader, small workshop owner or self-employed service provider — the kind of borrower who has a real business and real cash flows but no audited accounts, no salary slip and often no formal credit history. SBFC lends the money and takes a mortgage on the borrower's own house or shop as security. Alongside that it runs a fast-growing gold-loan book: short, small loans against household jewellery. As of March 2026 it managed ₹11,270 crore of loans across 251 branches in 15 states, and earned ₹451 crore of profit on it.

Four facts frame everything that follows:

  • The spread is enormous and it is legal. SBFC charged an average 17.61% on its loans in FY26 and paid 8.52% for its own money — a 9.09% spread, wider by 56 basis points than a year earlier. That is not predatory pricing; it is the price of credit in a segment where a bank's underwriting machinery simply cannot make the file work. After running costs of 3.93% of AUM and credit losses of 1.38%, what falls out is a 4.57% return on assets — roughly three times what a good Indian bank earns.
  • It is deliberately spread thin, and that is the whole strategy. Every listed comparable in this niche — Five-Star, Aptus, Aavas, Can Fin — built its edge on density: saturate a handful of districts, know every street, price the local risk better than an outsider can. SBFC did the opposite from day one: 15 states, no single geography allowed to dominate, hub-and-spoke clusters of five or six branches inside a 30-km radius. It traded local depth for immunity to a regional shock. Whether that trade works is the investment question.
  • A quarter of the growth now comes from gold. The gold-loan book grew 63% in FY26 to ₹2,374 crore and is now ~21% of AUM. It was not built from scratch — it arrived with an acquisition — and it now sits inside roughly 220 of the 251 branches, sharing the same rent, the same manager and the same customer.
  • The balance sheet is barely used. Capital adequacy is 32.8% and debt-to-equity is 1.9×. Management says it can grow AUM to roughly ₹18,000–19,000 crore before needing to raise a rupee. That is why a 4.57% ROA translates into only a ~14% ROE — and why the ROE number understates the machine.
In plain terms

A bank will happily lend ₹10 lakh against a house — if you can prove your income on paper. Millions of Indian business owners can't. They have inventory, a shop, daily cash, a bank passbook full of small deposits, and a property in their own name. SBFC's whole job is to sit with that person for a few hours, work out what the business actually earns, verify the property title, and price the loan at ~17.5% instead of a bank's ~10%. The extra 7.5 percentage points is what pays for the field visit, the branch, and the losses when a few of those borrowers stop paying.

01

Secured small-business lending 101

Why a ₹10 lakh loan is the hardest loan in India to make — and the most profitable one to make well.

Start with the borrower, because in this business the borrower is the industry. India has somewhere north of six crore micro and small enterprises. The overwhelming majority are proprietorships: a fabric shop, a two-machine job-work unit, a wholesale grain trader, a diagnostic lab, a kirana with a delivery scooter. They employ a handful of people, turn over between ₹20 lakh and ₹2 crore a year, keep books that exist mostly in a diary, and pay themselves out of the till.

Such a business needs money for entirely ordinary reasons — buy stock ahead of a festival, add a machine, take a bigger shop, fund a receivable that a customer is sitting on, or simply consolidate expensive borrowings from a local financier at 3% a month. The sums involved are small in absolute terms: ₹5 lakh, ₹10 lakh, ₹20 lakh. And that is precisely where the formal system breaks.

Why the bank says no

A bank's small-business credit process is built around documentation: two or three years of audited financials, GST returns that reconcile, an income-tax return that shows the profit, a current account whose statements can be spread into a cash-flow model. Give a bank those and it will lend at 9–11% all day. Take them away and the file cannot even be opened, because the officer has no defensible basis on which to write "income = ₹X" in the appraisal note. It is not malice; it is process.

There is a second, more brutal reason: unit economics. Sourcing, underwriting, legal and technical diligence on a property, and then servicing a loan costs roughly the same whether the loan is ₹10 lakh or ₹10 crore. On a ₹10 crore corporate loan a bank can afford a full appraisal team. On a ₹10 lakh loan at a 2% net spread, the entire annual revenue is ₹20,000 — less than the cost of one field visit and one legal opinion. The maths only closes if the spread is much wider.

In plain terms

The reason this niche exists is arithmetic, not villainy. At a 2% spread a ₹10 lakh loan earns ₹20,000 a year, which does not cover the cost of visiting the borrower's shop. At a 9% spread it earns ₹90,000, which does. Everything specialised lenders like SBFC do — the branch, the field officer, the in-house valuer, the collections team — is affordable only because the spread is wide, and the spread is wide only because the work is hard.

How the specialists solve it

The lenders who serve this segment replaced documents with three things:

  • Assessed income instead of declared income. A credit officer physically visits the shop, counts footfall, checks stock, looks at electricity bills and supplier ledgers, talks to two neighbouring shopkeepers, and builds a cash-flow estimate from the ground up. This is expensive and slow, and it is the actual product.
  • Collateral that hurts to lose. The security is almost always the borrower's self-occupied house or the shop they trade from — not an investment property. That distinction matters enormously. A borrower will default on an unsecured loan long before they let the family home go. The collateral works less as a recovery mechanism and more as a behavioural anchor: it changes where this loan sits in the borrower's repayment queue.
  • Low loan-to-value. Lend ₹10 lakh against a property worth ₹20 lakh or more. Even in a distressed sale, the principal comes back. Recovery is slow — India's property enforcement, even under the SARFAESI Act, takes quarters not weeks — but the ultimate loss given default is far lower than the headline NPA suggests.

Put those three together and you get the defining characteristic of this asset class: NPAs look high, losses look low. A 2–3% gross NPA in secured-MSME lending is normal and not alarming, because the recovery on those accounts eventually arrives. In unsecured lending, a 2.6% GNPA would mean something close to a 2.6% write-off. Here it means perhaps a third of that, spread over a couple of years.

A word on the label. "MSME loan", "LAP" (loan against property) and "business loan against property" are used almost interchangeably in this industry, which confuses first-time readers. The technically precise description of SBFC's core product is: a term loan to a small-business owner, underwritten on assessed business cash flow, secured by a mortgage on residential or commercial property. The RBI counts most of it as MSME lending; the market calls it LAP.
02

What SBFC actually sells

Two products, one branch, very different clocks.

SBFC runs what looks like a single lending business but is really two, deliberately kept in the same box. The larger is secured MSME lending: ₹8,873 crore at March 2026, up 22% year on year, about 79% of the book. The smaller and faster is loans against gold: ₹2,374 crore, up 63%, about 21%. They share branches, staff and infrastructure, but almost nothing else.

Product one: the secured MSME loan

A typical file: a borrower running a business for at least three years, seeking around ₹10 lakh (approx.), over a stated tenor of eight to fifteen years but with an expected behavioural life of four to five (these loans get refinanced or prepaid early when the business does well), secured on property at a conservative loan-to-value, priced in the high teens. Roughly 75% of SBFC's customers are borrowing against their property for the first time — meaning the asset being mortgaged has never been pledged to anyone before.

In plain terms

That "first-time" statistic is more important than it looks. It means SBFC is not refinancing someone else's loan or taking a second charge behind a bank. It is monetising a completely unencumbered asset that had been sitting idle on the borrower's balance sheet — often the only real asset the family owns. Clean title, first charge, no competing claimant. It is also why the relationship tends to be sticky: the customer has now built a formal credit record where none existed, and SBFC holds the papers.

Product two: the gold loan

Mechanically the opposite. The customer walks in with jewellery, it is weighed and assayed at the counter, and cash goes out within the hour against roughly 55–60% of the gold's value — SBFC has held its portfolio LTV at about 56–57%, comfortably under the 60% internal ceiling and well inside regulatory limits. Tenor is short, often three to twelve months, and the book churns: the same ₹100 of capital can be lent several times in a year. If the customer doesn't repay, the collateral is auctioned — a process measured in weeks, not the quarters that a property enforcement takes.

The pairing is not random. The MSME loan is high-yield but slow to originate and slow to recover; it locks capital up for years. The gold loan is fast in both directions. Held together, they give SBFC a book that is simultaneously long-duration and self-liquidating — and they let a branch that would otherwise be idle between MSME files run a continuous gold counter. One roof, two clocks.

03

The company & who owns it

A career banker, a private-equity cheque, and an NBFC licence bought off the shelf.

SBFC's origin story is unusual for this corner of Indian finance, where most successful lenders are second- or third-generation family firms that grew out of a regional money-lending business. SBFC was assembled, deliberately, by professionals with institutional money behind them.

The lending licence itself dates to 2008, sitting inside a small NBFC that did other things. In 2017, Aseem Dhru — who had spent close to two decades at HDFC Bank, running HDFC Securities and heading the bank's business-banking franchise — left to build a small-business lender of his own, and acquired that licence with backing from private-equity investors. The vehicle was renamed Small Business FinCredit India, later shortened to SBFC Finance. The promoter group today is anchored by Singapore-based Clermont-affiliated and Arpwood-affiliated entities (the precise composition is best read off the latest shareholding filing — treat the attribution here as approx.), and holds 52.31% as of the June 2026 quarter. Dhru remains the executive at the top of the firm; recent disclosures list him as Executive Vice-Chairman, having been Managing Director and CEO from the outset (title as of the latest filing — approx.).

In plain terms

The founder did not grow up in a lending family and did not inherit a customer base. He spent nineteen years learning, inside India's most process-disciplined private bank, exactly which small-business files a bank has to reject — and then built a company to say yes to precisely those files at a price that works. That is a different founding logic from a regional NBFC that expanded outward from its home district, and it shows up in almost every design choice SBFC has made since.

04

The anti-density bet

Every rival in this niche won by going deep in one region. SBFC decided that was the risk, not the moat.

This is the section that matters. If you understand nothing else about SBFC, understand this: it is running a strategy that is the direct inverse of every listed comparable it will be benchmarked against, and its financial results should be read through that lens.

The orthodox playbook in Indian secured retail lending is density. Pick a region — Tamil Nadu, Andhra, Gujarat, Rajasthan — and saturate it. Open branches four kilometres apart. Hire staff who grew up in that district and speak the language of the local trade. Over five or ten years you build something a spreadsheet cannot replicate: an institutional memory of which streets flood, which trades are cyclical, which surnames pay, what a shop on that road is really worth. Five-Star Business Finance built a ₹12,000-crore-odd book (approx.) doing exactly this across a handful of southern states. Aptus did it in Tamil Nadu and Andhra. Aavas did it in Rajasthan and its neighbours. Density is a genuine, defensible edge, and it is the standard answer to "how do you underwrite people with no documents?"

SBFC looked at that and saw a different picture: a portfolio where one bad monsoon, one state-level policy shock, one regional industry collapse, or one local political disruption hits a third of the book at once. Its founding design was therefore anti-concentration. Spread across states from the start. Do not let any single geography dominate the AUM. Accept that you will know each market less well than the local incumbent does, and buy insurance against correlated shocks with the difference.

Density buys you underwriting precision. Diversification buys you survivability. SBFC bought the second and now has to prove it did not overpay in the first.

The branch arithmetic — the number that gives the strategy away

Here is where it gets concrete, and where SBFC stops resembling its peers entirely. Divide the book by the branches:

Read that again. SBFC runs a book roughly the size of Five-Star's out of about a third of the branches, spread over three times as many states. Each SBFC branch carries around ₹45 crore of loans; for mature branches — the 60% that are more than three years old — the figure is above ₹66 crore. A density-model peer's branch carries a sixth of that.

That single ratio is SBFC's answer to the obvious objection. If you cannot have state-level depth, you concentrate depth at the branch level instead: fewer, larger, older branches, each of which has been sitting in its own micro-market for years accumulating exactly the local memory the density model produces — just in a pocket 30 kilometres wide rather than a state 300 kilometres wide. SBFC expands in hub-and-spoke clusters of five or six branches inside a 30-km radius, so a new branch is never a lone outpost; it is a spoke off a hub that already knows the terrain.

In plain terms

Two ways to know a market. One: put 150 branches in one state so the company collectively knows everything about that state. Two: put 17 branches in each of 15 states, but make each branch three times bigger and let it stay put for years, so the branch knows everything about its own 30 kilometres. SBFC picked the second. It gets you most of the local knowledge without betting the company on one state's weather, politics or dominant industry.

The cost of this choice is not zero and should not be waved away. A branch in a state where you have fourteen other branches has no institutional back-up when things go wrong — no regional collections muscle to redeploy, no political familiarity, no long relationship with local recovery agents and courts. Enforcement of a mortgage is a state-level legal process, and doing it in fifteen jurisdictions is genuinely harder than doing it in four. The efficiency arithmetic partially offsets this — operating costs fell 46 basis points to 3.93% of AUM in FY26 because big mature branches are cheap to run — but the underwriting question stands.

05

The gold book it bought

A fifth of the balance sheet, growing at 63%, and it did not cost a single new branch.

Most investors meet SBFC as a secured-MSME lender and are mildly surprised to find a gold-loan business inside it. The surprise deepens on inspection: SBFC did not organically build this franchise, and it is not running it out of a separate network.

The seed came with the 2019 acquisition of Karvy Financial Services (approx.), which brought an existing gold-loan portfolio and infrastructure. Since then SBFC has done something simple and quietly clever: rather than open dedicated gold branches the way Muthoot and Manappuram do, it has installed gold counters inside the branches it already operates. Roughly 220 of its 251 branches now do gold loans. The rent, the manager, the security, the vault and the electricity are already paid for by the MSME business.

In plain terms

This is why the gold book could grow 63% in a year without the cost line blowing out. The marginal cost of adding a gold counter to a branch that already exists is a vault, an assayer, and some working capital — a fraction of what a standalone gold-loan branch costs. It also explains part of the 46-basis-point drop in operating costs as a share of AUM: SBFC added a whole second revenue stream on top of fixed costs it was already carrying.

What gold does for the shape of the company

  • Liquidity. A three-to-twelve-month book against a collateral that can be auctioned in weeks is close to a liquid asset. For an NBFC — an institution that borrows short-ish and lends long, and therefore lives or dies by asset-liability matching — a self-liquidating fifth of the balance sheet is genuine structural comfort.
  • Velocity. The same rupee gets lent repeatedly through a year. Disbursement volumes in gold are far larger than the outstanding book, which flatters growth optics but also means the business generates fee and interest income at a much faster cadence than the MSME book does.
  • Customer overlap. The gold customer and the small-business customer are frequently the same household at different moments — jewellery for a short cash crunch, property for a long capital need. One branch, one relationship, two products across the borrower's life cycle.
  • A different risk profile. Gold-loan credit risk is really price risk and operational risk, not borrower risk. If gold prices fall sharply, LTVs rise and some borrowers walk away from the collateral. SBFC's answer is to hold portfolio LTV around 56–57% — a meaningful cushion.
The regulatory caveat. The RBI has repeatedly tightened its supervision of gold lending — on valuation and assaying practices, on auction transparency, on LTV monitoring, on the use of business correspondents for sourcing, and on end-use documentation. It has taken hard action against specific lenders for lapses. A gold book that is 21% of AUM and growing at 63% is a book growing into an actively supervised area, and process discipline here is not optional. SBFC's stated sub-60% LTV policy and in-branch (rather than agent-based) model both sit on the conservative side of where the regulator has been pushing, but this is a genuine ongoing compliance exposure.

There is a strategic reading of the gold book too. It is the part of SBFC that grows fastest, consumes the least capital per rupee of income, and can be scaled or shrunk quickly. In a year when the MSME environment turns difficult, the gold counter keeps the branch busy and the P&L moving. That optionality is worth something that no ratio on the fact sheet captures.

Three of those four moving parts went SBFC's way in FY26, and emphatically so. Borrowing cost fell 83 basis points as rates eased and the company's credit standing improved with scale and listed-company disclosure. Operating cost fell 69 basis points as branches matured and the gold counter loaded more income onto the same fixed base. In plain terms

The cost structure

Operating expenses ran at 3.93% of AUM in FY26 and management guides to a further 20–25 basis point reduction. Translated into the more familiar banking metric, the cost-to-income ratio works out to roughly 35% (approx., derived from reported ratios rather than reported directly). For a business that sends people to physically inspect shops and properties, that is efficient — and the reason is the branch arithmetic from the previous section. Big, mature, multi-product branches amortise their fixed cost over a much larger book.

07

Sourcing, underwriting, collections

Who finds the customer determines who owns the risk.

There is a single structural choice inside SBFC that explains more about its credit outcomes than any policy document: it sources its core business itself. No direct selling agents, no connectors, no third-party channel partners bringing in files for the flagship MSME book. Management has said it has no immediate plans to change this.

To see why this matters, consider what a DSA-driven model actually does. An agent is paid a commission on disbursement. The agent's incentive is volume, and the agent's knowledge of the customer's true business is whatever the agent chooses to pass on. Files arrive pre-packaged. In a good market this is a wonderfully cheap way to grow — you rent someone else's distribution and add branches slowly. In a bad market, you discover that your book was assembled by people with no stake in whether it repays. Almost every Indian NBFC credit accident of the last fifteen years has an intermediated-sourcing chapter in it.

The critical property of this chain: the person who said yes to the loan is in the same building as the person who has to collect it. That is a much shorter accountability loop than a commission-sourced model can produce.

The in-house model costs more up front — you carry the salespeople on your own payroll rather than paying a variable commission, which is precisely why an operating cost of 3.93% of AUM is respectable rather than merely low. What you buy for that money is that the loan officer, the branch manager and the collections team all belong to the same institution and all answer for the same file. In a segment where the credit decision rests on a human judgment about a business with no audited accounts, the identity of the human doing the judging is not a detail.

In plain terms

An agent paid per loan disbursed has finished earning the moment the money goes out. A branch employee whose branch's arrears are reviewed every month has not. SBFC pays more to keep sourcing inside the company so that the incentive to be right about a borrower survives past the disbursement date.

The one place it does partner

SBFC does use co-origination — arrangements where a bank funds a share of a loan that SBFC originates and services — and guides to this running at about 18–19% of AUM. This is a different animal from DSA sourcing: SBFC still finds and underwrites the customer, but shares the balance sheet. It lowers the capital and funding needed per rupee lent and improves return on equity, at the cost of sharing the economics. It is the sensible use of a partner — for funding, not for judgment.

Scale and the human problem

The structural challenge in a business like this is that its underwriting quality is embodied in people, and people have to be recruited and trained faster than the book grows. SBFC added 21 branches in the March 2026 quarter alone and guides to stabilising around 275. Every one of those branches needs a manager who can look at a fabric shop and estimate its monthly surplus within 15%. That skill takes years to build and cannot be bought in a hurry. Watching whether credit cost holds as the network grows is, in effect, watching whether the training pipeline is keeping up with the branch pipeline.

08

Funding & the unused balance sheet

A 4.57% return on assets that produces only a 14% return on equity — and why that is a choice.

Here is the counter-intuitive fact about SBFC that most first-time readers miss. It earns a return on assets that any bank in India would envy — 4.57% — and converts it into a return on equity of only about 14%. A bank earning 1.8% on assets routinely converts that into 16–18% on equity. What gives?

Leverage. SBFC runs at 1.9× debt-to-equity with a capital adequacy ratio of 32.8%, against a regulatory minimum in the mid-teens. It is carrying roughly twice the capital its balance sheet requires. Tangible net worth was ₹3,465 crore at March 2026 supporting ₹11,270 crore of assets. A conventionally geared NBFC with the same book would run at 4–5× and, holding the asset returns constant, would report an ROE in the low-to-mid twenties.

In plain terms

Return on equity is return on assets multiplied by how much you borrow. SBFC's asset returns are excellent; its borrowing is deliberately modest. The 14% ROE is not evidence of a mediocre business — it is evidence of an over-capitalised one. This is the single most common misreading of the stock, and it cuts both ways: the ROE will rise mechanically as leverage normalises, but it also means shareholders are currently earning a bank-like return on money that is sitting idle.

Management's stated position is that existing capital supports growth to roughly ₹18,000–19,000 crore of AUM — around 60–70% above the current book, or roughly two years of growth at the guided 5–7% per quarter — before it needs to return to the market. That is a specific, checkable claim and it sets up a specific expectation: ROE should grind upward through FY27 and FY28 purely from gearing, without a single basis point of improvement in yields, costs or credit. If it does not, something else has gone wrong.

Where the money comes from

An NBFC has no deposits. Every rupee it lends is a rupee it borrowed — from banks (term loans and working-capital lines), from debt markets (non-convertible debentures), from development institutions and refinance bodies, and through securitisation and direct assignment of pools of loans it has already made. SBFC's borrowing cost fell to 8.52% in FY26, down 83 basis points, reflecting both easier system liquidity and the improving credit view of the company (it carries investment-grade ratings from multiple agencies, reaffirmed through FY26).

This is also the structural fragility of any NBFC, and it deserves to be stated plainly rather than buried. SBFC's spread depends on borrowing at 8.5%. If system liquidity tightens, if the rate cycle turns, or if sentiment towards NBFC credit sours for reasons that have nothing to do with SBFC, the funding cost moves first and the lending rate follows only with a lag — because the existing book is largely already priced. A 100-basis-point move in funding costs is worth roughly a fifth of the current spread. High capital adequacy and a self-liquidating gold book are precisely the shock absorbers you want against that, and SBFC has more of both than most.

09

The rivals

A crowded niche where everyone has discovered the same 9% spread.

The uncomfortable truth about secured-MSME lending is that it is no longer a secret. A decade ago the wide spreads reflected genuine scarcity of anyone willing to do the work. Today the segment is contested from four directions at once.

Two dynamics inside that table deserve emphasis. The first is graduation risk. SBFC's best outcome for a customer — the business grows, the credit record builds, the borrower becomes formally bankable — is also the moment a bank can offer that customer 11% and take them away. Success in this business creates its own attrition. It is why behavioural loan life is four to five years against a stated tenor of eight to fifteen, and why AUM growth requires constantly replacing customers who did well.

The second is that the FY26 yield of 17.61% held, rising 9 basis points, in a year of intensifying competition. That is arguably a more important data point about pricing power than any market-share statistic. Yields in a crowding niche usually leak. SBFC's did not.

In plain terms

The competitive question is not "will someone else lend to shopkeepers?" — dozens will. It is "can anyone reach this specific borrower more cheaply than SBFC?" Because the cost is not capital, it is the field visit. A bank with 6% funding still needs a person to go and count the stock in a shop in a district it has no branch in. Until that changes, the lender with the branch nearby and the trained officer inside it holds the position — regardless of who has cheaper money.

For lenders, price-to-book is the honest yardstick, because a lender's earnings are a function of how much equity it chooses to put to work. At 2.8× book on a 14% ROE, SBFC is not cheap on the face of it — 2.8× book usually implies a high-teens or better sustainable return. The bridge between the two is the leverage argument from section 08: the same book at 3.5–4× gearing produces a materially higher ROE without any operational improvement, and management has told the market it has two years of growth funded before it needs equity.

In plain terms

Why price-to-book rather than price-to-earnings for a lender? Because a lender's profit is not produced by a factory whose output is fixed — it is produced by equity, multiplied by however much the lender chooses to borrow against it. Two identical loan books can report very different earnings depending only on gearing. Book value is the raw material; the multiple you pay on it is the market's verdict on how well the company converts it into return. At 2.8× book, the market is paying ₹2.80 for every ₹1 of SBFC's capital — and it will only get that back if the return on that capital moves well above today's 14%.

Set against that, the stock has spent the last twelve months between ₹79.6 and ₹123 — a 55% range on a company whose fundamentals moved in one direction all year. That volatility is partly the private-equity overhang and partly the market's genuine uncertainty about which of the two stories below is true.

The number that settles it

Credit cost — 1.38% of AUM in FY26, up roughly 30 basis points year on year. This is the metric to watch, and it is specific to SBFC's particular bet. Every other line in FY26 improved for reasons that were partly environmental: funding got cheaper because rates eased, opex fell because branches aged. Credit cost is the only line that measures whether SBFC can underwrite an informal-income borrower without the regional density that its peers rely on. If credit cost holds near 1.3–1.5% while AUM compounds at 25%-plus and the branch count climbs toward 275, the anti-density thesis is proved and the ROE will rise on gearing alone. If it drifts toward 2%+ as the network spreads, the market will conclude that the geographic diversification bought shock-resistance at the price of underwriting depth — and 2.8× book will not survive that conclusion.

Note what this framing rules out. The case for SBFC is not a margin-expansion story: the spread is already 9.09% and yields have nowhere obvious to go but sideways. It is not an operating-leverage story either: at 3.93% opex against management guidance of a further 20–25 basis points, most of that gain is banked. Growth is well telegraphed. What is genuinely unresolved is the loss line — and it is unresolved precisely because the strategy that produces it has never been run at scale through a downturn in this market.

11

Future, risks, bull/bear

What has to happen, what could break, and where the two views actually diverge.

Management's FY27 guidance is unusually concrete, which is helpful: AUM growth of 5–7% per quarter (roughly 22–31% annualised), branch count stabilising around 275, operating cost down a further 20–25 basis points, co-origination at 18–19% of AUM, and gold LTV held below 60%. None of that requires a new market, a new product or a new funding structure. It is the same machine, run harder.

The risks, stated properly

  • The borrower is economically fragile. A shopkeeper with ₹40 lakh of annual turnover has no buffer. A demonetisation, a GST transition, a local lockdown, a sharp input-cost move or a bad festival season translates into missed EMIs within a quarter. This segment does not degrade gracefully; it degrades together. The property collateral limits the ultimate loss, not the disruption.
  • The book is young and has never been stress-tested at this size. AUM was ₹3,192 crore in FY22 and is ₹11,270 crore now. Covid hit a book a fraction of today's. A large share of the loans on the balance sheet were written in the last thirty-six months and have not yet passed through the years when defaults usually surface. Fast-growing loan books flatter their own ratios: the denominator grows faster than problems can emerge.
  • Diversification cuts both ways. Fifteen states means fifteen sets of local property markets, courts, enforcement practices and political environments — and fewer branches in each to build depth. The very design that immunises SBFC against a single-state shock also denies it the local underwriting advantage that its listed peers cite as their moat.
  • Competition is intensifying from four directions — the density-model specialists, affordable housing financiers reaching down, banks and small-finance banks reaching down-market with cheaper funding, and dedicated gold-loan networks. Yields held in FY26; there is no guarantee they hold through a slower credit cycle when everyone chases the same borrower.
  • Funding is the structural vulnerability of any NBFC. No deposits, borrowing at 8.52%, a book largely priced already. A liquidity squeeze or an NBFC-sector credit scare compresses the spread from the wrong end. High capital adequacy and a liquid gold book are the mitigants, and they are better than most peers carry — but they are mitigants, not immunity.
  • Regulation is live, particularly on gold. The RBI has been actively tightening gold-lending practice — valuation, auction conduct, LTV monitoring, sourcing channels — and has acted against specific lenders. A fifth of SBFC's book and a disproportionate share of its growth sit in that supervised area.
  • The private-equity register is an overhang. 52.31% sits with sponsors whose business model is eventual exit, in a company that says it does not need capital for two years. Block supply is a recurring feature of the stock, not an anomaly.
  • Valuation leaves the debate open. 2.8× book on a 14% ROE is a bet that the ROE goes meaningfully higher. That is a reasonable bet given the leverage headroom — but it is a bet, and the FY26 credit-cost drift is the first small piece of evidence against it.
In plain terms

The two failure modes are different in kind, and it is worth separating them. One is a demand failure: small businesses stop borrowing, growth slows, the branch network sits underused. That is painful but recoverable — the capital is intact and the branches wait. The other is a credit failure: the loans already written turn out to have been underwritten on optimistic estimates of what those shops really earned. That one is not recoverable quickly, because the evidence arrives eighteen months after the mistake was made and by then the book has doubled. Growth risk and credit risk are not the same risk, and only the second one is existential here.

The case for

  • A structurally under-served market. Crores of small businesses still borrow informally at 2–3% a month. SBFC's 17.6% is a bargain to that customer, and the addressable pool is measured in lakhs of crores.
  • Growth is internal, not speculative. Only ~30% district penetration inside the 15 states already occupied. Expansion means filling in known territory off existing hubs, not gambling on new geographies.
  • Two years of growth already funded. 32.8% capital adequacy, 1.9× gearing, AUM headroom to ₹18,000–19,000 crore without a raise. ROE should rise on gearing alone.
  • Asset quality is improving as it scales. GNPA down 13 bps to 2.61%; ECL cushion at 2.1× the regulatory minimum. The book is not being grown by loosening standards.
  • Costs are still falling. Opex down 69 bps to 3.93%, funding down 83 bps to 8.52%; big mature branches carrying two products over one fixed-cost base.
  • The gold book is a genuine second engine — 63% growth, liquid, capital-light, bolted onto branches that already exist.
  • Pricing power is holding. Yield rose 9 bps in a year the competition intensified.

The case against

  • Credit cost went the wrong way. Up ~30 bps to 1.38% in a year when every other line improved — and it is the only line that measures the thing the strategy is betting on.
  • Untested through a real downturn at this size. The book has more than tripled since FY22 and most of it was written recently. Seasoning has not happened yet.
  • No density means no local moat. Fifteen states, ~17 branches each. Against a Five-Star that has 750 branches concentrated in five, SBFC has traded away the specific advantage this asset class was built on.
  • Success creates attrition. Every customer who prospers becomes bankable and gets refinanced away at 11%. Growth requires perpetual replacement.
  • Gold is a regulated, contested arena where Muthoot and Manappuram have decades of process and thousands of branches, and where the RBI is actively tightening.
  • Sponsor supply. A 52% private-equity holding in a company that does not need capital is future selling pressure.
  • 2.8× book on 14% ROE requires the leverage-driven ROE improvement to arrive on schedule and the loss line to behave while it does.

The bottom line

SBFC took the one decision in Indian secured retail lending that nobody else took: it refused to have a home state. Every listed comparable in this niche built its underwriting edge by knowing one region better than anyone; SBFC concluded that knowing one region very well is also how you lose a third of your book in a single bad year, and it built fifteen shallow presences instead of four deep ones — then compensated by making each branch three times larger and letting it sit in its 30-kilometre patch long enough to grow its own local memory. FY26 said that trade is working: ₹11,270 crore of assets, a 9.09% spread, a gross NPA that fell while the book grew 29%, and profits up 31%.

The next two years will say whether it keeps working, and there is a specific gate to watch it through. At 5–7% AUM growth per quarter the book reaches roughly ₹14,000 crore by March 2027 and bumps against the ₹18,000–19,000 crore capital ceiling sometime in FY28. Somewhere in that stretch, SBFC will come back to the market for equity. The credit-cost number it carries into that conversation — whether the 1.38% of FY26 has settled back toward 1.2% or drifted past 1.6% — will determine whether it raises at a premium to today's 2.8× book or a discount to it. Fifteen states' worth of branch managers, each estimating what a fabric shop really earns, will decide which.
