00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

ACME Solar Holdings builds, owns and operates large-scale renewable-energy power plants — utility-scale solar farms, and increasingly wind, hybrid (solar-plus-wind) and battery-backed "firm power" projects — and sells the electricity they generate under long-term, ~25-year power-purchase agreements (PPAs) to government off-takers at a fixed price per unit. It is what the industry calls an Independent Power Producer, or IPP: it doesn't sell panels or build for others, it owns the assets and collects the power bill for decades. Listed only in November 2024, it is one of India's larger pure-play renewable IPPs.

Three facts frame everything:

  • An annuity business wearing an infrastructure balance sheet. ACME had 2,990 MW operational at end-FY26 and a total contracted portfolio of ~8,071 MW (operational + under construction), with ~5,081 MW still being built. Because sunlight and wind are free, once a plant is built almost all its revenue drops through as profit — FY26 EBITDA margin was around 90%. But building those plants is enormously capital-hungry, so the company carries heavy project debt (~₹12,800 Cr net).
  • Growth you can see years in advance. Revenue jumped ~59% to ₹2,507 Cr in FY26 and PAT nearly doubled to ₹498 Cr, purely because more contracted megawatts switched on. Management targets scaling the fleet from ~2.9 GW to ~7 GW operational by FY28 — the pipeline is already won and financed, so the near-term growth is unusually visible.
  • The debate is leverage vs. annuity. Bulls see a decades-long, inflation-insensitive, government-contracted green-infrastructure cash machine riding India's ~500 GW clean-energy build-out. Bears see a highly-leveraged, execution-heavy developer exposed to interest rates, grid delays and the shaky finances of state electricity distribution companies. Both are looking at the same company.
In plain terms

Think of ACME as a company that builds a giant power plant, signs a 25-year rental contract for its electricity before laying a single panel, borrows most of the money to build it, and then just collects a fixed cheque every month for a quarter-century. The sun and wind cost nothing, so running the plant is cheap — the whole game is (1) borrowing to build it, (2) making sure it actually gets built on time and connected to the grid, and (3) making sure the buyer pays on time. Get those right and you own a bond-like income stream with a green label. Get them wrong and the interest bill eats you alive. Everything that follows is an unpacking of those three risks and the enormous tailwind pushing against them.

01

Renewable-IPP economics 101: how a solar plant earns money

No engineering degree required — just the handful of numbers that drive the whole model.

A renewable IPP is, at heart, a capital-recovery machine. It spends a large lump of money upfront to build a plant, then earns it back slowly over 20–25 years by selling the electricity at a pre-agreed price. Because the "fuel" (sunlight, wind) is free and running costs are tiny, the economics are unusually simple — and unusually dependent on two things: how much power the plant produces, and how cheaply the company borrowed to build it.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and ACME's number)
Capacity (MW / GW)The nameplate size of the plant — how much power it can produce at full tilt. ACME: 2,990 MW operational, ~8,071 MW total portfolio (1,000 MW = 1 GW).
PLF (plant load factor)What fraction of that nameplate a plant actually delivers over a year — the "capacity utilisation" of a power plant. Solar is naturally low (the sun sets): ACME's solar PLF runs ~23–24%. Wind and hybrid are higher; storage lifts the useful output.
Tariff (₹/unit)The fixed price the off-taker pays per unit (kWh) of electricity, locked in for the whole PPA. ACME's projects span roughly ₹2.5–6.3/unit depending on type (plain solar is cheapest; firm/peak power earns most).
PPA (power-purchase agreement)The ~25-year contract that fixes who buys the power and at what price. This is the asset — it turns a pile of panels into a predictable, decades-long income stream.
Generation (MU)Actual electricity sold, in million units. ACME generated ~6,464 MU in FY26 (+61%). Revenue ≈ generation × tariff.
The simple revenue equation

Roughly: Revenue ≈ Capacity × PLF × 8,760 hours × Tariff. Notice what's missing: fuel cost. A thermal plant burns coal or gas it must buy every day; a solar plant's input is sunlight, which is free forever. That single fact is why renewable IPPs earn ~90% EBITDA margins — almost the entire tariff, minus a little for operations and maintenance, is gross profit. The catch is that ACME has to build the plant before it earns a rupee, and it borrows most of that money — so the real "cost of goods" for a solar plant isn't fuel, it's interest and depreciation, which sit below the EBITDA line. That's the crucial mental shift: renewables move the cost from the fuel bill to the finance bill.

Why the model is called "annuity-like"

  • The price is fixed for 25 years. Once the PPA is signed, the tariff generally doesn't change — so the revenue per unit is known decades out, insensitive to inflation or power-market swings. That predictability is exactly why these assets are compared to bonds.
  • The buyer is usually a government entity. Most PPAs are with central agencies like SECI (Solar Energy Corporation of India) or NTPC, or with state distribution companies — so counterparty credit is (mostly) sovereign-flavoured, even if payment timing can wobble.
  • The output is near-guaranteed by physics. The sun rises on a schedule; a well-sited plant's annual generation is statistically predictable (developers quote a "P90" — the output level exceeded 90% of years). ACME's actual solar PLF has tracked close to its P90, a sign the plants perform as modelled.
02

The business model: build it, then collect the annuity

Five steps turn a competitive auction into a decade of cash flow — read this once and the whole company clicks.

ACME's entire operation is a repeating five-step cycle: win a contract, sign the PPA, finance and build the plant, switch it on, then collect the tariff for 25 years. Each new project is another slice of contracted cash flow bolted onto the fleet. Understanding this pipeline is understanding the business.

The value is created at Steps 1–2 (a won, signed PPA) but the cash only arrives from Step 4 onward — so the whole model is a bet on flawlessly executing Step 3, funded by debt, again and again.

The three-bucket portfolio — where visibility comes from

Analysts track a renewable IPP by splitting its megawatts into three buckets, each telling you a different thing:

  • Operational — plants already switched on and earning. This is today's revenue. ACME: 2,990 MW.
  • Under construction — contracted, financed, being built. This is tomorrow's revenue, largely locked. ACME: ~5,081 MW.
  • Awarded / contracted (pre-construction) — won in auction, PPA signed or being signed, not yet started. This is the growth pipeline. ACME's total portfolio of ~8,071 MW, of which ~6,570 MW already has PPAs signed.
Why "asset-heavy and leveraged" is the whole point

Unlike an asset-light services firm, a renewable IPP wants to own lots of expensive assets — the assets are the annuity. Building a gigawatt of solar costs thousands of crores, and the standard way to fund it is project finance: borrow ~70–80% of each project's cost against that project's own future PPA cash flows, and put in ~20–30% equity. The debt is deliberate and structural, not a sign of distress — the fixed 25-year tariff is exactly the kind of predictable cash flow lenders love to lend against. The flip side, covered in section 08, is that this makes the equity value acutely sensitive to interest rates and refinancing: when your revenue is fixed but your biggest cost is interest, the direction of rates matters enormously.

Why the mix shift matters more than the megawatts

A gigawatt of plain solar and a gigawatt of firm, storage-backed power look identical on a capacity chart — but they are very different businesses. Plain solar sells cheap, intermittent daytime electrons; firm power sells reliability, which the grid is increasingly desperate for and willing to pay up for. As ACME tilts its new wins toward FDRE and hybrids, its average tariff and contract quality rise even if the headline MW growth looks the same. Watch the mix, not just the total — a portfolio getting "firmer" is quietly getting more valuable per megawatt.

05

From plain solar to firm power: the FDRE shift

The single most important strategic change in Indian renewables — and ACME is positioning right in it.

For a decade, Indian solar had one problem it couldn't shake: it only works when the sun shines. A grid can't run on power that vanishes every evening exactly as households switch on lights and cook dinner. The industry's answer — and the theme that now defines the whole sector's next chapter — is the shift from plain, "must-run" solar to Firm & Dispatchable Renewable Energy (FDRE): projects that combine solar, wind and batteries to deliver reliable, on-demand, round-the-clock green power. This is the growth story sitting on top of the growth story.

The intermittency problem, and how firm power solves it

  • Plain solar generates a fat midday peak and nothing at night — great for daytime load, useless for the evening demand peak. Grids can only absorb so much of it before it becomes a nuisance.
  • Hybrids (solar + wind at one point) help, because wind often blows when the sun doesn't — but they're still weather-dependent.
  • FDRE / round-the-clock (RTC) adds battery storage: store the cheap midday solar, discharge it in the evening peak, and contractually guarantee a minimum availability. The buyer gets something close to a conventional dispatchable plant — but green.
Why FDRE earns better economics

A plain-solar PPA might clear at ~₹2.5–3.0/unit. A firm, storage-backed peak-power contract clears far higher — ACME signed a 300 MW / 1,200 MWh assured peak-power project with SECI at ₹6.28/unit, and firm/hybrid awards in the ₹4.4–4.7/unit range. The buyer happily pays roughly double because they're buying reliability, not just electrons. So FDRE does two things at once: it wins the contracts that plain solar increasingly can't (grids now auction firm power preferentially), and it earns a materially higher tariff per megawatt. For a developer that can execute the added complexity of integrating storage, it's the most attractive segment in the market — and ACME has made it a strategic priority, targeting roughly half its capacity in FDRE and ~10 GWh of operational batteries over the next couple of years.

06

The PPA and off-taker book

Who buys the power, at what price, for how long — and where the payment risk hides.

An IPP's revenue quality is only as good as its off-takers — the entities that sign the PPAs and pay the bills. ACME's book is anchored to central, high-rated counterparties, which is a genuine strength, but the sector's perennial weak spot is the financial health of the state distribution companies (discoms) that ultimately consume much of the power.

The off-taker spectrum (best to worst credit)
Off-taker typeWhoPayment risk
Central agenciesSECI (Solar Energy Corp of India), NTPC — AAA-rated central intermediaries that sign the PPA and on-sell to statesLow. They act as a payment-security buffer between the developer and weaker states. The bulk of ACME's marquee contracts sit here.
State discoms (direct)State electricity distribution utilities buying directlyHigher. Many discoms are loss-making and chronically slow to pay — the sector's single biggest recurring headache.
Commercial & industrial (C&I)Corporates buying green power directly (open access)Variable, but growing; better tariffs, more counterparty diligence needed. A smaller slice for ACME today.

Recent contract wins — the pipeline in action

  • 300 MW / 1,200 MWh assured peak power — 25-year PPA with SECI at ₹6.28/unit; ISTS-connected. A flagship firm-power win.
  • 190 MW wind-solar hybrid — PPA with SECI at a fixed ₹4.72/unit.
  • 50 MW FDRE — awarded by Tata Power at ₹4.43/kWh, showing traction beyond central agencies.
  • 301 MW SECI award — added to the development pipeline during FY26, lifting the under-construction book past 5 GW.
Why the 25-year tenor is the real asset

Each of these contracts fixes a price for a quarter of a century. That's what converts a construction project into an annuity: ACME knows, today, roughly what a plant commissioned in 2027 will earn every year until the early 2050s. That visibility is what lets it borrow heavily (lenders can size debt against a known cash flow), and what lets analysts value the equity as a discounted stream of contracted cash flows. The risk isn't the price — that's locked. The risk is whether the counterparty pays on time (discom delays can stretch receivables and strangle cash flow) and whether the grid actually takes the power (curtailment — being told to switch off when the grid is congested — is a real, if usually minor, drag). The SECI/NTPC buffer is precisely designed to blunt the first risk.

07

The 500 GW tailwind: India's energy transition

The structural force behind every renewable IPP — a decade-long, government-mandated build-out of historic scale.

ACME doesn't operate in a normal market; it operates inside one of the largest energy build-outs in human history. India has set a target of ~500 GW of non-fossil power capacity by 2030 — up from ~175 GW of renewables targeted for 2022 — and aims for half of all installed capacity to be non-fossil by decade's end. For a company that builds and owns renewable plants, that is a demand tailwind measured in hundreds of gigawatts of new auctions. This is the reason the whole sector exists at its current scale.

Roughly 300 GW of new non-fossil capacity is targeted this decade; clean-power auctions hit a record ~59 GW in 2024 alone (2.3× the prior year). The auction machine that feeds developers like ACME is running hot.

What the transition means for a developer like ACME

  • A near-bottomless auction pipeline. To hit 500 GW, agencies must keep auctioning tens of gigawatts a year — a steady conveyor of new PPAs for developers who can execute.
  • A shift toward firm power. As the grid saturates with daytime solar, new auctions increasingly demand hybrids, storage and FDRE (section 05) — playing to where ACME is pivoting.
  • Falling technology costs. Module and battery prices have fallen for years, improving project returns — though this cuts both ways (see the module-cost risk in section 11).
The honest caveat — a build-out with bottlenecks: the target is enormous but the plumbing struggles to keep up. Transmission (grid connectivity) lags project readiness; land acquisition is slow; and — tellingly — as of late 2024 roughly 9–40 GW of auctioned renewable capacity sat "stranded" for want of signed off-take agreements, because financially-stressed discoms were slow to commit to buying the power. The tailwind is real and huge; it is not frictionless. A developer's edge is executing through these bottlenecks — securing land, grid and PPAs — faster than rivals.
08

Leverage and the cost of money

The defining risk of every renewable IPP — when your revenue is fixed, your interest bill decides your profit.

Here is the uncomfortable truth beneath the ~90% EBITDA margin: a renewable IPP is a leveraged infrastructure play, and for a leveraged business with fixed revenue, the interest bill is the swing factor between a great investment and a poor one. ACME funds its plants ~70–80% with debt, so its net debt has climbed steeply as it builds — to roughly ₹12,800 Cr at end-FY26, up sharply from the prior year as the ~5 GW construction book ramped. Understanding this is understanding the real risk.

Why interest rates matter more here than almost anywhere

In a normal company, if costs rise you can try to raise prices. An IPP can't — its tariff is locked for 25 years. So its revenue is fixed, but a big chunk of its cost (interest) is not fixed forever: project debt gets refinanced over the asset's life, and new projects are funded at whatever rates prevail. That makes the equity value unusually sensitive to interest rates. Falling rates are a powerful tailwind — the same fixed cash flow, financed more cheaply, throws off more equity return, and existing debt can be refinanced lower. Rising rates do the reverse and can squeeze projects bid at thin margins. This is why renewable-IPP investors watch the rate cycle as closely as they watch megawatts.

The offsetting comforts

  • The debt is matched to the asset. Long-dated project debt against 25-year contracted cash flows is a natural fit — lenders are comfortable, and the cash flow to service it is contractual, not cyclical.
  • The IPO deleveraged deliberately. ACME used the bulk of its ₹2,395 Cr fresh IPO proceeds to repay subsidiary debt, improving the credit profile ahead of the build-out — a signal that leverage is actively managed, not ignored.
  • Cash PAT dwarfs reported PAT. Because depreciation is a large, non-cash charge, the cash the business throws off is far higher than net profit — supporting debt service and reinvestment. (FY25 cash PAT more than doubled to ~₹559 Cr.)
The bottom line on leverage: the debt is structural and largely sensible — but it is large and rising, and it makes ACME a geared bet. A stumble in execution, a spike in rates, or a stretch in discom receivables hits the equity harder than it would a debt-free company. This is the price of the annuity model, and it is the number-one thing a cautious investor stress-tests.

How to think about the valuation

At ~₹26,900 Cr market cap on ~₹498 Cr of FY26 profit, ACME trades on a high trailing P/E (roughly the mid-50s×) — but P/E is the wrong primary lens for a leveraged, fast-growing IPP, because it ignores the debt and the not-yet-earning pipeline. The market values these companies on EV/EBITDA (enterprise value — equity plus net debt — over EBITDA, ~16–17× trailing) and on a DCF of contracted cash flows (discounting the 25-year PPA annuities of the whole ~8 GW portfolio). On a forward basis — against the ~₹5,900 Cr revenue / ~₹950 Cr PAT the company targets for FY28 — the multiples compress sharply, which is the bull's core argument: you're paying a full price today for capacity that is largely built or funded and will roughly double the earnings base. The bear's retort: that only works if the pipeline commissions on time, rates behave, and discoms pay — and a geared stock priced for perfect execution de-rates hard if any of those slip.

A word on the numbers: FY26 figures (revenue ₹2,507 Cr, EBITDA ₹2,265 Cr, PAT ₹498 Cr, operational 2,990 MW, net debt ~₹12,800 Cr) are drawn from ACME's FY26 results and investor materials and cross-checked against multiple market sources. The FY28 targets (~7 GW, ~₹5,900 Cr revenue, ~₹950 Cr PAT) are management guidance and analyst estimates, not committed results — long-dated pipeline and capacity targets are inherently uncertain and should be treated as directional.

The green-hydrogen optionality — real, but keep it in its box

The wider ACME Group has genuine green-hydrogen and green-ammonia ambitions — an early integrated green-ammonia plant at Bikaner, large projects planned in Oman and India, and a stated goal of a ~10-million-tonne-per-annum green-ammonia/hydrogen-equivalent portfolio by 2032. If any of it is injected into the listed entity, that changes the calculus; until then, the listco is a renewable IPP first.

The next few years are less about winning new auctions and more about pouring concrete on the ones already won — that's where the contracted growth turns into cash.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A contracted green-infrastructure annuity — geared, execution-heavy, and priced for the pipeline to land.

ACME Solar is one of the more direct listed ways to own India's renewable build-out: a pure-play IPP with a large, contracted, multi-gigawatt pipeline and a strategic tilt toward the firm power the grid increasingly needs. The investment debate is a clean one — a long-duration, government-contracted, annuity-like green-infrastructure cash flow versus a leveraged, execution-dependent, discom-exposed developer priced for flawless delivery.

The bull case

  • Contracted annuity — ~8 GW portfolio on ~25-year PPAs; ~90% EBITDA margins; largely inflation-insensitive, bond-like cash flows.
  • Visible growth — ~5 GW under construction already funded; ~7 GW operational target by FY28 roughly doubles the earnings base.
  • Firm-power tilt — early, scaled positioning in FDRE / storage, the highest-tariff, fastest-growing segment.
  • Mega-tailwind — India's ~500 GW-by-2030 target guarantees a deep auction pipeline for a decade.
  • Aligned promoter & deleveraging intent — ~83% founder-held; IPO proceeds used to cut debt; hydrogen optionality on top.

The bear case

  • Heavy leverage — ~₹12.8k Cr net debt and rising; equity value highly sensitive to interest rates and refinancing.
  • Execution risk — the ~5 GW build depends on land, transmission/grid connectivity and module supply landing on schedule.
  • Off-taker / discom risk — weak state discom finances mean payment delays; curtailment and stranded-capacity risk across the sector.
  • Module & policy — solar-module costs, import dependence and domestic-content (ALMM) rules can pressure project returns.
  • Full valuation & float — priced for the pipeline to land; a geared stock de-rates hard on any slip, and a large promoter stake is a future overhang.
The bottom line

ACME Solar is a leveraged bet on India's energy transition executed through a contracted-annuity business model. The revenue side is about as good as infrastructure gets — fixed prices, long tenors, quasi-sovereign buyers, a huge tailwind. The risk sits entirely on the funding and delivery side: heavy debt, a build-out that must land on time, and counterparties who don't always pay promptly. The story over the next two years is written in three numbers each quarter — operational MW added (is the pipeline commissioning?), interest cost / net debt (is the leverage staying serviceable?), and receivable days (are the discoms paying?). Get those right and the annuity compounds into the multiple; stumble, and the gearing that magnifies the upside magnifies the fall.
