Exide Industries, the battery cash cow betting on lithium

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Exide Industries makes batteries — the lead-acid kind that starts your car, backs up your home inverter, keeps a telecom tower alive and powers a train's lighting. It is, by a wide margin, India's largest and most trusted battery brand, with roughly 40% of the automotive battery market and a distribution network that reaches nearly every town in the country. It is also a ~₹17,000 Cr-revenue, zero-debt, dividend-paying business that throws off steady cash from a market it has dominated for decades.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The aftermarket is the real business. Exide sells to carmakers (OEM, factory-fit, thin margin), but the money is in replacement — every vehicle on the road needs a fresh battery every ~3–5 years, a vast, recurring, brand-driven annuity where Exide has pricing power. Add industrial batteries (home inverters, UPS, telecom, solar, railways) and you have a diversified, defensive cash machine earning an ~11% EBITDA margin and a core-business ROCE of ~29%.
  • Lithium is the existential question. As vehicles electrify, EVs use lithium-ion cells, not lead-acid starter batteries. Exide is answering with a bet-the-franchise move: a 12 GWh lithium-ion gigafactory in Bengaluru via its wholly owned arm Exide Energy Solutions (EESL), built with technology from China's SVOLT. It has already sunk ₹4,802 Cr into EESL and Phase-1 (6 GWh) is being commercialised around end-FY26. This is the whole debate.
  • A cash cow funding a moonshot. The old lead-acid business is a slow-growing, high-return cow; the lithium venture is a capital-hungry, loss-making, technology-risk-laden bet on the future. That gap shows in the numbers: standalone PAT ₹1,111 Cr versus consolidated PAT ₹860 Cr — the ~₹250 Cr difference is largely EESL's development losses. The stock trades on hope for the lithium option as much as on the steady cash.
In plain terms

Think of Exide as a company with two clocks running at once. One is a reliable old grandfather clock — the lead-acid battery business — that has kept perfect time for 75 years: it dominates a market where its brand is the product, and every few years it re-sells a battery to the same customer, forever. The other is a brand-new, very expensive digital clock it is still building in a lab — the lithium-ion gigafactory — that could either become the timekeeper of the electric age or turn out to be a costly experiment. The investment question is simple to state and hard to answer: is the old clock's cash enough to build the new one before the old one starts losing time?

01

Battery economics 101: how a battery maker earns

No chemistry degree required — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

A battery company is, at heart, a manufacturer of a consumable, wearing part. Unlike a car or a fridge that a customer buys once a decade, a battery dies on a schedule — and the customer must buy another. That single fact — a product that reliably wears out and must be replaced — is what turns a commodity manufacturer into a business with an annuity attached. Three ideas explain almost everything.

The only ideas you need
IdeaWhat it means (and why it matters for Exide)
Lead-acid batteryA rechargeable battery using lead plates in sulphuric acid — cheap, robust, recyclable, and the standard for starting engines and backing up power. Heavy and low energy-density, but proven and inexpensive. This is Exide's core product.
OEM vs aftermarketOEM = fitted at the factory into a new vehicle (won on price, thin margin, but the entry ticket). Aftermarket/replacement = the customer buys a fresh battery when the old one fails, choosing on brand and availability — high margin. The split between these two is the single biggest driver of profitability.
The replacement cycleAn automotive battery lasts roughly 3–5 years. Every battery Exide (or anyone) ever sold as OEM becomes, years later, a replacement sale — and the buyer overwhelmingly reaches for the brand they trust. The installed base of vehicles is therefore a forward order book for replacement demand.
The flywheel that makes this a great business

Here is the mechanism the whole investment case rests on. Exide wins a carmaker's OEM contract at wafer-thin margins — it barely makes money on that sale. But that battery, stamped "Exide", rides around in the customer's car for ~4 years building brand familiarity. When it dies, the owner walks into a shop and asks for "an Exide" by name — and that replacement sale, made through Exide's own dealer network, earns a fat margin. Multiply by tens of millions of vehicles on Indian roads, and the aftermarket becomes a huge, recurring, price-inelastic river of profit. OEM is the loss-leader that seeds the aftermarket annuity. Grow the installed base today, harvest the replacement cash for a decade.

Why lead-acid is not going away soon

  • Every internal-combustion vehicle still needs one. Even a petrol or diesel car that will never electrify needs a 12-volt lead-acid battery to start — and India's ICE fleet is enormous and still growing.
  • Even EVs carry a small lead-acid battery for their 12-volt auxiliary systems (lights, electronics), so the product doesn't vanish even in an all-electric world.
  • The replacement lag is a shock absorber. Because replacement demand reflects vehicles sold 3–5 years ago, the aftermarket keeps flowing long after new-vehicle mix shifts. A decline in lead-acid is a slow tide, not a cliff.
  • Industrial demand is structural — home inverters, UPS backup, telecom, railways and solar keep lead-acid volumes healthy regardless of what happens on the road.

Read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Exide sells the same core competence — designing and mass-producing reliable lead-acid batteries — into two large, quite different end-markets. Understanding how each behaves, and how OEM feeds the aftermarket, is the key to reading the company.

The OEM → aftermarket funnel

The two channels aren't independent — they form a pipeline. Winning a vehicle at the factory today plants the seed of a high-margin replacement harvest years later. The further down this funnel a rupee of revenue sits, the more profitable and defensible it is.

The magic is Stage 3–4: margin and brand loyalty compound on an installed base Exide has been building for decades. A new entrant can win a factory contract; it cannot conjure 75 years of trusted replacement demand.
Why the aftermarket is the crown jewel

OEM contracts are a grind: carmakers squeeze suppliers, so factory-fit batteries earn little. But the replacement market is a different world. When a battery dies, the customer is stranded and wants it fixed today, from a name they trust, at whatever the nearby dealer stocks. Price sensitivity collapses; brand and distribution win. That is why Exide (and its rival Amara Raja) can raise prices to pass on cost inflation in the aftermarket, and why the replacement channel carries far richer margins than OEM. The whole strategic point of chasing OEM share — even at a loss-leading margin — is to feed this annuity. It is the closest thing a manufacturing business gets to a subscription.

03

The company and who controls it

From a 1947 Bengal factory to a 75-year household name.

Exide is one of India's oldest continuously operating manufacturers. Its roots run back to the Chloride storage-battery lineage of the early 20th century; the Indian company was incorporated in 1947 and has sold batteries under the iconic "Exide" name for generations, becoming synonymous with the product itself.

Timeline

  • 1947
    Incorporated as Associated Battery Makers (Eastern) Ltd; first manufacturing unit set up at Shyamnagar, West Bengal — part of the Chloride storage-battery heritage.
  • 1972 → 1995
    Renamed Chloride India, then Exide Industries Limited (1995), consolidating around the "Exide" brand that had become a national byword for batteries.
  • 2000s
    Cements market leadership across automotive and industrial batteries; builds one of India's densest dealer/distribution networks; expands lead recycling for backward integration.
  • 2005–2021
    Owns a life-insurance business (Exide Life) alongside batteries — a diversification that long clouded how the market valued the core.
  • Jan 2022
    Sells 100% of Exide Life to HDFC Life for ₹6,687 Cr — clearing the insurance overhang and freeing capital to pivot toward lithium-ion.
  • 2022–2024
    Forms Exide Energy Solutions (EESL), signs a technology tie-up with China's SVOLT, and breaks ground on a 12 GWh lithium-ion gigafactory in Bengaluru. Signs EV-battery MoU with Hyundai & Kia (Apr 2024).
  • FY26
    Standalone revenue ₹17,269 Cr, PAT ₹1,111 Cr, ₹2 dividend, zero-debt core. Cumulative EESL investment reaches ₹4,802 Cr; Phase-1 (6 GWh) Li-ion line nears commercialisation.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the promoter group holds ~46% (≈45.99% as of early 2026) — a controlling, long-standing stake associated with the Rajan Raheja group interests. A stable, majority-anchored register.
  • Professional management: Exide is run by professional executives rather than a founder-operator; the lithium arm, EESL, is led by its own dedicated management team (MD & CEO Arun Mittal) reflecting how different a business cell-manufacturing is from lead-acid.
  • Institutional ownership: the balance of the register is held by domestic and foreign institutions and retail — Exide is a widely tracked, index-weight large-cap.
  • Balance sheet: the core lead-acid business is net-cash / zero-debt, funding both dividends and the enormous lithium capex largely from internal accruals plus monetisation of the HDFC Life stake.
04

Automotive batteries & the aftermarket engine

The larger of the two end-markets — and where the profit annuity lives.

Automotive is roughly 60% of Exide's revenue and spans everything with an engine: cars, SUVs, two-wheelers, commercial vehicles, tractors and off-road machinery. Within it, the crucial distinction is OEM versus replacement — and FY26 saw both firing, with auto OEM revenue growing over 25% year-on-year and the domestic 2-wheeler and 4-wheeler replacement markets hitting record quarterly revenues.

Why the aftermarket is a defensive fortress

Automotive demand looks cyclical — new-car sales rise and fall with the economy. But Exide's replacement revenue is far steadier, because it tracks the installed base of vehicles already on the road, not this year's showroom traffic. Even in a weak year for new-car sales, the tens of millions of existing vehicles keep needing fresh batteries on their own clock. This decoupling is why Exide's earnings are more resilient than a pure auto-parts supplier's — a big chunk of demand is a maintenance necessity, not a discretionary purchase. It is the single most attractive feature of the lead-acid business, and the reason it functions as a reliable cash cow.

05

Industrial batteries: power when the grid isn't there

A quieter ~35% of revenue that diversifies away from the car cycle.

The second engine has nothing to do with vehicles. Industrial batteries store energy for backup and infrastructure — anywhere a reliable power supply matters and the grid can't be trusted. In a country with patchy power and booming data, telecom and solar infrastructure, this is a structural growth market, and it makes Exide far more than an auto-parts play.

Why industrial matters strategically

Two reasons. First, diversification: industrial demand marches to a different drum than the auto cycle — home backup, telecom, solar and railways don't slump when car sales do, smoothing Exide's overall earnings. Second, and more subtly, industrial is Exide's natural on-ramp to the energy-storage future. The skills and customers in solar storage, grid backup and UPS are exactly where lithium-ion energy-storage systems (ESS) will grow — so the industrial franchise is both a cash contributor today and a strategic bridgehead for the lithium products EESL hopes to sell tomorrow. In FY26, home UPS and solar showed strong double-digit growth even as telecom stayed soft.

06

The moat: brand, distribution & the lead loop

Why a commodity product supports a durable, high-return franchise.

A lead-acid battery is, chemically, a commodity — so why does Exide earn a ~29% core return on capital and hold ~40% share? The answer is three reinforcing advantages that a new entrant simply cannot buy off the shelf: an iconic brand, an unmatched distribution network, and backward integration into lead recycling.

  • Brand as default choice. In the replacement aisle, where the buyer is stranded and time-pressed, "Exide" functions as the safe, trusted default — the way "Xerox" once meant photocopying. That brand equity, built over 75 years, is worth real pricing power precisely in the highest-margin channel.
  • Distribution density. Exide's network of thousands of dealers, sub-dealers and retail touchpoints reaches into small towns across India. When a battery dies, availability wins — and Exide is simply there, more places than anyone. Replicating that footprint would take a rival years and enormous cost.
  • Backward integration via lead recycling. Lead is ~65–70% of a battery's cost, and old batteries are ~99% recyclable. Through its subsidiary Chloride Metals, Exide runs lead recycling smelters (in West Bengal, Maharashtra and Karnataka), securing a cheaper, greener, more stable supply of its key raw material — an advantage in cost, ESG and supply security all at once.
The closed-loop that competitors envy

Here's a quietly powerful feature. A battery Exide sells is, at end of life, a battery Exide can buy back and recycle — recovering the lead to make a new one. Because dealers collect dead batteries at the point of the replacement sale, Exide's own aftermarket network becomes its raw-material collection network. That "urban mining" loop lowers input cost, insulates against volatile primary lead prices, and meets tightening environmental rules on hazardous lead disposal (which increasingly push scrap toward organised recyclers like Exide). Brand pulls the sale, distribution captures it, and recycling feeds the next one — a genuinely circular, self-reinforcing system.

Lead & raw materials: the margin see-saw

The number that makes margins wobble quarter to quarter — and how Exide manages it.

Because lead is the dominant input, Exide's gross margin is highly sensitive to commodity prices — lead on the London Metal Exchange, plus other inputs like sulphur, tin and separators. The story of any given quarter's margin is usually a story about raw-material cost versus how much Exide can pass through in price. FY26 was a live case study.

Despite sulphur prices surging roughly 5× and a ~₹150 Cr commodity headwind, calibrated price hikes let Exide hold — and slightly expand — its EBITDA margin in Q4 FY26. That is pass-through power in action.
How pass-through actually works — and its limits

When lead or sulphur spikes, Exide raises battery prices to protect margin. It can do this most easily in the aftermarket (brand-loyal, price-inelastic buyers) and least easily in OEM (carmakers resist and contracts lag). So there is always a timing gap: costs jump today, price hikes flow through over the next quarter or two, and margins dip in between before recovering. This is why Exide's margin see-saws around a mid-teens gross / low-double-digit EBITDA level rather than sitting still. The lesson for reading the company: a soft-margin quarter driven by lead prices is usually a timing problem, not a broken business — provided Exide retains the pricing power to catch up, which its brand and recycling loop generally give it.

The recycling hedge: in-house lead recycling (Chloride Metals) dampens exposure to primary LME lead swings — a structural advantage that partly explains why Exide can hold margins through commodity storms better than a smelter-less rival would.
08

The lithium-ion bet — the defining, existential question

The single force that will decide Exide's next 20 years. Read this section twice.

Everything above describes a fine, defensive cash cow. But over Exide hangs one enormous question: what happens to a lead-acid champion as the world electrifies? Electric vehicles don't run on lead-acid starter batteries — they run on lithium-ion cells, a completely different chemistry that Exide has never manufactured. If EVs take over and Exide stays a lead-acid company, its core traction battery market erodes over time. So Exide is making the biggest bet in its history: becoming a lithium-ion cell manufacturer itself.

Exide Energy Solutions (EESL) — the greenfield gigafactory

The vehicle for the bet is Exide Energy Solutions Ltd (EESL), a wholly owned subsidiary building a lithium-ion cell gigafactory in Bengaluru, Karnataka. This is not a tweak to the old business — it is a ground-up entry into one of the hardest, most capital-intensive manufacturing games on earth, cell-making, where global scale players like CATL and BYD dominate.

Why this is genuinely make-or-break

The bet cuts both ways, and both are existential. On the defensive side, if Exide doesn't build a lithium capability, it risks watching its automotive traction market slowly migrate to EV cell suppliers over the coming decades — becoming a shrinking legacy business. On the offensive side, India's EV and energy-storage battery demand is projected to explode — government estimates point to ~210 GWh of annual demand by 2030 against a tiny domestic production base today — and whoever builds credible local cell capacity captures a vast new market with import-substitution and PLI tailwinds. Exide is spending thousands of crores to make sure it is on the right side of the electrification wave rather than swept under it. Get it right and Exide adds a second growth engine bigger than the first; get it wrong and it has burned a decade of cash-cow profits on a factory that can't compete with Chinese scale.

The risks are as large as the prize

  • Execution & technology risk. Cell manufacturing is unforgiving — yields, quality and cost are brutally hard to master. Exide is a first-time cell maker leaning on a foreign technology partner; a smooth ramp is far from guaranteed.
  • The loss / investment phase. EESL is pre-profit and will drag consolidated earnings for years — visible in the ~₹250 Cr gap between standalone and consolidated FY26 PAT. Expect losses to deepen as the plant ramps before (hopefully) inflecting to profit.
  • Capex drain. Thousands of crores are flowing out the door into a business earning nothing yet, depressing consolidated returns and consuming the cash cow's dividends-in-waiting.
  • Competition & scale. Global giants (CATL, BYD) have decades of head start and colossal scale; domestic rivals (Amara Raja, Reliance, Ola) are building too. Being early in India doesn't guarantee winning.
  • Demand timing. If India's EV adoption is slower than hoped, the gigafactory's capacity could sit underutilised — the worst outcome for a fixed-cost-heavy plant.
The lead-acid business is the cash cow. The lithium gigafactory is what that cash is being spent to build. The entire equity story is whether the cow can fund the moonshot before disruption catches up.

The rivals and the cell race

A comfortable lead-acid duopoly — and a crowded, uncertain sprint to lithium.

In lead-acid, Exide's world is a cosy duopoly with one serious rival. In lithium-ion, the field is wide open and far more crowded — the same rival plus deep-pocketed new entrants and global cell giants. The competitive picture is really two different maps.

The Exide vs Amara Raja rivalry, decoded

For decades the Indian battery market has been an Exide–Amara Raja duopoly. Exide has the bigger brand, wider distribution and larger scale; Amara Raja (the Amaron brand) has often been the sharper operator, posting higher margins and returns. Now the rivalry has jumped chemistries: both are betting big on gigafactories — Exide's 12 GWh in Karnataka, Amara Raja's 16 GWh in Telangana — a combined ~₹16,000 Cr wager on lithium. The stakes: whoever executes cell-manufacturing better inherits the electric future; whoever stumbles risks ceding it to the other and to Reliance and the global giants. For an investor, the two lead-acid champions offer a similar core thesis with different execution track records — and the lithium race is the new battleground where the historical pecking order could be reset.

The insurance unwind & capital allocation

How Exide cleared an old overhang and redirected the capital into lithium.

For years, Exide's valuation carried a peculiar overhang: it wasn't just a battery company — it also owned a life-insurance business, Exide Life. Investors disliked the mismatch (a battery maker running an insurer), and it muddied how the market valued the core. In January 2022, Exide cut the knot: it sold 100% of Exide Life to HDFC Life for ₹6,687 Cr, taking part cash (₹726 Cr) and part in HDFC Life shares (a ~4%+ stake).

  • The overhang cleared. Selling the insurer let the market value Exide as what it is — a battery company — and handed it a war chest at exactly the moment it needed capital for lithium.
  • Capital redirected to EESL. The proceeds and the HDFC Life stake became a funding source for the gigafactory. Monetising the residual HDFC Life shares over time is a natural, low-risk piggy bank for the capital-hungry lithium build.
  • A cleaner story. Post-sale, the equity narrative simplified to two moving parts: a stable lead-acid cash cow and a high-optionality lithium venture — the sum-of-the-parts framing analysts now use.
Reading Exide as a sum-of-the-parts

Because the two businesses are so different, the cleanest way to think about Exide is sum-of-the-parts (SOTP): value the lead-acid cash cow on a normal earnings multiple (a stable, ~29%-ROCE, dividend-paying franchise), then add an option value for the lithium venture (mostly pre-revenue, worth whatever you believe its future EV/storage business becomes), and net off the capital being consumed. The honest tension is that today the market is asked to pay up-front for a lithium option that is still burning cash — the consolidated numbers look worse than the standalone precisely because EESL losses are dragging. Bulls value the option generously (a scarce, home-grown cell champion in a 210 GWh-by-2030 market); bears note you're funding a risky, unproven bet with a cow's cash and paying a premium for hope. Both are looking at the same two clocks — they just weight them differently.

How to think about the multiple

A slow-growing (mid-single-digit revenue), ~11%-margin lead-acid business would not normally command ~39× consolidated earnings on its own. The premium is the market paying for the lithium option — the possibility that EESL becomes a large, profitable cell business in a booming market. That means two things. First, a chunk of the price rests on hope, not current cash flow, so the stock can be volatile as EESL news (customer wins, ramp milestones, losses) flows in. Second, the downside cushion is the cow: the lead-acid franchise, net-cash and ~29% core ROCE, provides a floor of real earnings and dividends. The push-pull between a cheap-ish core and an expensive-ish option is exactly why reasonable people disagree on the stock — and why quarterly EESL progress and lead-acid margins are the two things to watch.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A dependable cash cow strapped to a high-stakes bet on the electric age.

Exide is two investments in one wrapper: a defensive, dominant, dividend-paying lead-acid franchise, and a speculative, capital-hungry lithium-ion venture. The core is low-risk; the venture is high-risk, high-reward. Whether the whole is attractive depends entirely on how you price the second clock.

The bull case

  • Cash-cow core — ~40% market share, ~29% core ROCE, zero debt, brand-driven aftermarket annuity that funds everything.
  • Pricing power — held and expanded margins through a 5× sulphur spike via calibrated price hikes; lead recycling hedges input cost.
  • Lithium optionality — an early, large, home-grown cell bet into a ~210 GWh-by-2030 market, with Hyundai/Kia already signed and PLI/import-substitution tailwinds.
  • Clean capital story — insurance overhang sold; HDFC Life stake a piggy bank; expansion self-funded.
  • Structural runway — home UPS, solar and industrial storage growing regardless of the auto cycle.

The bear case

  • Priced on hope — ~39× consolidated earnings on a slow-growing core; a lot rests on lithium succeeding.
  • Execution & technology risk — first-time cell maker leaning on a foreign partner; ramp, yield and cost are brutally hard.
  • Loss drag — EESL burns cash and depresses consolidated returns for years before any payoff.
  • Fierce competition — Amara Raja, Reliance, Ola and global giants (CATL, BYD) all chasing the same cells; being early ≠ winning.
  • Cyclical & commodity exposure — margins see-saw on lead/sulphur; OEM squeeze; slow EV adoption could strand gigafactory capacity.
The bottom line

Exide is a genuinely high-quality legacy franchise making a genuinely high-stakes pivot. The lead-acid business is a fortress — dominant, cash-generative, defended by brand, distribution and a recycling loop — and on its own would be a solid, if unexciting, compounder. Bolted onto it is a lithium-ion gigafactory that could be Exide's second act or its most expensive mistake. The next few years will be written in two numbers each quarter: core lead-acid margin (is the cow still healthy and passing through costs?) and EESL progress (customer wins, capacity ramp, and the path from losses to profit). Get the lithium ramp right and Exide re-rates from a battery maker into an energy-storage platform; stumble, and the market marks down an option it paid up for — while the cow, at least, keeps paying its dividend.
