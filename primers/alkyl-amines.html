Alkyl Amines Chemicals, the molecule before the molecule

A complete walk through one of India's leading makers of aliphatic amines — the humble, invisible building-block chemicals that end up inside your medicines, your crop sprays, your tyres and your drinking water. How a company most people have never heard of became a quiet, debt-free compounder, why its whole profit lives in the spread between a few cheap feedstocks and a family of higher-value molecules, and why its earnings today still sit below where they were five years ago.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Alkyl Amines Chemicals Limited makes aliphatic amines — a family of simple, reactive building-block chemicals that almost nobody buys directly but that end up inside an enormous range of finished goods. Its core products are methylamines and ethylamines (the "mono / di / tri" molecules), a wide basket of amine derivatives, and a set of specialty chemicals led by acetonitrile. These are intermediates: a pharma company buys an amine to build a drug molecule around it; an agrochemical maker uses one as a rung in synthesising a herbicide; a rubber-chemicals or water-treatment firm uses another. Alkyl Amines is one of India's largest and most established producers in this niche, running roughly 20 plants across three sites in Maharashtra and Gujarat.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It sells to industries, not to you. Demand for amines is entirely derived — it tracks how much its customers in pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, rubber chemicals, water treatment and personal care are producing. Alkyl Amines never sees the shopper; it sits one or two steps upstream of the drug, the pesticide, the tyre. So its cycle is the chemical cycle, and its best years are when downstream pharma and agrochem are running hot.
  • The moat is cost and reliability at scale, not a patent. Amines themselves are well-known molecules — the edge is being able to make a broad basket of them cheaply, consistently and in volume, year after year, from a low-cost integrated base. That process know-how, the multi-plant flexibility and the import-substitution position against Chinese supply are what let Alkyl Amines earn a premium return on a commodity-sounding product.
  • It is a debt-free compounder that stopped compounding. Here is the number that should stop you: FY26 net profit of ~₹180 Cr was lower than the ~₹295 Cr it earned in FY21 — five years earlier — even though revenue over the same span rose from ~₹1,242 Cr to ~₹1,536 Cr. The balance sheet is pristine (essentially no net debt), the franchise is intact, but a super-cycle in FY21 set a peak the company has spent five years climbing back toward. Understanding why is the whole story.
In plain terms

Alkyl Amines is an ingredient supplier to the chemical economy. It takes a handful of cheap, widely-traded feedstocks — mainly methanol, ammonia and ethanol — and reacts them into a family of amine molecules worth several times more per kilo, which pharma, crop-science and industrial customers then build into finished products. The business is not the molecules, which anyone can name; it is the ability to make dozens of them at the lowest reliable cost in the country and sell every kilo the plants can run. Its profit is the spread between cheap inputs and higher-value outputs — and that spread ballooned in the FY21 chemical super-cycle, then normalised as Chinese supply came back and downstream demand cooled. The company is still very good; the cycle simply took away the once-in-a-decade margin it briefly enjoyed.

01

Amines 101: what these chemicals are and why they matter

No chemistry degree needed — just what an amine is, and where it ends up.

An amine is one of the most basic and useful shapes in organic chemistry: an ammonia molecule (NH₃) with one or more of its hydrogen atoms swapped for a carbon chain. Swap in a methyl group and you get a methylamine; swap in an ethyl group and you get an ethylamine. That small change turns a gas into a reactive, versatile handle that chemists use to bolt molecules together. Amines are, quite literally, connectors — the reactive corner that a drug, a dye or a pesticide is built around. That is why they are called intermediates or building blocks: they rarely appear in a finished product by name, but a startling share of finished chemistry passes through one.

The one idea that unlocks the sector: demand is borrowed from downstream

Nobody wakes up wanting to buy tri-ethylamine. Alkyl Amines' demand is derived — it exists only because someone downstream is making a medicine, a herbicide, a rubber accelerator or a water-treatment chemical, and needs an amine as a rung in that synthesis. That single fact explains the whole rhythm of the business: when global pharma and agrochemical production is buoyant, amine offtake and pricing firm up; when those industries de-stock or slow (as agrochem did badly through FY24-FY25), the amine maker feels it one step upstream, magnified. It also explains why a broad basket matters — being spread across pharma, agrochem, rubber, water and personal care means no single downstream slump sinks the whole company at once.

Where an Alkyl Amines molecule actually ends up

  • Pharmaceuticals — the largest end-market. Amines and derivatives (and acetonitrile as a solvent) are used in synthesising a wide range of active pharmaceutical ingredients. A resilient, high-value, quality-sensitive demand base.
  • Agrochemicals — herbicides, fungicides and insecticides use amines as intermediates. High-growth structurally, but sharply cyclical — the global agrochem de-stocking of FY24-FY25 was a real drag on amine demand.
  • Rubber chemicals — accelerators and anti-degradants for tyres and rubber goods draw on amine chemistry. Tied to the auto and tyre cycle.
  • Water treatment & personal care — flocculants, biocides, surfactants and specialty ingredients round out the basket, adding steadier, less-cyclical demand.
02

The product basket: methylamines, ethylamines, derivatives, acetonitrile

Four layers of value, from base gas to specialty solvent — and a deliberate climb up them.

Alkyl Amines is best understood as a ladder of related molecules. At the bottom sit the base amines made straight from cheap feedstock; each rung above adds a reaction step, more processing, more value and — usually — a better, steadier margin. The company's long-run strategy has been to keep climbing that ladder: from commodity amines, into derivatives, into specialty products like acetonitrile. The basket is broad by design, because breadth is what smooths a cyclical, derived-demand business.

The four rungs, and what each one is

  • Methylamines (MMA / DMA / TMA) — the foundational family, reacted from methanol and ammonia. Broadest downstream use and a core volume line, but the most commodity-like and the most exposed to methanol-price swings. Alkyl runs its principal methylamines capacity at Patalganga and Dahej.
  • Ethylamines (MEA / DEA / TEA) — the line where Alkyl has a genuine scale edge: it is India's largest ethylamines producer and among the larger ones globally. Scale here means lower unit cost and a defensible cost position rivals struggle to match. Made at Kurkumbh, with capacity expanded in FY24.
  • Amine derivatives — molecules built one step further on: DMA-HCl (a pharma intermediate where Alkyl is a world-scale supplier), choline chloride, acetamides and others. Higher value, stickier, quality-audited customers — a better place to be than plain base amines.
  • Acetonitrile & other specialty — the top rung: a high-value pharma solvent Alkyl makes at Dahej, plus other specialty chemicals. Import-substituting, structurally growing, but with prices that can swing violently (more in section 07).

Why the derivatives tilt is the smartest dial management owns

Most of what moves Alkyl's results — feedstock prices, the chemical cycle, Chinese supply — is set outside its walls. Product mix is one of the few things it genuinely controls. Base methylamines are near-commodities whose margin the feedstock spread dictates almost mechanically; derivatives like DMA-HCl and specialty products like acetonitrile are formulated, quality-audited, harder-to-switch molecules that earn a richer, steadier spread. Pushing the mix up the ladder — more derivatives, more specialty, proportionally less plain base amine — is how Alkyl lifts the quality of each tonne without waiting on methanol quotes or the agrochem cycle to cooperate. It will not rescue a bad down-cycle, but it makes the franchise worth more through one.

03

Why scale is the moat when the molecule is public

The edge in a commodity-chemistry business is cost, consistency and being here already.

A fair question hangs over any building-block chemical maker: if everyone knows how to make an amine, what stops a competitor from crushing the margin? The answer is that in bulk intermediate chemistry the recipe is rarely the moat — the ability to run that recipe at the lowest reliable cost, in volume, safely, for decades is. Alkyl Amines' advantage is an accumulation of unglamorous things that are genuinely hard to replicate quickly.

Demand is spread across those downstream industries in a deliberately balanced way: pharmaceuticals anchor roughly half the book with quality-sensitive, less price-elastic demand; agrochemicals add perhaps a quarter, bringing growth but also the sector's sharpest cyclicality; and a rubber, water-treatment and personal-care tail makes up the rest, providing ballast when any one end-market wobbles. That breadth is itself part of the moat — no single downstream slump can sink the whole company at once.

The four pillars of the moat

  • Low-cost, integrated scale. Roughly 20 plants across three sites, decades of debottlenecking, captive utilities and process optimisation add up to a unit cost that a new entrant cannot match on day one. In a business where the selling price is broadly set by the market, the low-cost producer keeps the widest spread on the same molecule.
  • Process know-how and reliability. Amine chemistry is corrosive, hazardous and quality-critical — pharma customers audit and qualify a supplier before trusting them with a molecule that goes into a drug. Decades of safe, consistent operation and qualified status are an intangible asset that takes years to build and a single serious failure to lose.
  • Breadth of basket. A wide range of amines and derivatives across pharma, agrochem, rubber, water and personal care means the company can flex production toward whatever end-market is strongest and is not hostage to any one product or customer segment.
  • Import substitution against China. For several amines and for acetonitrile, the realistic alternative to Alkyl is imported product, largely from China. Being a reliable domestic supplier — no shipping lead-times, no currency and freight risk, no supply-security worry — is a structural advantage Indian customers increasingly value, reinforced by the broader "reduce China dependence" theme in specialty chemicals.
The counter-intuitive shape of a good chemistry moat

It feels wrong that a company can build a durable franchise on molecules any competent chemist can name. But that is exactly the nature of the best bulk-intermediate businesses: the barrier is not secrecy, it is economics and trust. To take share from Alkyl, a rival must build comparable capacity, drive its costs down over years of learning, survive the safety and environmental burden of amine manufacture, and then persuade conservative pharma customers to re-qualify a new supplier for a molecule that goes into a regulated drug. Each of those is a slow, capital-hungry, failure-prone task. The moat is the sum of them — which is why a "commodity" chemical maker can quietly earn a mid-teens-to-high return on capital across a cycle.

04

The company, the family, and the balance sheet

A promoter-run, debt-free mid-cap with an unusually conservative financial culture.

Alkyl Amines has an old-economy, family-promoter character that is central to how it behaves. It is not a private-equity roll-up or a professionally-diffuse widely-held company; it is a founder-family business, run conservatively, that has grown by ploughing profits into more capacity rather than by leverage or acquisition. That culture shows up directly in the balance sheet — and in the stock's long-standing premium rating.

Ownership & financial character

  • Promoter-controlled. The founding Kotwal family holds roughly 72% of the company (about 72.0% as of mid-2026), with zero pledged shares — a high-conviction, aligned ownership structure. The balance is public float, including domestic institutions and a long tail of retail holders. Confirm the exact percentage in the latest shareholding filing.
  • Effectively debt-free. Borrowings are negligible (of the order of a crore or two against a ~₹1,500 Cr business), and the company self-funds its expansion from internal accruals. This is not a working-capital-heavy or leverage-dependent model — a genuine point of resilience through a down-cycle.
  • Capacity-led growth. The way Alkyl grows is by adding plants and debottlenecking existing ones — the Dahej acetonitrile line, the Kurkumbh ethylamines expansion — funded from cash flow, not by buying rivals. Growth is therefore steady and self-financed, but also gated by how fast it can build and fill capacity.
Why the conservative culture cuts both ways: a debt-free, family-run, cash-generative franchise is exactly what you want to own through a chemical down-cycle — it cannot be forced into distress, and it keeps investing when weaker rivals retrench. But the same conservatism means growth is incremental and self-paced, and the market has historically paid a rich multiple for the quality and safety, which leaves little slack if earnings disappoint. The balance sheet removes the financial risk; it does not remove the cyclical or valuation risk.
05

The spread per kilo: where the profit actually lives

The single most important idea in the case — and it is specific to a feedstock-to-amine converter.

Most people would track an amine maker by the price of amines. That is the wrong number. Alkyl Amines does not keep the price of a kilo of methylamine; it keeps the spread — what is left after the feedstock bill is paid out of the price the finished molecule fetches. The whole company can be read through one relationship: the gap between what methanol, ammonia and ethanol cost, and what the amine or derivative sells for, per kilo. Both ends of that gap move for their own reasons, on their own clocks — and the difference is the entire business.

Watch how little the top line explains. Revenue in FY21 was ~₹1,242 Cr; in FY26 it was ~₹1,536 Cr — up roughly a quarter. Yet operating margin more than halved, from ~35% to ~19%, and profit fell. For a feedstock-to-amine converter, revenue is a poor guide because it rises when feedstock and product prices are both high (bigger numbers, not necessarily bigger spread) and falls when they soften even as the spread widens. The kilos sold and the spread per kilo tell you far more than the headline.

The device that only fits an amine converter: (amine realisation − methanol/ammonia/ethanol cost) per kilo

Forget "the price of methylamine went up." The number that runs Alkyl Amines is (what the finished amine or derivative sells for) − (what the methanol, ammonia and ethanol to make it cost), measured per kilo and multiplied by the tonnes the plants can run and fill. The output price is set by global amine markets and, critically, by how much cheap Chinese supply is landing in India; the input price is set by the methanol, ammonia and ethanol markets. When both align — scarce supply, firm downstream demand, benign feedstock — the spread balloons, as it did in FY21. When Chinese supply returns and agrochem de-stocks while feedstock stays sticky, the spread compresses, as through FY24. The plants, the people and the products were the same in both years; the spread per kilo was the whole difference between a ~35% and a ~17% margin.

This is why headline growth can mislead badly here. A year where feedstock prices spike will inflate revenue while quietly crushing the spread; a year where prices soften can shrink revenue while the spread — and profit — improves. For a building-block converter, the pair of numbers that matter are volume (are the new ethylamines and acetonitrile lines filling up?) and spread per kilo (is the amine-to-feedstock gap widening back toward its old self?). Hold that lens for the rest of this primer.

06

The FY21 ghost: a compounder whose profit went backwards

The fact that surprises almost everyone who looks at this "quality compounder" for the first time.

Alkyl Amines has long been filed by investors under "high-quality specialty-chemical compounder" — debt-free, high returns, family-run, richly valued. So here is the fact that stops people short: its net profit in FY26 (~₹180 Cr) was lower than in FY21 (~₹295 Cr) — five years earlier — despite revenue growing over the same span. A supposed compounder's earnings, in other words, went backwards for half a decade. The explanation is not that the company got worse. It is that FY21 was not a normal year.

Five years on, FY26 profit is still ~40% below the FY21 peak, even though the plants are bigger and revenue is higher. FY21 was a spread super-cycle — pandemic shortages, sky-high acetonitrile and amine prices, constrained Chinese supply — that lifted margins to a level the normal cycle simply does not sustain.

What happened? FY21 caught a rare alignment. The pandemic disrupted global chemical supply chains just as demand for pharma-linked intermediates and solvents surged; Chinese exports were constrained; and acetonitrile in particular — a pharma solvent Alkyl had just built capacity for — went through the roof. Alkyl's spreads and margins ballooned to a level (a ~35% operating margin) that reflected scarcity, not the steady-state economics of amine-making. As the world normalised — Chinese supply returned, agrochem customers ran down inventory through FY24, feedstock stayed sticky — the spread reverted, and with it the profit. The franchise did not decay; the tailwind did.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with

A genuinely good, debt-free, well-run company can post its best-ever profit and then not beat it for five years — and none of that is a sign of decline. It simply means the peak was cyclical, not structural. This is the single most important thing a first-time buyer of Alkyl Amines must internalise: much of the "compounding" reputation was earned in the years into FY21, and part of the FY21 number was a super-cycle windfall the company was always going to give back. The right way to value the business is on a through-cycle spread — somewhere between the FY21 ceiling and the FY24 floor — not by anchoring on either extreme. Read against that, FY26's recovery and Q1 FY27's sharp jump matter enormously: they are the evidence for whether the normalised spread is climbing back toward the mid-cycle, or stalling near the trough.

Why this reframes the whole stock: it means the debate over Alkyl Amines is not "is it a good company" — it plainly is — but "what is the normalised earning power, and what multiple should that carry?" A market that pays a premium multiple on trough-ish or recovering earnings is making a specific bet: that the spread mean-reverts up and volumes from new capacity compound on top. If that bet is right, today's optics look expensive but the reality is cheap; if the spread stays stuck near the trough, the premium multiple has nothing to grow into.
07

Acetonitrile and the specialty growth leg

The higher-value, higher-volatility rung the company is deliberately climbing.

If base amines are the ballast, acetonitrile and the specialty basket are the growth leg. Acetonitrile is a solvent that pharma manufacturing and laboratory analysis cannot do without — it is the standard mobile phase in HPLC, the workhorse analytical technique, and a reaction solvent across drug synthesis. India historically imported nearly all of it. Alkyl's dedicated Dahej plant, commissioned in FY22, made it a rare domestic producer — a textbook import-substitution play in a molecule with structurally growing, quality-sensitive demand.

But acetonitrile carries a quirk that every reader should understand: globally, much of it is produced as a by-product of acrylonitrile manufacture (used to make plastics and fibres). That means world supply is tied to something unrelated to acetonitrile demand — when acrylonitrile output falls, acetonitrile can suddenly go scarce and its price can spike violently; when acrylonitrile runs hot, acetonitrile floods and prices collapse. This is precisely why acetonitrile went stratospheric during the pandemic and then fell back hard. A dedicated, on-purpose producer like Alkyl benefits from the spikes but must weather the gluts.

There is a clean logic to the value ladder here. Each rung up — from base methylamines, to scale ethylamines, to amine derivatives like DMA-HCl, to acetonitrile and specialty — adds a reaction step, more processing and a better, stickier spread per kilo, but also, at the top, more price volatility. The strategy is to grow the top two rungs faster than the bottom, lifting the blended margin and the quality of earnings over a cycle even though the specialty rung is the one that swings hardest quarter to quarter.

The growth strategy has three threads working together. Fill the new capacity — the Dahej acetonitrile line and the expanded Kurkumbh ethylamines plant need to run at high utilisation to earn their keep and drive volume growth. Push the mix upward — more derivatives and specialty, proportionally less plain base amine, to lift the blended spread. And keep substituting imports — every kilo of amine or acetonitrile an Indian customer buys from Alkyl instead of from China is a kilo of structural, defensible domestic demand.

Why the specialty leg is worth the volatility

An investor might ask why Alkyl leans into acetonitrile at all, given how wildly its price swings. The answer is that over a full cycle a dedicated, low-cost domestic producer of a growing, import-dependent pharma solvent captures a structurally higher and — outside the swings — steadier spread than plain base amines can offer, plus it deepens the relationship with the same pharma customers who already buy its amines. The volatility is the price of admission to a better long-run margin and a stickier customer. The skill is running the base-amine ballast steadily enough that a bad acetonitrile quarter dents the result rather than defining it — which is exactly what the broad, balanced basket is built to do.

08

The China question and the chemical cycle

The two external forces that write most of an amine maker's year.

Strip Alkyl Amines to its essentials and two outside forces decide most of any year: the level of Chinese supply landing in India, and where the broader chemical cycle sits. The company manages around both — through cost, breadth and import-substitution positioning — but it authors neither. This is the true core of the risk profile, more than any single product line.

The uncomfortable summary a first-timer should internalise

Alkyl Amines' job is to run a tight, low-cost, broad amine operation and then endure the weather: Chinese-supply weather, feedstock weather, and downstream-demand weather. It can improve its odds — climb the derivatives ladder, fill new capacity, hold its cost edge, lean on its import-substitution position — but it cannot guarantee any single year, because the biggest inputs to that year are set in Chinese boardrooms, in global feedstock markets, and in the order books of pharma and agrochem customers. FY21 was a year all three cooperated spectacularly. FY24 was a year they did not. A disciplined investor prices the good years knowing the model does not control whether they repeat, and values the company on the average across them rather than the best of them.

Notice how the forces interact. Cheap Chinese supply (force 1) hurts most when the downstream cycle (force 3) is already soft, because there is no demand pull to absorb it — which is exactly what made FY24-FY25 painful. Conversely, an agrochem re-stock plus tighter Chinese exports plus benign feedstock would line up into the kind of spread that echoes FY21. The best years are alignments; the worst are collisions. Q1 FY27's sharp jump suggests the forces have begun tilting back toward alignment — the question the next few quarters answer is how far.

09

The peer set: Alkyl Amines and Balaji Amines

A small, consolidated Indian amines duopoly-plus, benchmarked against its closest listed rival.

Listed Indian aliphatic-amines manufacturing is a short list. The two names investors reach for first are Alkyl Amines and Balaji Amines — genuine peers with overlapping products, frequently compared, but distinct companies with different tilts. Beyond them sit a handful of broader specialty-chemical makers whose baskets touch amines. Because selling prices are broadly set by the market and by import parity, the competition here is fought on cost position, product mix and reliability, not on undercutting a shelf price.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, this is not a price war in the retail sense — with import parity and a market-set price for most amines, makers compete on cost, mix and dependability, and the lowest-cost, best-mixed producer keeps the widest spread on the same molecule. That is why Alkyl's scale in ethylamines and its derivatives climb matter so much. Second, the Alkyl-versus-Balaji comparison is worth watching precisely because they are distinct: divergences in their margins usually say more about each company's product mix and specific end-markets than about the amine cycle as a whole, and reading them together is one of the better ways to tell a company-specific stumble from a sector-wide one.

A pristine balance sheet and a premium multiple, resting on a spread that is only part-way recovered.

The one number that settles the thesis: the amine spread recovering into full new-capacity volumes

At a premium multiple on recovering earnings, the case does not need a re-rating — it needs the spread to keep climbing back toward mid-cycle while the new ethylamines and acetonitrile capacity fills up. So the metric that proves or breaks it is operating margin (the amine-to-feedstock spread), read together with volume growth, tracked quarter by quarter: is margin marching from the ~19% of FY26 back up toward the mid-20s and beyond, and are the expanded plants translating that into rising tonnes? Q1 FY27's ~91% profit jump is the first hard data point that says yes. If margin and volume compound together over FY27 — as that quarter hints — the premium multiple has a fast-growing earnings base to sit on, and today's optics understate the value. If the spread stalls near the trough while Chinese supply stays heavy, the same multiple has nothing to grow into and the stock is simply expensive. Spread times volume, judged against the FY21 ceiling and the FY24 floor — that is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A pristine, cost-advantaged franchise turning up off a trough — asked whether the spread mean-reverts.

Alkyl Amines enters FY27 in strong structural shape: a debt-free balance sheet, a cost-leading position in ethylamines, a widening derivatives and acetonitrile basket, an import-substitution tailwind against China, and — in Q1 FY27 — the first clear sign of the spread turning back up. What no amine maker can promise is the one thing that decides everything: that the gap between cheap feedstock and higher-value molecules keeps widening back toward its mid-cycle self rather than stalling near the trough. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Spread recovery underway — margin has lifted off the FY24 trough and Q1 FY27 profit jumped ~91% YoY; if the amine-to-feedstock spread keeps mean-reverting up, earnings compound fast off a low base.
  • Cost-leading scale — India's largest ethylamines maker with a low-cost, integrated, multi-plant base keeps the widest spread on a market-priced molecule.
  • Mix & specialty climb — the derivatives ladder and the Dahej acetonitrile line structurally lift and steady the blended margin as they fill.
  • Debt-free resilience — a pristine, self-funding balance sheet cannot be forced into distress and keeps investing through down-cycles when rivals retrench.
  • Import-substitution & China+1 — a reliable domestic supplier displacing Chinese imports in amines and acetonitrile is structural, defensible demand.

The bear case

  • Premium multiple on trough-ish earnings — ~44× on profit still ~40% below the FY21 peak; if the spread stalls, the rating has little to grow into.
  • Chinese supply & dumping — cheap imported amine and acetonitrile cap domestic prices and can compress the spread regardless of how well the plants run.
  • Feedstock & FX whipsaw — sticky methanol/ammonia/ethanol ahead of soft product prices squeezes margin quickly; scale blunts but does not remove this.
  • Downstream cyclicality — a renewed agrochem de-stock or pharma slowdown hits volume and pricing together, as FY24-FY25 showed.
  • Acetonitrile volatility — its by-product-linked global supply makes its price swing violently; the growth leg is also the most unpredictable one.
The bottom line

Alkyl Amines is a genuinely excellent building-block chemical maker — debt-free, cost-advantaged, broadly diversified across pharma, agrochem and industrial end-markets — whose molecules quietly end up inside a vast range of finished chemistry. The catch, and the whole investment question, is that its FY21 profit peak was a super-cycle windfall it has spent five years climbing back toward, and its premium valuation is a bet that the normalised spread mean-reverts upward from here. The near-term test is concrete and datable: whether the operating margin keeps climbing off FY26's ~19% back toward the mid-20s through FY27 as the ethylamines and acetonitrile capacity fills — with Q1 FY27's ~91% profit jump as the first, encouraging data point. Sustain that recovery and the premium multiple sits on fast-growing earnings and looks earned; let the spread stall near the trough while Chinese supply stays heavy, and FY26 will read as a way-station, not a springboard. The balance sheet has removed every risk except the one that matters most — the cycle.
