Ellenbarrie Industrial Gases, molecules with a local moat

A full walk through one of India's oldest home-grown industrial-gas makers — the Kolkata company that pulls oxygen, nitrogen and argon out of ordinary air and pipes them into steel mills and hospital wards. 00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Ellenbarrie Industrial Gases Limited (EIGL) does something quietly remarkable: it separates ordinary air into its pure ingredients and sells them by the tonne. Its core products are oxygen, nitrogen and argon — the three gases that make up almost all the air around you — plus carbon dioxide, hydrogen, nitrous oxide, dissolved acetylene and a range of medical and specialty gases. Founded in 1973 in Kolkata, it is one of India's oldest and largest 100% Indian-owned industrial-gas companies, with a stronghold in the east of the country and a live push into the south and north. It listed on the NSE and BSE on 1 July 2025 after a ₹852 Cr IPO.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The raw material is free; the value is in the plant. Air is 21% oxygen and 78% nitrogen, and it costs nothing. What Ellenbarrie sells is the work of cooling air to around minus-190°C, pulling it apart in a distillation column (an "air-separation unit," or ASU), and delivering the pure gas exactly where a customer needs it. The product is a commodity; the business is capital, engineering and logistics.
  • It behaves like a toll road, not a chemicals maker. The best gas revenue comes from on-site plants — an ASU built next to a big customer like a steel mill, running under a long-term take-or-pay contract where the customer pays whether or not it draws the full volume, with power and inflation passed through. That is an annuity. Layered on top are bulk liquid deliveries by cryogenic tanker and cylinder sales to smaller users. The mix, not the sticker price of a gas, decides the quality of earnings.
  • The economics are startlingly good for its size. In FY25 EIGL turned ~₹348 Cr of revenue into an EBITDA margin of ~35% and a net margin near 24%, and management is steering toward a ~40% EBITDA margin. FY26, its first full listed year, lifted net profit to ~₹104 Cr on roughly flat revenue — a jump driven far more by repaying debt and parking the IPO cash than by selling more gas. At a market cap around ₹4,540 Cr, the stock trades on a rich ~40-plus times earnings, pricing in years of the promised expansion actually landing.
In plain terms

Ellenbarrie builds expensive cold-boxes that boil the air apart and then sells the resulting oxygen and nitrogen to whoever is standing next door — a steel furnace that needs oxygen to burn off carbon, a hospital that needs medical oxygen, a workshop that needs argon to weld. The genius of the model is not the gas, which anyone can make; it is location and contract. Because liquefied gas is heavy, cold and cheap per tonne, it does not pay to truck it far — so whoever owns the plant nearest a cluster of customers effectively owns that patch of the map, often on decade-long contracts. Ellenbarrie owns several such patches in eastern and southern India. The FY26 profit jump flatters a year in which sales barely grew; the real question is whether the new capacity it is building converts into more of those sticky, high-margin contracts.

01

Gas from thin air: the industry in one page

No chemistry degree needed — just where the gas comes from, and what each one does.

Industrial gas is one of the least-understood corners of the economy, partly because the product is invisible and partly because it hides inside everything else — the steel in a car, the fizz in a drink, the oxygen in an ICU. At its root the business is simple: air is a free mixture of gases, and there is money in separating and purifying it. To follow Ellenbarrie you need to know four gases and one machine.

The one dependency that shapes the sector: gas is mostly electricity in disguise

An ASU's dominant running cost is not raw material — the air is free — it is power. Separating air is enormously energy-intensive: the plant runs huge compressors day and night. That single fact drives everything about how the industry contracts. On-site deals pass the electricity bill straight through to the customer, so the gas company earns a stable conversion margin regardless of what power costs. Where power is not passed through — in some bulk and cylinder sales — a spike in electricity tariffs eats directly into margin. Understanding a gas company therefore starts with a simple question: how much of its volume sits under contracts where someone else pays the power bill?

02

Three ways to sell a molecule

The defining structural fact of the business — the same oxygen earns very different money depending on how it is delivered.

A litre of oxygen is a litre of oxygen. But an industrial-gas company earns wildly different economics on it depending on the delivery mode, and the split between the three modes is the single best guide to the quality and durability of its earnings. Every gas major, from the global giants down to Ellenbarrie, runs the same three-lane model.

Ellenbarrie runs all three lanes. Its ASUs feed on-site and bulk-liquid supply to industrial customers, while its packaged and medical-gas business reaches hospitals and smaller users, especially across eastern India where its distribution network is densest. The strategic direction management has signalled is to add tonnage — larger on-site and bulk contracts anchored by new ASU capacity — because that is where scale, stickiness and margin compound. The higher-value specialty and medical gases, meanwhile, lift the average realisation per tonne without needing much extra volume.

Why the mix is the whole game

Two gas companies can report identical revenue and be utterly different businesses. One might earn it from a fleet of decade-long on-site contracts with the power bill passed through — cash that arrives whether the customer's factory is busy or idle. The other might scrape it together from thousands of cylinder deliveries priced in a competitive local spot market, re-won every month. The first deserves a bond-like multiple; the second, a distributor's. For Ellenbarrie, the investment case rests heavily on tilting the mix toward the first kind as it commissions new capacity — turning one-off equipment spend into contracted, inflation-protected annuities. Revenue growth that comes from tonnage contracts is worth far more than the same rupees earned from spot cylinders.

03

Why the map is the moat

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to bulk gases.

Most commodities are made in a few places and shipped everywhere — oil, steel, cement all travel across oceans. Industrial gas is the great exception, and that exception is the entire reason a small regional company like Ellenbarrie can survive alongside global giants. The reason is physics: gas is worth very little per tonne and costs a great deal to move, so beyond a certain radius it is simply uneconomic to deliver. Whoever owns the plant nearest a cluster of demand owns that market.

Consider the arithmetic. Oxygen and nitrogen sell for a modest few thousand rupees a tonne. To transport them any distance you must first liquefy them — an energy cost in itself — then keep them in a heavy, refrigerated cryogenic tanker, then truck that tanker on Indian roads at Indian diesel prices. Every kilometre eats into a thin per-tonne value. Push much past a couple of hundred kilometres and the freight can exceed the worth of the gas inside the tank. The practical result is that bulk gas is a regional, almost local, business: national maps do not matter, cluster maps do.

Oil is priced globally because it is worth shipping across the world. Oxygen is priced locally because it is barely worth shipping across a state.
The device that only fits a bulk-gas company: the molecule-mile

Think about the "molecule-mile" — how far a molecule of product can profitably travel before the cost of moving it swamps its value. For an iPhone, the molecule-mile is effectively the whole planet. For cement, it is a few hundred kilometres. For liquefied oxygen it is short — often under 200 kilometres to stay economic. That tiny molecule-mile is Ellenbarrie's moat. Each of its air-separation units effectively fences off a circle of demand around it that no distant rival can profitably serve, because their tanker would arrive having spent more on freight than the gas is worth. The competitive map of Indian industrial gas is therefore not one national contest but dozens of overlapping local circles — and inside its eastern-India circles, a home player that got there first and built the distribution is very hard to dislodge. The moat is drawn in kilometres, not patents.

This is why the industry is a comfortable oligopoly rather than a price war. New entry is deterred on three fronts at once: the capital to build an ASU is large and slow to earn back; the best anchor customers are already locked into 10–15-year contracts; and even a willing new plant only makes sense if there is enough nearby demand to fill it, because it cannot chase distant customers. So the incumbents in each region enjoy pricing discipline and long customer lives. Ellenbarrie's edge is that it has spent five decades building exactly that kind of regional density in the east, where the country's steel and metals belt sits.

The counter-intuitive upshot: being small and regional is not the handicap it would be in most industries. In gases, a focused local operator with plants sitting inside its customers' delivery radius can out-earn a far larger national rival that has no plant nearby — because the giant's oxygen simply cannot travel to compete. Scale in gases is regional density, not national size. That is why Ellenbarrie's plan is to replicate its eastern stronghold cluster-by-cluster in the south and north, not to fight the majors everywhere at once.

What Ellenbarrie actually runs

The plants, the products, and the geography of a fifty-year-old gas network.

Ellenbarrie is a manufacturing-and-distribution business built around a spine of air-separation units, supplemented by smaller on-site generators and a packaged-gas network. Its RHP describes a footprint of several facilities across four states — West Bengal, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Chhattisgarh — anchored by three standalone ASUs, with a large fourth unit under construction using IPO proceeds.

The product portfolio

Why "no imported raw material" quietly matters so much

A fertiliser or petrochemical maker lives or dies by the dollar price of the stuff it must buy in — phosphoric acid, naphtha, crude. Ellenbarrie buys none of that. Its feedstock is the air, which is free and never re-prices, and its main variable cost is domestic electricity, much of which it passes through to customers. That structural absence of an imported-input bill is one of the least-appreciated reasons gas companies enjoy such stable, high margins: there is no global commodity spike or rupee slide sitting on the cost line waiting to crush the spread. The business converts cheap, local power into a value-added product, and keeps most of the difference.

05

The company, the family, and the float

A half-century-old family enterprise that only reached the public market in 2025.

Ellenbarrie is unusual among recent IPOs in being genuinely old. It traces to 1973, has been built and run by the Agarwala family for its entire life, and remained wholly private and 100% Indian-owned until it listed in mid-2025. That long, closely-held history explains both its deep regional relationships and the fact that public investors have only a very short track record of audited, listed-company disclosure to judge it by.

Ownership & the IPO

  • Promoters: the founding Agarwala family (Padam Kumar Agarwala and Varun Agarwal), who continue to control and run the company. It has always been 100% Indian-owned — a point of identity that distinguishes it from the foreign-parented majors that dominate the sector. Confirm the exact post-IPO promoter percentage in the latest shareholding filing.
  • The IPO structure: the ~₹852 Cr issue combined a fresh issue (roughly ₹400 Cr, raising new money for the company) with an offer-for-sale (existing holders selling down). Only the fresh-issue portion adds cash to the balance sheet; the OFS is a change of ownership, not a capital raise.
  • Use of the fresh proceeds: the two big uses are ~₹210 Cr of debt repayment and ~₹104.5 Cr of capital expenditure for the new 220 TPD Uluberia-II air-separation unit, with the balance for general corporate purposes and potential inorganic growth. This is why FY26 profit rose sharply even on flat sales — see §07.
Why the "old company, new listing" combination cuts both ways: five decades of operating history give Ellenbarrie relationships, land, licences and regional density that a start-up could not buy — a real asset. But as a public company it is barely a year old, so investors are pricing a rich multiple on a very short window of listed-standard disclosure, a founder-controlled board, and management's own growth guidance rather than a long public record of delivering it. The history is deep; the accountability trail as a listed firm is thin.
06

The margin nobody expects from a tiny gas maker

The fact that surprises everyone: a small Indian regional out-earns the global giants on margin.

Here is what stops people short when they first look at Ellenbarrie's accounts. A ₹340-crore-revenue regional company, dwarfed many times over by Linde, Air Liquide and the other majors, posts an EBITDA margin around 35% and a net margin near 24% — richer than most of those giants earn, and management is guiding toward 40%. For a business that sells a commodity out of thin air, that is a remarkable spread, and understanding why is the key to the whole case.

Four forces combine to produce that margin, and each traces back to something already covered:

  • The delivery mix is favourable. A high share of on-site and bulk contracts — with power passed through and inflation escalators built in — means much of the revenue arrives as a stable conversion spread rather than a competed spot price (§02).
  • There is no imported-input bill. Air is free and electricity is largely passed through, so there is no volatile dollar-priced raw material sitting on the cost line, unlike almost every chemicals or fertiliser peer (§04).
  • Local pricing power. Inside each plant's delivery radius, distant rivals cannot profitably compete, so realisations hold up and discounting is disciplined (§03).
  • A richening product tilt. Adding argon, medical and specialty gases lifts average realisation per tonne without needing proportionally more volume or capital.
The genuine surprise to sit with

Intuition says the global giants — with their scale, technology and buying power — should earn the fattest margins, and a sub-scale regional should be the price-taker scraping by. In industrial gas the intuition is exactly backwards. Because the moat is local density rather than global scale, a focused operator sitting right inside its customers' delivery radius, with the power bill passed through and no imported input to whipsaw its costs, can quietly earn a higher margin than a multinational whose gas cannot even reach that market. Ellenbarrie's ~35% EBITDA margin is not an accounting quirk to be distrusted; it is the natural signature of a well-placed regional gas network. The open question is not whether the margin is real, but whether it survives as the company stretches into new regions where it does not yet have that density.

07

The post-IPO rewiring: profit up, sales flat

The one-off transformation the IPO bought — and why FY26's profit jump is not what it first looks like.

FY26 shows a striking pattern: net profit climbed from ~₹83 Cr to ~₹104 Cr — up roughly a quarter — while revenue barely moved, drifting around ₹342–348 Cr. In most companies, profit growing far faster than sales signals operating leverage or a pricing win. Here, most of it is something more mechanical and less repeatable: the IPO rewired the bottom of the income statement.

The mechanism has two parts, both funded by the July-2025 listing. First, the company used ~₹210 Cr of IPO proceeds to repay debt, which strips out a chunk of interest expense that previously sat between operating profit and net profit — every rupee of interest saved drops almost entirely to the bottom line. Second, the leftover fresh-issue cash sits on the balance sheet earning treasury income, adding to "other income" while the new plant is still being built. The result: a business selling roughly the same amount of gas as the year before reported meaningfully higher profit, chiefly because it stopped paying interest and started earning it.

The device that fits only this company, this year

Line up three numbers a single year apart: ~₹340 Cr of revenue produced ~₹83 Cr of profit in FY25 and ~₹104 Cr in FY26 — an extra ~₹20 Cr of earnings from selling essentially no extra gas. That gap is the fingerprint of a debt-repayment-and-treasury story, not a growth story: the ₹210 Cr of debt retired and the idle IPO cash did the work that volume normally would. It is a real, banked improvement — lower interest is permanent while the debt stays repaid — but it is a one-time re-basing, not a repeatable engine. You cannot repay the same ₹210 Cr twice. From here, the profit line can only keep climbing if the thing the IPO cash is being spent on — the new Uluberia-II ASU — starts turning into contracted tonnage. The balance-sheet gift is spent; the operating proof is still ahead.

That transition, not the one-off deleveraging, is what the rich valuation is actually paying for.

08

The steel tilt and the eastern stronghold

Where the demand comes from — and the concentration risk that comes with it.

Ellenbarrie's demand is not spread evenly across the economy. It leans heavily on steel and metals, and geographically on eastern India — a concentration that is both the source of its density moat and its clearest single risk. Understanding the customer base is understanding the cyclicality the business carries.

The steel connection runs deep because oxygen is not an optional extra in modern steelmaking — it is blasted into the furnace to burn off carbon and is consumed in large, continuous volumes. That makes a steel mill an ideal on-site anchor customer: a huge, steady, contracted draw that justifies building a dedicated ASU next door. It also means Ellenbarrie's fortunes ride partly on the metals cycle — when steel output and capacity utilisation are strong, oxygen offtake is strong; when the steel cycle turns down or a big customer idles a plant, the take-or-pay floor protects the on-site contracts but the bulk and merchant volumes soften.

The eastern-India stronghold is the geographic expression of the density moat. West Bengal and the surrounding states sit within India's coal-and-steel belt, and five decades of local presence have given Ellenbarrie the plants, distribution and relationships to dominate its home circles. The strategy now is to replicate that density in the south (Telangana, Andhra) and push north — not by parachuting gas in from afar, which physics forbids, but by planting new ASUs inside fresh clusters of demand and building out from them.

The concentration to keep both eyes on

The same tilt that gives Ellenbarrie its density advantage also gives it a concentration risk: a business anchored on steel and on one region is more exposed to a single cyclical downturn or a single large customer's fortunes than a diversified national gas network would be. The take-or-pay contracts blunt this — an on-site customer keeps paying even through a slow patch — but they do not eliminate it, and merchant volumes have no such floor. The expansion into new regions and end-markets (healthcare especially, which is structurally growing and counter-cyclical to heavy industry) is not just a growth play; it is the diversification that would make the earnings sturdier. Watching the steel share fall as healthcare and other sectors rise would be a genuine sign of a de-risking business, not merely a growing one.

09

The industry and the rivals

A giant-dominated national market where the real contest is fought circle by circle.

Indian industrial gas is dominated at the national level by a handful of large, mostly foreign-parented players, with Ellenbarrie as the notable home-owned regional challenger. But because the economics are local (§03), the national league table matters less than who holds density in each region — and that is where a focused operator can win.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things distinguish this rivalry from a normal industry. First, it is not usually a national price war, because a distant rival's gas cannot reach your local customers economically — so competition is really a series of local contests over who owns the plant nearest each cluster, and who locks up the anchor customers on long contracts. Second, Ellenbarrie's identity as the large 100% Indian-owned player is more than a slogan: against foreign-parented majors making global capital-allocation decisions, a nimble home operator can move faster on mid-sized regional opportunities the giants may deprioritise. The giants win the marquee mega-contracts and the frontier technology; the focused regional wins its home circles and the steady expansion into adjacent ones. Both can prosper at once, because the map has room for many local monopolies.

Financials & valuation

Excellent margins and a fortress balance sheet — priced for the growth to arrive on schedule.

The financial profile is genuinely high-quality: rich margins, low debt after the IPO, strong returns on capital, and a growing profit line. What makes the stock a debate is not the quality but the price — at a market cap around ₹4,540 Cr against ~₹104 Cr of FY26 profit, it trades on a rich multiple in the low-to-mid 40s, a rating that already assumes years of the promised expansion translating into contracted, high-margin tonnage.

The balance sheet is the quiet strength. Having used the IPO to repay ~₹210 Cr of debt, Ellenbarrie carries little leverage and a cash cushion, which matters in a capital-intensive business where the next leg of growth is funded by building plants. High margins plus low debt plus growing capacity is an attractive combination — the concern is simply that the market has already paid for it. A ~40-times multiple leaves no cushion if the new-capacity ramp is slower than guided, if a regional expansion is won only by discounting margin, or if the steel cycle turns.

The one number that settles the thesis: incremental EBITDA per tonne of new capacity commissioned

At a low-40s multiple, the case does not need a re-rating and cannot lean on the one-off deleveraging — it needs the new plants to fill up at the old margin. So the metric that proves or breaks Ellenbarrie is the incremental EBITDA earned per TPD of capacity as it is commissioned and contracted: does the 220 TPD Uluberia-II unit, and whatever follows, convert into on-site and bulk tonnage at the same ~35%-plus EBITDA margin the existing fleet enjoys? Track it through two companion signals — the share of volume under take-or-pay contracts (is growth sticky or spot?) and the steel-versus-diversified mix (is the expansion also de-risking the demand base?). If new capacity ramps quickly into contracted, high-margin volume, the premium multiple is earned and profit compounds toward the 20%-CAGR ambition. If the plants sit under-utilised or fill only by cutting price, the rating unwinds fast. Capacity commissioned times realised margin per tonne — that is the scoreboard, and the first real read comes as Uluberia-II ramps.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality regional gas network, richly priced, asked to prove it can grow at the same margin.

Ellenbarrie enters its second listed year with a genuinely attractive engine: sector-leading margins, a clean balance sheet, a fifty-year density moat in the east, and a new ASU coming on stream to feed expansion. What it must now demonstrate — and what its rich valuation demands — is that it can carry that quality into new regions and new capacity without diluting the margin that makes it special.

The bull case

  • Toll-road economics — on-site take-or-pay contracts with power pass-through give bond-like, inflation-protected cash flows inside a local-monopoly moat.
  • Best-in-class margins — ~35% EBITDA with a credible path to 40%, richer than the far larger majors, and structurally protected by the absence of any imported-input bill.
  • Funded growth — a debt-light, cash-rich balance sheet after the IPO, with the 220 TPD Uluberia-II ASU and further expansion to lift capacity and market share.
  • Structural demand — steel, healthcare and manufacturing all growing in India; medical oxygen a durable, partly counter-cyclical tailwind after COVID underlined its importance.
  • Home-owned agility — the large 100% Indian-owned player can move faster on mid-sized regional opportunities than globally-managed rivals.

The bear case

  • Priced for flawless execution — a low-40s P/E leaves little cushion if new-capacity utilisation, margins or regional expansion disappoint even modestly.
  • FY26 profit flattered — much of the jump came from one-off deleveraging and treasury income, not from selling more gas; that lever cannot be pulled again.
  • Concentration — a pronounced tilt to steel and to eastern India ties earnings to one cycle and one region until diversification proves out.
  • Density doesn't travel — the moat is local; winning new regions means building density from scratch against entrenched majors, possibly by conceding price or margin.
  • Short listed record & founder control — barely a year of listed-standard disclosure, a family-controlled board, and reliance on management's own growth guidance.
The bottom line

Ellenbarrie is a rare thing — a small company with a large company's economics: a fifty-year-old gas network that converts free air and cheap local power into some of the sector's fattest margins, protected by a moat drawn in kilometres rather than patents. The balance sheet is now a fortress, the demand backdrop is structural, and the strategy of replicating its eastern density elsewhere is coherent. The catch is entirely in the price: the market has already paid for that expansion to succeed at the current margin, and FY26's flattering, one-off profit step cannot be repeated. The next test is concrete and datable — whether the 220 TPD Uluberia-II unit ramps into contracted, take-or-pay tonnage at a 35%-plus EBITDA margin, and whether the steel-heavy demand base broadens as it does. Fill the new plant at the old margin, and a home-grown regional will have earned its premium; leave it half-utilised or win it only on price, and the rating built on management's map will have to be redrawn.
