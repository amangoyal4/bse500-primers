00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Aditya Vision Limited runs a chain of large-format consumer-electronics showrooms — the shops where a household in a small eastern-Indian town goes to buy its television, air-conditioner, refrigerator, washing machine, or smartphone. It is not a national name like Reliance Digital or Croma. It is a regional monarch: by a CRISIL estimate it holds more than half the organised electronics-retail market in Bihar and is the largest such retailer in Jharkhand, with over 200 stores spreading into Uttar Pradesh and Odisha. Headquartered in Patna and run by its founder, it was the first consumer-electronics retailer listed on both Indian exchanges.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The business is a store-rollout compounding machine, not a same-store cash cow. Aditya Vision grew revenue from ~₹899 Cr in FY22 to ~₹2,672 Cr in FY26 — roughly a 31% annual pace — chiefly by opening stores in towns that had no organised electronics showroom before it arrived. Profit compounded even faster off a small base. The engine is a repeatable store template stamped, town by town, across a region it knows intimately.
  • A store is a slow-burn asset, and that shapes how you read the numbers. A new showroom does perhaps ₹6 Cr of sales in year one and roughly ₹16 Cr by year three as the town learns it is there. So a year of aggressive openings dilutes every blended average — sales per store, sales per square foot, margin — even when each individual store is exactly on plan. The company's growth and its optics pull in opposite directions.
  • Nearly half of what it sells is bought on credit. In FY25 about 43% of sales were financed through consumer-finance partners — a farmer or salaried buyer taking a ₹35,000 refrigerator on a monthly EMI. That single fact turns an electronics retailer into something closer to a point-of-sale lending storefront, and it is the hidden accelerant behind the whole growth story.
In plain terms

Aditya Vision is a shopkeeper that has cracked one narrow thing extremely well: putting the first proper air-conditioned electronics showroom into a Bihar or Jharkhand town of eighty thousand people, staffing it with locals who speak the customer's language, offering an EMI that makes a ₹40,000 TV feel affordable, and then doing it again in the next town twenty kilometres away. National chains chase metros; Aditya Vision owns the places they overlook. The catch is that the market prices it like a fast compounder — on a rich earnings multiple — so the entire debate is whether it can keep opening stores that mature into ₹16-crore earners while defending the towns it already dominates. It is a rollout story, and rollout stories live and die on one number: how the old stores are still growing.

01

Big-box electronics retail 101: how a showroom earns

No jargon needed — just the shelf, the margin, and who walks in the door.

Consumer-durables retail is a gross-margin-over-fixed-cost business. A store buys a fridge from a manufacturer, marks it up, and sells it; the difference between the two prices is the gross margin. Out of that margin the store must pay its rent, its staff, its electricity and its financing. Whatever is left is operating profit. Because rent and salaries are largely fixed, the whole game is getting enough sales through a fixed footprint to cover those costs and then some — which is why throughput per store is the master variable. To read Aditya Vision you need five ideas.

The one structural fact that shapes this whole company: geography

India's organised electronics retail is a metro-and-large-city phenomenon. Reliance Digital, Croma and the regional giants of the south and west fight over Mumbai, Bengaluru and Chennai. Eastern India — Bihar, Jharkhand, eastern Uttar Pradesh — was, for a long time, left to unorganised mom-and-pop shops with narrow ranges, no finance desk and no after-sales service. Aditya Vision's entire existence rests on that gap: it brought an air-conditioned, wide-range, EMI-enabled, service-backed showroom to towns that had never had one. That is why its margins and returns look nothing like a metro retailer's — lower rent, lower competition, higher loyalty — and why the durability question is simply whether that gap stays open as the country's biggest retailers finally look east.

Who walks in, and when

  • The aspirational small-town household — the core customer, upgrading from a first TV to a smart TV, or buying a first air-conditioner as incomes and electrification rise. Price-sensitive, but increasingly willing to trade up when an EMI makes the monthly outgo small.
  • The seasonal calendar rules demand — the year is spiky. Summer (April–June) is air-conditioner and refrigerator season and can make or break a year; the festive stretch (Dussehra–Diwali, roughly October–November) drives televisions and big-ticket buys. A hot summer or a strong festive season lifts the whole company.
  • The repeat buyer and the referral — in a tight regional community, service reputation compounds. Aditya Vision leans hard on same-day delivery, quick installation and a dedicated helpline, because a satisfied buyer in a small town tells the whole neighbourhood.
02

The store as the unit: a three-year slow burn

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a young rollout retailer.

Most people value a retailer on its average store. For Aditya Vision that average is misleading, because the chain is young and growing fast, so the average is dragged down by dozens of stores that simply haven't grown up yet. The correct unit of analysis is a single store across its life — and that life has a very particular shape.

A store roughly doubles in year two and keeps climbing toward ~₹16 Cr by year three as the town discovers it. Now hold that curve against the rollout. If Aditya Vision opens 30 stores in a year, it has just added a cohort of ₹6-crore babies to a base of ₹16-crore adults — and every blended metric the market watches (sales per store, sales per square foot, EBITDA margin) gets pulled down by the new arrivals, even though each one is precisely on track. The faster the company grows, the worse its averages temporarily look. That is not deterioration; it is arithmetic. Conversely, in a year of few openings the averages would flatter, because the mix ages. This is the trap that catches first-time readers of the P&L.

The device that only fits a young store-rollout retailer: read the cohort, not the average

Here is the construct to carry through the rest of this primer. Aditya Vision's reported margin in any year is really a weighted average of two very different populations — immature stores losing or barely making money, and mature stores throwing off cash. When management once noted that roughly 80% of sales came from the half of stores open 2.5 years or longer, it was telling you exactly this: the grown-ups carry the chain while the babies drink from it. So a compressing blended margin during a fast-expansion year is evidence the strategy is working, not failing — provided the mature cohort's own same-store growth stays healthy. Value the mature store at its steady economics; treat every new store as an investment that will look like a drag for two years and an asset thereafter. Miss this, and you will misread a good year as a bad one.

03

The cluster moat: why owning towns beats owning a brand

Aditya Vision's edge is not a product — it is regional density, built town by town.

A national retailer's advantage is a famous name and a huge catalogue. Aditya Vision has neither at national scale, yet it out-earns most of them in its own backyard. Its moat is a different kind: geographic density. By saturating a contiguous region with stores, a warehouse network, service teams and brand recognition, it builds an economic fortress that a newcomer cannot easily storm — because the newcomer would have to replicate the whole cluster, not just plant one shop.

Management calls its expansion the "creeping cluster" approach: rather than scatter stores across the map, it fills in one geography deeply before creeping outward to the adjacent one. Bihar first, then Jharkhand, then eastern Uttar Pradesh and Odisha, with Madhya Pradesh flagged as a next frontier. The logic is that density compounds several advantages at once.

The market-share numbers show the strategy's payoff. A CRISIL estimate puts Aditya Vision at over 50% of organised electronics retail in Bihar — a genuinely dominant position — and it is the largest organised player in Jharkhand. Those are not the shares of a company competing on price against everyone; they are the shares of a company that got there first, densely, and made itself the obvious choice.

Why density is a better defence than a big brand name

A national brand can parachute a single flagship store into Patna and advertise it heavily — but one store cannot offer the same-day delivery, the fast installation, the wide local range or the neighbour's recommendation that a fifteen-store cluster can. To match Aditya Vision in Bihar, a rival would have to build a comparable cluster — warehouses, service fleet, dozens of leases, local hiring — and eat years of immature-store losses while doing it, all to fight an incumbent already at scale with lower costs. That is a steep, unglamorous wall to climb, and it is exactly why regional retail density, in overlooked markets, is more durable than it looks. The vulnerability is the mirror image: the same density that protects Bihar has to be rebuilt from scratch in every new state, and an unfamiliar geography offers none of the home-turf intelligence.

04

What it sells, and where the margin hides

A basket of very different products with very different economics — and a deliberate tilt.

Aditya Vision stocks over 10,000 products from the major domestic and global brands, but for understanding the P&L only one distinction matters: the split between fat-margin appliances and thin-margin phones. The two behave like different businesses sharing a shop floor, and the balance between them moves the blended gross margin more than almost anything else.

The strategic point is that Aditya Vision does not treat phones as a profit centre. Phones are traffic — a low-margin, high-frequency reason for a customer to walk in, from which the store hopes to sell an accessory, an EMI, or a return visit for a bigger appliance. The profit engine is the large-appliance basket — air-conditioners, refrigerators and washing machines — where margins are healthiest and where rising rural incomes, electrification and summer heat drive structural upgrade demand. This is why a hot summer is worth so much: it sells the highest-margin products in the shop.

The counter-intuitive bit: the lowest-margin product is strategically vital

It would be tempting to say Aditya Vision should sell fewer phones and more fridges to protect margin. But that misreads the store. The phone is the hook that brings a young, first-time customer through the door and onto a finance plan; the relationship it starts is what sells that customer an air-conditioner two summers later. A pure-appliance store would be more profitable per sale and far emptier. The skill is running a deliberately mixed basket — thin-margin traffic drivers alongside fat-margin destination purchases — and steering the overall mix well enough that the blended margin holds while footfall keeps rising. The 100-bps margin dip in FY26 was the visible cost of leaning into phone-led traffic; the bet is that it feeds tomorrow's appliance sale.

05

The EMI engine: half the shop runs on credit

The fact that surprises everyone — an electronics chain that is quietly a lending storefront.

Here is the number that reframes the whole company. In FY25, roughly 43% of Aditya Vision's sales were financed — bought not with cash but on a consumer loan or card EMI arranged, on the spot, at the store. In markets where a large share of customers are under-banked and cash-constrained, the availability of a small monthly instalment is frequently the factor that converts a wish into a purchase. Aditya Vision is, functionally, a point-of-sale distribution channel for consumer credit as much as it is a shop.

Even at ~43%, finance is central — and the gap to peers running 55%+ suggests room to push further. More finance penetration means more affordability, more conversion, and a larger average ticket. Figures approximate.

Mechanically, Aditya Vision partners with banks, NBFCs and card companies that underwrite and carry the loan; the retailer's role is origination at the till. Crucially, Aditya Vision often subsidises part of the finance cost — a "no-cost EMI" is not free to someone; the store absorbs a subvention (a slice of the interest) to make the offer attractive. That is a real cost, buried in margin, and it is one reason a phone-and-finance-heavy quarter can look margin-light. The pay-off is conversion and ticket size: an affordable instalment turns a browser into a buyer and nudges that buyer up to a larger, higher-margin appliance.

The dependency to sit with — and it is invisible in the revenue line

Because so much of the basket rides on third-party credit, Aditya Vision's sales are quietly hostage to the willingness of finance partners to lend to its customer base. In an easy-credit environment, approval rates are high and the EMI desk hums; if lenders tighten — a rise in small-ticket consumer-loan defaults, a regulatory clampdown on unsecured lending, an NBFC funding squeeze — approval rates fall, and a chunk of demand simply evaporates at the counter even though the customer still wants the fridge. This is a genuine, structural exposure that a cash-only retailer never carries, and it never shows up as a line in the accounts. The health of Aditya Vision is therefore tied not only to the monsoon and the summer, but to the credit cycle — a dependency most people never associate with a small-town electronics shop.

Why this cuts both ways: consumer finance is Aditya Vision's single biggest demand accelerant in under-penetrated markets — it is arguably the reason the model works at all where cash budgets are tight. But it also imports the fragility of the lending system into a retailer's revenue. Watch finance-penetration percentage and finance-partner appetite as closely as footfall; when credit is flowing they are a tailwind, and when it stops they are the first thing to bite.
06

Inventory and working capital: the cost of the shelves

A retailer's cash is tied up in stock — and a growing chain ties up ever more.

Behind the showroom sits the least glamorous, most cash-hungry part of the business: inventory. Every store has to be full of televisions, air-conditioners and phones a customer might want today, and all of that stock is paid for before it sells. For a fast-growing chain, funding this ever-larger pile of goods is the central working-capital challenge — and it is why a profitable retailer still carries meaningful debt.

Inventory days have risen with scale and expansion — more stores, wider range, and stock built ahead of price rises and supply risk. At FY26 year-end the inventory pile stood near ₹840 Cr. More stock is more cash locked up, and part of why borrowings climb even in good years.

There is a deliberate element here, not just drift. Management has at times built inventory ahead of expected OEM price increases — buying cheap now to sell dearer later — and holds wider stock to guard against supply disruptions. That is a defensible, even shrewd, use of the balance sheet in an inflationary input environment. But it has a cost: the ~₹573 Cr of debt the company carried at FY26 exists largely to finance stock and store rollout, not because the business is structurally unprofitable. The direct-from-OEM buying model (with its early-payment discounts) and a lean cost culture partly offset this, but a rollout retailer is, by nature, a consumer of working capital as it grows.

Why cash flow can lag reported profit — and why that's normal here

A retailer opening dozens of stores a year will routinely show operating cash flow that trails its reported profit, because each new store has to be filled with unsold inventory and fitted out before it earns a rupee. This is not a red flag in itself — it is the signature of a healthy expansion. The discipline to watch is whether inventory days and debt are rising faster than the store base and sales, or roughly in step with them. In step is the price of growth; racing ahead would signal stock that isn't selling or an over-stretched balance sheet. For a chain compounding sales at ~30%, some balance-sheet expansion is simply the fuel bill for the rollout — the question is always whether the tank is filling in proportion to the distance travelled.

07

The founder, the family, and who controls it

A founder-run regional retailer, built store by store from a single Patna shop.

Aditya Vision is very much a founder's company. It was built and is still led by Yashovardhan Sinha, its Chairman and Managing Director, who grew a single electronics store in Patna into the dominant chain of eastern India. That founder-operator character explains a great deal about the company's deep local knowledge and its patient, cluster-by-cluster expansion — and it is also where an investor's governance attention belongs.

  • 2000s

  • mid-2010s

  • FY22

  • FY23–FY25
    Rapid rollout — over 100 stores added across the stretch; revenue climbs from ~₹1,322 Cr to ~₹2,260 Cr; consumer finance and same-store growth become the operating watchwords.
  • FY26
    Record year: revenue ~₹2,672 Cr (+18%), net profit ~₹117 Cr (+11%), 207 stores; Q4 revenue +28% and PAT +36%; final dividend ₹1.25/share; expansion into new states flagged.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter & management: the Sinha family, led by founder-CMD Yashovardhan Sinha, holds roughly 47% of the company (about 47.1% as of mid-2026). That is a controlling but not overwhelming stake — and it has drifted down over recent years as the family sold some holding into a rising market. Investors should confirm the exact promoter percentage in the latest shareholding filing.
  • Institutional presence: notably, institutions have built a substantial position — FIIs ~15% and DIIs ~22% as of mid-2026 — unusual conviction for a regional small-cap, and a sign the compounding story has been widely noticed. This also means the stock is more scrutinised, and more richly priced, than a typical Bihar-centric business.
  • Related interests: the founding family is associated with other retail ventures (including a separate grocery/consumer-retail entity). Concentrated founder control across multiple businesses is common in Indian family retail, but it puts the onus on minorities to watch related-party dealings, capital allocation and management bandwidth.
Why the founder factor cuts both ways: Aditya Vision's edge — knowing exactly which small town will support a store, hiring locally, running a lean rollout — is inseparable from its founder-operator DNA; this is not a business a committee could have built. But that same concentration means key-person dependence (the strategy lives substantially in one person's judgement), a promoter stake that has been trimmed rather than added to, and a family with other ventures competing for attention. None of these is a red flag on its own; together they are the governance lens through which a founder-led compounder should always be read.
08

The national threat — and why the east is different

The bear's core worry: what happens when Reliance and Croma finally come east.

The obvious risk to a regional champion is that the giants notice. Reliance Digital, Tata's Croma and the deep-pocketed online platforms have vastly more capital, buying power and brand than Aditya Vision, and all are pushing into smaller cities. If organised, well-funded competition floods Bihar and Jharkhand, the incumbent's fat regional margins could compress. This is the single most important debate about the durability of the business.

The counter-argument — the reason the incumbent has held on — is that a big-box format built for metros does not simply transplant into a Bihar district town. The economics that make Aditya Vision work (cheap rent, local hiring, dense service, first-mover loyalty, a finance desk tuned to local customers) are precisely what a national chain finds hardest to replicate profitably at small scale. A single Reliance Digital in a city is not a cluster; it lacks the same-day delivery and neighbourhood word-of-mouth that a fifteen-store Aditya Vision network provides. And online retail, for all its price advantage, still struggles with the large-appliance experience — the in-person demo, the instant EMI, the next-day installation of a heavy air-conditioner.

The genuine open question a first-timer should not pretend is settled

Regional retail moats are real but not permanent. History offers examples of dominant regional retailers who thrived for a decade and then ceded ground when national scale finally arrived with the patience to lose money locally for years. Aditya Vision's defence rests on a wager that its cluster economics are simply more profitable per store in these towns than a national rival's would be — so a giant would have to burn a lot of capital, for a long time, to dislodge an incumbent who is already the low-cost operator on its home turf. That wager has held so far, and the market-share data supports it. But it is a wager, not a law, and the honest bear case is that it only has to fail in a handful of the biggest towns to dent the returns. Watch the mature-store SSSG in cities where a national rival opens; that is where the erosion, if it comes, will show up first.

Same-store sales: the number that cuts through the noise

Strip out the new stores and one figure tells you if the engine is truly healthy.

Because a rollout retailer's blended averages are muddied by immature stores (section 02), the cleanest gauge of underlying health is same-store sales growth (SSSG) — how fast the stores open at least a full year are growing on their own. It answers the question that headline revenue growth cannot: is Aditya Vision genuinely selling more to the towns it already occupies, or merely bolting on new towns to keep the top line rising?

SSSG in the mid-to-high teens means the existing stores are growing strongly on their own — not just being averaged up by new openings. This is the vital-sign reading for a rollout retailer, and it has stayed robust. Figures from company commentary.

Sustained double-digit SSSG is a powerful signal for two reasons. First, it means the growth is not merely extensive but intensive — the towns Aditya Vision already dominates are deepening, as more households upgrade and finance penetration rises, not just multiplying. Second, high SSSG on the mature base is what mathematically rescues the blended metrics from the new-store drag: as long as the grown-up stores keep compounding in the teens, the immature-store dilution is a temporary, self-correcting phenomenon rather than a structural decline. It is the number that reconciles fast expansion with healthy underlying economics.

Why SSSG is the real scoreboard — and what would break it

If you could watch only one operating number for Aditya Vision, it should be SSSG on the mature store cohort. When it holds in the teens, the whole thesis is intact: old stores compounding, new stores diluting temporarily but on track, the rollout self-funding its own optics. The moment SSSG slips toward low single digits — especially in towns where a national rival has just opened — is the moment to worry, because it would signal that the incumbent's home-turf demand is being competed away or is simply maturing out. Revenue growth can be bought by opening stores; SSSG cannot be faked. It is the difference between a chain that is genuinely deepening its grip and one that is merely running faster to stand still.

10

Financials & valuation

A high-quality compounder on a demanding multiple — the question is not quality but price.

Aditya Vision's financial record is, frankly, excellent: revenue up roughly fivefold in five years, profit up faster, returns on capital in the high teens, all self-funded off a small regional base. That track record is exactly why the market has awarded it a rich rating — the stock trades on a demanding trailing earnings multiple, the kind reserved for retailers the market expects to keep compounding. Unlike a cheap cyclical, the debate here is not whether the business is good; it is whether the price already assumes a decade of flawless rollout.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A proven regional compounder with a long runway — priced as if the run continues.

Aditya Vision enters FY27 with real momentum: a record year behind it, SSSG in the high teens, a dominant home region, and a store runway that stretches across the east and into new states like Madhya Pradesh. What it must prove — to justify a premium multiple — is that it can keep opening stores that mature into ₹16-crore earners while defending its heartland against national chains and an occasionally fickle credit cycle. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Long, proven rollout runway — a repeatable store template with 6–8 month breakeven and 3–4 year payback, and thousands of under-served towns still ahead across the east and new states.
  • Genuine regional moat — >50% organised share in Bihar and #1 in Jharkhand, built on density, service and finance that a national chain finds hard to replicate profitably at small scale.
  • Robust same-store growth — SSSG in the mid-to-high teens shows the existing base is deepening, not just being averaged up by new stores.
  • Structural demand — rising rural incomes, electrification and appliance penetration in eastern India, amplified by a deep consumer-finance offering.
  • High returns, self-funded — ~19% ROE and a founder who has compounded the business patiently for two decades.

The bear case

  • Demanding valuation — a mid-fifties trailing P/E prices in years of flawless compounding; any slip in rollout or SSSG could hurt the stock disproportionately.
  • National competition coming east — Reliance Digital, Croma and online players have far deeper pockets; sustained entry into the core towns could erode the fat regional margin.
  • Credit-cycle dependence — ~43% of sales are financed; a tightening in consumer lending or NBFC funding could cut demand at the counter overnight.
  • Working-capital hunger & margin mix — rising inventory days and debt fund the rollout; a heavier phone mix and finance subvention pressure the thin blended margin.
  • Founder concentration & new-geography risk — key-person dependence, a trimmed promoter stake, and unproven economics as the cluster model is transplanted to unfamiliar states.
The bottom line

Aditya Vision is one of the few genuinely well-run regional retail franchises on the Indian market — a founder-built machine that turned overlooked eastern towns into a dominant, high-return chain, and did it store by patient store. The strategy is real, the moat is real, and the runway is long. The tension is entirely one of price: the market already treats it as a durable compounder and charges accordingly, so the reward for owning it is not a cheap entry but the continuation of a decade-long habit of opening stores that grow up into cash machines. The next test is concrete and datable: whether mature-store SSSG holds in the teens through the FY27 summer and festive seasons even as new-state stores are opened and national rivals probe the core towns. Keep the same-store engine humming and the rollout on schedule, and the premium is earned town by town; let SSSG fade in the heartland, and a fifty-plus multiple on a regional retailer will feel every bit as stretched as it looks. The whole company, in the end, is a wager that the next Bihar town wants an air-conditioned showroom as badly as the last one did — and that no one else gets there first.
