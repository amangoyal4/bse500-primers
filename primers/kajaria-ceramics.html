Kajaria Ceramics, the tile brand that rents its kilns

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Kajaria Ceramics makes tiles — the ceramic and vitrified squares that cover floors and walls in homes, shops and offices — plus a smaller range of bathroom sanitaryware, faucets and tile adhesives. It is India's largest tile manufacturer and the country's No.1 tile brand, selling roughly 118.5 million square metres of tiles a year through a network of 1,900+ dealers and 5,000+ retail touchpoints, run from Delhi by the founding Kajaria family.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A branded, near-debt-free cash machine. FY26 revenue was ~₹4,833 Cr with a ~17.9% EBITDA margin and ~₹485 Cr of net profit (+65% YoY). It earns a ~23% return on capital, carries almost no net debt, and returned cash via a ₹297 Cr buyback and a ₹14/share dividend. This is a consumer-facing brand that happens to sell a building material.
  • It doesn't fire most of its own tiles. Kajaria's nine plants can make ~88 MSM a year, yet it sells ~118 MSM. The gap — around a third of everything with the Kajaria name on it — is bought in from independent kilns in the Morbi cluster of Gujarat, then given Kajaria's brand, design and distribution. It is as much a brand-and-logistics company as a manufacturer.
  • Two forces set the tape. The structural tailwind is the unorganised-to-organised shift — branded players like Kajaria taking share from Morbi's hundreds of no-name units as buyers trade up on design and trust. The swing factor is natural gas: kilns run on gas, gas is a quarter to a third of a tile's cost, and its price whips margins around from year to year. FY26's profit jump was mostly the gas price falling back.
In plain terms

Kajaria is a premium label stuck onto a cost-driven commodity. The tile itself is fired clay — a low-tech, gas-guzzling product that a thousand small Gujarat kilns can also make. Kajaria's job is to make that commodity feel like a choice: designer collections, a trusted name a homeowner will pay 10–20% extra for, and a dealer within reach in almost every town. It manufactures where that makes sense and buys the rest from Morbi at a discount, keeping the brand margin for itself. The catch: the one input it cannot brand its way around — the gas that fires every tile — is priced by global energy markets, not by Kajaria.

01

Tiles 101: what they are and why anyone buys them

No construction background needed — just what's on the floor and why it was chosen.

A tile is a thin slab of clay (and minerals) that has been pressed into shape, printed or glazed with a surface, and fired in a kiln at 1,100–1,200°C until it turns hard and water-resistant. You buy tiles by the square metre, to cover a floor or a wall. Almost all demand comes from two occasions: new construction (a home, shop or office being built) and renovation (redoing an old floor, kitchen or bathroom). Renovation matters more than people expect — it is steadier than new-build and grows as India's housing stock ages.

The tile family, decoded

  • Ceramic tiles — the traditional product: a clay body with a glazed top. Softer, more porous, cheaper. Used mostly on walls (kitchens, bathrooms) and lighter-traffic floors. The commodity end of the range.
  • Vitrified tiles (GVT / PGVT) — fired hotter with more glass-forming minerals so the body vitrifies (turns glassy): denser, near-zero water absorption, far harder. This is the premium, fast-growing end — big-format floor tiles, wood- and marble-look designs. "GVT" is glazed vitrified; "PGVT" is polished glazed vitrified. Higher price per square metre, higher margin.
  • Polished / double-charge / full-body — variations on vitrified for commercial floors and high-wear areas, where the colour runs deeper into the tile so wear doesn't show.
Why the mix shift is the quiet profit lever

The single most useful thing to track inside a tile company is the drift from ceramic toward vitrified. A wall tile might sell for a few hundred rupees a square metre; a large-format designer PGVT floor tile sells for much more. As Indian buyers renovate and trade up — bigger tiles, marble looks, seamless floors — the average realisation per square metre rises even if the number of tiles sold barely moves. Kajaria's blended realisation sits around ₹360–365 per square metre; nudging that up through design and format is how a low-tech product earns a consumer-brand margin.

The demand drivers a generalist should hold in mind: housing and real-estate activity (the biggest single swing), renovation cycles, rising incomes and urbanisation (more homes, better finishes), and government housing and infrastructure spending. Tiles are a mid-to-late-cycle building product — they go in near the end of a build, so tile demand tends to lag a construction upturn by a few quarters.

02

The industry and the Morbi question

The one town that explains the whole tile market — and why it's both Kajaria's rival and its supplier.

India is the world's second-largest tile producer and its single largest exporter — a fact that surprises most people, who assume the tiles under their feet were imported. The domestic market is worth roughly US$10–11 billion and growing at high-single-digits. But its defining feature is fragmentation: around 70% of the industry is unorganised — hundreds of small, no-brand manufacturers — and almost all of them sit in one place.

Morbi: the tile capital most Indians have never heard of

Morbi, a town in Gujarat, produces an estimated ~90% of India's tiles from 1,800-plus kilns running around the clock. It is a genuine industrial cluster — cheap gas access (until recently), shared know-how, ceramic-machinery makers next door, and a port for exports. Morbi is why India can flood global markets: national tile exports run around 590 million square metres, roughly ₹20,000 Cr a year, most of it shipped from Morbi. It is simultaneously the source of the cheap, unbranded competition that pressures Kajaria's prices and, increasingly, the place Kajaria buys tiles from.

The structural bet behind every branded tile stock: organised players keep taking share from unorganised Morbi units. Kajaria alone holds ~17% of the organised market — the largest single share.

Why the share is shifting to brands

  • GST and formalisation. A unified tax and tighter compliance eroded the cash-economy cost edge that let tiny Morbi units undercut brands. Every crackdown on under-invoicing narrows the price gap.
  • Design and trust. As buyers renovate and premiumise, they want catalogues, warranties, consistent quality and a name — things a no-brand kiln can't offer. Trading up is inherently a move toward brands.
  • Gas shocks and consolidation. Spikes in natural-gas prices hit thinly-capitalised Morbi units hardest; some simply stop firing. Each crisis pushes volume toward players with balance sheets — a point we return to in section 08.
  • Import walls. India has used anti-dumping duties to keep out cheap Chinese and other imports, protecting domestic producers; abroad, India's own exporters face duties (US countervailing duties of ~3–3.5%, a fresh Mexican probe from 2025), which can redirect Morbi capacity back into the home market and intensify local competition.

03

The asset-light model: making some, buying the rest

The design choice that lets Kajaria grow faster than its own kilns — and the surprise most first-time readers miss.

Here is the fact that reframes the whole company: in FY26 Kajaria sold ~118.5 MSM of tiles but its own plants can only fire ~88 MSM. The difference — around 32 MSM, roughly one in every three-and-a-half tiles it sells — was manufactured by someone else: independent kilns in Morbi that produce to Kajaria's specification, which Kajaria then brands, quality-checks and sells as its own. Management expects outsourced volume to rise toward ~40 MSM. A big branded tile company, in other words, is deliberately not making a large slice of its own product.

The value Kajaria adds isn't only firing clay — it's the brand, the design library, quality control and the distribution that turns an anonymous Morbi tile into a premium purchase.

Why do it this way?

  • Capital-light growth. Building a tile plant is slow and capital-hungry. Outsourcing lets Kajaria add saleable volume without sinking cash into kilns — its ~23% return on capital owes a lot to this. It expands with its brand and its balance sheet, not just its concrete.
  • Cost arbitrage. Morbi's clustered, low-overhead units can make a basic tile cheaper than Kajaria's own plants. Kajaria buys at Morbi's cost and sells at Kajaria's price, pocketing the brand spread on the outsourced portion.
  • Flexibility and risk-spreading. It sources from multiple Morbi suppliers so no single kiln can hold it hostage, and can flex outsourced volume up or down with demand while keeping its own plants running full (the most efficient way to run a kiln, which you don't want to switch off).
  • The premium stays in-house. Kajaria concentrates its own plants on the higher-value vitrified and designer tiles, and outsources more of the commoditised end — keeping the hard-to-copy work close and renting out the easy-to-copy work.
The trade-off: the model hands away some control. Outsourced tiles carry Kajaria's name but not its factory floor, so quality assurance and consistency become a distribution-and-inspection discipline rather than an in-house guarantee. And the arbitrage only works while Morbi capacity is plentiful and cheap — the moment gas or consolidation tightens Morbi supply, the outsourced tiles cost more and the brand spread on them thins.
04

The company and who controls it

A Delhi family business that turned a single tile plant into the national brand.

Kajaria is a promoter-run family company. Ashok Kajaria set up the first plant at Sikandrabad, Uttar Pradesh, in the late 1980s with Spanish technical collaboration, and built the brand over three decades into the market leader. His sons Chetan Kajaria and Rishi Kajaria now run it as Joint Managing Directors. The promoter family holds ~47.7% — a large, aligned stake that keeps control firmly with the founders.

Timeline

  • 1990s–2000s

  • 2010s
    Shifts decisively into vitrified tiles; opens plants in the South (Srikalahasti AP, Balanagar Telangana) and Nepal, and sets up JV/subsidiary units in Morbi for glazed vitrified tiles.
  • 2014
    Enters bathware — sanitaryware and faucets — under the Kajaria brand, backed by investor WestBridge (which holds 15% of Kajaria Bathware).
  • ~2019
    Adds plywood and scales tile adhesives — attempts to become a broader home-surfaces brand.
  • FY26
    Record profit: PAT ~₹485 Cr (+65%) as gas costs eased; EBITDA margin back to ~17.9%. Announces a ₹297 Cr buyback and reports plywood as discontinued — pruning back to tiles + bathware + adhesives.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Kajaria family (~47.7%). Ashok Kajaria (Chairman & MD) with sons Chetan and Rishi Kajaria (Joint MDs) — a second-generation, hands-on family running both brand and operations.
  • Institutional partner: WestBridge (via Aravali Investment Holdings) owns 15% of the bathware subsidiary — a segment-level backer for the diversification push, alongside the usual mutual-fund and FII register on the listed parent.
  • Balance sheet: effectively net cash — token debt against ~₹3,000 Cr of reserves — funding capex, the adjacencies and buybacks internally rather than through borrowing.
05

The product ladder and premiumisation

Where the growth and the margin actually come from inside the tile business.

Tiles are ~88% of Kajaria's revenue; the rest is bathware and adhesives. Within tiles, the story is less about selling more squares and more about selling better ones. FY26 tile volume grew only ~3% (a soft, real-estate-linked year), so the work of moving the P&L falls to realisation — the average price per square metre — which climbs as the mix shifts up the ladder.

How Kajaria climbs the ladder

Three moves lift realisation without needing a housing boom. Format: bigger tiles (600×1200 mm and up, even slabs) command far more per square metre than a standard 300 mm square. Finish: polished glazed vitrified, marble- and wood-looks, anti-skid and specialty surfaces sell at a premium to plain glazed ceramic. Brand pull: a homeowner redoing a living room will pay up for a designer collection with a name behind it. The trap is discounting: when demand softens, Kajaria has at times cut prices to clear slow-moving stock (it flagged ~240 bp of lower realisation from SKU-rationalisation discounts in a recent quarter) — proof that realisation is a lever the market can push back down.

06

Bathware and the adjacencies

The bet that a tile brand can sell you the whole bathroom — and the drag it has carried.

Beyond tiles, Kajaria has tried to become a broader home-surfaces brand by extending into products a tile customer buys in the same renovation: sanitaryware (toilets, washbasins), faucets (taps and fittings), and tile adhesives (the specialist glue that fixes modern large tiles, replacing old cement mortar). Plywood was a fourth leg — now reported as a discontinued business in FY26, a tidy admission that not every adjacency worked.

Why adjacencies are logical but not free money

The rationale is real: the same dealer, the same renovating customer, the same brand trust — a tile buyer is a natural bathware and adhesive buyer, so distribution overlaps and each new line rides the existing network. But bathware and faucets are their own competitive worlds, dominated by focused specialists (Cera, HSIL/Hindware, Jaquar in fittings), where Kajaria is a challenger, not the leader. These lines have run at thinner margins than tiles and, at points, been a drag on group profitability while they scale. Management now guides to double-digit volume growth in bathware and sanitaryware — the question is whether the adjacencies eventually earn tile-like returns or stay a strategically-useful but margin-dilutive add-on. Pruning plywood is a sign of discipline about which bets to keep feeding.

07

Dealers, showrooms and the brand

The real asset isn't the kilns — it's the shelf space and the name.

If Kajaria outsources a third of its tiles, what exactly is the company you're buying? The answer is its distribution and brand. Tiles are heavy, bulky and locally bought — a homeowner or contractor wants to see them, and wants a dealer nearby. Kajaria's edge is that it has, over decades, built the widest branded footprint in the country: 1,900+ dealers feeding 5,000+ retail touchpoints, plus company showrooms and display galleries where designer collections are staged.

  • Reach as a moat. A new brand can copy a tile design overnight, but it cannot conjure 1,900 dealer relationships or 5,000 shelves. Distribution is the slow-built barrier that keeps Kajaria in front of the buyer at the moment of choice.
  • Showrooms sell the premium. Large-format and designer tiles are chosen on how they look in a room. Kajaria's galleries and dealer displays are where the higher-realisation vitrified range gets sold — the shop floor is the premiumisation engine.
  • The brand carries the outsourced tiles. Because customers trust the name, Kajaria can put its label on a bought-in Morbi tile and still command a premium — the brand is precisely what lets the asset-light model work.
  • Pull-through economics. Once a dealer stocks Kajaria tiles, adding Kajaria adhesives, faucets and sanitaryware to the same counter costs little incrementally — the network is a platform, not just a pipe.
Kajaria manufactures tiles, but what it owns is the shortest distance between a homeowner's renovation and a branded tile — the dealer down the road.
08

The gas whip — the input Kajaria can't brand around

The one force that decides margins from year to year — and, counter-intuitively, the one that most helps the brand.

Every tile is fired in a kiln, and in India those kilns run on natural gas. Gas is roughly a quarter to a third of a tile's manufacturing cost — the biggest single variable. Because tile prices can't be reset overnight, a move in the gas price flows almost straight to the bottom line: as a rule of thumb, a ~10% change in gas prices swings roughly 3–3.5% of gross margin. This is the whip that jerks tile-maker profits around regardless of how well the brand is doing.

Revenue rose only ~4% in FY26, yet PAT jumped ~65%. The extra profit came overwhelmingly from a ~440 bp margin gain as gas costs fell back — an outside force, not a management triumph.
The counter-intuitive part: a gas spike is bad for margins but good for the brand

When gas prices surge, Kajaria's own margins get squeezed like everyone's — but the unorganised Morbi units get squeezed far harder. They run on thin cash and old fixed-price contracts; when spot gas jumps (recent spikes ran well over 100%), many simply stop firing their kilns. Kajaria, with a net-cash balance sheet, 75–90 days of stock and the option to lean on multiple suppliers, keeps selling and takes share. So each gas crisis does two opposite things at once: it dents Kajaria's near-term margin and accelerates the organised-to-branded consolidation that is the long-term bull case. That is the structural quirk of this industry — the same shock that hurts the P&L this quarter strengthens the franchise over the cycle.

The uncomfortable implication for a shareholder: Kajaria's most important profit variable is set in global energy markets, not in Delhi. A brand can defend price; it cannot defend against the cost of gas. That is why a single great year (FY26) should be read carefully — a chunk of the ~65% profit jump is a gas-price gift that could reverse. The right way to judge the business is to look through the gas cycle at volume-share gains, not to extrapolate a peak-margin quarter.
09

Financials & valuation

A clean, cash-generative brand — priced like a consumer compounder, not a building material.

Kajaria's financial character is a high-return, near-debt-free brand with lumpy, gas-driven margins layered on top of steady volume. Revenue has ground higher for years; profit is far more volatile because of the gas whip. FY26 was a strong up-year on both counts.

The metric that settles the thesis

At ~₹19,700 Cr market cap on ~₹485 Cr of FY26 profit, Kajaria trades near 38× earnings and ~6× book — a premium consumer-durable multiple, well above where a pure building material would sit. That price is a bet on the organised-share story, not on margins (which are a gas lottery Kajaria doesn't control) and not on realisation alone (which the market can discount away). So the one number that proves or breaks the case is the gap between Kajaria's tile-volume growth and the industry's — the share it is actually taking from Morbi, cycle in and cycle out. FY26's ~3% tile volume is the live worry: in a soft demand year, the share-gain engine barely showed. Bulls need that wedge to widen into double digits as consolidation bites; if volume keeps tracking the market, the 38× multiple is paying for a premium the numbers aren't delivering.

The growth runway

Four levers, stacked — none of them a housing boom.

Kajaria doesn't need a construction super-cycle to grow; it has several self-help levers that work even in a flat market. The catch is that most of them are gradual, and one big one (volume) still depends on the housing demand it can't manufacture.

Three things a bull is really counting on

  • Demand normalising. FY26's ~3% volume was a soft-housing year. The bull case assumes real-estate and renovation demand re-accelerates, turning share gains into visible double-digit volume.
  • Gas staying calm. A benign gas environment lets the FY26 ~18% margin band (management guides ~17–19%) hold — and quietly keeps starving weak Morbi competitors.
  • Adjacencies turning the corner. Bathware and adhesives moving from margin drag to margin contributor would broaden the earnings base beyond the tile cycle.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine brand and a genuine cost problem, sharing one balance sheet.

Kajaria is a high-quality franchise: the market-leading tile brand, a net-cash balance sheet, ~23% returns, and a distribution reach rivals can't quickly copy. What it is not is a smooth compounder — it sits on top of a gas-priced commodity and a demand-cyclical end-market, and it is being asked to earn a consumer-brand multiple through both.

The bull case

  • Brand + distribution moat — No.1 tile brand, 1,900+ dealers, 5,000+ touchpoints; the widest branded shelf in the country.
  • Structural consolidation — organised share heading from ~46% to ~55%; GST, quality and gas shocks all push volume to Kajaria.
  • Asset-light, high-return — outsourcing lets it grow saleable volume without capex; ~23% RoCE, net cash, buybacks.
  • Premiumisation runway — mix shift to vitrified and large-format keeps lifting realisation without needing a boom.
  • Gas shocks strengthen the franchise — every crisis thins the Morbi field the brand competes against.

The bear case

  • Margins it doesn't control — gas sets the profit; FY26's ~65% PAT jump was largely a gas gift that can reverse.
  • Soft volume — ~3% FY26 tile growth means the share-gain engine barely showed in a weak-demand year.
  • Brutal competition — cheap unorganised Morbi supply, imports, and branded rivals (Somany, Prism, Orient Bell, AGL) cap pricing.
  • Adjacency drag — bathware and faucets face entrenched specialists (Cera, Hindware, Jaquar) and have diluted margins; plywood was already dropped.
  • Consumer-brand valuation on a cyclical — ~38× earnings and ~6× book leaves the stock exposed if volume or gas disappoints.
The bottom line

Kajaria has already proven the hard part — that a branded label can sit on a gas-fired commodity and earn ~23% on capital while the maker down the road in Morbi earns a fraction of that. The open question is whether the brand can keep widening its lead in the years the gas cycle isn't doing the work for it. The proof point to watch is concrete and near: whether the ~40 MSM Kajaria plans to outsource ships with its margin intact and its tile volume pulls clear of the industry as demand normalises. Rent Morbi's kilns without importing Morbi's price war, and the premium multiple is earned; let volume keep tracking the market through a calm-gas year, and FY26 will look less like a new base and more like a good weather report.
