00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

NTPC Green Energy Ltd (NGEL) is the renewable-energy arm of NTPC — the giant, state-owned company that generates roughly a quarter of India's electricity. NGEL builds and operates solar and wind farms and sells the electricity, almost entirely under long-term contracts, to state distribution companies and government agencies. It came to market in a landmark ₹10,000-crore IPO in November 2024 — one of India's largest ever — and as of March 2026 it runs about 10 GW of operational renewable capacity, having posted revenue of ₹2,858 crore and net profit of ₹522 crore in FY26.

Three facts frame the whole story:

  • It is one of India's biggest renewable-growth stories — and it has a state giant behind it. NGEL is targeting 60 GW of renewable capacity by 2032 — a six-fold jump from today — as the green engine of the NTPC group's larger clean-energy push. Being ~89% owned by NTPC is the whole edge: it borrows cheaply, wins land and contracts easily, and executes with the muscle of India's largest utility. In FY26 alone it added over 4 GW.
  • Its cash flows are annuity-like, not volatile. Almost every megawatt NGEL builds is tied to a 25-year power-purchase agreement (PPA) at a fixed tariff, with creditworthy off-takers like SECI and state discoms. There is no fuel to buy (sunlight and wind are free), so once a farm is built and contracted, it throws off a predictable, bond-like stream of cash — reflected in an eye-catching ~87% EBITDA margin.
  • The growth is being bought with a mountain of capex and debt — and the earnings are tiny for now. NGEL spent over ₹15,000 crore of capex in FY26 and net debt has climbed to ~₹31,700 crore. Profit is small relative to the huge asset base (ROE only ~3–4%), yet the stock trades on a P/E of well over 100× — because the market is paying for the 60 GW that's coming, not the 10 GW that's here.
In plain terms

Think of NTPC Green as a company that builds solar and wind farms and then rents them out for 25 years at a fixed price. The "rent" (the PPA tariff) is agreed up front and barely changes, and because sun and wind are free, almost all of that rent turns into profit once the loan on the farm is paid down. The catch is that building each farm costs a fortune up front, so NGEL is borrowing and spending heavily to build as many as it can, as fast as it can — aiming to be roughly six times its current size by 2032. Its secret weapon is its parent, NTPC: being the state power giant's green arm means cheap money, easy land, and guaranteed buyers. The debate is simple — it's a genuine, well-backed growth story, but the share price already assumes an enormous amount of that future has arrived.

01

Renewables economics 101: how a solar-and-wind developer earns

No engineering degree required — just how a farm full of panels turns into a 25-year income stream.

Before we go deep, it's worth understanding the machine — because a renewables developer like NTPC Green earns very differently from an old-fashioned coal or gas power plant. Once you grasp this one model, every number below makes sense.

The four-step money machine

  • 1 · Win a contract. The developer bids in a government auction (run by SECI, NTPC, or a state agency) to supply a block of renewable power. Winning gives it a PPA — a 25-year contract to sell electricity at a fixed tariff (say ₹2.5–4.2 per unit). The buyer (an "off-taker") is usually a state distribution company or SECI itself.
  • 2 · Build the farm. The developer spends a big lump of money up front — buying land, panels or turbines, and building the grid connection. A gigawatt of solar can cost roughly ₹4,000–5,000 crore. This is the capex-heavy part, and it's why the business is so capital-intensive.
  • 3 · Generate & sell. Sun and wind arrive for free; the farm converts them to electricity and delivers it to the off-taker at the fixed PPA price. There is no fuel bill — the single biggest cost of a coal plant simply doesn't exist here.
  • 4 · Bank the annuity. For the next 25 years, the farm quietly earns roughly the same money every year, whatever happens to fuel prices or the economy. After the construction loan is repaid, almost all of it is cash profit.
Why the EBITDA margin is a jaw-dropping ~87%

A supermarket keeps a few cents of every rupee; a renewables developer keeps almost the whole thing. Because there is no fuel to buy and very little to run (a solar farm has almost no moving parts), once the farm is built, nearly every rupee of revenue drops through to operating profit — hence NGEL's ~87% EBITDA margin. But — and this is the crucial twist — that fat operating margin then meets two big costs a coal plant doesn't have in the same way: heavy depreciation (writing down the enormous cost of the asset over its life) and heavy interest (on the debt used to build it). So "operating profit" is huge, but "net profit" is modest. Keep this gap between EBITDA and PAT in mind — it explains almost everything odd about NGEL's numbers.

02

The company and the NTPC parent

Why "who owns it" is the most important sentence in this whole primer.

NTPC Green Energy is the renewable-energy vehicle of NTPC Ltd — the Maharatna, majority government-owned utility that operates ~89 GW of installed capacity and generates roughly a quarter of all the electricity in India. NGEL was carved out to hold and grow the group's solar and wind assets, listed on the exchanges in November 2024, and remains ~89% owned by NTPC. To understand NGEL you have to understand that it is, in effect, the green growth arm of the Indian state's power giant — and that this parentage is its single biggest competitive advantage.

Ownership & the parent relationship

  • Promoter — NTPC Ltd: ~89%. This is an overwhelmingly parent-controlled company. NTPC is itself majority-owned by the Government of India, so NGEL sits firmly inside the public sector. The tiny ~11% public float is part of why the stock is so volatile and richly priced.
  • What the parent brings — the whole thesis. Being NTPC's green arm delivers advantages a standalone developer would kill for: a low cost of capital (NTPC's balance sheet and sovereign-adjacent credit), land and clearances (a state giant navigates acquisition and permitting far more easily), PPA access and off-taker trust (NTPC itself is a huge, creditworthy power buyer and route-to-market), and deep project-execution muscle honed over decades of building power plants at scale.
  • The governance nuance. The flip side of a 90%-parent, government-linked structure is that NGEL's strategy, capital allocation and even asset transfers are shaped by the group and, ultimately, by policy priorities — not purely by minority-shareholder returns. Related-party dealings with NTPC are a permanent feature. This is a company where the parent's goals and the government's decarbonisation agenda drive the bus.
Why the parent matters more here than for almost any peer

Every renewables developer faces the same three enemies: getting cheap money, getting land, and getting a buyer. A private developer like ReNew or Adani Green has to fight for all three. NTPC Green inherits all three from its parent. NTPC can raise debt at some of the lowest rates in Indian corporate finance; as a state giant it can acquire land and clear approvals where private players stall; and it has an in-house, deeply creditworthy off-taker in NTPC's own trading arm plus trusted relationships with every state discom. That's why the 60 GW target — which would sound fanciful from a startup — is genuinely credible here. The trade-off is that you're buying a minority slice of a company run for a state parent's strategic ends, so you take the governance and capital-allocation strings that come with that.

03

The capacity engine — 10 GW and climbing fast

The core asset: a portfolio of solar and wind farms, mostly bolted onto giant parks.

The heart of NTPC Green is its fleet of operating farms. As of 31 March 2026 the group crossed 10 GW — 10,076 MW of operational capacity, having added a record 4,175 MW in FY26 (of which 2,065 MW came in the March quarter alone). The portfolio is solar-heavy with a meaningful wind component, spread across resource-rich states — above all the enormous Khavda renewable park in Gujarat.

What the fleet is made of

  • Solar is the backbone. The bulk of NGEL's operating capacity is utility-scale solar, concentrated in a handful of mega-parks. The Khavda park in Gujarat alone contributes ~2,560 MW, with the Khavda-I (1,255 MW) and Khavda-II blocks fully or nearly commissioned in FY26. Building at "park" scale — shared land, transmission and infrastructure — is cheaper and faster per megawatt.
  • Wind adds diversity and firmness. A meaningful slice of the portfolio is wind, which usefully generates at different times of day and year than solar (wind often blows harder at night and in the monsoon), smoothing the overall output profile.
  • Hybrid, storage & round-the-clock projects are the growing frontier — combining solar + wind (+ increasingly batteries) at one site to deliver steadier, more valuable power. NGEL has won several "firm and dispatchable renewable" and round-the-clock tenders.
  • The pipeline dwarfs the operating fleet. Counting projects under construction and contracted-but-not-yet-built, NGEL's total portfolio is roughly ~19–20 GW — i.e. for every gigawatt operating, there's roughly another already in the build-and-contract pipeline. That's what makes the growth visible rather than hypothetical.
Why "parks" and scale are the developer's real skill

Anyone can put solar panels on a roof. The hard, valuable skill in utility-scale renewables is doing it at gigawatt scale, on time and on budget — assembling thousands of acres of land, arranging the high-voltage grid connection, procuring panels and turbines cheaply, and commissioning block after block on schedule. A giant shared "park" like Khavda lets a developer stamp out capacity far more cheaply than scattered small sites. NTPC Green's edge is that its parent has done exactly this — build big power infrastructure — for decades. The company that can add 4 GW in a single year, as NGEL did in FY26, is demonstrating precisely the execution capability the whole 60 GW plan depends on.

04

The 60 GW ambition — the defining story

The one thing an analyst leads with: a six-fold build-out, backed by the NTPC balance sheet.

If you remember one thing about NTPC Green, make it this: it is in a full-tilt, multi-decade build-out. NGEL is the vehicle for the NTPC group's target of 60 GW of renewable capacity by 2032 — part of NTPC's broader goal of ~149 GW of total capacity by then. From ~10 GW today, that is a plan to become roughly six times larger in six years, transforming a mid-sized developer into one of the largest renewable-power companies on earth.

Why this ambition is credible — and why it's still hard

An aggressive target is cheap; a funded, contracted, executable one is not. What makes NGEL's 60 GW plausible where a startup's would be fantasy is the combination behind it:

  • A parent that can fund it. Reaching 60 GW implies well over ₹2.5–3 lakh crore of cumulative capex across the decade. NTPC's balance sheet, credit rating and cash generation make that financeable at a low cost of capital that private peers cannot match.
  • Demand is not the constraint. India's national renewable targets (500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030) mean SECI and the states are auctioning capacity continuously. NGEL doesn't have to find buyers — it has to win auctions and build. Most of what it builds is contracted before construction.
  • Proven execution cadence. Adding 4 GW in FY26 and guiding to ~8 GW/year shows the machine can scale. The parent's decades of infrastructure delivery de-risk the "can they actually build it?" question.
What sets NTPC Green apart from NHPC and JSW Energy: NHPC is a regulated hydro generator earning a fixed return on a slow-growing asset base — stable but sleepy. JSW Energy is a diversified merchant-and-thermal-plus-RE generator leaning on energy storage. NTPC Green is neither: it is a near-pure, contracted, fast-scaling solar-and-wind developer with a state parent's balance sheet — closer in kind to Adani Green or ReNew, but with public-sector backing and a lower cost of capital. Its lead metric isn't a P/E or a dividend; it's gigawatts added per year against the 60 GW goal.

The honest risk in the ambition

The plan's credibility rests on execution and funding staying on track for the better part of a decade. Any of the following would slow it: module or land bottlenecks, transmission delays (a chronic Indian problem — farms built with nowhere to send the power), rising interest rates raising the cost of the debt-funded build, or tariff pressure as auctions get more competitive. The target is a direction of travel backed by real capability, not a guarantee — and the gap between "10 GW built" and "60 GW promised" is exactly what the valuation debate (§10) is about.

05

Long-term PPAs — the annuity that makes it all work

Why a solar farm is really a 25-year, inflation-resistant bond wearing a hard hat.

The reason investors treat NTPC Green as a quality growth story rather than a speculative one is the nature of its revenue: it is overwhelmingly locked into long-term power-purchase agreements. Nearly every megawatt is tied to a 25-year PPA at a fixed tariff, signed with a creditworthy off-taker before the farm is even built. This is what converts a capital-intensive construction business into a stable, predictable annuity machine.

What a PPA is, in plain English — and why it's so valuable

A power-purchase agreement is simply a long contract that says: "I will sell you this much electricity for the next 25 years at this fixed price." For a renewables developer, that's close to magic. Because there's no fuel cost, the tariff is essentially all revenue against a fixed, up-front build cost — so once the loan is paid off, the farm is a cash geyser for the rest of the contract. And because the price is locked for 25 years, the developer doesn't care what happens to power markets, coal prices or the economy. That predictability is why lenders happily fund these projects and why investors pay a premium for the earnings: a rupee you can count on for 25 years is worth far more than a rupee that swings with the spot market. A contracted renewable farm is, financially, one of the closest things in the real economy to a long-dated inflation-resistant bond.

Who buys the power — the off-takers

  • SECI (Solar Energy Corporation of India) — a government-owned central agency that auctions renewable capacity and then acts as the intermediary buyer, on-selling to states. A SECI-backed PPA carries an implicit central-government comfort, making it a high-quality contract.
  • State distribution companies (discoms) — the state electricity utilities that ultimately supply homes and businesses. These are the classic off-takers, though some carry weaker credit and a history of delayed payments.
  • NTPC itself and other agencies (GUVNL, Indian Railways, etc.) — NGEL sells to a spread of creditworthy buyers; as of its IPO disclosures it had ~15 off-takers across dozens of solar and wind projects, diversifying counterparty risk.

A merchant generator gambles on tomorrow's power price. NGEL locks the price for 25 years before it turns a shovel — trading away spot upside for bankable, financeable certainty. That certainty is what lets it borrow so much, so cheaply.

The counterparty catch — why PPAs aren't risk-free

PPAs de-risk price, but they leave one real exposure: will the buyer pay, and pay on time? India's state discoms are famously stretched financially, and delayed payments to generators are a recurring sector headache. NGEL mitigates this by skewing toward central/SECI-backed contracts and stronger off-takers, and by the implicit backing of being an NTPC company. Tariffs are the other watch-item: as auctions get more competitive, newly-won PPAs come at lower tariffs, so growth increasingly comes at slimmer per-unit margins. Neither undoes the annuity model — but they're the fine print a careful reader keeps in view.

Renewables made clean power cheap. The 25-year PPA is what makes it bankable — and bankability is what lets NTPC Green borrow tens of thousands of crores to build the future.
06

Capex, funding & leverage — the defining financial dynamic

The enormous spend to build tens of GW, how it's paid for, and the risk that comes with it.

Here is the central financial tension of the whole business. Building renewable capacity is front-loaded and expensive: you spend the money now and earn it back over 25 years. To hit its targets, NGEL is running one of the largest capex programmes in Indian power — over ₹15,000 crore of capex in FY26 alone — and it funds that with a mix of equity (including the ₹10,000-crore IPO) and, increasingly, debt. Net debt has climbed to roughly ₹31,700 crore, and the board has cleared up to ₹5,000 crore of fresh borrowing to keep the build going.

Why heavy debt is normal — but still needs watching

For a contracted-renewables developer, borrowing heavily isn't reckless — it's the right way to build, because each loan is matched to a 25-year PPA that will repay it with predictable, low-risk cash. Lenders love this "project finance" logic: the asset pays for itself. So a debt/equity ratio of ~1.5× on a book of contracted farms is far safer than the same ratio on a merchant plant gambling on spot prices. But — leverage cuts both ways. The more you borrow, the more the whole plan depends on (1) building on time and budget, (2) off-takers paying, and (3) interest rates staying manageable. Finance costs already run near ₹900 crore a year and will climb as debt grows. If rates rise or a big project slips, the debt becomes a heavier drag — and with modest current earnings, there's limited cushion. The right thing to track, year after year, is whether the growing, contracted cash flow keeps outrunning the growing debt.

07

Green hydrogen, storage & new energy — the optionality

The bets beyond plain solar and wind — huge, early-stage, and potentially transformative.

NTPC Green isn't only building solar and wind farms. It is positioning for the next layer of the energy transition — green hydrogen, energy storage, and round-the-clock renewable supply. These are early-stage and capital-hungry, but they're where the parent's ambition and India's policy tailwinds point, and they give NGEL optionality that a pure solar developer lacks.

Green hydrogen — the Pudimadaka mega-bet

NGEL is the lead developer of the Pudimadaka Green Hydrogen Hub in Andhra Pradesh — the first hub under India's National Green Hydrogen Mission, with the Prime Minister laying its foundation stone in January 2025. The vision is vast: a hub anchored by ~7 GW of dedicated renewable energy producing green hydrogen and derivatives (green ammonia, methanol, urea, sustainable aviation fuel), with an eye on exporting green chemicals. This is a multi-year, multi-lakh-crore ambition — enormous optionality if the global green-hydrogen economy takes off, but unproven and years from meaningful earnings.

What green hydrogen is, and why the fuss

Hydrogen burns (or reacts in a fuel cell) with no carbon emissions — the only by-product is water. The problem is that today most hydrogen is made from natural gas, which emits CO₂. "Green" hydrogen is made by using renewable electricity to split water (electrolysis) — genuinely clean, but currently expensive. It matters because it can decarbonise the things batteries can't easily reach: steel-making, fertiliser, shipping, long-haul industry. For NGEL, green hydrogen is a way to turn its cheap renewable power into a high-value, exportable green product. It's a big, speculative call on where the energy world is heading — think of it as a long-dated option, not a near-term earner.

Storage & round-the-clock power

  • Battery storage (BESS) and pumped hydro let NGEL soak up surplus midday solar and release it at the evening peak — turning intermittent renewables into firm, on-demand power. As India's grid fills with solar and wind, storage becomes the scarce, high-value layer, and NGEL is bidding into "firm and dispatchable renewable" and round-the-clock tenders that pair generation with storage.
  • Why it matters: a buyer will pay a premium for power available whenever needed rather than only when the sun shines. Owning storage lets NGEL sell a more valuable, 24×7 green product — and defends against the falling tariffs of plain daytime solar.

None of this is yet a big earnings contributor — the core business is still building solar and wind under PPAs. But the new-energy layer is real, well-funded by group ambition, and aligned with national policy. For a long-horizon investor it's genuine upside optionality; for a sceptic it's another call on capital that has to prove its returns.

Acquisitions & the ONGC JV — buying scale to hit the target

How much of the growth is built vs bought — and the partnerships accelerating the ramp.

Most of NGEL's growth is organic — winning auctions and building farms — but it is also buying capacity to accelerate toward 60 GW, and doing so partly through a marquee joint venture with ONGC (another government-owned energy giant). Buying operating capacity is faster than building it, and pooling with ONGC brings extra balance-sheet firepower.

Why two state giants teaming up is a big deal

ONGC (oil & gas) and NTPC (power) are two of India's largest state-owned energy companies. Both need to decarbonise and diversify as the world shifts from fossil fuels to clean energy. By pooling into a JV, they combine two enormous balance sheets and two sets of relationships to buy and build renewables at a scale neither would attempt alone — and to compete with the private heavyweights (Adani, ReNew). For NGEL, the JV is a force-multiplier on the 60 GW plan: more capital, more platforms, faster. It also underlines the theme running through this whole primer — NTPC Green's advantages flow from being plugged into the machinery of the Indian state's energy transition.

Financials & the earnings puzzle

Fat operating margins, growing revenue — and a net profit that looks tiny next to the asset base.

FY26 (year ended March 2026) was a year of strong top-line and operating growth but only modest profit growth: revenue rose ~29% to ₹2,858 crore, operating EBITDA rose ~29% to ₹2,475 crore (a ~87% margin), but net profit grew only ~10% to ₹522 crore. Understanding why profit lags so far behind revenue and EBITDA is the key to reading this company correctly.

The earnings puzzle, solved: where does the ~87% margin go?

Look at the gap: ₹2,858 Cr revenue → ₹2,475 Cr EBITDA → but only ₹522 Cr net profit. Two costs sit between EBITDA and the bottom line, and both are huge for a capital-intensive builder. First, depreciation: NGEL is writing down the enormous cost of all those farms over their ~25-year lives — a big, non-cash charge that grows every time a new farm switches on. Second, interest (~₹887 Cr in FY26): the cost of the debt used to build. So the very thing driving growth — building lots of new, debt-funded capacity — also loads on depreciation and interest that hold back reported profit in the near term. That's why Q4 FY26 profit actually fell year-on-year even as capacity and revenue jumped: the new plants added cost (depreciation + interest) faster than they added profit in their first months. As those farms mature and their debt amortises, the profit should catch up — but for now, the bottom line understates the cash-generating power of the fleet, which is exactly why analysts watch EBITDA and capacity, not just PAT.

10

Valuation — the premium debate

A stock priced for the 60 GW that's coming, not the 10 GW that's here.

NTPC Green trades at a valuation that only makes sense if you look years ahead. At a share price around ₹95–96 and a market cap of roughly ₹80,600 crore (July 2026), the stock sits on a trailing P/E well north of 100× (reported ~140–150×) and price-to-book around 4.2× — against a current ROE of only ~3–4%. On today's earnings, that is extraordinarily expensive. The entire case for owning it rests on the future capacity and cash flows, not the present ones.

Why a 100×+ multiple on a "utility" — and why bears push back

A traditional utility is valued like a bond: steady, cheap multiple, fat dividend. NTPC Green is valued like a growth stock because the market is looking straight past today's ~3% ROE to a company that could be six times larger by 2032, throwing off six times the (high-margin, contracted) EBITDA, funded cheaply by a state parent. If the 60 GW lands and earns decent returns, today's earnings massively understate the future — so the "expensive" multiple is really on tomorrow's profit. That's the bull maths, and it's why some investors happily pay up for the scarcest thing in Indian markets: a large, clean, state-backed renewables compounder. The bears counter with three hard facts: (1) ROE is genuinely tiny right now and there's no guarantee the returns on all this capital rise much; (2) the balance sheet is levering up fast; and (3) at 140×+ earnings, almost any stumble — a tariff disappointment, a transmission delay, a rate rise, a discom payment problem — could trigger a sharp de-rating (the stock has already fallen well below its post-listing highs). Both camps stare at the same 60 GW plan and disagree entirely on the price to pay for it.

The market isn't pricing NTPC Green for the 10 GW it runs today — it's pricing it for the 60 GW it's trying to become by 2032.

Of all the big names, NGEL is closest to Adani Green and ReNew in what it does (build and contract solar + wind at scale), but closest to NHPC and NTPC in who owns it (the Indian state). 12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine, state-backed renewables compounder — priced for the growth to actually arrive.

NTPC Green is one of the clearest ways to own India's renewable build-out: a fast-scaling solar-and-wind developer with annuity-like contracted cash flows and the balance sheet of the country's biggest power utility behind it. The debate is not whether the growth is real — it plainly is — but about execution, funding, returns, and the price you pay.

The bull case

  • Enormous, credible growth runway — a target of 60 GW by 2032 from ~10 GW today, with ~8 GW/year guidance and 4 GW already added in FY26.
  • Unmatched parent backing — ~89% NTPC ownership brings a low cost of capital, land, PPA access and execution muscle no private peer can match.
  • Annuity-quality cash flows — near-100% contracted under 25-year PPAs with SECI/state off-takers; ~87% EBITDA margin; no fuel risk.
  • New-energy optionality — the Pudimadaka green-hydrogen hub, storage and round-the-clock projects offer long-dated upside.
  • Earnings should catch up — as young, debt-funded farms mature and debt amortises, ROE and PAT should rise from today's depressed base.

The bear case

  • Extreme valuation — P/E of 140×+ and P/B ~4.2× on ~3–4% ROE leave almost no room for any disappointment.
  • Rising leverage — net debt ~₹31,700 Cr, debt/equity ~1.5× and climbing; finance costs already ~₹887 Cr and rising; free cash flow negative by design.
  • Thin current returns — the whole thesis needs the huge capital deployed to earn its cost of capital as it scales; that's still to be proven.
  • Execution & sector risks — transmission delays, module/land bottlenecks, falling auction tariffs, and stretched discom off-takers who can pay late.
  • State-ownership strings — ~89% parent control, related-party dealings, and policy-driven priorities mean minority holders aren't fully in the driver's seat.
The bottom line

NTPC Green is a genuine, high-quality bet on the single biggest structural theme in Indian power — the renewable build-out — wrapped in the funding advantages of the state's largest utility. The business risk is low: contracted, no-fuel, annuity cash flows behind a six-fold growth plan that its parent can credibly fund. The financial and valuation risks are what dominate. The story over the next few years will be told in a handful of numbers: the pace of capacity commissioning (is the 60 GW on track?), the net-debt trajectory and interest cost (is the growth financeable as rates move?), and above all whether ROE climbs as the young fleet matures (do all these gigawatts actually earn?). Deliver those, and a premium multiple is at least arguable; stumble on funding, tariffs or execution with a 140×-earnings stock, and there is a long way to fall. It is, in the truest sense, a stock you buy for 2032, not for 2026.
