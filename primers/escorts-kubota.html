Escorts Kubota, India's tractor major, now a Kubota company

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Escorts Kubota makes tractors and construction equipment. It is one of India's oldest and best-known tractor houses — the maker of the Farmtrac and Powertrac brands — and today the #3–4 player in the Indian tractor market, especially strong across North India. The twist that defines the company now: after decades run by the founding Nanda family, it is majority-owned and controlled by Japan's Kubota Corporation, a global agricultural-machinery giant, which raised its stake, renamed the company "Escorts Kubota" in 2022, and is reshaping it into Kubota's India manufacturing and export base.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A cyclical tractor major having a good year. FY26 (year ended March 2026) was a record: revenue ~₹11,473 Cr (+12.6%), EBITDA ~₹1,513 Cr (+28.5%) at a ~13.2% margin, and a record 1,33,670 tractors sold (+15.7%). Tractors (the "Agri Machinery" segment) are ~85% of revenue; construction equipment is the other ~15%.
  • It just got simpler — and richer in cash. In FY26 Escorts Kubota sold its third leg, the Railway Equipment Division, to Sona Comstar (Sona BLW) for an enterprise value of ₹1,600 Cr, to focus on tractors and construction. That one-off gain lifted reported profit to ~₹2,409 Cr (vs ~₹1,381 Cr from continuing operations), and swelled an already-huge cash pile to ~₹8,000 Cr+ of net cash on a debt-free balance sheet.
  • Two stories in one stock. On the reported numbers it looks cheap (~15× earnings), but that multiple is flattered by the railway sale; on clean continuing profit it is closer to ~23×. The bull case isn't the cheapness — it's the Kubota optionality: a global parent turning Escorts into an export hub (a new greenfield plant, higher-horsepower tractors, Kubota's worldwide dealer network) that could reduce the company's dependence on India's boom-and-bust tractor cycle.
In plain terms

Think of Escorts Kubota as a proven Indian tractor brand that a Japanese multinational has bought the controlling wheel of. The domestic business — selling Farmtrac and Powertrac tractors to Indian farmers — is a good, cash-generative franchise, but it rises and falls with the monsoon, crop prices and rural mood, so profits are lumpy. Kubota's plan is to bolt this onto its global machine: use India's low-cost factories to build tractors for the world, feed in Kubota's technology and higher-power models, and sell through Kubota's dealers abroad. If that works, a cyclical, mostly-India tractor maker slowly becomes a steadier, export-fed global supplier. The catch: that transformation is years away and still small today, so for now you own a cash-rich cyclical with a long-dated option attached.

01

How a tractor maker actually earns

No engineering degree required — just the handful of levers that drive the P&L.

A tractor company is, financially, a volume-times-price business with heavy operating leverage. It designs and assembles machines, sells them through a dealer network, and earns a margin on each one. Because a big chunk of its costs (factories, R&D, fixed staff, dealer support) are fixed, how many units it ships in a year swings profits far more than revenue — which is exactly why a cyclical demand pattern matters so much. Five levers tell you most of the story.

The levers that move the numbers
LeverWhat it means (and Escorts Kubota's number)
Volume (units)How many tractors are sold in a year — the single biggest driver. FY26: a record 1,33,670 tractors (+15.7% YoY).
Realisation (₹/unit)The average price per tractor, driven by horsepower (HP) mix and features. Higher-HP tractors sell for more and carry richer margins; premiumisation lifts this.
Market shareThe company's slice of total industry volume. Escorts Kubota held ~10.9% of the domestic market in FY26 (up from ~9.9%) — its share is much higher in North India.
EBITDA marginOperating profit as a % of sales — the payoff from scale, pricing and cost control. FY26: ~13.2%, up sharply from ~11% as volumes and mix improved.
Operating leverageBecause costs are largely fixed, a good year (high volumes) expands margins fast — and a bad monsoon year compresses them just as fast. This is the whole cyclical swing.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (Units × price-per-unit × margin) − fixed costs. So Escorts Kubota grows in three ways — sell more tractors (volume, tied to the farm cycle), sell dearer tractors (higher-HP, more features, exports), and run the factories harder (operating leverage). The reason the stock is called "cyclical" is that the first lever — units — is largely outside the company's control: it's set by rain, crop prices and rural cash flow. In a good year, rising volume, richer mix and fixed-cost leverage all pull margins up together (as in FY26); in a drought year, they all pull down together. That symmetry is the defining feature of every listed tractor maker.

Why horsepower and mix matter

  • HP bands — Indian tractors span roughly 20HP to 75HP+. The mass market sits around 31–50HP; the fastest-value-adding growth is in higher-HP tractors (50HP+), where realisations and margins are better. This is precisely the band Kubota's technology targets.
  • Farmtrac vs Powertrac — Escorts runs a two-brand strategy: Farmtrac is the premium, higher-HP, better-featured line; Powertrac is the value, fuel-efficient workhorse. Splitting brands lets one company chase both the aspirational and the price-sensitive farmer without cannibalising itself.
  • Non-tractor add-ons — engines, implements, spares and financing tie-ups add revenue per customer and smooth the pure-tractor cycle a little.
02

The Kubota takeover: from a family firm to a Japanese-controlled company

The most important corporate event in Escorts' history — read this once and the whole thesis clicks.

Escorts was, for three-quarters of a century, a Nanda-family industrial house — founded in 1944, it pioneered farm mechanisation in India and built iconic products from Rajdoot motorcycles to Farmtrac tractors. What changed everything was a step-by-step takeover by Kubota Corporation of Japan — a ~130-year-old, multi-billion-dollar global maker of tractors, engines, construction and water/agri equipment — which moved from a small shareholder to the controlling promoter, and renamed the listed company Escorts Kubota Limited in 2022.

How the takeover happened, step by step

  • 1944–48
    Brothers H.P. Nanda and Yudi Nanda found Escorts as a trading agency; it soon pioneers farm mechanisation in India, importing and then building farm machines.
  • 1961
    Sets up its Faridabad plant and rolls out its first tractor via a technical tie-up with URSUS of Poland — the start of the tractor business.
  • 1969
    Launches construction equipment (cranes, dumpers) — later the pioneer of India's "Pick-'n-Carry" hydraulic mobile cranes, a segment it still leads.
  • 1990s
    Establishes the Farmtrac (premium) and Powertrac (value) tractor brands that anchor the business today.
  • Mar 2020
    Kubota enters: takes a ~10% stake in Escorts for ~₹1,042 Cr via preferential allotment, while Escorts buys 40% of Kubota's Indian arm — cementing a deepening alliance.
  • Nov 2021 – Jun 2022
    Kubota commits ~₹9,400 Cr to take control — a ₹1,873 Cr preferential issue plus an open offer — lifting its stake to ~44.8%. The company is renamed Escorts Kubota Limited; Kubota becomes joint promoter alongside the Nandas.
  • 2023–24
    Residual Escorts–Kubota joint ventures are amalgamated into the listed entity, and share cancellations lift Kubota's holding to ~54% — clear majority control. Total promoter (Kubota + Nanda) stands at ~68%.
  • FY26
    Escorts Kubota divests its Railway Equipment Division to Sona Comstar for ₹1,600 Cr and announces a greenfield export plant in Uttar Pradesh — the clearest signs yet of a Kubota-driven refocus on agri + construction and exports.

Who controls and runs it now

  • Kubota Corporation — the controlling promoter with roughly 54%. Kubota is a global top-tier agri-machinery company (strong in Japan, the US, and South-East Asia), and its motive is explicit: it expects the basic-tractor market to keep expanding in emerging economies, above all India, and wants an India manufacturing-and-export base to serve them.
  • The Nanda family — still on board with ~11–12%, and Nikhil Nanda (grandson of the founder) continues as Chairman & Managing Director. So this is not a hostile takeover that ejected the founders — it's a controlled transition where the family stays involved under Japanese ownership.
  • Total promoter holding ~68% — leaving a relatively small ~32% public float, which supports the share price but also raises the perennial minority-shareholder question of what a dominant parent does with the company's large cash pile (see §08).
Why the parentage is the whole point

A standalone Indian tractor maker is a fine but cyclical, largely domestic business — it lives and dies by the Indian monsoon. Bolt it onto Kubota, and three things change. First, technology: access to Kubota's global engineering, especially in higher-horsepower and more sophisticated machines where Escorts was historically weaker. Second, distribution: the potential to sell India-built tractors through Kubota's worldwide dealer network — an export leg Escorts could never have built alone. Third, discipline and focus: the railway sale and the export-plant plan show a parent willing to prune non-core businesses and invest for a global role. The investment question is simply how much of that promise actually shows up in the numbers, and how fast.

The businesses: two engines today, three until FY26

Tractors dominate; construction equipment rides on; railway was sold off.

Historically Escorts ran three businesses — Agri Machinery (tractors), Construction Equipment, and Railway Equipment. In FY26 it sold the railway leg, leaving a cleaner two-engine company where tractors do the heavy lifting and construction equipment adds a cyclical-but-complementary second stream.

1 · Agri Machinery — the tractor core (~85% of revenue)

  • What it is: the Farmtrac and Powertrac tractor brands, plus engines and farm implements. FY26 volume was a record 1,33,670 units, up 15.7%.
  • Where it's strong: historically a North-India champion (Punjab, Haryana, UP, Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh), with a lighter footprint in the South and West — a geographic skew the company is working to correct.
  • Why it matters: this is the segment the whole equity story runs on — the cyclicality, the market-share fight, the Kubota technology and the export ambition all live here.

2 · Construction Equipment — the second engine (~15%)

  • What it is: Pick-'n-Carry hydraulic mobile cranes (a category Escorts pioneered in India and still leads globally by volume), plus backhoe loaders, compactors and material-handling machines. FY26 volume ~5,794 units.
  • Why it's here: it rides India's infrastructure and construction spending — a different demand driver from farming, so it partially diversifies the group away from the pure monsoon cycle (though it has its own construction cycle).
  • The tension: it is lower-margin and more competitive than tractors, and had a softer patch in parts of FY26 — useful ballast, not the growth engine.

3 · Railway Equipment — the leg that was sold

  • What it was: brakes, couplers, suspension and components for Indian Railways rolling stock — a steady, less-cyclical B2B annuity (FY25 revenue ~₹913 Cr, PBT ~₹188 Cr).
  • What happened: in October 2024 Escorts Kubota agreed to sell it to Sona BLW / Sona Comstar on a slump-sale basis for an enterprise value of ₹1,600 Cr; the transfer took effect from 1 June 2025.
  • Why sell a good annuity? To focus on agri and construction in line with Kubota's global vision, and to crystallise a large cash sum. The trade-off: the group gave up a genuinely counter-cyclical, high-return business — one of the honest debits of the "focus" strategy.
Read the profit numbers carefully: FY26 reported PAT of ~₹2,409 Cr (+92.5%) is inflated by the one-time gain on the railway sale. The number that reflects the ongoing business is continuing-operations PAT of ~₹1,381 Cr (+24.4%). Any valuation on the reported figure will look artificially cheap — always separate the one-off from the run-rate.
04

The Indian tractor cycle: monsoon, money and mood

The demand engine no tractor maker controls — and the reason the stock is cyclical.

Tractor demand in India is one of the more forecastable-yet-uncontrollable cycles in the market. It turns on a chain of rural-economy inputs — rainfall → crop output → farm incomes → farmer confidence → tractor purchases. Get a good monsoon and firm crop prices, and volumes surge (as in FY26); get a drought or an income squeeze, and the whole industry contracts. Understanding this chain explains both why FY26 was a record and why the next year always carries risk.

Every link is largely outside the company's control. That's why forecasting tractor volumes is really a forecast of rainfall, crop prices and rural sentiment — and why margins swing so hard year to year.

What powered the FY26 up-cycle

  • A good monsoon and healthy reservoir levels supported a strong sowing and harvest, lifting rural incomes.
  • Higher MSPs (minimum support prices) and firm crop realisations put more cash in farmers' hands.
  • Government farm-mechanisation push and subsidy support underpinned demand — and the industry crossed a landmark ~10 lakh (1 million) tractors in a single year for the first time in FY26.
  • Replacement demand and a broadly supportive rural-credit environment did the rest.
Why "cyclical" cuts both ways for the stock

In an up-year, three tailwinds stack: more units (volume), a richer mix (farmers trade up to higher-HP tractors when flush), and operating leverage (fixed costs spread over more machines). That's why FY26 EBITDA grew ~28% on ~13% revenue growth — margins expanded because all three worked at once. But the same mechanics run in reverse in a bad monsoon year: units fall, mix worsens, and fixed costs suddenly weigh heavily, so profits can drop far more than revenue. This is the core reason a tractor stock rarely gets a high, stable "compounder" multiple on its domestic business alone — the market knows the earnings are lumpy. It is also exactly why the Kubota export leg matters: exports are driven by different cycles in different countries, so they can smooth the ride.

The one number to watch every year: the monsoon forecast (and reservoir levels) going into the June–September season. It is the single best leading indicator of the next year's tractor volumes — and therefore of Escorts Kubota's earnings direction — no matter how well the company itself is run.
05

Market share and the rivals

A consolidated market led by Mahindra — where Escorts fights for the #3 slot.

India's tractor market is large, consolidated and fiercely competitive, dominated by one giant and a cluster of strong challengers. Escorts Kubota is a solid #3–4 player nationally — roughly 10.9% domestic share in FY26, up from ~9.9% — but its share is materially higher in its North-India heartland. The competitive question is whether Kubota's technology and the mix push can lift that share, especially in the South and West where Escorts is historically under-represented.

The strategic gap to close

Two structural weaknesses define Escorts' competitive position, and both are what the Kubota deal is meant to fix. First, geography: it is a North-India brand in a country where the South and West are large, growing tractor markets — so there's share to be gained simply by travelling better. Second, the higher-HP and export segments, where Escorts historically lagged Mahindra and the export-heavy players like Sonalika and John Deere. Kubota brings technology for bigger, more sophisticated tractors and a global distribution network — the two ingredients Escorts most needed and could least build alone. Whether that translates into share gains rather than just capability is the thing to judge over the next few years.

06

The Kubota export & technology thesis

The single force that could change what kind of company this is — and the heart of the bull case.

Everything above describes a good, cyclical, mostly-domestic tractor maker. This section is about the bet that it becomes something more. The core thesis for owning Escorts Kubota is that Kubota turns it into the group's India manufacturing and EXPORT hub — using low-cost Indian factories, Kubota's global technology, and Kubota's worldwide dealer network to build an export-led growth leg that reduces reliance on the boom-and-bust Indian monsoon cycle. It is the demand-side transformation the whole re-rating story rests on.

The three pillars of the export thesis

  • A dedicated greenfield export plant. Escorts Kubota has announced a new greenfield manufacturing facility in Uttar Pradesh — investment guided at roughly ₹3,000–3,500 Cr over 4–5 years (and potentially ₹5,000 Cr+ over a longer horizon) — built explicitly to expand export capacity, including to the United States, by around FY28–29. This is the physical embodiment of the hub strategy.
  • Kubota's global distribution. Exports are increasingly routed through the "Kubota Global" channel — in FY26, roughly 60% of Escorts' tractor exports went through Kubota's own network. That is access to markets (US, South-East Asia, Africa, Europe) an independent Escorts could never have reached at scale.
  • Technology and premiumisation. Kubota's engineering supports the move into higher-horsepower and more sophisticated tractors, where realisations and margins are richer — lifting the domestic mix and making Escorts' machines exportable to developed markets with tougher requirements.

The bull case in one mechanism — and the honest caveat

The mechanism: a domestic tractor maker earns lumpy profits because all its demand comes from one cycle (the Indian monsoon). Add a large, growing export business selling into many countries with different cycles, and the earnings stream gets steadier and larger — which, if the market believes it, justifies a higher, more stable multiple (a "re-rating"). Layer on richer higher-HP mix and you get margin expansion too. That is the entire optionality investors are paying for beyond the cheap-looking cyclical.

The caveat: exports are still only ~5% of volume, the flagship export plant won't meaningfully contribute until ~FY28–29, and export demand carries its own risks — currency, tariffs (US tariff policy is a live variable) and geopolitics. In fact Escorts has already seen export softness in some quarters from geopolitical tension. So this is a genuine, well-backed long-term thesis — but it is a multi-year story, not a this-year earnings driver. Own it for the option, and size the option honestly.

07

Farm mechanisation: the long structural runway

The slow-burn tailwind underneath the yearly cycle.

Beneath the noisy annual cycle sits a genuinely long structural story: India is still under-mechanised. A large share of farm operations is done by hand or by animals, farm labour is getting scarcer and dearer as workers move to cities, and rising rural incomes plus government support are steadily pulling more farmers toward machines. Over a decade-plus horizon, that means the total tractor market keeps growing — even as individual years zig-zag with the rain.

What keeps the long-run demand rising

  • Labour economics — rural wages rise and farm labour grows scarce as workers migrate; a tractor increasingly pays for itself versus hiring hands.
  • Rising incomes & credit — higher farm incomes, better rural finance and equipment subsidies steadily lower the barrier to a first (or replacement) tractor.
  • Government push — farm-mechanisation schemes, subsidies and infrastructure spending are policy priorities that support both tractors and construction equipment.
  • Under-penetration — mechanisation levels remain well below developed-country norms, leaving a long catch-up runway; smaller-HP and second-tractor demand extends it further.
How to hold the two ideas together: the cycle (§04) tells you whether next year is up or down; the structural runway (this section) tells you the multi-year trend is upward. A tractor investor has to respect both — buy a cyclical for its cheapness in a down-year, but the reason the down-years are worth buying is that the long-run curve keeps rising underneath them. For Escorts Kubota, the Kubota export leg is a way to add a second structural runway (global demand) on top of the domestic one.
08

The cash pile and the capital-allocation question

A famously cash-rich balance sheet — and the debate over what to do with it.

One number sets Escorts Kubota apart from most cyclicals: it sits on an enormous net-cash balance sheet. Against total debt of only ~₹162 Cr, it holds roughly ₹8,000 Cr+ of cash and investments — swelled further by Kubota's earlier capital infusion and the FY26 railway-sale proceeds. That fortress is a genuine strength, but it also creates the central minority-shareholder question: what does a Japanese-controlled company do with all that idle cash?

Why the cash pile is a double-edged sword

  • The upside: it lets the company self-fund the greenfield export plant and any technology investment without borrowing, survive a bad monsoon year comfortably, and pay generous dividends (including a ₹18 special dividend in FY26). For a cyclical, a fortress balance sheet is a real quality marker.
  • The drag: a big pile of low-yielding cash depresses return ratios — it's part of why headline ROCE (~14%) and ROE (~19%) look moderate rather than spectacular for an otherwise good business. Cash earning bank rates dilutes the returns the operating business actually generates.
  • The question: with a dominant ~54% parent, minorities watch closely for how the cash is deployed — reinvestment (the export plant), acquisitions, buybacks, or bigger dividends. Efficient deployment could unlock value; idle accumulation would keep weighing on returns.
In plain terms

Escorts Kubota is rich but not yet fully efficient. Imagine a shopkeeper with a thriving store and a huge cash box in the back that earns almost nothing. The store is good; the cash box just sits there dragging down the average return on everything he owns. The FY26 railway sale added even more to that box. The bull hopes the cash gets put to work — the export plant, technology, maybe returns to shareholders — turning a dead asset into a productive one and lifting the return ratios. The bear worries a parent-controlled company simply hoards it, so the returns stay merely-okay and minorities never see the benefit. Watching the pace and quality of cash deployment is one of the most important things a shareholder can track here.

How to think about the multiple

At a ~₹32,500 Cr market cap, the stock trades at only ~15× reported earnings — which looks cheap for a market leader with a fortress balance sheet. But that reported number is padded by the railway sale. On clean continuing profit of ~₹1,381 Cr, the P/E is closer to ~23× — a fuller, fairer reading. And because ~₹8,000 Cr of the market cap is backed by cash, the operating business is being valued at a lower effective multiple than the headline suggests. So the honest framing is: not obviously cheap, not obviously expensive. What you're really deciding is whether to pay a mid-20s multiple for a cyclical up-year — justified only if you believe the Kubota export-and-technology optionality (§06) turns this into a steadier, faster-growing, more global company over time. Strip out that belief and it's a fairly-valued cyclical having a good year; add it back and the same price can look like early-stage re-rating.

The growth runway

Domestic cycle recovery, share gains, the export plant, and a cash war-chest to fund it all.

Escorts Kubota has an unusually clear set of growth levers stacked on top of each other — a mix of near-term cyclical recovery, medium-term share and mix gains, and a long-dated export transformation, all funded by the cash pile without a rupee of new debt.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cash-rich cyclical with a genuine long-dated option — priced fairly, not cheaply.

Escorts Kubota is one of the more interesting risk/reward setups in Indian industrials: a proven, market-leading tractor brand, a fortress balance sheet, and a global parent with the technology and distribution to reshape it — but a domestic-cyclical core, a big block of idle cash, and an export thesis that is still mostly promise. The debate is honest and two-sided.

The bull case

  • Kubota optionality — technology, higher-HP products and a global dealer network that could build a steady export leg and re-rate the stock.
  • Fortress balance sheet — ~₹8,000 Cr+ net cash, debt-free, self-funds the export plant and pays special dividends.
  • Record operating year — FY26 volumes, margins and share all up; leader in a consolidating, structurally growing market.
  • Long mechanisation runway — India under-penetrated; a second structural runway (exports) layered on top.
  • Focus & discipline — railway sale shows a parent willing to prune non-core and concentrate on agri + construction.

The bear case

  • Domestic cyclicality — profits swing with the monsoon, crop prices and rural mood; FY26 is an up-year that a bad monsoon could reverse.
  • Export thesis unproven — exports are only ~5% of volume; the flagship plant won't contribute until ~FY28–29, and faces tariff/currency/geopolitical risk.
  • Not actually cheap — ~23× on continuing earnings; the low "reported" P/E is a mirage from the one-off railway gain.
  • Idle cash drags returns — ROCE/ROE held to moderate levels; deployment under a dominant parent is a minority-shareholder question mark.
  • Fierce competition — Mahindra's dominance, plus TAFE, Sonalika and John Deere all fighting for the same share and export markets.
The bottom line

Escorts Kubota is a high-quality, cash-rich, market-leading tractor maker in an up-cycle, wrapped around a long-dated Kubota export option. The business quality and balance sheet are genuinely strong; the valuation is fair rather than a bargain once you strip out the railway one-off; and the exciting part of the story — the export transformation — is real but years from mattering to earnings. So the honest verdict is a stock you own for two reasons at once: a decent cyclical you're not overpaying for, plus a call option on Kubota turning it global. The numbers to watch each year are simple — the monsoon and tractor volumes (is the cycle with you?), export growth and the UP plant (is the transformation happening?), and cash deployment (is the war-chest being put to work?). Get those right and a fair price today looks cheap in hindsight; get the export bet wrong and you're left with a well-run cyclical and a very large, very idle bank balance.
