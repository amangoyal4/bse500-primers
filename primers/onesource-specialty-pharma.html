Onesource Specialty Pharma, the contract maker of GLP-1 pens

A full walk through India's first pure-play, multi-modality specialty CDMO — a business that doesn't sell its own drugs but builds the hardest-to-make finished formats (sterile injectables, biologics, drug-device pens and softgels) for the world's innovators. How it was carved out of Strides Pharma and listed in January 2025, why the obesity-drug boom lands squarely in its lap, and why a company earning ₹74 Cr of profit carries a ~₹20,700 Cr price tag.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Onesource Specialty Pharma is a contract manufacturer for the pharmaceutical industry — but a very particular kind. It doesn't own or sell brands of its own. Instead, global drug companies and biotech firms hand it a molecule and a specification, and Onesource develops and manufactures the finished, hard-to-make product: a sterile injectable in a vial or pre-filled syringe, a biologic drug, a drug-device combination like an auto-injector pen, or a soft-gelatin capsule. It is India's first listed pure-play, multi-modality specialty CDMO (Contract Development and Manufacturing Organisation), spun out of Strides Pharma and listed on 24 January 2025.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It plays where manufacturing is hardest. Anyone can press a paracetamol tablet. Very few can run an aseptic fill-finish line that puts a sterile biologic into a cartridge, then assembles that cartridge into a working injector pen — under USFDA and EU scrutiny, with zero tolerance for contamination. Onesource has built its whole identity around those high-barrier formats across four modalities: biologics, sterile injectables, drug-device combinations, and soft gelatin, from five USFDA- and EU-approved sites in Bengaluru.
  • The obesity-drug wave lands in its sweet spot. The GLP-1 class (Ozempic, Wegovy and their peers) is the fastest-growing drug category on earth — and most GLP-1s are injectable peptides delivered by pens and auto-injectors. That is precisely fill-finish plus device assembly, exactly what Onesource is built to do. In FY26 it began launching semaglutide for multiple customers as the molecule went off-patent in India, and is spending ~US$100M to expand GLP-1 pen capacity.
  • The price is a bet on tomorrow, not today. FY26 revenue was ~₹1,422 Cr and adjusted profit just ~₹74 Cr — yet the market values it near ₹20,700 Cr. That gap only resolves if management hits its own FY28 target of ~US$400M revenue at ~40% EBITDA margin. The ramp, not the current run-rate, is what you're paying for.
In plain terms

Onesource is the outsourced factory-and-lab for other companies' complex medicines — the firm a Danish or American drugmaker calls when it needs someone to actually fill millions of sterile cartridges, snap them into pens, and ship a finished obesity or diabetes injector that a patient can use at home. It keeps none of the brand and none of the marketing; it keeps the manufacturing margin and the switching costs that come from being deeply embedded in a regulated supply chain. The catch is that it was recently carved out of a bigger company and is still building the plants and filling the order book, so today's profit is small and lumpy while the promise — GLP-1 pens at global scale — is still largely ahead of it.

01

What a CDMO actually is — and why innovators use one

No pharma background required — just the logic of who makes what, and for whom.

Bringing a drug to patients has two halves: inventing and owning it (the science, the patents, the clinical trials, the brand) and physically making it (developing a stable formulation, then manufacturing it at scale under regulatory rules). A CDMO does the second half for companies that specialise in the first. The customer keeps the intellectual property and the marketing; the CDMO supplies development know-how and factory capacity.

The two letters in the middle matter. "CD" is Contract Development — turning a raw molecule into a manufacturable product: choosing the formulation, proving it's stable, designing the process, filing the technical paperwork with regulators. "M" is Manufacturing — running the actual production lines at commercial volume, batch after batch, passing inspection every time. A firm strong at both can hold a customer from early development all the way through years of commercial supply.

Why a big drugmaker outsources at all

It looks odd that a company like a global innovator would let someone else make its product. Four reasons explain it. (1) Capital. A sterile injectable or biologics plant costs hundreds of crores and years to build and validate — capital an innovator would rather spend on research. (2) Specialised skill. Aseptic fill-finish and device assembly are their own deep crafts; a CDMO that does nothing else does them better. (3) Capacity peaks. A blockbuster launch can need volumes no single in-house plant can absorb, so innovators dual-source. (4) Speed. Renting a validated, inspection-ready line beats building one. The CDMO's return for absorbing that burden is a steady, contracted manufacturing margin and a customer who finds it painful to switch.

The stickiness is regulatory, not just commercial

Here is the feature that makes a good CDMO defensible. Once a customer's product is registered to be made at a specific Onesource line — with that site named in the regulatory filing and inspected by the USFDA — moving it elsewhere means re-validating and re-filing, which can take a year or more and risk supply. So a molecule that lands and passes inspection tends to stay for its commercial life. Revenue that's hard to win is also, once won, hard to lose. That is the quiet economic engine underneath all the technical vocabulary.

  • The customers — global innovator pharma companies and biotech firms; Onesource serves 50+ clients across regulated markets (US, EU, Canada) and India.
  • The revenue shape — a mix of one-off development fees (early, smaller) and recurring commercial manufacturing supply (later, larger and stickier). A young CDMO's job is to convert development projects into commercial supply — the point where the money compounds.
  • The gate on everything — regulatory clearance. A single failed USFDA inspection can freeze a site's exports. Compliance isn't overhead here; it is the licence to operate.
02

The four modalities — Onesource's capability stack

Most CDMOs do one thing well. Onesource deliberately spans four — and the finished, difficult formats are the point.

The word that recurs in every Onesource description is "multi-modality". It means the company doesn't just make one kind of product; it operates across four distinct manufacturing worlds, each with its own science, plant and regulatory pathway. Crucially, these aren't commodity oral pills — they are the finished delivery formats that are technically hard and therefore high-value.

Why "multi-modality" is a genuine edge, not marketing

A customer developing, say, an injectable biosimilar delivered in a pen would normally have to string together three vendors: one to make the biologic, one to fill it sterile, one to assemble the device. Coordinating that hand-off across three companies and three regulatory filings is slow and risky. Onesource can offer the whole sequence under one roof, one quality system, one point of accountability. For a biotech without its own plants, that end-to-end simplicity is worth paying for — and it is very hard for a single-modality rival to match without years of capital and inspections.

03

Why sterile + device is a higher-barrier niche

The single most important reason Onesource is not "just another Indian pharma contract maker".

India has hundreds of contract manufacturers making oral tablets and bulk APIs — a crowded, price-competitive world. Onesource has deliberately planted itself in a much narrower, much harder patch: sterile injectables and drug-device combinations. Understanding why those are harder explains why the barriers are real and the margins can be rich.

What "aseptic" really demands

A tablet can tolerate a little dust; an injectable cannot. A sterile drug goes straight into the bloodstream, so the manufacturing environment must be free of living micro-organisms and particulates, continuously. That means cleanrooms with engineered airflow, isolators and robotics that keep human hands away from the product, constant environmental monitoring, and validation regimes that document sterility batch after batch. Building and qualifying such a line takes years and enormous capital; running it flawlessly takes a culture most factories never develop. Global sterile-injectable capacity is genuinely scarce — which is why demand surges (like GLP-1) translate quickly into pricing power for whoever already has validated lines.

Then you add a machine to the medicine

A drug-device combination raises the bar again, because now the product is also a piece of precision hardware. An auto-injector pen must deliver an exact dose, reliably, in an untrained patient's hands, thousands of times without failure. Making one means marrying pharmaceutical sterility with mechanical engineering and high-speed assembly: integrating a filled drug cartridge with springs, needles and housings, then functionally testing units. Onesource says it manages 11 different device platforms — each needing its own tooling and recalibration. That combination of disciplines is rare, and regulators inspect it as both a drug and a device.

The barrier translated into money

Barriers to entry are worth caring about only if they show up in economics. Here they do, in two ways. First, scarcity pricing: when a drug class explodes and there isn't enough sterile-injectable or device capacity in the world, customers pay up and sign multi-year commitments to lock in the lines that exist. Second, durable relationships: a device platform validated for a customer's product is not casually re-tendered every year. So the payoff for surviving the years of capital and inspections is a book of high-margin, long-dated, sticky revenue — the FY28 target of ~40% EBITDA margins is management's bet that filling these hard lines delivers exactly that operating leverage.

04

The demerger — how Onesource was born from Strides

A value-unlock spin-off, an old shell reborn, and a share-swap that made the child bigger than the parent.

Onesource did not start from a blank sheet in 2025. It is the result of a corporate reorganisation that pulled several existing manufacturing businesses out of the Strides Pharma group and stitched them into a single, separately-listed specialty CDMO. Strides — the Bengaluru drugmaker built by entrepreneur Arun Kumar — announced the plan in 2023: carve the CDMO and soft-gelatin operations out of the parent so that a fast-growing, higher-multiple business would no longer be buried inside a slower generics company.

What was merged into what

  • The CDMO and soft-gelatin businesses of Strides Pharma Science were demerged out of the parent.
  • The CDMO business of Steriscience Specialties (a related sterile-injectables venture) was folded in.
  • These were housed inside Stelis Biopharma — Strides' biologics arm — which was renamed Onesource Specialty Pharma. The biologics capability came with it.
  • The National Company Law Tribunal sanctioned the scheme on 14 November 2024; the shares listed on BSE and NSE on 24 January 2025.
  • Strides shareholders received 1 Onesource share for every 2 Strides shares they held — so Onesource arrived with a ready-made public shareholder base rather than an IPO.
The surprise a generalist should sit with

Onesource looks like a shiny 2025 debutant, but it is legally an old vehicle — its corporate identity traces to Stelis Biopharma, incorporated back in 2007, which spent years as a cash-consuming biologics-and-biosimilars bet inside the Strides orbit. In other words, the "new" GLP-1 CDMO is the same entity that was, not long ago, a loss-making biologics experiment — repurposed, recapitalised and rebranded around a much sharper commercial thesis. And here is the second twist: after listing, the carved-out child rapidly came to carry a market value well above its former parent Strides. The spin-off didn't just unlock value; it inverted the hierarchy — the factory that used to sit inside Strides is now worth more than Strides itself.

Why demerge at all?

The logic is a familiar one in equity markets: a differentiated, high-growth CDMO trapped inside a diversified generics company gets valued like the average, not the best, of its parts. Separated and listed on its own, it can attract investors who want exactly that exposure, raise its own capital for capacity, and be measured on its own metrics. Ahead of listing, Onesource raised ~₹801 Cr (about US$95M) from marquee domestic and foreign institutions at a pre-money valuation of ~US$1.65 billion (~₹14,500 Cr) — a rich starting mark that signalled how the market intended to price the GLP-1 and drug-device story from day one.

05

The company and who controls it

Five Bengaluru sites, a Strides-lineage promoter, and a broad institutional register.

Onesource is a Bengaluru business through and through — its manufacturing, its people and its promoter DNA all trace to the Strides ecosystem there. It runs five state-of-the-art facilities in Bengaluru, all approved by major regulators including the USFDA and EU authorities, and employs more than 1,200 professionals, including over 200 techno-commercial experts who sell and manage the CDMO relationships.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter: the Strides / Arun Kumar group, holding roughly 30%. Arun Kumar — the founder of Strides and a serial pharma entrepreneur — is the guiding figure behind the CDMO thesis.
  • Institutional weight: the register is unusually institutional for a young listing — around 19% FIIs and ~19% domestic institutions, with the public holding roughly a third. That reflects the pre-listing placement to marquee funds rather than a retail IPO.
  • Watch-item — promoter pledging: a meaningful chunk of promoter shares has historically been pledged (a legacy of the Strides group's financing style). Pledged promoter stock is a governance flag worth tracking, because it can force selling in a sharp drawdown.
  • Balance sheet: unlike debt-free peers, Onesource is carrying rising net debt (roughly ₹900 Cr by late 2025, up from ~₹170 Cr in March 2025) as it funds a heavy capacity build — a deliberate, growth-stage choice, but a real difference from an asset-light compounder.
Governance nuance: Onesource inherits both the strengths and the habits of the Strides house — deep manufacturing and dealmaking experience, but also a financing culture comfortable with leverage and pledging. For a business whose entire equity case rests on execution over the next three years, the quality of capital allocation and the pledge trajectory are worth watching as closely as the order book.
06

The GLP-1 wave — the crux of the whole case

The structural force unique to Onesource: the world's fastest-growing drug class is shaped exactly like what it makes.

Every part of this business becomes clearer once you see the drug class it is built to serve. GLP-1 receptor agonists — semaglutide (Ozempic, Wegovy), tirzepatide and their successors — are the medicines behind the global obesity-and-diabetes boom. The market for them is projected to run into the hundreds of billions of dollars by 2030. And the physical form most of them take is the one thing Onesource is engineered around: an injectable peptide, delivered by a pen or auto-injector, filled into a cartridge under sterile conditions.

Read that sentence against the four modalities. A GLP-1 pen needs: a peptide drug (bordering on biologics territory), a sterile aseptic fill into a cartridge, and precision device assembly into an injector. That is Stages 3, 4 and 5 of the value chain from Section 02, back to back — Onesource's exact differentiation. Few Indian companies, and not that many globally, can do all three at scale under regulatory approval. This is why a newly-listed Indian CDMO can credibly present itself as a maker of GLP-1 pens: the capability set was assembled for precisely this shape of product.

The generic-semaglutide unlock — and a surprise about timing

Here is the counter-intuitive part. The GLP-1 opportunity most people picture is the US patent cliff — years away. But Onesource's FY26 kicker came from a different direction: semaglutide's patent expired in India first, opening the door to generic semaglutide in one of the world's largest markets ahead of the West. Onesource began manufacturing it for multiple Indian customer brands as they raced to launch, and secured approvals to supply Canada. So the first wave of GLP-1 revenue arrived not from Western innovators but from emerging-market generics — a reminder that the obesity gold-rush reaches India and its neighbours early, and that Onesource is positioned to make the pens as those launches scale.

The capacity build behind the story

A supply opportunity is only real if you have the lines to fill it. Onesource is spending aggressively to be ready:

  • A ~US$100M capex programme at its flagship facility (roughly US$80M committed so far) to expand fill-finish and device capacity.
  • A dedicated push on GLP-1 pen production — cartridge filling, assembly and finish — to meet demand as semaglutide volumes scale across markets.
  • Depth across 11 device platforms, so it can serve different customers' pen and injector designs rather than being locked to one.
The honest caveat on the crux: most of the GLP-1 value is still ahead, not booked. FY26 showed the first India launches, but the large, durable Western-innovator and biosimilar-scale volumes — the ones that would fill those new lines at 40% margins — are a future the company is building toward, not a run-rate it has already achieved. The GLP-1 thesis is a well-shaped opportunity meeting a well-shaped capability; whether it converts on schedule is the open question the valuation is wagering on.
07

Customers, contracts and the concentration question

50+ global clients, marquee partnerships — and the young-CDMO risk that a few molecules move the numbers.

A CDMO's franchise is its customer book and the depth of each relationship. Onesource serves more than 50 global pharmaceutical and biotech clients, and has begun landing the kind of marquee, multi-year partnerships that validate its capability to the wider market.

The relationships that signal quality

  • Formycon (Germany) — a strategic manufacturing partnership for biosimilars. A specialist European biosimilars developer choosing Onesource for manufacturing is exactly the third-party validation a young biologics CDMO needs.
  • Indian semaglutide customers — multiple domestic brands sourcing generic semaglutide as the patent lapsed, turning the drug-device capability into near-term revenue.
  • US injectables and soft-gel customers — the established base inherited from Strides, providing recurring commercial supply while the newer biologics and device lines ramp.
  • Regulated-market breadth — supply approvals spanning the US, EU, Canada and India, so the client base isn't tied to a single geography's demand.
The double-edge of being young

Fifty-plus customers sounds diversified, but a CDMO's revenue is driven by a handful of large commercial molecules, not by the count of names on the roster. Early in its life, Onesource's results can swing hard on the ramp — or the slippage — of just a few marquee programmes. That is precisely what FY26 revealed: revenue and margins moved sharply quarter to quarter as specific launches landed or waited. Concentration in a few molecules and a few anchor customers is the structural reality of a scaling CDMO; it cuts both ways, amplifying good quarters and bad ones alike until the book broadens.

Regulatory dependency, stated plainly: because supply approvals name specific sites, Onesource lives or dies by inspection outcomes. A reassuring data point: management reported 49 regulatory inspections and customer audits in FY26 with zero critical observations. A less comfortable one: the USFDA classified its Bengaluru drug-device combination facility as VAI (Voluntary Action Indicated) — meaning observations were raised but not serious enough to block operations. For a firm whose device site is central to the GLP-1 thesis, turning that into clean outcomes is not optional.

The industry and the rivals

A booming outsourcing market — but a crowded one, with global giants at the top.

Onesource rides a genuine structural tailwind: pharma outsourcing is growing fast as innovators shed manufacturing to focus on discovery, and as the GLP-1 surge strains the world's sterile and device capacity. But it competes in a landscape with far larger, better-capitalised players — Indian peers moving up the value chain, and global CDMOs that already dominate sterile and device work.

Where Onesource can actually win

Against giants like Catalent and Lonza, Onesource cannot win on scale. Its wedge is cost-competitive, regulatory-grade capacity in a format the world is short of, at a moment when GLP-1 demand is forcing customers to add and diversify suppliers. India's advantage in skilled labour and capital efficiency lets Onesource build sterile and device lines for less than a Western incumbent — and a biotech or generics firm that can't get a slot at a Lonza has reason to qualify an Onesource. The strategic test is whether it can convert that opening into anchor commercial contracts with large customers before the current capacity crunch eases and the majors expand into the gap.

How to read a ~₹20,700 Cr price on ~₹74 Cr of profit

On FY26 earnings the multiple is meaningless-high — a P/E in the hundreds — because profit is tiny while capacity is being built ahead of revenue. The sensible lens is enterprise value against forward EBITDA. On FY26 EBITDA of ~₹304 Cr, the ~₹21,600 Cr enterprise value is roughly 70× — extraordinarily rich. But run it against the FY28 guidance instead: ~US$400M revenue (~₹3,400 Cr) at ~40% margin implies ~₹1,360 Cr of EBITDA, which pulls the forward multiple down to the mid-teens. So the same price is either absurd or reasonable depending entirely on whether you believe the FY28 numbers. There is no valuation debate here that isn't really an argument about the ramp.

The non-transferable arithmetic: the same five Bengaluru buildings that generated ~₹304 Cr of EBITDA in FY26 are being valued as though they will generate ~₹1,360 Cr by FY28 — roughly a 4.5× jump in operating profit from the same core footprint in two years, mostly by filling injectable and device lines with GLP-1 and biologics volume. The market has already paid for most of that leap. Onesource is not expensive because it earns a lot; it is expensive because it is priced as if it soon will.
10

The FY28 gate — the one number that settles it

Strip away the noise and a single, testable target decides whether the price makes sense.

Onesource has done the market a favour by naming its own scorecard. Management guides to ~US$400M of organic revenue at ~40% EBITDA margin by FY28. Everything about the stock — the rich multiple, the capex, the GLP-1 excitement — collapses into whether that gate is cleared. It is the metric that settles the thesis, and it is refreshingly specific.

What has to go right to hit it

  • Volume conversion — development projects and early launches must become large commercial supply, especially GLP-1 fill-finish and device assembly filling the new lines.
  • Margin mechanics — as those high-fixed-cost sterile and device lines fill, the FY26 margin dip must reverse toward the ~40% mark; utilisation is the lever.
  • Clean regulation — the Bengaluru sites, including the VAI-classified device facility, must stay inspection-ready; a single serious USFDA action would stall the ramp.
  • Customer breadth — the book must widen beyond a few anchor molecules so results stop swinging quarter to quarter.

Occupancy settles a hospital; combined ratio settles an insurer; for Onesource it is the GLP-1-and-biologics revenue ramp toward US$400M at ~40% EBITDA. Track that one line and the rest is commentary. If quarterly revenue climbs toward a ~₹850 Cr run-rate and margins march back through the 30s, the ramp is real and the multiple was earned early. If revenue stalls near the FY26 level and margins linger in the low 20s, the FY28 gate slips — and a price built on FY28 has to reset to a nearer, smaller reality.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely differentiated CDMO, priced for a ramp it has yet to deliver.

Onesource is one of the more distinctive stories on the exchange: a differentiated, multi-modality specialty CDMO sitting in the physical crossfire of the biggest drug boom of the decade. The business is real and the positioning is genuine. The debate is whether the execution and the calendar match a price that has already banked the win.

The bull case

  • Format tailwind — GLP-1s are injectable, pen-delivered peptides; the world is short of sterile and device capacity, and Onesource has validated lines and is adding more.
  • High-barrier niche — sterile fill-finish, biologics and 11 device platforms are hard to replicate and sticky once a molecule is registered on a line.
  • Named, credible targets — ~US$400M / ~40% EBITDA FY28 guidance gives a clear, testable scorecard, with capacity largely funded.
  • Marquee validation — Formycon biosimilars deal, Canada approvals, 49 FY26 audits with zero critical observations.
  • Scarcity of the asset — India's only listed pure-play specialty CDMO of this shape; institutional appetite is strong.

The bear case

  • Valuation ahead of delivery — ~70× FY26 EV/EBITDA; the price already assumes the FY28 ramp, so slippage re-rates it hard.
  • Lumpy, molecule-concentrated results — Q3 FY26 nearly broke even; a few programmes still swing the whole quarter.
  • Regulatory exposure — sterile/device sites face USFDA inspection risk; the device facility already carries a VAI classification.
  • Rising leverage & pledging — net debt climbing to fund capex, and a Strides-lineage habit of promoter share pledging.
  • Future, not present, GLP-1 — the large Western and biosimilar volumes that justify the multiple are still to be won, amid deep-pocketed global rivals.
The bottom line

Onesource is a rare thing in Indian pharma — a company built to make the finished, sterile, device-borne products the rest of the industry finds hardest, at the exact moment the world's fastest-growing drug class needs exactly those pens filled. The capability is not in doubt. What is unproven is the conversion: turning a well-shaped opportunity and freshly-built Bengaluru lines into US$400M of revenue at 40% margins by FY28. The next real milestone to watch is concrete and dated — the ~US$100M GLP-1 pen expansion reaching full commercial fill-finish for off-patent semaglutide, and the device site turning its USFDA VAI into a clean bill. Clear those, and the FY28 gate comes into view; miss them, and a price written for FY28 will have to be re-read against a much smaller present.
