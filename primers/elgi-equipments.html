00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Elgi Equipments makes air compressors — the machines that squeeze ordinary air into a high-pressure supply that factories use to power tools, run production lines and drive automation. It is India's second-largest compressor maker (~22% market share, behind global leader Atlas Copco), sells into 120 countries with more than 2 million machines installed worldwide, and is run out of Coimbatore, Tamil Nadu by a company founded in 1960.

Three facts frame everything:

  • An engineering business with an annuity attached. Consolidated revenue was ~₹3,951 Cr in FY26 (up ~13%), with a ~14.8% EBITDA margin and PAT of ~₹430 Cr (up ~23%). The balance sheet is net-cash (~₹620 Cr), and return on capital employed is a high ~27% — because every compressor sold pulls years of high-margin spares and service behind it.
  • Two-thirds Indian champion, one-half global challenger. Roughly half the revenue now comes from outside India — the US, Europe, Australia, the Middle East — built through direct subsidiaries and a string of acquisitions. Elgi has openly declared it wants to be the world's #2 air-compressor company; it sits around #6 today.
  • The whole debate is the overseas margin gap. The India business earns high-teens operating margins; the international arms — especially Europe — still run near break-even after years of investment. The bet the ~42× earnings multiple is paying for is that those overseas subsidiaries eventually earn India-like margins as they reach scale. If they do, profit compounds far faster than sales. If they don't, this is an expensive stock for a mid-cycle industrial.
In plain terms

Elgi sells a picks-and-shovels utility to industry. Almost no factory can run without compressed air, and the compressor that supplies it is bought once but serviced for a decade — so the real prize is the installed base, a growing fleet of machines that each drip out spares, filters and service contracts at fat margins for years. Elgi has spent the last fifteen years using that steady Indian cash engine to fund an unusually bold overseas push: buying and building compressor businesses across three continents to climb from a regional leader into a genuine global name. The catch is that going global is slow and expensive — a new country takes years to reach the profitability the home market already throws off.

01

Compressed air 101: the "fourth utility"

No engineering degree required — just why a tank of squeezed air is worth paying for.

Walk into almost any factory, workshop, hospital, bottling plant or textile mill and you'll find, humming in a corner, an air compressor. Compressed air is so universal in industry that engineers call it the "fourth utility" — as essential to a plant as electricity, water and gas. A compressor takes free atmospheric air and squeezes it into a smaller volume at high pressure; that stored energy is then piped around the plant to do work.

What compressed air actually does

  • Powers tools and machines — impact wrenches, spray guns, sandblasters, packaging machines, robotic grippers. Pneumatic (air-driven) tools are lighter, cheaper and safer than electric ones on a factory floor.
  • Runs production lines — air cylinders open and close valves, push products along conveyors, clamp parts and stamp components in split-second automation.
  • Feeds critical processes — in food, pharma, electronics and medical use, clean air touches the product itself, so purity is non-negotiable.
  • Serves specialist trades — mining, construction, oil-and-gas, railways and dental clinics all run on portable or fixed compressed-air systems.

Because the applications are everywhere, demand for compressors tracks the broad pulse of industry: manufacturing output, capital investment, construction and infrastructure. That breadth is a strength — no single customer or sector dominates — but it also ties the business to the industrial cycle.

The one number that reframes the whole product

Here is the fact that changes how you read this business: over a compressor's working life, the machine itself is the cheapest part. Roughly 70–80% of the total cost of owning a compressor is the electricity to run it; the purchase price is only a small slice, and spares and service make up the rest. A compressor is, functionally, a device that converts a very large electricity bill into useful work. That single ratio explains two of Elgi's most important strategies — why energy efficiency is worth paying a premium for (a machine that's a few percent more efficient saves more over its life than its entire sticker price), and why the aftermarket that keeps a machine running efficiently is such a valuable, sticky business.

02

How Elgi earns: the machine, then the annuity

Two revenue engines with very different economics — this is the section that makes the model click.

Elgi's revenue comes in two forms, and they behave completely differently. There's the equipment sale — a new compressor bought by a factory — which is cyclical, competitive and lower-margin. And there's the aftermarket — the spare parts, filters, lubricants, service contracts and overhauls that every installed machine consumes for the rest of its life — which is recurring, defensible and high-margin. Understanding the relationship between the two is the key to the business.

Every equipment sale is loss-leading in spirit and annuity-building in effect: the sticker sale is the smaller, harder-won prize; the decade of service behind it is the compounding one.

Why the aftermarket is the quiet prize

  • It recurs. A compressor runs for 8–15 years and needs regular servicing, consumables and spares throughout. Revenue arrives whether or not the customer is buying new machines this year — a shock absorber against the equipment cycle.
  • It's stickier. Once a machine is installed and under a service plan, the customer rarely shops around for spares; the original maker has the parts, the manuals and the trust. Genuine spares also protect the machine's efficiency (see the electricity-bill point above), so cutting corners is a false economy for the customer.
  • It's higher-margin. Aftermarket sales carry structurally better margins than new-equipment sales, so a rising aftermarket share lifts the whole company's profitability.
  • It scales with the fleet. Today the aftermarket is roughly 16–18% of Elgi's global revenue; management wants to push it toward 20–22% over five years. Every machine sold anywhere in the world quietly enlarges the pool it draws from.
Why this matters for the global story specifically

The aftermarket is also the hidden logic of Elgi's overseas expansion. When Elgi enters a new country, the early years are dominated by the hard, low-margin work of selling machines and building distribution. But each of those sales is seeding an installed base that will throw off service revenue for a decade. So an international market that looks unprofitable today is, in part, an aftermarket annuity being planted for tomorrow. That's the mechanism bulls are counting on — and the reason patience (or impatience) with the overseas margin drag is the crux of the whole investment debate.

03

The company and who controls it

A 65-year-old Coimbatore engineering house, professionally run, with an owner-manager's long horizon.

Elgi was founded in 1960 in Coimbatore by L.G. Govindaswamy Naidu — the name "ELGi" is drawn from his initials. It began in automotive garage equipment (car lifts and service gear, still a business today) before moving into air compressors, which became the heart of the company. It is now led by Dr. Jairam Varadaraj, the managing director, whose stewardship has defined the modern, globally-ambitious Elgi.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter holding ~31%. The founding family retains a controlling but not overwhelming stake (the largest single promoter entity holds ~20%), with the balance held by institutions and the public — a listed company run with professional discipline rather than a tightly-held family shop.
  • Owner-manager horizon. The "#2 by 2027" ambition — a multi-decade, deliberately margin-diluting global build-out — is exactly the kind of patient bet that a family-anchored management can sustain through cycles where a purely quarterly-driven board might not.
  • Balance sheet. Net cash (~₹620 Cr at FY26) and a ~27% return on capital employed — the international expansion has been funded from internal cash flow and prudent balance-sheet use, not aggressive leverage.
A genuine surprise: most Indian mid-caps that go abroad do so timidly — an export order here, a distributor there. Elgi did the opposite. A Coimbatore engineering firm publicly committed, back in 2010, to outrank all but one of the world's compressor makers, and then bought and built operations across the US, Italy, France and Australia to get there. That a ~₹18,000-Cr company from Tamil Nadu is openly gunning for the global silver medal in its category — challenging Sweden's Atlas Copco — is the fact that most surprises a newcomer.

The product range, broken into parts

A full-line compressor maker, plus a legacy garage-equipment business.

Elgi's defining commercial strength is breadth: it makes compressors across nearly the full range of technologies, pressures and sizes, so it can serve a tiny workshop and a giant continuous-process plant from the same catalogue. That completeness is what lets it stand as a genuine alternative to the global majors rather than a niche specialist.

The compressor catalogue (jargon decoded)

  • Rotary screw (oil-lubricated) — the volume core: reliable, continuous air for factories. Where Elgi's Indian scale and cost advantage bite hardest.
  • Oil-free screw & centrifugal — the premium tier for industries where air purity is critical. The AB air-bearing series is the technical showcase — large oil-free machines that put Elgi in a segment historically ruled by a few global names.
  • Reciprocating (piston) — smaller, lower-cost machines for workshops and light industry, including the LD direct-drive range.
  • Portable — diesel-driven trailer units for construction, mining and drilling, strengthened by the Italian Rotair acquisition.
  • Railway, marine & specialist — application-specific compressors and air systems, plus dryers, filters and accessories that round out a complete compressed-air room.
  • Automotive equipment — the original business: car lifts, wheel aligners, tyre changers and garage lubrication systems, sold to workshops and service networks.
Why full-line breadth is a moat, not clutter

A factory that standardises on Elgi for its small workshop compressors is far more likely to buy Elgi when it needs a big oil-free machine — one supplier, one service relationship, one set of spares. Breadth lets Elgi land a customer at any size and grow with them, and it spreads the fixed cost of a distribution and service network across many products. A single-product rival has to win each battle from scratch; a full-line player fights the war on its own terms. The flip side is that maintaining a full range is R&D- and inventory-heavy — which is one reason working capital and product-development spend run higher here than at a narrow specialist.

05

The India franchise: the cash engine

Number two at home, and the profit pool that funds everything else.

In India, Elgi is the clear #2 compressor company with roughly 22% market share, second only to the local arm of global leader Atlas Copco. The domestic business is the mature, profitable heart of the group — it earns high-teens operating margins, generates strong cash, and is levered to India's long industrial and manufacturing upcycle (the "Make in India" capex wave, new factories, infrastructure and formalisation of small industry).

The Indian demand drivers are broad and structural: rising manufacturing investment, factory automation, growth in construction and infrastructure, and the steady shift of small unorganised workshops toward branded, energy-efficient machines. Because compressed air is used across so many industries, no single downturn sinks the whole book — but a general industrial slowdown does slow order intake.

The relationship between the two halves

The Indian business does double duty. It is a fine standalone franchise — a market leader with a dense service network and a growing aftermarket. But its more important role in the equity story is as the funding source for the global ambition. The high-teens margins and reliable cash from India are what pay for the years of thin margins abroad while overseas markets are built out. In effect, a shareholder in Elgi owns a solid, cash-generative Indian compressor leader plus a multi-year, self-financed venture bet on becoming a global champion — and the value of the second piece depends entirely on whether the overseas margins ever catch up to the home ones.

06

The global-#2 ambition — the crux

The single strategy that defines Elgi as an investment: going global, and the margin drag it costs on the way.

In 2010, Elgi's management set a target that still governs the company: to become the world's second-largest air-compressor maker by 2027, challenging Sweden's Atlas Copco for the top of a global market worth well over $20 billion. Elgi sits around #6 today. The path from there to the podium runs through markets — the US, Europe, Australia, the Middle East — where Elgi started as an unknown, and where the only way to win is to build direct sales, service and distribution from scratch, or buy it. That build-out is the reason Elgi is both exciting and expensive.

Why going global costs margin before it makes margin

The logic of an owned overseas presence is sound — you capture the full price, control the service quality and, crucially, own the aftermarket annuity. But the sequence is brutal on near-term profits:

  • You pay the fixed costs first. A new-country operation carries the full weight of salaries, branches, service vans and inventory long before volume is large enough to cover them.
  • Brand trust is slow. Industrial buyers are conservative; they don't switch compressor suppliers casually. Winning share from entrenched incumbents takes years of proof.
  • The aftermarket lags the equipment. The high-margin service revenue only builds once the installed base is large — so early years are all low-margin equipment sales and heavy investment, with the annuity still years out.

The clearest scar of this is Europe, where Elgi's subsidiaries have run at or near break-even for years — a genuine, ongoing drag that management describes as "improving but still modest." The US has been the brighter international story, though even there tariffs have bitten (see the risks section). The result is a striking split: a company earning ~27% on capital overall, held back at the margin line by international arms that have not yet earned their keep.

The metric that settles the whole argument

Forget the noise; one thing decides whether Elgi is a great compounder or an over-priced industrial: whether the international subsidiaries — Europe above all — converge toward the high-teens margins the India business already earns. Management targets a group EBITDA margin of around 18%, versus ~14.8% today. Almost the entire gap is the overseas drag. If Europe and the newer geographies turn the corner as their installed bases mature, group margins rise, the aftermarket share climbs toward 20–22%, and profit grows far faster than revenue. If they stay stuck near break-even, Elgi is a fine Indian business wrapped around a permanently dilutive global adventure — and a ~42× multiple is hard to defend. Watch that convergence, quarter by quarter, above every other number.

07

Oil-free, energy efficiency & the premiumisation bet

Moving up from cheap-and-reliable to clean-and-efficient — where the margins and the moat live.

The second leg of Elgi's strategy sits inside the product itself: a deliberate climb from commodity oil-injected machines into oil-free and energy-efficient compressors. This is where the "70–80% of lifetime cost is electricity" fact from Section 01 becomes a business plan.

Why oil-free is the high ground

  • Purity commands a premium. In food and beverage, pharmaceuticals, electronics, medical air and textiles, even a trace of oil in the compressed air can ruin a batch. Those industries pay up for guaranteed oil-free air — a higher-margin, harder-to-copy product than a standard screw machine.
  • It's technically demanding. Oil-free and centrifugal air-bearing machines (Elgi's AB series) require serious engineering. Building a credible oil-free range is what separates a global contender from a regional volume player — and it's Elgi's ticket into segments historically owned by the majors.
  • It rides a durable tailwind. As Indian and global manufacturing shifts toward higher-value, cleaner production (pharma, semiconductors, processed food), the oil-free share of demand structurally grows.
Elgi's distinctive pitch — oil-free at oil-injected prices

Elgi's stated global philosophy is deliberately provocative: to offer oil-free compressors at the same price as oil-injected machines. If a customer can get clean air without paying the traditional oil-free premium, the value proposition against Atlas Copco or Ingersoll Rand becomes hard to ignore. Pair that with a genuinely efficient machine, and the customer's real prize — the shrinking electricity bill over ten years — dwarfs any difference in sticker price. This is the sharpest edge of Elgi's challenger position: not "cheaper machines," but "the same clean, efficient air for less lifetime cost." Whether it can hold that pricing while earning acceptable margins is the tension inside the premiumisation bet.

08

The industry and the rivals

A big, consolidated global market led by a handful of names — and a Chinese cost threat below.

The global compressor market is large (well over $20 billion), mostly organised, and dominated by a small set of global majors. Elgi competes against companies many times its size at the top, and against low-cost Chinese makers at the bottom — a two-front position that shapes both its pricing and its strategy.

The competitive squeeze — and Elgi's answer to it

Elgi is caught between giants above and cost-cutters below, which is exactly why its strategy is value, not price. It can't outspend Atlas Copco on R&D or distribution, and it won't win a race to the bottom against Chinese volume makers. Its bet is the middle path made compelling: a full range, credible oil-free technology, genuine energy efficiency, and an owned service network that captures the aftermarket — offered at a total-cost-of-ownership the majors struggle to match. It's a defensible position, but a demanding one: it requires Elgi to keep out-engineering the cheap and out-pricing the premium, simultaneously, in market after market.

The regulatory & macro backdrop: compressors are unregulated products, but the business is exposed to trade policy (import tariffs in the US and elsewhere have directly dented North-American profits), commodity prices (steel and castings are major inputs), and currency (with ~half of revenue overseas, forex swings move reported earnings). None is existential, but together they add cyclicality and noise to any given quarter.

Financials & valuation

Steady growth, improving margins, a fortress balance sheet — and a premium price.

Elgi's numbers describe a quality industrial: consistent double-digit revenue growth, margins that are slowly widening as the mix improves, high returns on capital and no net debt. The debate isn't the quality of the business — it's whether the ~42× earnings multiple already prices in the overseas margin recovery that hasn't happened yet.

How to read the ~42× multiple

At a ~₹18,200 Cr market cap on ~₹430 Cr of FY26 profit, Elgi trades near 42× trailing earnings — rich for a company earning mid-teens margins in a cyclical industry. That price rests on two beliefs: that the aftermarket annuity gives the earnings unusual quality and resilience, and that the overseas margin drag is a temporary investment phase, not a permanent feature — so group margins march from ~14.8% toward ~18% and profit outgrows revenue for years. The counter-view is straightforward: a ~15%-margin industrial with meaningful cyclicality and a chronically loss-adjacent European arm is being valued like a proven global compounder before the proof is in. The multiple, in other words, is an advance payment on a margin convergence that has been promised for over a decade and is still only partway delivered.

10

The growth runway

Four stacked levers — and a 2027 finish line that management set itself.

Elgi's growth doesn't rely on a single bet; it stacks several independent levers, each of which can contribute even if another disappoints. Together they underpin management's medium-term goal of steady low-double-digit revenue growth with margins climbing toward ~18%.

  • India's industrial upcycle — the home market's manufacturing-capex, automation and formalisation tailwind keeps the high-margin cash engine growing.
  • International scale-up — the US and other geographies growing into profitability, with Europe the swing factor. This is the biggest single source of potential margin upside.
  • Aftermarket compounding — the installed base of 2M+ machines throwing off a rising, high-margin service annuity, targeted to grow from ~16–18% to 20–22% of revenue.
  • Premium mix shift — oil-free, energy-efficient and centrifugal machines lifting both average selling price and margin as customers move up the value ladder.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality industrial with a genuine global ambition — priced as if the ambition is already achieved.

Elgi is a well-run, net-cash, high-return engineering company with a real technology story and a rare global ambition for an Indian mid-cap. The investment argument turns almost entirely on one question — does the overseas expansion become margin-accretive, or does it stay a permanent drag — and on whether the current price already assumes the happy answer.

The bull case

  • Aftermarket annuity — 2M+ installed machines throwing off recurring, high-margin spares and service that compound with every sale.
  • Margin convergence ahead — as overseas subsidiaries mature, group margins can climb from ~14.8% toward the ~18% target, so profit outgrows revenue.
  • Value-led oil-free / efficiency edge — clean, efficient air at a lower lifetime cost is a durable way to take share from the majors.
  • Net cash, ~27% RoCE — a fortress balance sheet self-funding a global build-out.
  • Aligned, patient ownership — a family-anchored management pursuing a genuinely long-horizon goal.

The bear case

  • Premium valuation — ~42× earnings for a mid-teens-margin cyclical industrial is a lot to pay before the margin story is proven.
  • The European drag persists — subsidiaries near break-even for years; convergence has been promised since 2010 and is only partly delivered.
  • Cyclicality — compressor demand rides industrial capex; a manufacturing slowdown hits equipment orders.
  • Tariffs, steel & forex — US tariffs have dented North-American profit; steel costs and currency add volatility with ~half of revenue overseas.
  • Formidable competition — Atlas Copco and Ingersoll Rand above, low-cost Chinese makers below.
The bottom line

Elgi is one of the more admirable industrial franchises in India: a market leader at home, a real technologist in oil-free air, and a company that chose the hard road of building an owned global business rather than merely exporting from Coimbatore. The business quality is not in doubt. What's in doubt is arithmetic — a ~42× multiple leans heavily on the overseas half earning something close to what the Indian half already does. The company set itself a public finish line: the world's #2 compressor maker by 2027. That milestone, and the margin line that would have to travel with it — group EBITDA moving from ~14.8% toward ~18% as Europe finally turns — is the concrete test now only a couple of years away. Reach it, and the price looks like foresight; miss it, and Elgi is a fine company that was valued as a finished one too early.
