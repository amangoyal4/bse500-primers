00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Aptus Value Housing Finance is a housing finance company (HFC) — a specialist lender that gives out home loans. But it is a very particular kind of HFC. It does not chase salaried professionals in big cities buying ₹80-lakh flats. It lends small home loans — averaging roughly ₹8–10 lakh — to low-income, self-employed, informal-sector families in small towns and semi-urban South India: the vegetable vendor, the roadside trader, the tailor, the daily-wager building or buying a first pukka house. Every loan is secured against that self-occupied home. Banks find these borrowers uncreditworthy — no salary slip, no formal income proof — so Aptus underwrites them by hand, at the branch, feet on the street.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The unit economics are extraordinary. Aptus lends at a high blended yield (roughly ~17% — these borrowers have few alternatives), keeps operating costs tight, and loses very little to bad debt (FY26 credit cost just ~0.51%) because the loan sits on the borrower's own home at a low loan-to-value. The result is a return on assets of ~8% — better than almost any blue-chip bank in India.
  • It is the rare lender with too much capital. Most finance companies borrow heavily to juice returns. Aptus barely does: FY26 gearing was just ~1.56x debt-to-equity and its capital-adequacy ratio a startling ~75%. That fortress balance sheet is a legacy of its private-equity funding history. It makes the book bulletproof — but it also holds the return on equity down, because so much shareholder money sits idle rather than working.
  • Growth is steady and self-funded. FY26 AUM grew ~21% to ₹13,107 Cr, disbursements ~₹4,009 Cr, and net profit rose ~26% to ₹943 Cr. Management guides to ~22–24% AUM growth in FY27. The stock trades near ~3x book and ~16x earnings — a premium to book that the market pays for that best-in-class ROA.
In plain terms

Aptus is a hand-stitched mortgage lender for people the banking system ignores. Its customer built a two-room house on a plot in a Tamil Nadu or Andhra town and needs ₹9 lakh to finish it — but earns cash from a shop or a trade with no payslip to show. Aptus sends a person to sit in that shop, assess the real income, value the house, and lend against it at a rate that reflects the risk and the lack of alternatives. Because the loan is on the family's own roof, they pay it before almost anything else, and losses stay tiny. Do that thousands of times across the South and you get a business that earns a huge return on every rupee of assets — while, oddly, sitting on a mountain of unused capital that could make those returns even larger.

01

Affordable housing finance 101: the corner of lending banks skip

No finance degree needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

A home loan is the simplest product in finance: a lender advances money to buy or build a house, takes the house as security, and is repaid in monthly instalments over 10–20 years. If the borrower stops paying, the lender can — in theory — take the house. For a salaried buyer with documented income and a title-clean flat, dozens of banks compete and the rate is low. Affordable housing finance is what happens at the other end of that market: small loans, informal borrowers, semi-urban property — the segment mainstream banks find too fiddly and too risky to serve at scale.

The economics that decide everything: yield minus losses

A lender's profit is, at its core, one subtraction: what it earns on loans, minus what it pays for money, minus what it loses to defaults, minus its running costs. Affordable-housing lenders like Aptus win at both ends of that sum. They earn a lot (a ~17% yield, because their informal borrowers can't get a cheap bank loan) and they lose almost nothing (credit cost ~0.5%, because the loan is on the family's own home). A mainstream bank earning 8–9% on a salaried home loan and a fraction of that in margin cannot come close to those economics. The entire attraction of this niche is that being willing to do hard, manual underwriting of "un-bankable" people is paid for very, very well.

Why banks leave this segment alone

  • No paperwork to lend against. A bank's credit model wants a salary slip, a tax return, a bureau score. A self-employed vegetable trader has none of it. Assessing that person's real income takes a human visit, not an algorithm — expensive, slow, and un-scalable for a large bank.
  • Small tickets, high touch. A ₹9 lakh loan costs almost as much to originate and service as a ₹90 lakh one, but earns a tenth of the interest in absolute rupees. Banks would rather write one big-city mortgage than ten small-town ones.
  • Local knowledge required. Valuing a half-built house in a semi-urban block, checking a murky property title, judging a family's standing in the community — this is deeply local work that a branch-light national bank can't easily replicate.

That gap is the whole opportunity. A specialist willing to build a dense branch network, hire and train local staff, and underwrite by hand can serve millions of first-time home-buyers the formal system overlooks — and be richly rewarded for the effort. Aptus is one of a small clutch of listed companies (Aavas, Home First, Aadhar, India Shelter) built precisely to mine it.

What Aptus actually lends, and to whom

One collateral — the home — behind three related products.

Aptus is not a single-product lender, but everything it does is anchored to the same security: a self-occupied residential property. Around that anchor it offers three related loan types, all to broadly the same customer — the low-income, self-employed household — and all underwritten the same hands-on way.

The three loan lines, decoded

  • Home loans — the core. Modest loans (average ticket roughly ₹8–10 lakh) to buy, construct or extend a first home. These are the lowest-yielding but lowest-risk product, and they define Aptus's identity as a pure housing financier rather than a general NBFC.
  • Loans against property & small-business loans — the adjacency. The same self-employed family often needs working capital for the shop or trade that generates its income. Aptus lends that money too, secured on the self-occupied property (capped at a conservative share of its value). These carry higher yields — pushing toward the low-20s% — and lift the blended rate of the whole book. This is the piece most like a business lender, and it is also where a little more of the credit risk sits.
  • Top-ups, repeat loans & insurance distribution — the flywheel. A borrower who has paid cleanly for a few years is a known, low-risk quantity. Aptus can extend a top-up loan or cross-sell home-loan insurance, deepening the relationship at very low incremental cost. Repeat, proven customers are the cheapest and safest lending a company like this can do.
Why the collateral, not the income, is the real security

For a salaried mortgage, the lender leans on the borrower's income — the reliable monthly payslip — and treats the house as a backstop. Aptus works the other way round. The informal borrower's income is genuinely hard to verify and can be lumpy, so Aptus leans first on the collateral and the borrower's stake in it. When a family has put a large chunk of its own savings into a home it lives in, has a low loan against it, and knows the local branch officer by name, the incentive to keep paying is overwhelming — this is the one asset the household will protect above all others. The house is not the fallback; it is the reason the loan gets repaid at all.

A distinction worth nailing: Aptus is often mentioned in the same breath as Five-Star Business Finance — another South-India secured lender to the self-employed. But they sell different things. Five-Star lends for business needs against property; Aptus is a true housing financier whose defining product is the loan to buy or build the home itself. The borrower overlaps; the loan purpose, the collateral logic and the regulatory box (HFC vs NBFC) do not.
03

The company, the founder, and who owns it now

A Chennai-built lender, founded by a veteran, and just handed from its PE backers to the public market.

Aptus was founded in 2009 in Chennai by M. Anandan, a finance veteran who had earlier built and run Cholamandalam Investment & Finance — one of the Murugappa group's flagship NBFCs. He set out to do one thing well: lend for homes to the self-employed poor of the South, a segment he understood from decades in retail finance. Deep-pocketed private-equity investors — most prominently WestBridge Capital — funded the build-out, which is the origin of the company's unusually heavy capital base.

Ownership & structure

  • Founder: M. Anandan remains the promoter and guiding figure (executive chairman), with the professional management team running day-to-day operations. Founder/promoter holding is meaningful but not majority — this has long been a substantially institution-owned company (holding percentages should be read off the latest shareholding filing; treat any single figure as approx.).
  • The WestBridge exit: in FY26 WestBridge Capital — historically the single largest shareholder — sold down its entire position in the open market. That is a genuinely notable event: it removes a large overhang of PE stock but also the anchor of a long-term strategic holder, leaving a more dispersed institutional register (domestic and foreign funds).
  • Group structure: the listed entity is the HFC, Aptus Value Housing Finance India Ltd. Alongside it sits a wholly-owned NBFC subsidiary (Aptus Finance India) that carries the non-housing/business-loan portfolio — the two-entity setup lets Aptus do both regulated housing loans and the higher-yield secured business loans cleanly.
The founder-and-capital lineage matters: a company built by a retail-finance veteran and funded through repeated equity rounds, rather than by piling on debt, ends up with two defining traits — disciplined, conservative underwriting (Anandan's stamp) and a balance sheet drowning in capital (the PE legacy). Both are central to why Aptus looks the way it does today: pristine credit, sky-high capital adequacy, and a return on equity that is deliberately, structurally understated.
04

The feet-on-street moat: underwriting the un-bankable

The part that can't be copied from a spreadsheet — and the reason losses stay tiny.

The most important thing Aptus does is also the least visible: it works out how much money an informal, undocumented household actually earns, and whether it can repay a loan for the next fifteen years. There is no data feed for that. It is done by people, in person, one file at a time — and doing it well is the entire moat.

Consider the problem. A prospective borrower runs a small provisions shop, keeps no formal accounts, and files no income-tax return. A bank's automated credit engine simply rejects the file. Aptus instead sends a trained field officer to sit in the shop, count the daily footfall, examine the stock and the cash box, talk to neighbours and suppliers, inspect the home being financed, and reconstruct a realistic monthly cash flow from the ground up. That assessment — not a document — is the basis of the loan. It is slow, human and hard to scale, which is exactly why it is defensible.

Three deliberate choices make the model work. First, everything is in-house — sourcing the customer, underwriting the loan and collecting the instalment are all done by Aptus's own employees, not outsourced to direct-selling agents whose incentive is volume, not quality. Second, the branch is the unit of the business: a dense network of small branches puts staff physically close to borrowers, so they can visit, assess and — crucially — collect. Third, the collateral discipline is absolute: no loan without a clear, self-occupied property at a conservative LTV. The company would rather pass on a loan than stretch on the security.

Why this is a moat and not just a cost

It is tempting to see manual, branch-heavy underwriting as an inefficiency a smarter, tech-driven rival will one day undercut. The counter is that the knowledge lives in the local relationship, and relationships don't digitise. Knowing which streets flood, which builder cuts corners, which trades are seasonal, and which family will always find the money to protect its house — that is accumulated, on-the-ground judgement, branch by branch, that a fintech app cannot download. The proof is in the loss rate: a book of loans to "un-bankable" people that writes off well under 1% a year is not luck; it is underwriting. The high cost of doing it by hand is the price of the very low cost of being wrong.

A genuine surprise: the people most would consider Aptus's riskiest customers — daily-wage earners and cashflow-lumpy micro-traders with no payslip — sit behind one of the lowest loss rates in Indian retail lending. The reason is counter-intuitive: precisely because these families have no safety net and no easy re-financing option, the home they've sunk their savings into is sacrosanct, and the instalment on it is the last payment they will ever skip.

The yield-minus-loss spread: where the profit is made

The subtraction that explains Aptus's whole income statement — in one picture.

Strip a lender down to its essence and it is a machine for capturing a spread: the rate it charges, less the rate it pays for money, less what it loses to defaults, less what it costs to run. For Aptus, that chain of subtractions is unusually generous at every step. Follow the rupee from the ~17% it charges a borrower down to the profit that lands in shareholders' pockets.

Aptus earns a ~17% blended yield, pays ~8.3% for its money (a spread of ~8.9%), runs a fat ~10.4% net interest margin on average assets — and then loses only about half a percent of the book to bad debt. That combination, not any single number, is the engine.

Look at the last two bars together, because they are the crux. Aptus runs a ~10% net interest margin — roughly triple what a mainstream bank earns on a home loan — and then hands back barely half a percent of it to loan losses. Most lenders that charge high rates do so because they expect heavy defaults; the high yield is danger money. Aptus is the unusual case where the high yield reflects the borrower's lack of alternatives, not the borrower's likelihood of defaulting — so the fat margin drops almost cleanly to the bottom line.

The device that only fits Aptus: a ~17% yield on a first-timer's own roof, with a ~0.5% loss

Here is the single sentence that captures this company and would fit almost no other lender: Aptus charges a ~17% yield to families the banks reject, on a loan secured by the very home those families live in — and loses only about half a percent of it to default. The two halves of that sentence usually cannot coexist. A ~17% rate normally signals a book that bleeds; a ~0.5% loss rate normally signals a book of safe, low-yielding prime borrowers. Aptus earns the first and delivers the second because of the one structural fact that ties them together — the collateral is the borrower's own occupied house at a low LTV, the asset a poor family will do anything to keep. The spread it captures is the reward for being willing to price and underwrite a risk that, correctly assembled, barely materialises.

One caveat sits inside this happy picture. FY26 saw the margin come in a little — NIM eased to ~10.4% from ~11.1% the year before as Aptus's own borrowing costs rose, and credit cost ticked up to ~0.51% (from ~0.28%) as the higher-yielding non-housing book seasoned and a few regions saw weaker recoveries. Both moves are small and off an exceptional base — but they are the first sign that even this best-in-class spread is not immune to the rate cycle and to portfolio ageing. The spread is wide; it is not fixed.

06

The low-leverage, high-ROA paradox

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — and it is unique to this company.

Every lender lives on two return numbers, and Aptus is a rare company where they point in opposite directions. Its return on assets (ROA) is spectacular. Its return on equity (ROE) is merely good. Understanding why those two diverge — and what would close the gap — is the entire Aptus thesis.

First, the two numbers

Return on assets asks: for every ₹100 of loans, how much profit does the business make? Aptus's answer — ~8% — is astonishing. Most banks make 1–2% ROA; a very good one makes 2%. Aptus makes four times that, straight out of the yield-minus-loss spread from the last section. On this measure it is one of the most profitable lenders in the country, full stop.

Return on equity asks a different question: for every ₹100 of shareholders' own money, how much profit? Here Aptus scores a respectable but unremarkable ~18%, rising toward ~21% at the FY26 exit — good, but nowhere near four times a bank's. The reason the two returns split apart is leverage.

A typical HFC borrows ₹4–5 for every ₹1 of equity; Aptus borrows barely ₹1.6, and holds a ~75% capital-adequacy ratio against a ~15% floor. That fortress makes losses irrelevant — but it means each rupee of equity backs far fewer loans, which is why a world-beating 8% ROA converts into only an ~18–21% ROE.

Here is the mechanism in one line. ROE is roughly ROA multiplied by leverage. A normal HFC turns a 2–3% ROA into an 18% ROE by borrowing four or five times its equity. Aptus turns a far superior 8% ROA into a similar ROE because it borrows almost nothing — its leverage multiplier is tiny. Its extraordinary ROA is being wasted on an under-levered balance sheet. The company is, unusually, over-capitalised: a ~75% capital-adequacy ratio against a regulatory floor near 15% means it holds roughly five times the capital it is required to.

The paradox at the heart of Aptus — and the lever hidden inside it

Aptus earns one of the best returns on assets of any lender in India partly because it uses so little debt: a fortress balance sheet lets it lend calmly, price for risk, and never be forced to sell a loan in a bad moment. But that same conservatism caps its return on equity — the profit-per-shareholder-rupee that ultimately drives the share price. So Aptus faces the opposite problem to almost every other finance company: not "how do we survive with too much debt?" but "how do we deploy too much equity?" And embedded in that awkward question is the clearest upside lever in the story. If Aptus simply gears up over time — grows its loan book faster than its equity by borrowing more against a pristine, proven book — it can lift ROE materially without touching its best-in-class ROA. Rising ROE at FY26 (toward ~21%) is the first evidence that lever is already being pulled. The rare lender whose headroom for improvement comes from taking more risk on the funding side, not less.

Most lenders are constrained by their balance sheet. Aptus is constrained by having too much of one — and closing that gap is the plan, not a problem.

There is a subtlety worth stating plainly. Levering up is a genuine lever, but it is not free: more debt means more sensitivity to funding costs and to any future rise in defaults, and it slowly trades away the very fortress that makes today's book so safe. The bet is that Aptus's underwriting is good enough to carry more leverage without the losses that would normally accompany it. If that holds, ROE grinds higher for years on a book that stays clean. If it does not — if gearing rises just as credit cost drifts up — the two forces work against each other. The direction of travel, though, is clear and deliberate: from fortress toward efficiency.

07

Geography: a deep-South book, cautiously going wider

The concentration that built the moat — and the expansion that tests it.

Aptus is, today, overwhelmingly a South-Indian lender. Its branches and its book are concentrated in Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Karnataka — the four states where its founder's networks are deepest and where the affordable-home market is large and familiar. That density is both the source of its underwriting edge and the biggest single risk to it.

The case for concentration is that it is exactly what makes the manual model work. A tight geographic footprint means branches, staff, valuers and collectors all operate on home turf — they know the local property markets, builders, titles and repayment culture intimately. Spread that same team thin across the whole country and the local knowledge that keeps losses near zero would dilute. Depth, here, is a feature.

The case against is the flip side of the same coin. A book this concentrated is exposed to anything that hits the South as a region — a state-level policy shock, a local property downturn, a monsoon failure or crop-price collapse that squeezes the semi-urban economy, or simply intensifying competition as every affordable-housing lender crowds the same profitable states. A nationally diversified lender can absorb a regional shock; a South-heavy one feels it in full.

Why the expansion is the thing to watch — carefully

Aptus's answer to concentration risk is measured geographic expansion — adding branches at the edges of its core states and stepping cautiously into newer central and western geographies. This is the right strategic move and also the moment of maximum execution risk. The company's entire loss record was built on home-turf underwriting; every new district is territory where its people don't yet know which builder cuts corners or which street floods. Grow too fast into unfamiliar geographies and the pristine credit metrics — the whole reason to own the stock — are precisely what gets tested first. The expansion is necessary; the pace of it is the variable to watch, because it is where a clean book could first crack.

The tension in one line: the same geographic density that produces Aptus's near-zero losses is the concentration risk investors most worry about — and the diversification that would ease the worry is exactly what could dilute the underwriting edge. There is no costless way out of that trade-off; there is only executing it slowly and well.
08

Asset quality & collections: the number that can't be faked

For a lender to poor, informal borrowers, this section is the whole ballgame.

Any lender can grow fast by lending loosely; the bill arrives two or three years later as bad debt. For a company lending to low-income, undocumented borrowers, asset quality is not one metric among many — it is the test of whether the entire model is real. Aptus's record here is the foundation of its valuation.

Why collections, not disbursals, are the real skill

It is easy to give a loan; the craft is getting it back. In this segment, the difference between a good year and a bad one is made in collections — the branch officer who knows the borrower, notices trouble early, restructures a lumpy month, and shows up in person before an overdue instalment becomes a default. Aptus keeps collections in-house for exactly this reason: an outsourced agent chasing a receipt has neither the local knowledge nor the relationship to work a soft default back to health. The near-zero loss rate is not a lending achievement so much as a collections achievement — and it is the hardest part of the model for a rival to replicate, because it is thousands of individual relationships doing their quiet work every month.

The ~75% capital-adequacy ratio is the ultimate backstop under all of this. Even if a severe, correlated shock hit the South-Indian informal economy and losses spiked several-fold, Aptus holds so much capital that its solvency would not be in question — it would take a truly catastrophic, sustained default wave to impair the equity. That buffer is the same over-capitalisation that depresses ROE; here it earns its keep as an insurance policy the company has, in effect, massively over-bought.

09

Funding the book: the other side of the balance sheet

Where the money comes from — and why the cost of it now matters more.

A lender's profit depends as much on what it pays for money as on what it charges. Aptus borrows from banks, the National Housing Bank's refinance lines, and the debt markets, and lends that money out at its ~17% yield. For most of its life the cost side barely mattered — it borrowed so little relative to its equity that funding was almost an afterthought. As the company deliberately gears up, that changes.

Two features define Aptus's funding today. First, its cost of borrowing is moderate and well-managed — around ~8.3% in FY26, benefiting from strong credit ratings (a fortress balance sheet earns cheap money) and access to NHB refinance meant precisely for housing lenders to the underserved. Second, and more importantly for the future, it has enormous untapped borrowing capacity: at ~1.6x gearing against peers at 4–5x, Aptus could roughly double or triple its debt and still be conservatively funded. The funding runway is the mechanical enabler of the leverage lever from Section 06.

Why funding cost is the new swing factor

When a lender is barely levered, a rise in its borrowing rate is a rounding error — it has little debt to re-price. When it starts gearing up, that same rise bites. FY26 already showed the effect: as Aptus's cost of funds edged higher, its net interest margin slipped from ~11.1% to ~10.4%. Multiply that sensitivity across a much larger, more-levered book and the cost of borrowing becomes a genuine swing factor in profits — a variable that used to be irrelevant and is becoming central. The good news is that Aptus lends at rates high enough to absorb a lot of funding-cost movement before the model breaks; the caution is that the margin cushion, however fat, now has to be watched as the debt load rises.

There is a structural comfort here too. Because Aptus lends long (10–20 year mortgages) and borrows shorter, it runs some maturity mismatch — as every lender does — but its vast capital base and NHB access make its funding unusually stable and its liquidity position strong. The company is not one that has to worry about a funding line being pulled; if anything, its problem has always been the reverse — persuading itself to use more of the cheap borrowing available to it.

The industry and the rivals

A crowding niche — a clutch of listed affordable-HFCs, plus banks moving down-market.

Affordable housing finance has become one of the most-watched corners of Indian lending, and Aptus competes in a well-defined peer set. These are companies doing broadly the same thing — small home loans to the informal, semi-urban borrower — differentiated by geography, average ticket, and how much they lean on business loans versus pure housing.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two dynamics matter. First, this is a growing pond, not a shrinking one — India's affordable-home shortage is vast, so a handful of well-run HFCs can all compound for years without fighting over the same customers, at least while the segment expands. Second, and more threatening, is the slow drift of banks and large NBFCs down-market. They have far cheaper money than Aptus, and if they crack the hard part — underwriting informal income at scale — they could compete away the fat yields that make this niche special. Aptus's defence is precisely the thing banks find hardest to replicate: the local, manual, relationship-driven underwriting and collections that a national bank's model isn't built for. The competitive question, ultimately, is whether that human moat holds as bigger, cheaper lenders keep pressing on the niche.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A pristine, over-capitalised lender — asked to prove it can grow wider without growing looser.

Aptus enters FY27 in an enviable position: a ~₹13,000 Cr book compounding in the low-20s%, one of the best ROAs in Indian lending, a fortress balance sheet, and a clear, mechanical lever — leverage — to lift its one merely-average metric. The debate is not whether the business works; it plainly does. It is whether the exceptional returns survive scale, expansion, and a slowly seasoning book.

The bull case

  • Best-in-class unit economics — a ~17% yield and ~0.5% loss rate produce a ~8% ROA few lenders anywhere can match, straight from the collateral-backed model.
  • The leverage lever — at ~1.6x gearing and ~75% capital adequacy, Aptus can grow AUM on debt and lift ROE toward the mid-20s% without denting ROA.
  • Long runway — India's affordable-home shortage is vast; ~22–24% AUM growth guidance rests on a genuinely under-served market.
  • Fortress balance sheet — enormous capital buffer makes the book near-unbreakable and funding cheap and stable.
  • Deep underwriting moat — in-house, manual sourcing, underwriting and collections that banks and fintechs struggle to replicate.

The bear case

  • Geographic concentration — a South-heavy book is exposed to a regional shock, policy change or local downturn a diversified lender would shrug off.
  • Asset quality drifting — GNPA rose to ~1.52% and credit cost roughly doubled in FY26; the higher-yield business book is seasoning and could keep softening.
  • The leverage lever cuts both ways — gearing up lifts ROE but adds funding-cost and default sensitivity, trading away the fortress that makes the book so safe.
  • Crowding niche — Aavas, Home First, Aadhar, India Shelter and banks/Bajaj moving down-market could compress the fat yields over time.
  • Key-person & expansion risk — founder-anchored; and every new district is untested ground for a model built on home-turf local knowledge. Rich ~3x book leaves little slack if returns fade.
The bottom line

Aptus has done the hard thing — building a lender that earns a world-class return on assets by underwriting, by hand, the very borrowers the banking system rejects, and losing almost nothing doing it. What it has not yet done is convert that exceptional profitability into an exceptional return on equity, because it is weighed down by the happiest of burdens: a balance sheet with far more capital than it uses. The next chapter of the story is that conversion — gearing a proven book higher while pushing carefully out of its Southern heartland, and doing both without letting the ~0.5% loss rate that is its entire calling card creep upward. Watch three prints each year, together: the ROE ticking up as gearing rises, the credit cost staying near half a percent as the book grows and widens, and the GNPA holding around 1.5% rather than climbing. If those three hold hands quarter after quarter, Aptus turns its over-capitalisation from a drag into fuel. If credit cost keeps drifting as expansion accelerates, the market will stop paying three times book for a return on equity that was supposed to be rising.
