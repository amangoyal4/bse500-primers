00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Apollo Tyres makes tyres — the round rubber ones on trucks, buses, cars, tractors and two-wheelers. It is one of India's top three tyre makers (alongside MRF and CEAT), the clear leader in the truck-and-bus segment, and — unusually for an Indian tyre company — it also runs a substantial European business through the premium Vredestein brand, with plants in the Netherlands and Hungary. It is controlled by the Kanwar family and headquartered in Gurugram.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Two geographies, one company. Roughly three-quarters of revenue is India (where Apollo leads in truck-and-bus radials and is a strong number-two-ish player in passenger-car tyres) and about a fifth is Europe (the Vredestein premium car and agricultural brand). Europe gives developed-market premium exposure — and higher segment margins — but also European-demand cyclicality that has been a recent drag.
  • The replacement market is the real profit engine. Around 65% of Apollo's sales are "replacement" — the tyres you buy at a dealer when your old ones wear out — which is branded, higher-margin and recurring, versus the lower-margin "OEM" tyres sold to vehicle factories. Understanding that split (Section 01) is the single most useful thing to grasp about any tyre company.
  • A commodity-cyclical business that just got financially healthier. Raw materials (natural and synthetic rubber, carbon black) are ~60–65% of cost, so margins swing hard with rubber and crude prices. FY26 was a recovery year: revenue ₹28,471 Cr (+9%), net profit ₹1,372 Cr (+22%), EBITDA margin back up to 14.6%, and — crucially — net debt cut to around ₹1,300 Cr from a peak above ₹6,000 Cr, with management now preaching "less capex, more returns."
In plain terms

Think of Apollo as a branded consumer-and-industrial products company that happens to sell rubber circles. Its factories turn rubber, carbon black and crude-oil derivatives into tyres; its real value sits in two things the commodity doesn't capture — a trusted brand (Apollo in India, Vredestein in Europe) and a vast dealer network that puts the right tyre in front of a truck operator or car owner when they need it. The catch is that it is cyclical twice over: demand rises and falls with the auto and freight economy, and margins rise and fall with rubber and oil prices — so a great year and a poor year can look very different even though the business hasn't changed.

01

Tyre economics 101: OEM vs replacement

No auto-industry background needed — just the one distinction that drives all tyre profits.

A tyre gets sold into a vehicle in two completely different ways, and the difference explains almost everything about how tyre companies make money. Get this one idea and the rest of the primer clicks into place.

The two markets for a tyre
ChannelWhat it isWhy it matters
OEM (factory-fit)Tyres sold to vehicle makers (Tata Motors, Maruti, Ashok Leyland…) to fit on brand-new vehicles rolling off the line.Lower margin. Big buyers negotiate hard on price. But it seeds the brand — the tyre a buyer first drives on shapes what they buy again later.
ReplacementTyres sold through dealers to owners whose original tyres have worn out — every vehicle needs new tyres every few years.The profit engine. Branded, higher-margin, and recurring — a built-in annuity from the huge stock of vehicles already on the road.

Here is the mechanism, step by step. A truck maker buys, say, ten tyres from Apollo at a thin margin to build a new truck (OEM). That truck then runs for years, wearing through set after set of replacement tyres — and because the operator drove out of the factory on Apollo rubber and had a good experience, they tend to buy Apollo again at the dealer, this time at a healthier margin. The OEM sale is the loss-leader introduction; the replacement sales over the vehicle's life are where the money is made.

The replacement market is far larger and more profitable than OEM because it is fed by the entire installed base of vehicles already on the road — not just this year's new-vehicle sales.
Why replacement is worth watching most

Because replacement demand comes from the stock of vehicles already on the road (which changes slowly), it is steadier than OEM demand (which lurches with each year's new-vehicle sales). Replacement is also where a strong brand and dealer network earn their keep — a truck fleet owner picking tyres at a dealer will pay up for a name they trust to last. That's why Apollo, with about 65% of sales in replacement and clear leadership in truck-and-bus, has a more defensible profit base than the OEM-heavy mix might suggest. When you read a tyre company's results, the two numbers that matter most are replacement volume growth and the raw-material cost trend.

02

The product map: which tyres, and why they differ

Not all tyres are equal — the category mix shapes the whole business.

"Tyres" is really several different businesses with different customers, sizes, technology and economics. In India, the industry is measured less by number of tyres and more by value, and by value the biggest, most important category is the truck-and-bus radial (TBR) — precisely where Apollo is strongest.

Radial vs bias — a one-line technology note

You'll see "radial" a lot. A radial tyre has its internal cords laid at 90° for a stronger, cooler-running, longer-lasting tyre; the older bias/cross-ply design is cheaper but wears faster. Cars went radial decades ago, but Indian trucks "radialised" much later — and that shift from bias to radial truck tyres (TBR) was a multi-year growth wave that Apollo rode hard as the category leader. Radialisation is now largely done in trucks, so future TBR growth comes more from freight-economy volume and premiumisation than from the one-off technology switch.

03

The raw-material cycle: why margins whipsaw

The single biggest swing factor in any tyre company's profits — and it's mostly outside the company's control.

A tyre is, by cost, mostly a bundle of commodities. Raw materials are roughly 60–65% of a tyre maker's total cost — a far higher share than in most branded-goods businesses — so when those commodity prices move, margins move with them, and fast. This is the mechanism behind the wild swings you see in tyre earnings from year to year.

Roughly 45% of the input basket is crude-oil linked (carbon black, synthetic rubber, chemicals), and ~30% is natural rubber. India also runs a structural natural-rubber deficit and imports a large share, so domestic prices track global markets.

Two commodities dominate: natural rubber (an agricultural product, grown in Kerala and the North-East but largely imported from South-East Asia to cover India's deficit) and the crude-oil complex (carbon black and synthetic rubber are petroleum derivatives). When natural rubber spikes — as it did by more than 50% during 2024 — or crude jumps on a Middle-East flare-up, a tyre maker's costs balloon almost immediately, while it can only raise selling prices with a lag and only as much as competition allows. That lag is the margin squeeze.

The pass-through lag — the heart of tyre cyclicality

Picture the sequence. Rubber prices rise → the tyre maker's gross margin falls the same quarter, because it's still making tyres from the now-more-expensive rubber → the company announces price hikes at the dealer, but these take a few months to stick and often can't fully match the cost jump if rivals hold back → margins stay squeezed until either costs ease or price hikes catch up. Run it in reverse — rubber and crude fall — and the same lag works in the company's favour: selling prices stay firm while input costs drop, and margins expand. FY26's margin recovery to 14.6% (from a squeezed 13.7% in FY25) partly reflects this cycle turning friendlier, plus better volumes and mix. This is why tyre margins are best judged across a cycle, not in any single quarter.

The inverted-duty wrinkle: India's import-duty structure taxes natural rubber (an input) more heavily than finished tyres in some cases — an "inverted duty structure" the industry lobbies against, because it raises domestic input costs while leaving the door open to cheaper imported finished tyres. Watch also the recurring threat of cheap Chinese tyre imports being diverted into India, which pressures pricing at the value end of the market.
04

The business model and the moat

Where a commodity-input business actually earns durable returns.

If a tyre is 60%+ commodities, what stops it from being a pure price-taking commodity business? The answer — and the reason a strong tyre maker earns decent returns while a weak one struggles — is brand plus distribution, concentrated in the replacement market.

The two-part moat

  • Brand trust in a safety product. A tyre is the only thing connecting a vehicle to the road. Truck fleet owners care intensely about cost-per-kilometre and reliability; car buyers about grip and safety. A brand that consistently delivers earns pricing power in replacement — Apollo in India, and the premium Vredestein name in Europe, where it competes near Michelin/Continental/Goodyear tiers.
  • Distribution density. Tyres are heavy, bulky and bought when needed now. A dense network of dealers and branded outlets (Apollo runs thousands of branded touchpoints across India) means the right tyre is in stock, near the customer, with service. Replicating that reach is slow and expensive — a real barrier to new entrants and imports.

Why the replacement tilt is the quality signal

Because OEM is low-margin and cyclical while replacement is higher-margin and steadier, a company's replacement share of sales is a rough gauge of profit quality. Apollo's ~65% replacement weighting, anchored by TBR leadership, gives it a more resilient earnings base than an OEM-dependent rival. The trade-off: OEM relationships still matter because they seed future replacement demand, so a tyre maker can't simply walk away from the low-margin factory-fit business — it's the top of the funnel.

The honest limit of the moat

Brand and distribution give a good tyre maker durable but not unlimited pricing power. It is not a software business: capacity is expensive, the product is substitutable, and a price war (or an import surge) can compress everyone's margins at once. So the moat shows up not as sky-high margins, but as the ability to hold share and earn a decent return on capital through the cycle — and, for the best operators, to premiumise faster than rivals. That's the game Apollo is now explicitly playing: fewer, higher-value tyres rather than chasing volume at any price.

05

The company and who controls it

A Kanwar-family group that grew from one Kerala plant into a global tyre maker — with one famous near-miss.

Apollo Tyres was founded in 1972 and is run by the Kanwar family: patriarch Onkar Kanwar as Chairman and his son Neeraj Kanwar as Vice-Chairman & Managing Director. From a first plant in Kerala it built national leadership in Indian tyres, then made two bold overseas moves — one that transformed it (Vredestein) and one that nearly broke it (Cooper Tire).

Timeline

  • 1972–77
    Apollo Tyres incorporated (1972); first plant commissioned at Perambra, Kerala (1977). The domestic base is built through the 1980s–90s.
  • 2006
    Acquires Dunlop's South Africa operations — an early, ultimately trimmed, international foray.
  • 2009
    Acquires the Dutch premium brand Vredestein (Apollo Vredestein) — the foundation of its European business and premium passenger/agri positioning.
  • 2013–14
    The Cooper Tire episode. Apollo agrees a ~US$2.5bn takeover of America's Cooper Tire — which would have made it a global top-tier maker. The deal collapsed amid legal disputes and a China JV standoff. A defining "the one that got away."
  • 2017
    Inaugurates a greenfield plant in Hungary (Gyöngyöshalász, ~€475m) — its first tyre factory built from scratch in Europe, expanding Vredestein capacity.
  • 2018
    Commissions a large new plant in Andhra Pradesh (near Tirupati) to expand TBR and PCR capacity — the domestic half of a heavy capex wave.
  • FY20–FY24
    Peak-capex years give way to a deleveraging drive: net debt falls from a peak above ₹6,000 Cr toward ₹2,500 Cr; RoCE targets formalised.
  • FY26
    Record revenue ₹28,471 Cr, PAT up 22% to ₹1,372 Cr, net debt cut to ~₹1,300 Cr; a fresh ₹5,810 Cr FY27–FY29 capex plan approved — but under a "returns-first" discipline.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Kanwar family holds roughly a third of the company (promoter stake commonly cited around the mid-30s percent — treat the exact figure as approximate and check the latest shareholding filing). Onkar Kanwar (Chairman) and Neeraj Kanwar (Vice-Chairman & MD) drive strategy; the group is headquartered in Gurugram.
  • Global footprint: manufacturing across multiple plants in India plus one each in the Netherlands and Hungary; an R&D centre in Europe; sales into 100+ countries under the Apollo and Vredestein brands.
  • Balance sheet: after years of heavy investment, now lightly levered (net debt/EBITDA ~0.4x at end-FY26) and paying a rising dividend (₹6/share for FY26) — a marked change from the debt-heavy build-out phase.
Why the Cooper saga still matters: the failed 2013 bid is more than history — it shaped Apollo's identity. Instead of becoming a global giant by acquisition, Apollo doubled down on organic expansion (the Hungary and Andhra plants), which drove the very capex-and-debt cycle it is now working off. The current "less capex, more returns" philosophy is, in part, the lesson learned from that era of ambitious spending.

The bull and bear of the Europe business, in one place

The bull view: Vredestein gives Apollo something no purely-Indian peer has — a genuine premium European brand, higher-margin than the Indian average, developed-market technology (winter tyres, high-performance car tyres, agricultural specialities), and geographic diversification so a weak India year can be partly offset by Europe (and vice-versa). The bear view: Europe is a mature, slow-growing, fiercely competitive market where Michelin, Continental, Goodyear, Bridgestone and Pirelli set the pace; when European car and farm demand softens — as it has recently — the Europe business becomes a drag on group growth, and the Hungary plant's fixed costs bite when volumes are weak. The honest read: Europe is optionality with a cost — premium upside in good times, cyclical dead-weight in bad ones.

07

Capex and the deleveraging journey

The story of "we built a lot, borrowed a lot, and are now harvesting" — and the discipline that changed it.

For much of the last decade the knock on Apollo was simple: it spent heavily — on the Andhra plant, on Hungary, on capacity — which raised debt and depressed returns on capital, so the market treated it as a capex-hungry, return-diluting business. The most important recent shift is that this reversed. Apollo has deleveraged sharply and reframed itself around returns, not volume.

Net debt/EBITDA fell from ~3.3x (FY20) to ~0.4x (FY26). A capex-heavy, debt-laden balance sheet became a lightly-geared one in roughly five years.

The strategic reframing is captured in the group's own "less capex, more returns" language and its RoCE (return on capital employed) discipline: management set a through-cycle target band of roughly 12–15%, and in the recent up-cycle has been running around or above the top of it (RoCE around 13.5% in the latest quarter, and it has touched ~16% in strong periods). The point of the discipline: only invest where returns clear the hurdle, run existing plants harder, and let cash flow pay down debt and dividends rather than perpetually funding new concrete.

The tension the next few years will resolve

Here's the honest catch. Apollo has just approved a fresh ₹5,810 Cr capex plan across FY27–FY29 (about ₹3,000 Cr in FY27 alone). That is real money, and it reopens the question the deleveraging was meant to close: does new spending again dilute returns and lift debt? Management's answer is that this round is disciplined and returns-gated — capacity added only where demand and pricing justify it, largely fundable from operating cash flow given the now-light balance sheet. The bet the market is weighing is whether Apollo has genuinely become a returns-first operator, or whether the old capex reflex reasserts itself. The proof will be in whether RoCE holds in the target band while this capex is deployed.

The industry and the rivals

A concentrated domestic oligopoly at home, and the global majors plus cheap imports at the edges.

The Indian tyre market is a concentrated oligopoly — a handful of large players share most of the value — but each rival is strong in a different category, and Apollo also faces global premium brands (especially in Europe) and the perennial threat of cheap imports. Knowing who's who tells you where Apollo's leadership is secure and where it is contested.

How to read the rivalry

Two things stand out. First, category specialisation: MRF is the broad leader and two-wheeler stronghold, CEAT is the fast-premiumising mid-cap, BKT owns the high-margin off-highway export niche, and Apollo owns truck-and-bus. Rivals rarely dislodge each other from their home turf quickly — the moats are real. Second, the valuation pecking order: the market pays up most for the specialists with the cleanest, highest-return models (BKT for its OHT niche, MRF for its scale and brand), while Apollo has historically traded at a more modest multiple — partly reflecting its capital intensity and the European cyclical drag, partly leaving room for a re-rating if the returns-discipline story sticks. The competitive question for Apollo is whether it can premiumise (richer PCR mix, Vredestein technology, EV-ready tyres) fast enough to lift its through-cycle returns toward the specialists'.

How to think about the valuation

At roughly ₹27,700 Cr market cap (share ~₹435, June 2026) on about ₹1,372 Cr of FY26 profit, Apollo trades near 20× trailing earnings, and — adding its small net debt — around 7× EV/EBITDA. That is a modest multiple by Indian standards, and deliberately so: a capital-intensive, twice-cyclical business with a European drag doesn't command a premium rating. The key trap with any cyclical is the "P/E illusion" — the stock can look cheapest (low P/E) at the top of the margin cycle when earnings are inflated, and dearest (high P/E) at the bottom when earnings are depressed. The sensible frame is mid-cycle earnings: normalise margins somewhere between FY24's ~17.5% and FY25's ~13.7%, and ask whether the price is fair on that average. The re-rating case rests on the returns-discipline and deleveraging story lifting Apollo's through-cycle profitability and multiple over time.

Cross-check caveat: published trailing-P/E figures for Apollo vary widely by source and date (some cite the low-20s, others the mid-30s) because they use different earnings windows across a volatile-margin business — including the one-off-hit June 2025 quarter. Always check which period a quoted multiple uses before comparing.

The growth runway

Premiumisation, a returns-gated capex plan, and the EV-tyre angle.

Apollo's growth from here is less about chasing raw volume and more about value: richer product mix, share gains in the categories it leads, disciplined new capacity, and structural tailwinds like SUV-isation and electric vehicles. The framing has shifted from "grow the tonnage" to "grow the profit per tyre."

The EV-tyre angle, explained simply

Electric vehicles are heavier (big battery) and deliver instant torque (strong, immediate acceleration), which chews through tyres faster and demands purpose-built rubber — different tread, materials and construction, often with low rolling resistance to protect range and noise-damping for the quiet cabin. For a tyre maker this is a quiet positive: EVs mean more frequent replacement (good for the annuity) and a reason to sell a premium, engineered tyre rather than a commodity one. It is not a transformational needle-mover on its own, but it reinforces the premiumisation direction — and it's a place where Vredestein's European R&D can help Apollo stay current.

The next chapter is less about pouring more concrete and more about earning more per tyre — premium mix, disciplined capacity, and letting the balance sheet stay light.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

An improving-discipline India leader with European optionality — versus a commodity-cyclical, capital-heavy, competitive business. Both are true.

Apollo sits at an interesting hinge. The improvement story is real — deleveraged balance sheet, returns discipline, TBR leadership, premiumisation, Europe optionality. So is the caution — margins whipsaw with commodities, Europe is a drag, capital intensity keeps returns modest, and competition is fierce. The debate is genuinely balanced, which is why the stock carries a middling multiple.

The bull case

  • India TBR leader — ~28–30% share of the highest-value category, anchored in the steadier, higher-margin replacement market (~65% of sales).
  • Balance sheet transformed — net debt cut from >₹6,000 Cr to ~₹1,300 Cr; net debt/EBITDA ~0.4x; rising dividend.
  • Returns discipline — explicit 12–15% RoCE band, "less capex, more returns," running at/above target in the up-cycle.
  • Europe premium optionality — Vredestein gives higher-margin developed-market exposure no pure-Indian peer has; a European recovery is upside.
  • Premiumisation + EV tailwind — richer PCR/SUV mix and EV-tyre demand lift value per tyre; modest, re-rateable valuation.

The bear case

  • Twice cyclical — earnings swing with both auto/freight demand and rubber/crude prices; FY25 showed how fast margins compress.
  • Europe is a current drag — soft car and farm demand keeps the higher-margin arm flat, hurting group growth and plant utilisation.
  • Capital intensity caps returns — a fresh ₹5,810 Cr capex plan reopens the "will it dilute returns and lift debt again?" question.
  • Intense competition + imports — MRF, CEAT, JK, BKT and global majors, plus the recurring cheap-Chinese-import threat and an inverted duty structure.
  • Modest through-cycle profitability — RoCE and margins sit below the sector's premium specialists (BKT, MRF), limiting the re-rating unless discipline durably holds.
The bottom line

Apollo Tyres is a solid, improving, cyclical business — the leader in India's most valuable tyre category, with a genuinely differentiated European premium arm and a balance sheet that has been transformed from debt-heavy to light. It is not a high-return compounder like a branded-goods or software business; it is a well-run industrial cyclical that has earned the right to be judged on its through-cycle returns rather than its worst or best quarter. The story over the next few years turns on two things: whether the returns discipline survives the new capex cycle (RoCE staying in the 12–15% band while spending), and whether Europe recovers from optionality-with-a-cost back into a margin contributor. Get both right and the modest multiple can re-rate; get them wrong and it stays a cheap-for-a-reason cyclical.
