00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

IIFL Capital Services is the capital-markets arm of the IIFL group — a full-service broking and financial-products house. It runs retail and institutional share-broking, sells third-party financial products (mutual funds, bonds, PMS, alternatives) to clients, does investment banking (helping companies raise capital and do deals), and lends against shares. It was known for nearly three decades as IIFL Securities and was renamed IIFL Capital Services in late 2024 to signal that it is now much more than a broker.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It earns from market activity — so it booms and cools with the market. When Indians are trading and investing enthusiastically, brokerage, distribution and banking fees all swell; when markets go quiet, they shrink together. FY26 showed it starkly: revenue was flat at ₹2,439 Cr, but net profit fell 21% to ₹564 Cr from FY25's record ₹713 Cr — the same firm, a cooler market.
  • It is one of three separate "IIFL" companies — don't confuse them. The old India Infoline empire split in 2019 into three independently listed businesses: IIFL Finance (a lending NBFC), this capital-markets business, and the wealth arm now listed as 360 ONE. They share a heritage and a brand, not a balance sheet.
  • It is a premium, not a mass-market, broker. With only around four-lakh-odd active clients — a fraction of the millions at discount apps like Zerodha or Groww — IIFL Capital earns far more per client through advice, distribution and banking. Its edge is depth of wallet, not breadth of app downloads.
In plain terms

Picture IIFL Capital Services as a toll-collector on India's capital markets. Every time someone trades a share through it, buys a mutual fund it distributes, borrows against a stock holding, or hires it to float an IPO, a small fee drops into the bucket. In a hot market, millions of those little tolls pile up fast; in a dull one, the traffic thins and the bucket fills slowly. The business itself barely changes year to year — what changes is the weather over the market it sits on. That is the single most important thing to understand about it: this is a good, profitable, well-run franchise whose earnings are strapped to a cycle it does not control.

01

How a full-service broker actually earns

No finance degree required — just the four ways money comes in the door.

A "capital-markets" firm sounds abstract, but its income is very concrete. It sits between savers-and-investors on one side and companies-and-markets on the other, and takes a small cut of the traffic flowing between them. For IIFL Capital, that traffic arrives through four distinct pipes — and knowing them is knowing the whole business.

The four income pipes of a full-service house
PipeWhat it is — and how the money is made
BrokingBrokerage on trades. Retail and institutional clients buy and sell shares, futures and options through IIFL; it charges a commission on each trade. The busier the market, the more trades, the more brokerage. This is the largest and most cycle-sensitive pipe.
DistributionCommissions and trail on products sold. IIFL sells mutual funds, bonds, portfolio-management schemes (PMS) and alternative funds (AIFs) to clients, earning an upfront fee and — crucially — an annual trail for as long as the client stays invested. This is the stickiest, most recurring pipe.
Investment bankingAdvisory and issuance fees. As a SEBI-registered merchant banker, IIFL helps companies raise money (IPOs, share sales, bonds) and execute deals, charging a fee on each mandate. Lumpy and deal-dependent, but high-margin when the pipeline is full.
Interest / MTFInterest on money lent to clients. Through margin trading facility (MTF), IIFL funds part of a client's share purchase and charges interest on the loan. A growing book that earns a spread — but it consumes capital and adds finance costs.
The simple income equation

Roughly: Revenue ≈ brokerage on trades + distribution commissions/trail + banking fees + interest on client funding. Profit is what's left after paying staff, technology, branch and financing costs. The beauty of the model is that it needs little heavy machinery — no factories, no inventory — so in a good year a large slice of extra revenue drops straight to profit. The curse is the mirror image: because costs (people, offices, technology, borrowing) are largely fixed, when revenue dips in a weak market, profit falls much faster. That asymmetry — profit rising and falling harder than revenue — is why FY26 saw flat sales but a fifth of the profit vanish.

Why this is a cyclical business, not a steady one

  • Volumes follow sentiment. Trading activity, IPO appetite and fund inflows all surge when markets rise and confidence is high, and dry up when they fall. The firm's top line is essentially a bet on how active India's investors feel this quarter.
  • The operating leverage cuts both ways. Fixed costs mean a booming market gushes profit (FY25) and a flat one drains it (FY26). The revenue line can look calm while the profit line lurches.
  • Diversification softens — but does not remove — the swing. Distribution trail and banking fees are less trade-dependent than brokerage, so a four-pipe house rides the cycle more smoothly than a pure broker. But all four pipes ultimately drink from the same well: the health of India's capital markets.
02

How the money flows: activity in, fees out

Read this once and the whole firm — and its cyclicality — clicks into place.

A broking house is a conveyor belt for market activity. Client enthusiasm and market volumes come in at one end; a spread of fees is skimmed off along the way and harvested as revenue at the other. The single dial that governs how fast the belt runs is the market cycle — which is exactly why the same firm can print a record profit one year and a shrunken one the next.

Turn up Stage 1 (a hot market) and every downstream fee swells at once — that was FY25. Turn it down (a flat market) and the same belt yields less, magnified by fixed costs — that was FY26.

Why the profit line lurches more than the revenue line

If a firm has ₹100 of revenue and ₹75 of largely fixed costs, it keeps ₹25 of profit. Let revenue slip just 8% to ₹92 while costs barely move, and profit falls to ₹17 — a 32% drop from an 8% revenue dip. That is operating leverage, and it is the arithmetic behind IIFL Capital's FY26: revenue held flat, but a softer, less profitable trading mix plus higher financing costs on a bigger loan book pulled net profit down 21%. Nothing broke; the leverage simply worked in reverse.

Where the cash actually sits — a subtlety worth knowing

A broker also holds a large pool of client money and securities that are not its own — funds parked to settle trades, shares held in custody. Regulators are fierce about keeping that pool strictly separate from the firm's own money, because dipping into it is the classic broking sin. (As Section 08 covers, IIFL Securities was itself penalised over exactly this a decade ago.) The firm's real earning assets are smaller and simpler: its brand, its distribution relationships, its banking mandates, and the margin-funding book on which it earns interest. Understanding that the big custody numbers are not the firm's own capital keeps you from over-rating the balance sheet.

03

The company, the group split, and the rename

From a 1990s research start-up to three separate listed companies that share one famous name.

To understand IIFL Capital Services you have to untangle the "IIFL" name, because it now belongs to three different listed companies. They all descend from India Infoline, a financial-information and broking business founded in the mid-1990s by first-generation entrepreneurs Nirmal Jain and R. Venkataraman. What began as an equity-research and online-broking pioneer grew into a sprawling financial-services group — and in 2019 it deliberately broke itself into focused pieces.

Three "IIFL"s — a cheat-sheet so you don't buy the wrong one

Because the name causes genuine confusion, keep the three siblings straight. IIFL Finance is a lender — it makes gold, home and business loans and carries credit risk on its own balance sheet. 360 ONE (the old IIFL Wealth) is a wealth manager for the ultra-rich, earning recurring fees on assets it advises. And IIFL Capital Services — this company — is the capital-markets house: broking, distribution, investment banking and margin funding. They descend from one 1990s group and still share a brand, but they are three separate listed companies with three separate risk profiles. A headline about "IIFL" defaulting on a bond or facing a lending probe usually means IIFL Finance, not this firm — reading the ticker carefully saves real mistakes.

04

Geared to the market cycle

The single force that shapes every number — and the one chart that tells the whole story.

Here is the defining truth about IIFL Capital Services, in one comparison: in FY25 it earned a record ₹713 crore of net profit; in FY26, the very next year, it earned ₹564 crore — a 21% fall. Same firm. Same four pipes. Same people, same platforms, same clients. Revenue barely moved (roughly flat at ₹2,439 crore). What moved was the market — a cooler, choppier, lower-turnover year — and because a broking house is strapped to that cycle through fixed costs, a modest change in market mood swung a fifth of the profit. That single year-on-year jolt is the business.

Why FY25 vs FY26 is the whole primer in two numbers

Most companies' profits are anchored by something physical — a factory's output, a store's footfall, a contract book. A broker's profit is anchored by market sentiment, which can turn in a quarter. FY25 caught a euphoric market: record trading volumes, a flood of IPOs, buoyant fund inflows — and the firm's fixed cost base meant most of that extra revenue became profit. FY26 caught the comedown: derivatives volumes squeezed by new rules, thinner cash-market activity, a more cautious investor — and the same fixed costs meant the profit gave way faster than revenue. If you remember one thing, remember this: you are not primarily buying a company here, you are buying a leveraged claim on how active India's investors will be.

Why this is the section that matters: almost every debate about IIFL Capital — is the ₹564 Cr the "real" earning power or was ₹713 Cr? is the stock cheap or dear? — collapses into one question: where are we in the market cycle? A trough-year profit at a peak-year multiple is a trap; a peak-year profit at a trough-year multiple is a gift. The honest reader watches broking + distribution revenue and client growth against where the cycle sits, not in isolation.

The four revenue engines, up close

Where the ₹2,439 crore actually comes from — and which pipes are growing.

A pure discount broker earns almost entirely from one pipe (brokerage). IIFL Capital's defence against the cycle is that it runs four, and the mix is deliberately shifting toward the stickier, less trade-dependent ones. In FY26 the split of operational revenue looked roughly like this.

Engine 1 — Retail broking (the cyclical core)

The largest pipe and the one most exposed to sentiment. IIFL serves a relatively premium, advised retail base rather than a mass discount audience, so its brokerage per client is high — but it is still the stream that swells and shrinks most with trading volumes. The new derivatives rules (Section 08) hit exactly this pipe, which is a large part of why FY26 profit softened.

Engine 2 — Institutional equities & investment banking

The desk that serves foreign and domestic institutions and advises companies on raising capital. In FY26 this combined stream grew ~11% to roughly ₹712 crore, buoyed by a busy issuance market — and here the firm punches above its size: IIFL topped India's FY26 IPO league table, closing 45 investment-banking transactions. That a mid-cap broker leads the country's IPO underwriting is a real credential. The stream is lumpy — a fat IPO pipeline can make a year — but it is higher-margin and a genuine differentiator versus discount brokers, who do none of it.

Engine 3 — Financial-product distribution (the sticky compounder)

The most strategically important engine. IIFL sells mutual funds, bonds, PMS and alternatives, earning an annual trail that recurs as long as clients stay invested — income that keeps flowing even in a dull trading year. This is the pipe management is leaning into hardest, and the numbers show why: distribution AUM leapt from ₹31,000 crore to about ₹52,000 crore over the year, with the mutual-fund slice rising from ~₹14,000 crore to ~₹21,000 crore.

Engine 4 — Margin-trading interest (MTF)

IIFL lends clients part of the money to buy shares and earns interest on the loan. The book has been growing fast (up ~55% year-on-year), which lifts interest income — but it also raises finance costs (up ~17% to around ₹210 crore in FY26 as the firm borrowed to fund the book) and consumes capital. It is a useful spread business in good times and a drag on margin when rates rise or the book must be funded expensively.

The mix shift is the quiet story inside FY26

Look past the flat headline revenue and a deliberate rebalancing is underway. The most cyclical pipe — retail broking — actually fell 9% in FY26 as the F&O curbs bit. Yet total revenue held, because the steadier pipes picked up the slack: institutional and banking rose 11%, and distribution rose 16% while its trail-earning asset pool grew by two-thirds. In other words, in the firm's worst broking year in a while, the parts designed to be cycle-resistant did exactly that. That is the single most encouraging structural signal in the results — evidence that the four-pipe design is doing its job of blunting the cycle, not just decorating the pitch.

06

The retail-investor boom — the tailwind at its back

The structural force lifting the whole industry — and why it is not a one-way street.

IIFL Capital rides one of the great structural stories in Indian finance: the financialisation of household savings. For decades Indians parked wealth in gold, property and bank deposits. Over the last several years, tens of millions have opened demat accounts and begun investing in shares and mutual funds — pushing the country's demat-account count from around 40 million before the pandemic to over 200 million by late 2025. Every one of those accounts is a potential source of brokerage, distribution and funding revenue for firms like IIFL.

Why a growing base is a tailwind but not a guarantee

A bigger investing population lifts the whole industry's long-run revenue — more accounts, more trades, more fund inflows, more IPO demand. But two caveats temper it. First, this expanding base is being fought over by discount brokers who win sheer numbers with near-zero pricing (Section 07), so the new millions do not all become high-value IIFL clients. Second, the boom itself is cyclical — new-account openings and trading activity themselves surge and stall with the market. The tailwind is real and multi-year, but it blows in gusts, not a steady breeze, and IIFL captures the high-value slice of it rather than the volume.

Depth of wallet, not breadth of app

Here is the counter-intuitive shape of IIFL Capital versus the apps it is often lumped with. Discount platforms trumpet client counts in the millions — Zerodha and Groww each serve well over ten million. IIFL's active retail base is a small fraction of that (a 2025 broker review put it around four lakh). Yet it earns hundreds of crores of profit from that comparatively narrow base, because it monetises each client far more deeply — advice, PMS, bonds, banking, funding — so its revenue per client dwarfs a discount broker's. Its huge derivatives turnover (average daily volumes in the lakhs of crores) comes from active, high-value traders and institutions, not app-store breadth. IIFL is not trying to win the download war; it is farming a smaller field far more intensively — a genuinely different business model from the mass discounters.

07

Full-service vs discount — the industry battle

Two business models fighting for the same investors — and where IIFL sits.

Indian broking has split into two camps. Discount brokers (Zerodha, Groww, Angel One, Upstox) offer bare-bones, ultra-cheap, app-first trading and win on sheer client numbers. Full-service houses (IIFL Capital, Motilal Oswal, Anand Rathi, and wealth-tilted firms like 360 ONE and Nuvama) bundle advice, research, distribution, banking and funding, and win on revenue-per-client. IIFL sits firmly in the full-service camp — smaller in headcount of clients than the discounters, but earning far more from each.

The competitive tension to watch

The discounters have permanently reset the price of a plain trade toward zero, which steadily squeezes pure brokerage margins industry-wide. IIFL's answer is not to out-cheap them — it can't — but to out-serve them: wrap the trade in advice, distribution and banking so the client pays for value, not just execution. The live question is whether that full-service premium holds as a new generation of investors, raised on free apps, grows wealthier. If advised, product-rich relationships remain the way affluent Indians invest, IIFL's model compounds; if even the wealthy migrate to self-directed cheap platforms, the premium erodes. IIFL's rising distribution AUM is the early evidence that, for now, the advised model is winning its slice.

08

The regulator and the risk stack

The rules that shape the business — and a past run-in every investor should know.

A broker lives or dies by regulation and reputation, because it handles other people's money. IIFL Capital's risk stack has three layers worth understanding: a specific past regulatory run-in, an industry-wide rule change that hit FY26, and the structural cyclicality we have already met.

The SEBI client-funds saga — raised, litigated, and won

The most talked-about item on IIFL's record is a regulatory fight it ultimately won. SEBI alleged that in an earlier period (broadly 2011–2017) IIFL Securities had mis-handled client funds — failing to keep clients' money strictly separate from its own. This led to a ₹1 crore penalty in 2022 and, in June 2023, a far harsher order: a two-year ban on onboarding new clients. The stock fell ~18% on the news, and days later the Securities Appellate Tribunal (SAT) stayed the order. Then, in December 2023, SAT set the SEBI order aside entirely — ruling that there was no misuse of client funds, that SEBI had wrongly applied a 2016 circular retrospectively, and cutting the penalty to just ₹20 lakh for a minor technical lapse (not prefixing "client" to certain account names). The onboarding ban never took lasting effect.

What the investor should hold onto: the scary June-2023 headline was reversed within months, and the tribunal found the core allegation unproven — so this is a resolved matter, not an overhang. But the episode is still instructive: a single SEBI order, even one later overturned, wiped ~18% off the stock overnight. It is a live illustration of how tightly a broker's fortunes are tied to the regulator's stance and to a spotless-conduct reputation — the very thing a full-service premium is paid for.

The derivatives (F&O) clampdown

The industry-wide event of the period: from October 2024, SEBI tightened the rules on equity derivatives (futures & options) — cutting weekly index expiries to one per exchange, roughly tripling contract size to ~₹15 lakh, raising expiry-day margins and forcing upfront collection of option premiums — explicitly to cool frenzied retail F&O speculation (SEBI's own data showed individual F&O traders lost about ₹1.06 lakh crore in FY25). Because derivatives had become the bulk of retail trading volume, the curbs directly shrank a major brokerage pool: index-option turnover fell sharply in the months after. IIFL's own retail-broking revenue dropped 9% in FY26 largely for this reason. It is the clearest example of the model's exposure to the regulator's pen: a rule aimed at protecting retail investors also thins the fee stream brokers earn from them.

The rest of the risk stack

  • Market-cycle sensitivity — the master risk (Section 04). A prolonged bear market or dull trading environment would compress all four pipes at once, magnified by fixed costs.
  • Pricing pressure from discount brokers — the secular squeeze on plain-brokerage margins from near-zero-cost competitors.
  • MTF and funding risk — a bigger margin-lending book earns interest but consumes capital, adds finance cost, and carries credit/liquidity risk if markets fall sharply and collateral values drop.
  • Regulatory change risk — beyond F&O, SEBI can alter brokerage caps, upfront-margin rules, distribution commissions or PMS/AIF norms in ways that reshape economics overnight.
  • Valuation-cycle risk — the trap of capitalising a peak-year profit as if it were permanent (or a trough-year profit as if it were the ceiling).

How to think about the valuation

IIFL Capital trades around a ~₹10,550 crore market cap — roughly 18–19× FY26 earnings, with a return on equity near 20% and a modest dividend yield. On the surface that is a middling multiple for a 20%-RoE business. The catch is the denominator. If FY26's ₹564 crore is close to mid-cycle earning power, the stock is fairly-to-attractively priced. If FY26 was still an above-average year and a real market downturn could push profit back toward FY23's ₹250 crore, then today's multiple is quietly expensive on trough earnings. Conversely, if the next bull run repeats FY25, the earnings — and the stock — could re-rate hard. 11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A good, well-run capital-markets house — the debate is the cycle, not the quality.

IIFL Capital Services is a genuinely capable full-service capital-markets franchise: high returns, four income pipes, a premium advised client base, and a fast-growing recurring-income engine, all riding India's long financialisation wave. The investment debate is almost entirely about the cycle — whether FY26's earnings are a floor, a fair middle, or a peak — and about the pricing pressure and regulatory risk that shadow the whole industry.

The bull case

  • High-return, asset-light model — ~20% RoE, ~23% RoCE, little heavy capital tied up, cash-generative.
  • Diversified four-pipe mix — distribution (+16%) and institutional/IB (+11%) are growing and steadying the cyclical broking core.
  • Recurring income scaling — distribution AUM up ~68% to ~₹52,000 Cr; annuity-like trail cushions down-years.
  • Premium franchise, deep wallet — high revenue per client from a small, advised base — a real edge over discounters.
  • Structural tailwind — India's demat base has multiplied five-fold since FY20; the addressable market keeps expanding.
  • Deep-pocketed backer incoming — Fairfax's move to majority control brings patient, long-horizon capital to a cyclical business; the 2023 SEBI overhang is resolved.

The bear case

  • Cyclicality — profit fell 21% in FY26 on a flat top line; a real bear market could compress all four pipes at once.
  • Regulatory pen — the F&O clampdown already thinned brokerage; SEBI can reshape economics further at will, and even an order later overturned (2023) can wipe ~18% off the stock overnight.
  • Change of control — Fairfax's move to majority ownership shifts control from the founders; the strategy and priorities under a new controlling shareholder are an unknown.
  • Discount-broker squeeze — near-zero pricing from mass platforms keeps pressure on plain-brokerage margins.
  • Valuation on the wrong year — ~18–19× on FY26 is cheap if that's the floor and dear if it's the ceiling.
The bottom line

IIFL Capital Services is a well-run, high-return, four-pipe capital-markets house whose earnings are strapped to a cycle it does not control. The business quality is not really in doubt; the question is entirely one of timing and mix — whether the recurring distribution engine can grow fast enough to blunt the next down-cycle, and where in the market's mood-swing you are buying. The clearest read on all of it is the pairing the firm itself reports every quarter: broking-plus-distribution revenue and client growth, set against the market's temperature. If distribution AUM keeps compounding and the advised base deepens through the next dull patch, IIFL will have proved it can grow the steady half of itself faster than the market can shake the cyclical half — and that, more than any single year's ₹564-crore or ₹713-crore headline, is the milestone that decides the case.
