Larsen & Toubro, the contractor that builds India

A complete walk through India's largest engineering & construction conglomerate — how an EPC contractor actually earns money, why the ₹7.4-lakh-crore order book is the single number that matters, the sprawling sum-of-the-parts (roads and refineries plus listed IT, defence and an NBFC), the heavy Middle-East dependence, and the "lean, asset-light" pivot that turned this into a ₹17,000-crore-profit machine trading at ~35× earnings.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Larsen & Toubro is India's largest engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) company and one of its biggest companies of any kind. It builds the physical India you can see — metros, expressways, airports, ports, bridges, power plants, refineries, water systems and defence hardware — and, through listed subsidiaries, also runs a large IT-services business and a consumer-lending NBFC. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) it did ₹2,85,874 crore of revenue and earned a record ₹17,238 crore of recurring profit.

Four facts frame everything:

  • It runs on an order book, not a product. L&T doesn't sell off-the-shelf goods; it wins multi-year contracts to design and build large projects. At 31 March 2026 its order book hit an all-time high of ₹7,40,327 crore (+28%) — roughly 2.6 years of revenue already booked. FY26 order inflow was a record ₹4,35,590 crore (+22%). This is the master KPI; everything else flows from it.
  • It is a conglomerate, deliberately. Two construction giants (Infrastructure and Energy) generate the bulk of revenue at thin EPC margins; bolted on are far higher-margin businesses — IT & Technology Services (~19% margin), defence/hi-tech manufacturing, an NBFC, and a fast-growing precision-engineering arm. The whole is valued partly "sum of the parts."
  • It has gone "lean" and asset-light. Management spent a decade exiting capital-heavy assets (ports, roads, IDPL) and tightening cash collection. In FY26 net working capital fell to ~4% of revenue (from ~11%) and net debt-to-equity improved to 0.35 — turning a sprawling builder into a high-return capital allocator. ROE is ~16%.
  • It has no promoter. Unusually for an Indian giant, no family or group controls L&T. It is broadly held — the L&T Employees Welfare Foundation (~13.5%) is the largest single block, with LIC (~11%) and other institutions holding the rest. It is professionally managed.
In plain terms

Think of L&T as India's master builder, wearing four hats at once. Hat one and two: a giant general contractor that wins tenders to build roads, refineries and metros, then earns a slim margin on flawless, on-time delivery. Hat three: an owner of three separately-listed companies — an IT services firm (LTIMindtree), an engineering-R&D firm (LTTS), and a lender (L&T Finance) — whose stock-market value it captures. Hat four: a high-tech arms-and-electronics maker betting on defence, semiconductors and green hydrogen. The key thing to grasp is that a contractor's future is visible: the order book tells you what it will build for the next two-to-three years, so the questions are whether new orders keep coming, whether it gets paid on time, and whether it executes at margin.

01

EPC economics 101: how a contractor earns

No engineering degree required — just how a builder turns a contract into profit.

An EPC contractor is, financially, a project-execution business. It bids for a fixed-price (or cost-plus) contract to Engineer, Procure materials for, and Construct something large. It books revenue over the years it takes to build (percentage-of-completion), and earns a thin margin if it delivers on time, on budget, and without disputes. Understand four ideas and the whole model clicks.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and L&T's number)

Why margins are thin — and that's normal

A contractor doesn't own the road or refinery it builds — the customer does. L&T's job is to convert a contract into a finished asset for a few rupees of profit per hundred of revenue. The skill is not losing money: bidding accurately, buying steel and cement before prices move, finishing on schedule (delays eat the margin), and getting paid. A big, diversified, well-run contractor like L&T earns its keep through scale, engineering depth and reliability — the ability to take on the projects nobody else can, and deliver them without the cost overruns that sink smaller rivals. The reward for thin margins is enormous, predictable volume.

Where the risk lives

  • Fixed-price risk. If steel, copper or labour costs spike mid-project, a fixed-price contract can turn loss-making. Scale and procurement muscle are the defence.
  • Working-capital drag. The contractor pays for materials and labour now but gets paid in stages, often slowly (especially by governments). Cash can get trapped in unbilled work and retentions — see §07.
  • Execution & claims. Delays, design changes and disputed claims with clients can defer cash and dent margins for years.
  • Order-flow cyclicality. When government and corporate capex slows, new orders dry up — and the order book is what feeds tomorrow's revenue.
02

The order book — the single most important number

If you watch only one metric for L&T, watch this one.

For a manufacturer you watch sales; for a bank, loans. For an EPC contractor, you watch the order book — because it is next year's and the year-after's revenue, already contracted. The flow runs in one direction: orders come in, sit in the backlog, convert into revenue as work is executed, and finally turn into cash when the client pays. L&T's whole financial machine is this conveyor belt running at scale.

Why a rising order book is the bull case in one number

A growing backlog does two things at once. It guarantees revenue: ₹7.4 lakh crore of contracted work means the next ~2.6 years of building are largely locked in regardless of the economy. And it signals demand: a record inflow says clients — Indian governments, Gulf states, energy majors — are still handing L&T mega-projects. The danger sign would be the opposite: inflow falling (orders drying up) or the book growing only because projects are stuck (slow execution). So analysts watch two things every quarter: is inflow still growing, and is the book converting to revenue at a healthy clip? In FY26 both were yes.

03

The company and who controls it

An 80-year-old institution with no promoter — a rarity in India.

L&T was founded in 1938 by two Danish engineers, Henning Holck-Larsen and Søren Kristian Toubro, who were stranded in India by the Second World War. From a small machinery-import business it grew into India's engineering champion. Its most distinctive feature today is governance: there is no promoter. After fending off a hostile takeover attempt in 2001, the company built an employee-trust ownership structure, and it remains broadly, institutionally held — and professionally run.

Timeline

  • 1938
    Two Danish engineers, Holck-Larsen & Toubro, found the firm in Mumbai — initially representing European manufacturers.
  • 1940s–80s
    Pivots into manufacturing and heavy engineering, then construction; becomes India's pre-eminent EPC and engineering house.
  • 2001
    Grasim/Aditya Birla's stake build-up triggers a takeover scare; L&T restructures, hives off cement (to Grasim) and creates the employee-trust ownership that keeps it promoter-less.
  • 2010s
    Lists its IT and tech arms (LTI, LTTS, Mindtree) and L&T Finance; later begins a deliberate asset-light pivot, exiting roads, ports and other concession assets.
  • 2022
    Merges LTI and Mindtree into LTIMindtree, India's 6th-largest IT firm; launches the "Lakshya 2026" strategic plan.
  • FY26
    Record order book ₹7.40 L Cr, revenue ₹2.86 L Cr, recurring PAT ₹17,238 Cr; net working capital cut to ~4%; unveils Lakshya '31 (₹5.8 L Cr revenue target) and a ₹45,000 Cr push into green H₂, data centres, semiconductors and defence electronics.

Ownership & governance

  • No promoter. L&T is one of very few large Indian companies with no controlling family or group. The L&T Employees Welfare Foundation (~13.5%) is the largest single shareholder — a legacy of the 2001 takeover defence.
  • Institutionally held. Domestic institutions (LIC ~11% plus mutual funds) and foreign portfolio investors together own the majority; retail and others hold the rest. No single external investor controls the board.
  • Professionally managed. Run by a career-executive leadership (Chairman & MD S.N. Subrahmanyan), with strategy steered by the "Lakshya" five-year roadmaps rather than a founding family's preferences.
  • What this means: decisions are made on capital-allocation merit and are scrutinised by institutions — a governance positive — but it also means no deep-pocketed promoter to lean on, and management succession is a genuine watch-item for an institution this size.
04

The conglomerate, broken into parts

Two thin-margin giants, three high-margin engines — this is the whole map.

L&T reports in segments, and they could hardly be more different in character. Infrastructure and Energy are vast, low-margin construction businesses that do most of the revenue. IT & Technology Services, Hi-Tech Manufacturing and Financial Services are smaller in revenue but carry far higher margins. Understanding which is which is the key to valuing the whole.

Why the mix matters for valuation

Roughly two-thirds of revenue comes from construction at single-digit margins, but a large slice of profit and an even larger slice of market value comes from the high-margin, separately-listed pieces — IT and the NBFC. That's why L&T is valued partly on a "sum of the parts" basis: you value the core EPC business on its earnings, then add L&T's stake in the listed subsidiaries at their own market prices (see §05). When those subsidiaries' stock prices move, L&T's "look-through" value moves with them — a feature the core construction numbers alone won't show you.

05

The listed subsidiaries — the hidden portfolio

L&T owns controlling stakes in three separately-traded companies.

A big part of what you buy when you buy L&T is its stake in three publicly-listed companies. Each trades on its own; L&T consolidates them but doesn't own 100%. Their combined market value is large enough that "sum of the parts" investors treat them as a portfolio sitting inside the parent.

How "sum of the parts" works in plain terms

Imagine a holding company that owns 68% of one listed firm worth ₹1.5 lakh crore, two-thirds of another, and a majority of a third — plus its own large construction-and-engineering business. To value the whole, you add up: (a) the market value of those listed stakes, and (b) a fair multiple on the standalone EPC + defence earnings. Bulls argue the market often applies a "holdco discount" — pricing L&T below the visible sum of its pieces — which they see as hidden value. The flip side: if the listed subsidiaries' shares fall (e.g. an IT-sector de-rating), L&T's value falls too, even if its bridges and refineries are doing fine.

Watch the IT cycle: because IT & Tech Services carries ~19–20% margins versus ~7% for construction, it punches far above its revenue weight in profit. A global slowdown in IT spending therefore hits L&T's profitability and its sum-of-the-parts value harder than the construction-heavy revenue line implies.
06

The Middle East engine — and the dependence it creates

More than half the order book is now international, and the Gulf is the heart of it.

L&T is no longer just an India story. International work is now 52% of the order book and ~54% of revenue, and the single largest source is the Gulf — Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar and Kuwait — where a once-in-a-generation construction and energy boom (Saudi's giga-projects, Gulf power and water build-outs, big hydrocarbon EPC awards) has handed L&T some of its largest contracts ever. This is the company's biggest growth engine and its biggest concentration risk at once.

Why the Gulf, and why it cuts both ways

  • The opportunity: Gulf states are spending oil revenues on giga-cities, power, water, transport and gas infrastructure. L&T's hydrocarbon and infrastructure arms are trusted, scaled execution partners — winning "mega" and "ultra-mega" awards (single contracts above ₹15,000 Cr).
  • The margin trade: international EPC can be competitive and is a big reason blended construction margins are thin — but the volume is enormous and helps fill the order book at a time when Indian private capex has been patchier.
  • The dependence: with over half the backlog international and the Gulf dominant, L&T's fortunes are now tied to oil prices and Gulf government spending. A sustained oil downturn or a pause in Saudi/UAE mega-projects would slow the biggest source of new orders.
  • Currency & payment risk: international work adds FX exposure and, occasionally, slower or politically-sensitive payments — a reason cash discipline (§07) matters even more.
The honest tension: the Middle East is simultaneously L&T's strongest tailwind and its largest single concentration. India's own capex cycle (§08) is the natural hedge — when the Gulf cools, domestic ordering ideally fills the gap, and vice versa. The risk is a scenario where both soften together (e.g. a global slowdown plus low oil), which would hit inflow on two fronts at once.
07

Working capital & the "lean" pivot

The least glamorous number in EPC — and the one that quietly re-rated L&T.

A contractor's profit and its cash are two different things. You can book revenue on a project but wait months or years to actually collect — and meanwhile you've paid for steel, subcontractors and labour. That trapped cash is net working capital (NWC), and for decades it was L&T's Achilles heel. The big, under-appreciated story of recent years is how dramatically management has squeezed it down.

Why this is the quiet engine of the re-rating

Two structural shifts turned L&T from a sprawling, cash-hungry builder into a high-return capital allocator. First, the asset-light exit: over the past decade it sold capital-heavy concession assets — roads, ports, its electrical & automation unit — and stopped tying up balance sheet in long-gestation projects it didn't need to own. Second, cash discipline: tighter contract terms, advance payments (common on Gulf jobs), and relentless collection have shrunk working capital. The payoff is real: when cash isn't trapped in projects, the same profit generates far more free cash, which funds dividends, buybacks and the new-tech bets without piling on debt. For a contractor, cash conversion is the difference between a good business and a great one — and L&T moved decisively toward the latter.

08

India's capex supercycle — the structural tailwind

The macro force the whole bull case ultimately rests on.

L&T is, more than almost any other listed company, a direct play on India building itself out. When the government and corporates spend on roads, railways, metros, ports, power, water and factories, L&T wins a large share of the contracts. India's multi-year infrastructure and manufacturing capex push — backed by record central-government capital budgets, state spending, energy-transition investment and a "Make in India" manufacturing drive — is the structural demand engine behind the record order book.

  • Public infrastructure: highways, dedicated freight corridors, metros across dozens of cities, airports, and water/irrigation — the bread-and-butter of the Infrastructure segment.
  • Energy transition: power transmission, nuclear, and a growing pipeline in renewables, grid and green-hydrogen infrastructure feeding the Energy segment.
  • Manufacturing & "Make in India": new factories, electronics and semiconductor fabs, and defence indigenisation — areas L&T is positioning to both build for others and participate in itself (§09).
  • The Gulf hedge: when domestic ordering is lumpy, the Middle East (§06) keeps the backlog growing — and vice versa. The two large geographies smooth each other.
In plain terms — why L&T is the "index" of Indian capex

If you believe India spends the next decade building physical capacity — power for data centres and AI, transport for a growing economy, factories to reshore manufacturing, and clean-energy infrastructure — then someone has to do the building, and L&T is the largest, most capable, most diversified contractor to do it. That's the core long-term thesis: a national-scale capex cycle, and the prime contractor positioned to capture a leading share of it. The catch is that capex cycles are exactly that — cycles. Government spending can be deferred around elections and fiscal pressure; private capex waxes and wanes with confidence. The order book gives 2–3 years of insulation, but L&T's long-run growth is ultimately hostage to India (and the Gulf) keeping the taps open.

The Lakshya '31 framing: management's five-year roadmap explicitly targets riding this cycle — ~10–12% order-inflow CAGR, 12–15% revenue CAGR, and 16–17% ROE by FY31, with a revenue ambition of ~₹5.8 lakh crore. It is, in effect, a bet that the Indian + Gulf capex supercycle has years left to run.
09

The new bets — defence, chips, hydrogen, data centres

A ~₹45,000 Cr push to add high-tech, higher-margin engines beyond construction.

Alongside the core EPC business, L&T is making a deliberate, large bet on new, technology-led businesses — areas with structurally higher margins and longer growth runways than thin-margin construction. Under the "TECHing the LEAP" half of its Lakshya '31 plan, it has earmarked roughly ₹43,000–45,000 crore over five years for green hydrogen, data centres, semiconductors and industrial/defence electronics.

Why these bets, and the catch

The logic is to graft higher-margin, faster-growing engines onto a thin-margin construction base — and to do it in fields where L&T's engineering depth is a genuine edge (it already builds hydrogen plants, data centres and defence hardware for others). Defence is the most advanced: its Precision Engineering & Systems arm is scaling rapidly toward $1 billion of revenue on India's push to make weapons at home. The catch is that these are new, capital-hungry, unproven-at-scale businesses; semiconductors and green hydrogen in particular are long-gestation, competitive and execution-heavy. A ₹45,000 Cr commitment is real money — the bull sees future margin engines, the bear sees the risk of capital sunk into ventures that take years to pay back, if they do.

The next chapter for L&T is less about pouring more concrete and more about whether a master builder can become a high-tech manufacturer too.

Financials & valuation

Record profit, leaner balance sheet — at a full but not extreme multiple.

How to think about the ~35× multiple

At ~₹5.7 lakh crore market cap on ~₹17,000–19,000 Cr of FY26 profit, L&T trades around 35× trailing earnings — a premium to its own history and to a "pure" contractor, but not the nosebleed levels of some Indian compounders. The multiple is paying for three things: (1) visibility — a record order book that locks in years of revenue; (2) the conglomerate optionality — listed IT/NBFC stakes plus the high-margin defence and new-tech engines; and (3) the India capex narrative. Bulls argue an improving-ROE, cash-generative, capex-cycle leader deserves a premium. Bears point out that a contractor's earnings are inherently cyclical and lumpy, that EPC margins are thin and fixed-price risk is real, and that the new-tech bets could absorb capital for years before paying off — so a 35× multiple leaves less room for an execution stumble than the steady chart suggests.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A national champion in great shape — priced for the cycle to keep running.

L&T is one of the cleanest large-cap stories in India: the dominant contractor in a multi-year capex cycle, with a record backlog, a leaner balance sheet, listed-subsidiary optionality and credible high-tech bets. The debate is less about quality and more about cyclicality, margins and how much of the good news is already in the price.

The bull case

  • Record visibility — ₹7.4 L Cr order book (+28%) = ~2.6 years of revenue locked in; record FY26 inflow.
  • Capex supercycle — the prime contractor for India's infrastructure, energy-transition and manufacturing build-out, hedged by a Gulf boom.
  • Leaner & cash-rich — NWC cut to ~4% of revenue, op. cash flow nearly doubled, ROE rising toward 16–17% target.
  • Hidden portfolio — controlling stakes in listed LTIMindtree, LTTS and L&T Finance add sum-of-the-parts value.
  • New engines — defence (scaling to ~$1bn), plus green H₂, data centres and chips as future higher-margin growth.

The bear case

  • Cyclical & lumpy — EPC earnings swing with capex; a slowdown in India or the Gulf hits inflow and revenue.
  • Thin, fixed-price margins — ~7% construction EBITDA leaves little buffer for cost spikes, delays or disputed claims.
  • Middle-East concentration — over half the backlog is international, dominated by the Gulf and thus by oil prices.
  • New-tech capital risk — ₹45,000 Cr into hydrogen/chips/data centres could absorb cash for years before returns appear.
  • Full valuation & IT exposure — ~35× earnings prices in optimism; a global IT de-rating drags the sum-of-the-parts.
The bottom line

L&T is a structurally advantaged national champion — the largest, most diversified contractor in a country (and region) in a capex upswing, now with much better cash generation and a portfolio of high-margin businesses bolted on. The business risk is moderate and well-managed; the open questions are cyclical and valuation. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter: order inflow (is new work still coming?), working capital / cash flow (is it getting paid?), and execution margins (is it building at profit?). Keep those healthy and the capex-cycle thesis compounds; let any of them slip — or let the Gulf and India cool together — and a 35× multiple has room to give back.
