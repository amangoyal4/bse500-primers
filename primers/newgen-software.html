Newgen Software, the Delhi firm that sells software, not people

A complete walk through one of India's rare software-product companies — how a business built around its own low-code platform sells licensed IP to banks and governments across India, the US and the Middle East, why its economics look nothing like the body-shop "IT" firms it gets bundled with, and why the whole story sits in a single quiet shift: from lumpy one-time licences to smooth, recurring annuity revenue.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Newgen Software builds and sells its own software platform — a single low-code suite that lets banks, insurers and government departments digitise their most document-heavy, approval-heavy work (opening an account, originating a loan, settling an insurance claim, processing a trade-finance file) without writing mountains of custom code. Crucially, Newgen does not mainly rent out programmers by the hour the way most Indian "IT" firms do. It licenses intellectual property it built and owns, then earns for years as customers run that platform. That one difference — a product company wearing the same "Information Technology" label as the services giants — reshapes everything about how it earns, how it is valued, and where it can trip.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a product house, not a body shop. Newgen's revenue comes from its platform — OmniDocs (content/document management), its process-automation / iBPS workflow engine, and customer communication management (CCM) — sold as one low-code stack. Because it sells owned IP rather than headcount, gross margins are software-like and a customer can keep paying for the same platform for a decade. The trade-off: revenue can be lumpy, because a single large licence deal can swing a quarter.
  • The recurring base is quietly taking over. In FY26, annuity revenue reached ₹968 Cr — 62% of total sales, up sharply from 56% a year earlier. Subscription revenue grew 24% to ₹525 Cr and SaaS grew 36%. Underneath a headline that looks flat, the smooth, sticky part of the business is compounding while the one-time licence line shrinks.
  • FY26 looked slow on top, healthy underneath. Revenue rose just ~6% to ₹1,574 Cr as some large BFSI deals in India and the Middle East slipped, yet EBITDA still grew ~8% to ~₹406 Cr (a ~26% margin), the US grew 17%, and the company remained debt-free with a healthy cash pile. The stock trades near 55× earnings — a product-software multiple sitting on a business the market still half-files under services.
In plain terms

Most Indian "IT" companies are, at heart, staffing businesses: they bill for people's time, so revenue tracks headcount and utilisation. Newgen is a different animal — it wrote a software platform, owns the code, and sells the right to use it. Once a bank runs Newgen's system to open millions of accounts, ripping it out is expensive and risky, so the customer stays and keeps paying. That makes Newgen's economics resemble a global software-product firm — higher margins, IP-based, sticky — but with one rough edge the services firms don't have: a big licence sale is a discrete event, so a quarter can boom or stall on the timing of a handful of deals. The entire modern story is Newgen smoothing that lumpiness by shifting customers onto recurring subscriptions.

01

Product vs services: the distinction that defines the company

The one idea that separates Newgen from almost every other Indian "IT" stock.

To understand Newgen you first have to un-learn the reflex that "Indian IT = TCS, Infosys, Wipro". Those are magnificent businesses, but they are fundamentally services firms: they sell people's time and expertise. Newgen sells a product: software it wrote once and licenses many times. The two models earn money in almost opposite ways, and mixing them up is the single most common error a first-time reader makes about this stock.

Newgen is not a pure textbook product company — it still earns meaningful implementation and support fees to install and maintain its platform, and those involve people. But the engine is the owned IP. When a large bank standardises loan origination on Newgen, it is buying a platform, not a team, and that is why Newgen's margins, stickiness and valuation all lean toward the software-product end of the spectrum rather than the services end.

The distinction that changes the maths

A services firm that wants to double revenue must, roughly, double its people — and people get more expensive every year. A product firm that doubles revenue mostly re-sells code it already owns, so a chunk of that extra revenue drops toward profit. That is the prize Newgen is chasing, and it is why a modest-looking ₹1,574 Cr revenue base can support a ~26% operating margin and a rich multiple. The catch is symmetrical: because each large sale is discrete, Newgen can't smooth a soft quarter by simply keeping the meter running the way a staffing firm does. Owning the product is the source of both its high margins and its lumpiness.

02

What Newgen actually sells: one platform, three pillars

The low-code suite — read this once and the product clicks into place.

Newgen's product is a unified low-code platform. "Low-code" means an organisation can build and change complex digital processes largely by configuring and wiring together pre-built blocks rather than hand-coding everything from scratch — dragging a workflow into shape, connecting it to documents and communications, and going live in weeks instead of years. The platform brings three long-standing enterprise-software categories under one roof.

A single loan-origination journey touches all three: the customer's documents (OmniDocs), the credit-approval workflow (iBPS), and the sanction letter that goes out at the end (CCM) — which is why selling them as one stack is Newgen's edge.

The three pillars, decoded

  • Enterprise Content Management (OmniDocs) — the document backbone. Most bank and government processes are drowning in paper and files: application forms, identity documents, contracts, records that must be scanned, classified, stored, retrieved and audited. OmniDocs is Newgen's flagship for exactly this — the layer that turns a filing cabinet into a searchable, governed digital repository.
  • Business Process Management / process automation (iBPS) — the workflow engine. Every serious enterprise task is a chain of steps and approvals: a loan file moves from sales to credit to risk to sign-off. iBPS models and automates that journey so work routes itself, deadlines are enforced, and nothing falls through a crack. This is the "process automation" heart of the platform.
  • Customer Communication Management (CCM) — the output layer. Once a process runs, it must talk to the customer: statements, policy documents, regulatory notices, personalised letters at scale. CCM generates and delivers those communications across print, email and digital channels.

What ties the three together — and what Newgen leads with in its marketing — is that they sit on one low-code platform a customer can extend itself. A bank doesn't have to buy a content system from one vendor, a workflow tool from another, and a communications engine from a third, then pay integrators to stitch them together. It buys one Newgen stack that already speaks to itself. For document-and-approval-heavy industries, that unification is the pitch.

Why "one platform" is the whole selling point

The processes Newgen targets are the unglamorous plumbing of a bank or a ministry — account opening, trade finance, claims, records management, government service delivery. They are enormous, repetitive, regulated, and utterly mission-critical: if account opening breaks, the bank stops onboarding customers. Because these processes span documents and workflow and outbound communication, a stitched-together patchwork of separate tools is fragile and costly to change. Newgen's answer is a single configurable platform that owns all three layers, so a change to a process ripples through cleanly. That is why, once installed, it becomes the system the customer builds its operations on — and why it is so hard to remove.

03

The company, the founders, and who controls it

A founder-run Delhi/NCR software house, three decades in the making.

Newgen is not a start-up and not a spin-off — it is a founder-built product company that has been writing enterprise software from the Delhi/NCR region since 1992. It is still run by the people who started it, which is unusual and important: the leadership team has lived through multiple technology waves (imaging → ECM → BPM → low-code → SaaS) with the same product DNA intact.

A leadership team that has personally shepherded the platform through several such waves is a genuine, if intangible, moat: the institutional memory of why the product is built the way it is lives in the people who wrote it.

The licence-to-annuity shift: the crux of the whole case

The single most important thing happening inside Newgen — and it is unique to this company.

Newgen earns from its platform in four distinct ways, and they behave very differently. Sorting them into two buckets — one-time money and recurring money — is the key that unlocks the investment case, because the entire modern strategy is a deliberate migration from the first bucket to the second.

Subscription/SaaS and support together make up Newgen's annuity revenue — the smooth, predictable, high-visibility money that arrives whether or not a big new licence closes this quarter. And that bucket is winning. In FY26 annuity revenue reached ₹968 Cr, or 62% of total sales, up from 56% the year before — a genuinely large one-year move in mix. Subscription grew 24% to ₹525 Cr; SaaS specifically grew 36%. Meanwhile the one-time licence line actually shrank in the quarter where large deals slipped.

The device that only fits Newgen: install once, bill for a decade

Here is the mechanism no other primer can borrow. A single Newgen platform sale is not one payment — it is the start of a stream. The upfront licence lands once and lumpily. But the same customer then pays subscription or support on that platform every year, for ten years or more, and typically buys more modules and seats as it digitises new processes ("land and expand"). So each good deal doesn't just book revenue today; it permanently thickens the annuity. That is why FY26 could look almost flat on the top line (+6%) yet be a genuinely good year underneath: the recurring base jumped from 56% to 62% of sales in twelve months. The licence line is the noise; the annuity line is the signal — and the signal is compounding.

05

Why the quarters are lumpy — and why that's the whole debate

The uncomfortable flip side of being a product company.

The same thing that makes Newgen attractive — it sells owned IP at high margin — is what makes its quarters bounce around in a way that unsettles investors used to the metronome of a services firm. Understanding the lumpiness is understanding the risk.

Newgen's largest deals are big, custom-scoped platform commitments to banks, insurers and governments. These take many months to close, run through procurement committees and regulators, and land — when they land — as a chunky slug of licence and implementation revenue in one quarter. If a couple of those large deals slip from March into the next year, as happened in India and the Middle East in FY26, the reported top line can look soft even though the pipeline and the recurring base are perfectly healthy. Nothing is "wrong" with the business; the calendar simply didn't cooperate.

A genuine surprise: Newgen is an unshowy company from Delhi's software cluster — yet it competes for core-process platform deals against Pega and Appian inside US and Middle-Eastern banks, and its fastest-growing geography in FY26 was the United States, up 17%. The mental image of "Indian IT = offshore support desk" simply doesn't fit here: this is an Indian firm exporting its own product into the home markets of the global platform vendors — and increasingly getting paid in recurring dollars for it.

The second surprise is quieter: in the very year the headline growth disappointed, the quality of revenue improved — the sticky annuity mix rose six points. A "bad" year by the top-line yardstick was a good year by the yardstick that actually determines the company's long-term value.

How the annuity shift cures the lumpiness

The elegant part of Newgen's strategy is that the fix for lumpiness is the same as the fix for valuation. As subscription and support climb toward two-thirds of revenue and beyond, an ever-larger share of each year's sales is already booked before the year begins — it arrives regardless of whether a specific mega-deal closes in Q4 or Q1. So the more the mix tilts to annuity, the less a single slipped deal can wreck a quarter, and the more Newgen's reported numbers behave like the steady software-product company its multiple already assumes it is. The transition doesn't just raise revenue quality; it structurally dampens the volatility that has periodically spooked the stock.

The geography map: India, the US, EMEA and APAC

A genuinely global product company — with both the reach and the concentration that implies.

Newgen sells into 60-plus countries, and its revenue is spread across four broad regions that each behave differently. This global footprint is a real strength — it is not a company dependent on the Indian IT-budget cycle alone — but it also means Newgen is exposed to several regional and currency risks at once.

The US (+17%) and APAC (+14%) carried FY26 growth, while India and EMEA — historically the two largest contributors — were held back by slipped large deals and geopolitical uncertainty in the Middle East. The regional split is exactly why one soft market can dent a headline while another region is thriving.

  • United States — the strategic growth engine, where Newgen sells its platform into banks and financial institutions and grew 17% in FY26. Scaling US sales is a deliberate, investment-heavy priority.
  • EMEA / Middle East — a long-standing stronghold, particularly with Gulf banks and government entities; a region where Newgen has decades of references but also real exposure to regional geopolitics.

Why the geographic spread cuts both ways

Diversification across four regions means Newgen is not hostage to any single market's budget cycle — when India and the Middle East cooled in FY26, the US and APAC kept the company growing. That is real resilience. But the same map is a concentration story from another angle: Newgen leans heavily on India plus the Middle East as its two biggest revenue pools, so a synchronised slowdown or a few lost marquee accounts in those regions bites hard. And selling globally means selling in dollars, dirhams and rupees at once — currency swings become part of the reported result. The breadth is a genuine strength; the reliance on a couple of large regions is the risk hiding inside it.

07

BFSI & government: who buys, and why they don't leave

The customer base that makes the annuity so durable.

Newgen's customers are concentrated in exactly the industries with the most document-heavy, approval-heavy, heavily-regulated processes: banking, financial services and insurance (BFSI), plus government and public-sector bodies. This is not an accident of history — it is the natural home for a platform built to digitise account opening, lending, claims, trade finance and records.

Why does this customer base matter so much? Because BFSI and government buyers create the stickiest possible revenue. When a bank runs its account-opening or loan-origination on Newgen, that platform is wired into core operations, audited by regulators, and trusted with millions of live transactions. Replacing it is a multi-year, high-risk project no bank undertakes lightly. So customers stay — and, crucially, they grow: having digitised one process, they extend the same platform to the next (from account opening to lending to trade finance), adding modules and users. Each expansion is high-margin revenue on an existing relationship.

Why "boring and regulated" is the point

An outsider might see "document management for banks" and file it under dull. That dullness is precisely the moat. The processes Newgen automates are non-negotiable — a bank must open accounts, a government must process applications, an insurer must settle claims — so demand doesn't switch off in a downturn the way discretionary tech spending does. And because these systems are regulated and mission-critical, customers prize reliability and continuity over novelty; they don't rip out a working platform to chase a shinier one. Newgen's revenue quality flows directly from the unglamorous, indispensable, hard-to-replace nature of what it automates.

The land-and-expand engine: the reason a slow-headline year can still be a healthy one is that a large share of Newgen's growth comes from existing customers buying more, not just from winning new logos. That installed base is a renewable resource — every year, more processes at the same banks move onto the platform — which is what makes the annuity line compound so reliably beneath the lumpier new-deal revenue.

The rivals: Pega, Appian, OpenText and the giants

A challenger competing against far deeper pockets — on price and focus.

Newgen does not compete against Indian services firms; it competes against the global low-code, BPM and ECM software vendors. That is a demanding neighbourhood — its rivals are larger, better-capitalised, and sell into the same banks. Newgen's counter is to be the cost-competitive, BFSI-focused specialist that does the whole document-to-communication stack in one platform.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Newgen's rivals mostly out-resource it — Pega and Appian spend more on R&D and sales, and OpenText and IBM have decades-deep enterprise roots. So Newgen doesn't win by out-spending; it wins by being focused and cost-competitive: a single platform tuned for exactly the BFSI and government processes it targets, delivered at a lower total cost than a Pega deployment, with strong references in India and the Gulf. The strategic question is whether a mid-sized Indian challenger can keep pace with the product investment of much larger competitors while it also funds a US scale-up and a SaaS re-platforming — three expensive priorities at once. Focus is a real edge; out-investing giants indefinitely is the hard part.

The tailwind: low-code, digitisation and the SaaS re-platforming

Why demand for Newgen's kind of software is structurally rising.

Newgen rides a genuine, multi-year structural wave: the world's banks, insurers and governments are still, even now, in the middle of digitising their core processes — and doing it faster with low-code, and increasingly in the cloud. Three forces reinforce each other.

Why the SaaS wave is the friend Newgen needed

For years, moving a customer from a one-time licence to a subscription looked like a sacrifice — you swap a big upfront cheque for smaller annual ones, which can depress reported revenue in the near term. What the market shift does is remove that friction: buyers now prefer to consume software as a subscription, so Newgen's push toward SaaS is no longer swimming against customer preference — it is swimming with it. The digitisation and low-code waves supply the demand; the SaaS wave supplies the willingness to buy it in the recurring form Newgen most wants to sell. All three tailwinds point the same way, which is what gives the annuity transition a long runway rather than a one-year bump.

None of this makes the runway risk-free. Enterprise buyers can defer big digital projects when budgets tighten, the AI wave could eventually let customers build more themselves, and a SaaS transition legitimately dampens near-term reported revenue even when it is the right long-term move. But the direction of travel — more digitisation, more low-code, more cloud — is exactly the direction Newgen's platform and business model are already pointed.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A product company mid-transition — asked to prove the annuity can carry it.

Newgen enters FY27 as a debt-free, founder-run product company with a rising recurring base, a global footprint, and long structural tailwinds behind its platform. The debate is not whether the business is good — it plainly is — but whether it can convert a soft-deal year into renewed growth while defending a premium multiple against much larger rivals.

The bull case

  • Owned IP, software margins — a genuine product company with ~26% EBITDA margins and a debt-free balance sheet, not a wage-bound services shop.
  • Annuity compounding — recurring revenue at 62% of sales and rising; subscription +24%, SaaS +36% — the smooth base is taking over.
  • Sticky BFSI/government base — mission-critical platforms that run for a decade and expand module by module (land-and-expand).
  • Global reach — a real US growth engine (+17%) and Gulf strongholds, exporting an Indian product into the giants' home turf.
  • Structural tailwinds — digitisation, low-code and the SaaS shift all pull demand the way Newgen is already moving.

The bear case

  • Revenue lumpiness — large-licence timing makes quarters volatile; a slipped deal wrecks the optics, as FY26 showed.
  • Concentration — heavy on BFSI and on India + the Middle East; a regional slowdown or a few lost accounts bites hard.
  • Formidable competition — Pega, Appian, OpenText, IBM and hyperscaler low-code all out-resource a mid-sized challenger.
  • SaaS-transition drag — moving to subscription can depress near-term reported revenue even when it's the right long-term move.
  • Rich valuation — ~55× earnings leaves the stock exposed if annuity growth stalls or margins slip during the US scale-up.
The bottom line

Newgen is one of the few Indian software companies that genuinely sells a product rather than manpower, and FY26 was the year that identity showed through most clearly: a flat-looking top line masking a recurring base that jumped from 56% to 62% of revenue while margins expanded. The company that emerges from the transition — if the annuity keeps compounding — is a predictable, high-margin, globally diversified platform business that has earned its multiple. The next few years turn on one checkable thing: whether recurring revenue keeps growing double-digits and its share keeps climbing toward two-thirds and beyond, even as the licence line stays noisy. Track the annuity number each year — the day it becomes clearly the larger, faster-growing engine is the day Newgen stops being a lumpy licence story and becomes the software-product compounder its valuation already assumes.
