00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're actually buying, before we go deep.

Ujjivan Small Finance Bank is a Bengaluru-headquartered small finance bank (SFB) that grew out of a microfinance lender — Ujjivan Financial Services, a non-bank finance company (NBFC) that for years handed out tiny, collateral-free loans to groups of low-income women. Ujjivan won an SFB licence, started banking operations in February 2017, and in April 2024 absorbed its own listed parent in a reverse merger. Today it runs a full retail bank — deposits, ATMs, ~24 states — but its DNA and roughly half its loan book are still joint-liability group microfinance.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • It is a microfinance business wearing a bank's licence. The high-yielding, unsecured microfinance book is what made Ujjivan profitable — and what makes it cyclical. When the microfinance industry over-lends and borrowers get stretched (as happened across FY25), delinquencies rise, provisions balloon, and profit falls hard even if the deposit and secured businesses are doing fine. That is exactly what FY25 showed: PAT dropped ~43% to ₹726 Cr from a record year, almost entirely because of higher credit cost.
  • It is deliberately changing what it is. Management is steering the book away from pure unsecured microfinance toward secured lending — affordable housing, micro-mortgages, MSME, vehicle and gold loans. Secured was ~30% of loans in March 2024 and ~43.5% a year later, with a stated goal of 65–70% over time. The bet is that a majority-secured, deposit-funded bank deserves a steadier valuation than a microfinance monoline.
  • The funding side already looks like a bank. Ujjivan takes retail deposits — ₹37,630 Cr by March 2025, up ~20% in a year — which is cheaper and stickier than the wholesale borrowing an NBFC relies on. Building that deposit base (and its low-cost CASA slice) is the quiet moat that lets a former microfinance NBFC fund itself like a lender for the long haul.
The one idea to hold on to

Ujjivan is running two clocks at once. One clock is the microfinance stress cycle — ticking against it right now, driving up credit costs and dragging profit down. The other is the transformation clock — ticking in its favour, as the secured loan mix and the deposit base steadily grow. The investment question is not "is microfinance risky?" (it plainly is) but "will the transformation clock finish its work before the stress clock does real, lasting damage?" Everything in this primer is an attempt to help you read those two clocks against each other.

01

What a "small finance bank" actually is

A special RBI-created category — and how it differs from both a normal bank and the microfinance NBFC Ujjivan used to be.

A small finance bank is a licence category the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) created in 2015 with a specific social purpose: to push formal banking into the parts of India that big commercial banks under-serve — small farmers, micro-enterprises, informal workers, first-time borrowers. Several established microfinance lenders (Ujjivan, Equitas, Jana, Utkarsh, Suryoday and others) were granted these licences and converted from NBFCs into banks. Ujjivan was one of them.

An SFB is a full bank in most respects — it can take deposits, issue debit cards, run ATMs and net-banking, and lend across products — but it operates under tighter rules aimed at keeping it focused on financial inclusion. Three of those rules matter for understanding Ujjivan:

The economics that fall out of this table are the whole reason an SFB can be so profitable — and so volatile. Because it lends to riskier, under-served borrowers at high yields, and because it can now fund itself with cheaper deposits instead of expensive wholesale money, the gap it earns — the net interest margin (NIM) — is fat. Ujjivan's NIM was around 8.3% in FY25, roughly double what a large universal bank earns. That wide margin is the reward for taking on borrowers nobody else will serve. The catch is that those same borrowers default more in bad times, so the fat margin has to be big enough to absorb periodic spikes in loan losses. An SFB lives or dies on that trade.

SFB versus its old NBFC self — the crucial upgrade

The single biggest change when Ujjivan became a bank was where its money comes from. As a microfinance NBFC it borrowed at wholesale rates from banks and markets, then re-lent — a fragile model, because in a crisis (demonetisation, Covid, a liquidity freeze) the wholesale taps can shut overnight. As a bank it can gather public deposits: millions of small, sticky savings and fixed-deposit accounts that don't run at the first sign of trouble and cost less than wholesale funds. Converting from "borrow-and-lend NBFC" to "deposit-taking bank" is the structural prize of the SFB licence — and it is why the deposit franchise (section 05) is treated here as the real moat, not the microfinance book that gets all the attention.

02

Group microfinance — the engine, and why it swings

The lending method Ujjivan was built on: high yields, no collateral, and a credit cycle that turns.

Joint-liability group (JLG) microfinance is the technique that built Ujjivan and still drives a large share of its income. It works like this: instead of lending to one borrower against collateral, the lender forms a small group of low-income women — often five to ten — who guarantee each other's loans. There is no house or gold pledged; the collateral is social. If one member can't pay a weekly instalment, the group is expected to cover it, and default hurts the whole group's future access to credit. Peer pressure and mutual dependence do the work that a mortgage does in a normal loan.

This model has two defining features, and they are two sides of the same coin:

  • High yields. Microfinance loans are tiny, unsecured, high-touch (field officers collect frequently) and go to borrowers with no formal credit history. To cover that cost and risk, they carry high interest rates — which is why microfinance lending produces the fat NIMs that make SFBs so profitable in good years.
  • Cyclical credit risk. The same borrowers have thin financial cushions. When incomes wobble — a bad monsoon, local disruption, or simply over-borrowing when too many lenders chase the same households — repayment slips quickly and across whole regions at once, because the loans are unsecured and geographically clustered. Microfinance losses don't dribble; they arrive in waves.
The collateral in microfinance is a promise between neighbours. It holds beautifully in normal times — and frays exactly when a whole district's incomes fall together.

The reason this matters so much for Ujjivan in 2024–26 is that the Indian microfinance industry went through a classic over-lending boom: after Covid, lenders competed hard, the same households took loans from multiple institutions, and total borrower indebtedness climbed past what many could service. When repayment stress hit, it hit the whole sector — Ujjivan included. Section 06 covers that cycle in detail; here the point is structural. A microfinance book is a high-return, high-beta asset: it earns more than any secured loan in good years and loses more in bad ones. That volatility is not a bug Ujjivan can fix; it is the nature of the borrower. The only durable answer is to dilute it with steadier secured loans — which is precisely what management is now doing.

Why "unsecured" is the word that governs everything

In a home or vehicle loan, a default is partly cushioned: the bank can repossess and recover much of the money. In group microfinance there is nothing to seize — recovery depends entirely on the borrower's willingness and ability to keep paying. So when a microfinance borrower stops paying, the lender's loss is close to the full loan. That is why a microfinance-heavy bank's provisions can swing violently from one year to the next while a mortgage-heavy bank's stay tame. Ujjivan's entire strategic pivot — toward affordable housing, micro-mortgages and MSME — is, at bottom, a move from "nothing to seize" toward "something to seize," trading some yield for a lot less tail risk.

03

The reverse merger — when the child swallowed the parent

How Ujjivan collapsed a two-layer holding structure into one clean listed bank.

Until 2024, Ujjivan had an awkward two-company structure. At the top sat Ujjivan Financial Services Limited (UFSL) — the original microfinance NBFC, itself listed — which owned a majority of Ujjivan Small Finance Bank, also listed. Two listed entities, one on top of the other, both essentially the same underlying business. This existed because RBI rules had required the bank to be housed under a holding company. It created a permanent holding-company discount at the UFSL level and confusing, overlapping ownership.

In April 2024 that structure was dismantled through a reverse merger: instead of the parent absorbing the subsidiary (the usual direction), the subsidiary absorbed the parent. UFSL, the holding company, merged into Ujjivan Small Finance Bank. The National Company Law Tribunal in Bengaluru sanctioned the scheme (April 2024) and it became effective at the end of that month, with the bank allotting fresh shares to former UFSL shareholders (around 141 crore equity shares) to complete the swap.

Why go to all this trouble? A reverse merger like this does three useful things. It removes the holding-company discount that dragged on the parent's valuation. It gives the group a single, simple listed entity that is easier for investors to analyse and for institutions to own. And it aligns ownership — former parent shareholders now hold the bank directly, ending the odd situation of two listed claims on one business. For a bank whose whole strategy is to look more like a mainstream lender, cleaning up the corporate plumbing was a logical first step.

A genuine surprise: the "parent" company here effectively disappeared into its own subsidiary. In most Indian group restructurings the holding company survives and the operating business is tucked beneath it; Ujjivan did the reverse, dissolving the listed NBFC that had created the bank in the first place. The founder's original vehicle, Ujjivan Financial Services, no longer exists as a listed company — its identity now lives entirely inside the bank it spawned.
04

The deliberate shift toward a secured book

The strategic re-engineering: swap some microfinance yield for a lot less volatility.

The most important thing Ujjivan is doing — as opposed to what it was — is rebuilding the shape of its loan book. For most of its life the book was overwhelmingly unsecured group microfinance. Management's stated plan is to flip that: to make the majority of loans secured, spread across affordable housing, micro-mortgages, MSME, vehicle finance, gold and agriculture. Secured lending was about 30% of the book in March 2024 and roughly 43.5% a year later, with a longer-run target of 65–70%.

Why do this, when microfinance earns more? Three reasons, and they compound:

  • Lower loss volatility. Secured loans default less and recover more, so provisions swing far less violently. A book that is 65% secured simply cannot have the kind of year FY25 was for a book that is 70% unsecured.
  • A better valuation. The market pays a higher multiple for steady, diversified banks than for microfinance monolines. If Ujjivan can credibly show it is becoming the former, the re-rating is worth more than the yield it gives up.
  • Bigger, longer relationships. A home or MSME loan is larger and longer-dated than a one-year microfinance loan, and it anchors a customer who also keeps deposits and buys other products — deepening the franchise rather than just churning small loans.

The non-transferable device: same bank, two very different loss profiles

Here is the shift stated as one hard number. In FY25 Ujjivan's credit cost ran near 2.45% of the loan book — the direct reason profit fell 43% in a year when deposits still grew ~20% and the balance sheet kept expanding. Nothing about the franchise "broke": branches, deposits and the secured book all grew. One line — the cost of loans going bad, concentrated in the unsecured microfinance portfolio — did the damage. Now run the same shock through a book that is 65% secured instead of ~57% unsecured: the blended credit cost in a stress year would be materially lower, because two-thirds of the loans have collateral behind them. The entire secured pivot can be read as management trying to shrink the size of the next FY25 before it arrives. Ujjivan is not trying to abolish the microfinance cycle — it can't — it is trying to make each turn of it hurt less.

05

The deposit franchise — the funding moat

The least glamorous part of the story, and quite possibly the most important.

A bank is only as strong as its funding. On the lending side Ujjivan competes with dozens of microfinance and small-ticket lenders; on the funding side, the durable advantage is a base of retail deposits that is cheap, sticky and grows with the branch network. Ujjivan's deposits reached ₹37,630 Cr by March 2025, up ~20% year-on-year — growing faster than its loan book, which is exactly the direction a maturing bank wants.

Within deposits, the prized slice is CASA — current and savings accounts. These pay little or no interest, so a high CASA share means a lower overall cost of funds and a wider margin. Ujjivan's CASA ratio was around 25.5% at March 2025 — respectable for a young bank still building its everyday-banking relationships, but well below the 40%+ that big universal banks enjoy, and therefore a clear area it still needs to grind higher.

Why does this matter more than the loan headlines? Because funding is where the former-NBFC vulnerability lived. As a wholesale-funded microfinance company, Ujjivan's funding could tighten sharply in any market shock. As a deposit-funded bank, it has a base of small savers who stay put — and every rupee of new low-cost deposit widens the margin and reduces reliance on flighty wholesale money. The deposit franchise is what converts a risky lending model into a viable long-term bank.

The catch every SFB depositor should understand

There is a reason young small finance banks often advertise eye-catching savings and fixed-deposit rates: they are buying deposits they cannot yet attract on brand and convenience alone. Paying up for deposits lifts the cost of funds and pressures the margin — the opposite of what CASA does. So the deposit story has a quality dimension, not just a size one. The number that reveals whether Ujjivan is building a genuine funding moat (rather than renting expensive money) is the CASA ratio and the trend in cost of funds: if CASA climbs and funding costs ease as the branch network matures, the moat is real. If deposits only grow because Ujjivan keeps offering premium rates, the growth is real but the moat is thinner than it looks.

06

The 2024–25 microfinance stress cycle

The crux of the near-term investment case — why a good franchise had a bad year, and how bad it really was.

Everything about Ujjivan's recent numbers runs through one event: the industry-wide microfinance stress cycle that peaked across FY25. Understanding it is the difference between reading Ujjivan's profit drop as a franchise failure (it wasn't) and reading it as a cyclical loss (it was). The mechanics are worth walking through slowly, because they will repeat — the only question is how hard, and how well-defended Ujjivan is when they do.

How the cycle built up

After Covid, India's microfinance lenders competed aggressively to grow. The same low-income households ended up borrowing from multiple lenders at once, and total household indebtedness climbed past what many borrowers could comfortably service. When repayment capacity finally cracked, it cracked across the whole sector simultaneously — because these are unsecured, geographically clustered loans to financially thin borrowers. Delinquencies rose, and every microfinance-exposed lender, Ujjivan included, had to provide heavily for expected losses.

The industry's response — MFIN guardrails

The industry's self-regulatory body, MFIN (Microfinance Institutions Network), tightened its rules to stop the over-lending — capping how many lenders can lend to a single borrower and limiting total indebtedness per household. These guardrails are healthy for the long-run system, but in the short run they slow microfinance disbursement (fewer eligible borrowers, smaller loans), which pressures growth and income even as the bad-loan wave works through.

So the FY25 result — PAT down ~43% to ₹726 Cr from a record FY24 near ₹1,281 Cr — is best read as a credit-cost event, not a franchise event. The counter-intuitive read on the asset-quality numbers

At first glance a bank with a 2.2% GNPA and a 0.5% NNPA looks healthy — those are low numbers. The trap is thinking the low headline NPA means the microfinance stress was mild. It wasn't; it was severe. The pain simply flowed through the credit-cost line (provisions and write-offs, which crushed profit) rather than accumulating as visible bad loans on the balance sheet, precisely because Ujjivan recognised and cleaned up the losses fast. So the honest way to gauge microfinance stress at an SFB is to watch credit cost and slippages, not the tidy end-of-period GNPA. A microfinance lender can post a low GNPA and still have had a brutal year — because unsecured losses get written off quickly, they leave the balance sheet clean and the income statement bruised.

The forward question is simply whether FY25 was the peak of the stress or a way-station. If disbursement discipline (helped by the MFIN guardrails) and the growing secured mix mean credit costs normalise from here, FY25 will look like a cyclical trough in profitability. If borrower stress deepens or spreads, the unsecured book can bleed for several more quarters. That fork — normalising versus persisting credit cost — is the single biggest swing factor for the stock over the next year or two.

07

Who runs it — and the universal-bank ambition

Ownership, leadership continuity, and the licence upgrade Ujjivan is quietly chasing.

After the reverse merger, Ujjivan Small Finance Bank is a single, widely held listed entity — there is no dominant promoter family in the way there is at many Indian companies. The former holding company's shareholders (institutions, retail investors, and Ujjivan's founding ecosystem) now own the bank directly. That diffuse, institution-heavy ownership is normal for a bank of this vintage and is broadly a positive for governance and float — but it also means Ujjivan lacks a deep-pocketed promoter to lean on, which is one reason capital is raised from the market (the July 2026 AGM approved a QIP of up to ₹2,000 Cr).

Leadership continuity — a real, watchable risk

Ujjivan has had a bumpier leadership history than most banks its size, with senior-management churn at the MD/CEO level in recent years drawing investor attention. For a lender whose credit discipline in a volatile business depends heavily on the person setting risk appetite, management stability is not a footnote — it is part of the risk stack. The July 2026 AGM's approval of the MD & CEO's reappointment is the kind of continuity signal investors here watch closely; a bank re-engineering its whole loan book mid-cycle cannot afford a rudderless top.

The universal-bank prize

Ujjivan's longer-term ambition is to graduate from a small finance bank into a universal bank — the full licence that HDFC, ICICI and Axis hold, free of the SFB-specific constraints (the 75% priority-sector floor, the small-ticket rule) and with access to a wider, cheaper universe of business. In April 2026 the RBI acknowledged Ujjivan's diversification progress but asked for a more diversified loan mix before it would consider the upgrade, effectively telling the bank to keep shifting toward secured lending and to reapply later. That regulatory nudge is important: it ties the universal-bank dream directly to the secured-book pivot of section 04. The licence upgrade is not just a status symbol — it is the pay-off the whole transformation is aiming at.

Why the RBI's "not yet" is actually a roadmap

The RBI's April-2026 message reads like a rejection, but it is closer to a checklist. By pinning the universal-bank licence to a more diversified loan mix, the regulator has told Ujjivan exactly what to do to win the prize: keep pushing secured lending, keep shrinking the unsecured concentration, keep building the deposit base. In other words, the same actions that reduce cyclical risk (good for the equity story) are the ones that unlock the licence (also good for the equity story). For once, the regulatory hurdle and the shareholder interest point in the same direction — which is why watching the secured-mix trajectory doubles as watching Ujjivan's progress toward becoming a full bank.

08

Financials & valuation

A bank you judge on margin, credit cost and returns — read against the transformation.

Valuing a bank is different from valuing a manufacturer: there is no EBITDA and no order book. What matters is the net interest margin (the spread earned), the credit cost (losses on loans), the resulting return on assets and equity, and the growth and quality of deposits and advances — all read against the price-to-book multiple the market assigns. On valuation, the key gauge for a bank is price-to-book (P/B) — the market value versus the bank's net worth — because a bank's "factory" is its equity capital. At a market cap around ₹13,600 Cr (July 2026) against a net worth in the mid-₹6,000-Cr range, Ujjivan trades at a modest multiple of book, well below the premium multiples of large, secured, low-volatility banks. That is the market pricing in the microfinance risk. The bull says that multiple is too low for a franchise earning ~1.6% RoA even in a trough year and rapidly de-risking its book; the bear says a microfinance-heavy SFB deserves a cautious multiple until the cycle proves it can be tamed.

How the two cases actually differ

The disagreement is really about which number is the "normal" one. The bull anchors on a normalised Ujjivan: if credit cost falls back toward ~1.5% and the secured mix keeps climbing, RoA drifts toward ~2% and RoE back into the high teens — and a bank compounding book value at that rate should trade well above a low multiple of book. The bear anchors on the through-the-cycle Ujjivan: average in the bad years, accept that microfinance credit cost is lumpy and periodic, and the "real" RoE is lower and choppier than a clean year suggests — worth only a cautious multiple. Neither side disputes the FY25 facts; they dispute which year is representative. That is why the metric in the next section — the trend in credit cost against the secured mix — is what actually decides who is right.

09

The small-finance-bank peer set

Ujjivan is one of a cohort — comparison sharpens what makes it distinct.

Ujjivan belongs to a well-defined class of Indian small finance banks, most of which converted from microfinance or asset-finance NBFCs after 2015 and now face the same core question: how far and how fast to diversify away from high-yield, high-risk lending. Placing Ujjivan against its peers shows both that its microfinance-stress problem is a sector problem, and where it sits on the journey from monoline to mainstream bank.

Two lessons fall straight out of the comparison. First, the microfinance stress was a cohort event, not a Ujjivan failing — the microfinance-heavy SFBs all felt it, which is a caution against treating FY25's profit drop as company-specific. Second, the market's clear valuation hierarchy rewards secured diversification: AU, built secured from day one, commands a premium multiple and sailed through the cycle, while the microfinance-born names trade cautiously. That hierarchy is the prize Ujjivan's pivot is chasing — every point of secured mix is a step from the cautious end of the table toward the premium end.

What the peer set teaches

The cleanest way to frame Ujjivan is by its trajectory between two poles. At one pole sits a microfinance monoline — highest yield, highest volatility, lowest multiple. At the other sits a bank like AU — secured, steady, premium-rated. Ujjivan is deliberately walking from the first pole toward the second, and it is further along than most microfinance-origin peers. The investment question is one of pace and proof: can it keep converting the book fast enough, and hold asset quality steady enough, that the market starts pricing it nearer the AU end of the spectrum rather than the Suryoday end? The peers don't just contextualise Ujjivan — they map the exact re-rating path the bull case depends on.

The one metric that settles the thesis

If you track only one thing about Ujjivan, track this — and read it against three others.

For most banks the settling metric is a clean single number; for Ujjivan it is a relationship between two forces pulling in opposite directions. The thesis lives or dies on the trend in credit cost (and slippages) in the microfinance book, read against the pace of the secured-mix and deposit/CASA build. One is the damage; the other is the defence. What matters is which is winning.

~43.5%

~25.5%
CASA ratio — the funding moat. Rising it, and easing cost of funds, protects the margin.

Put concretely, here is how to read a future quarter. If credit cost is easing, the secured mix is climbing, NIM is holding around the high-single digits, and deposits/CASA keep growing, the transformation is outrunning the cycle and the low valuation should start to lift. If instead credit cost stays stuck or rises, slippages spread beyond the microfinance book, and NIM keeps compressing, the cycle is winning and the cautious multiple is right. The four dials — credit cost, secured mix, NIM, deposit/CASA growth — are the entire dashboard. Everything else is detail.

The scoreboard, in one line

Watch credit cost falling while the secured mix rises — that combination, sustained over several quarters, is the proof the bull case needs, because it means the microfinance losses are normalising and each future loss will be smaller. The failing signal is the mirror image: credit cost that refuses to normalise even as the secured mix grows, which would suggest the pivot is too slow to offset a structurally lumpy unsecured book. RoA and RoE are the summary scorecard those dials feed into — a normalised ~2% RoA and high-teens RoE is what a "won" transformation looks like. But the leading indicators are credit cost and secured mix, quarter after quarter. Track those two lines crossing, and you are reading the thesis in real time.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine transformation, a genuine cycle, and a valuation caught between them.

Ujjivan is a bank in the middle of changing what it is: a microfinance lender methodically converting itself into a diversified, secured, deposit-funded bank, while absorbing the worst microfinance stress cycle in years. The equity case is a wager that the transformation is real and durable enough to earn a better valuation, and that FY25's profit collapse was a cyclical low rather than a preview of a permanently lumpy future. That is a real bet with a real edge — and a real, well-understood failure mode.

The risk stack, laid out plainly

  • The microfinance asset-quality cycle (the defining one). Roughly half the book is still unsecured group microfinance. Another leg down in borrower stress — a bad monsoon, local disruption, renewed over-indebtedness — flows almost straight through to credit cost and profit, as FY25 showed.
  • Geographic and borrower concentration. Microfinance loans cluster by region and by borrower type (low-income women in specific states), so localised shocks hit disproportionately. Diversification reduces this but has not eliminated it.
  • Regulatory and self-regulatory change. The bank answers to RBI rules (priority-sector floors, the universal-bank bar) and MFIN guardrails that can slow microfinance growth. Rule changes can help long-run stability but crimp near-term income.
  • Deposit competition and CASA. A ~25.5% CASA ratio means Ujjivan still leans on costlier term deposits; if it must keep paying up to grow deposits, the margin advantage narrows.
  • Leadership continuity. Senior-management churn has been a recurring concern; a bank re-engineering its risk profile mid-cycle needs a steady hand at the top, making CEO/board stability a live risk factor rather than a footnote.
  • Execution and valuation. The secured pivot must be executed well — growing housing and MSME books without importing new credit problems — and the modest price-to-book leaves the stock exposed if credit cost stays elevated for longer than the bulls expect.

The bull case

  • A de-risking book — secured mix up from ~30% to ~43.5% in a year, heading for 65–70%; each point lowers future loss volatility.
  • Trough earnings, not broken ones — even in a brutal FY25 the bank earned ~1.6% RoA; normalised credit cost lifts RoA/RoE materially.
  • A real deposit franchise — deposits up ~20%, funding the bank cheaply and stickily like a mainstream lender.
  • A cleaner structure — the reverse merger removed the holdco overhang, leaving one simple, institution-owned listed bank.
  • A licence pay-off in sight — the same diversification that de-risks the book is what the RBI wants for a universal-bank upgrade.

The bear case

  • Still a microfinance bank — ~half the book is unsecured; the cycle can turn again before the pivot is finished.
  • Credit cost may not normalise on schedule — if stress persists, FY25's ~2.45% is a preview, not a peak.
  • Margin under pressure — mix shift toward secured lowers yield, and low CASA means funding isn't cheap yet; NIM can keep drifting down.
  • Management instability — leadership churn undercuts confidence in risk discipline exactly when it matters most.
  • A premium peer exists — AU shows what a fully secured SFB looks like; until Ujjivan gets there, the cautious multiple can persist for years.
The bottom line

Ujjivan Small Finance Bank is a microfinance lender caught in the act of becoming something steadier — a secured, deposit-funded bank — and FY25 was the year the market saw both halves of that identity at once: a franchise that kept growing (deposits +20%, secured book +56%) and a profit line that fell 43% because the old unsecured engine hit its worst stretch in years. Which half you weight is the whole decision. The concrete things to watch are not abstractions: the credit-cost trend over the next few quarters, the secured mix crossing 50% and heading toward the two-thirds goal, the CASA ratio grinding higher, and any RBI signal on the universal-bank licence. If those four move the right way together, Ujjivan stops being priced as a microfinance monoline and starts being priced as the diversified bank it is trying to become. If credit cost stays stubborn while the pivot drags, the market's caution will have been justified. The transformation is real; the question the next several quarters answer is simply whether it is fast enough.
