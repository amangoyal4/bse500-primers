00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Minda Corporation Limited — the listed flagship of the Spark Minda group, run by Ashok Minda — is a diversified automotive components supplier. It makes the parts a vehicle needs but a buyer rarely names: the locks, keys and immobilisers that secure it, the wiring harness that carries its electricity, the instrument cluster behind the handlebar or wheel, and the die-cast metal housings that hold it all together. Its customers are the vehicle makers (OEMs) — two-wheeler brands, commercial-vehicle and off-road builders, and passenger-car firms — plus a sizeable independent aftermarket.

Three facts frame the whole case:

  • It is NOT Uno Minda. There are two separate, independently listed companies with "Minda" in the name, run by two branches of the same founding family. This primer is about Minda Corporation / Spark Minda (Ashok Minda) — ticker MINDACORP — not the larger Uno Minda (UNOMINDA, N.K. Minda). Confusing the two is the single most common mistake investors make here; we untangle it in Section 02.
  • The growth lever is content, not just volume. Minda earns more not only when India builds more vehicles, but when each vehicle carries more Minda parts. FY26 revenue was ~₹6,185 Cr, up ~22% — well ahead of underlying auto-production growth — because premiumisation and electrification keep raising the rupee value of electrical and electronic content per vehicle. On an electric scooter that Minda content can be roughly three times what it is on an old petrol one.
  • It is re-rating from cyclical parts-maker to electronics compounder. Margins are still modest (~11.7% EBITDA) and the balance sheet carries some debt, but the market now prices Minda well above the traditional low-teens multiple of an auto-ancillary — on the back of a bought-in EV-electronics business (Flash Electronics), new switch and connected-systems orders, and a stated ambition to nearly triple revenue to ₹17,500 Cr by FY30.
In plain terms

Picture the underside of a scooter's handlebar or a truck's dashboard: the ignition lock, the key, the bundle of coloured wires, the speedo cluster, the little sensors. Minda Corporation is the company that designs and builds that unglamorous but essential hardware and sells it to the vehicle maker, fitted before the vehicle ever reaches a showroom. It has spent forty years as a "lock-and-key and wiring" supplier — and is now trying to become an electronics and EV-systems supplier, because every rupee of extra electronics a vehicle carries is a rupee Minda can try to win. The prize is that the shift to premium and electric vehicles multiplies that content; the catch is that it competes hard on price, rides the ups and downs of auto production, and is paying up to buy its way into the electronic future.

01

Auto-components 101: content-per-vehicle

No engineering degree required — just the handful of ideas that drive every parts-maker's economics.

A car or a two-wheeler you buy is assembled by an OEM — Hero, TVS, Bajaj, Maruti, Tata Motors, Ashok Leyland — but the OEM makes surprisingly little of it in-house. Most of the physical vehicle is bought in from a pyramid of suppliers. Understand that pyramid and you understand where Minda sits and how it makes money.

The supplier pyramid — where the parts come from
TierWhat it is (and who)
OEMThe vehicle brand that designs, assembles and sells the finished vehicle — Hero, TVS, Bajaj, Maruti, Tata Motors. It buys most components in.
Tier-1Sells finished systems/modules directly to the OEM — a complete wiring harness, a lock-set, a cluster. Minda Corporation is a Tier-1 across several products.
Tier-2 / Tier-3Supplies the Tier-1 with sub-parts and raw inputs — connectors, terminals, plastic mouldings, copper wire, electronic chips.
AftermarketA separate channel entirely: replacement parts sold after the vehicle is on the road, to repair shops and owners. Higher-margin, less cyclical.

The three numbers that decide a parts-maker's fate

  • Volume — how many vehicles get built. If India makes 20 million two-wheelers, every 2W part-maker's addressable pie is set by that number. This is the cyclical bit, and it's outside Minda's control.
  • Content-per-vehicle (CPV) — the rupee value of your parts on a single vehicle. This is the bit a good supplier can grow: add products, move up to higher-value electronics, win more of each vehicle. It is the most important idea in this whole primer.
  • Market share / kit win — of the vehicles that do get built, how many carry your part rather than a rival's. Won model-by-model, years ahead, and sticky once designed in.
The supplier's revenue equation

Roughly: Supplier revenue ≈ Vehicles built × Content-per-vehicle × Share won. A commodity parts-maker is a pure bet on the first term — it grows only as fast as the auto cycle. What separates a good ancillary is its ability to lift the middle term: to sell more, and more valuable, kit on the same vehicle. Minda's entire strategic story is an attempt to escape being a volume-taker by becoming a content-grower — pushing from mechanical locks toward electronic access, from simple wiring toward high-current EV harnesses, from a basic dial toward a connected digital cluster. Each step raises the rupees of Minda on a vehicle that the OEM was going to build anyway.

Two structural forces make CPV the right lens for the next decade. Premiumisation: buyers are trading up — a keyless smart-start scooter, a truck with a telematics-linked digital cluster — and every feature adds electronics. Electrification: an EV replaces a mechanical drivetrain with a high-voltage electrical one, which needs heavier wiring, new sensors, battery-management connections and fresh security architecture. Both trends push in the same direction: more electrical and electronic content per vehicle — exactly Minda's product territory.

02

The two Mindas — settling the confusion first

The genuine surprise most people miss: there are two listed "Minda" companies, and they are rivals.

Before a single number matters, clear this up, because half the internet gets it wrong. "Minda" is not one company on the stock market — it is two. Both descend from a single founder, both make auto components, both are large and listed, and they compete against each other. Buying one thinking you own the other is a real and frequent error.

The history, briefly. The founder, S.L. Minda, started an auto-components business in the north Indian belt around Delhi decades ago. Over time the family enterprise split along family lines into two independent groups: N.K. Minda built what is today Uno Minda (the switches, lighting and acoustics major), while Ashok Minda built Spark Minda, whose listed vehicle is Minda Corporation Limited. They share a surname, a heritage and — awkwardly — several product categories, but they are separate companies with separate managements, boards, factories and shareholders. There is no cross-holding that makes one an owner of the other.

Why it matters for an investor: the two are direct competitors in some products (both touch access/security and sensing), so news about "Minda" can move the wrong stock in your head. Uno Minda is the bigger, higher-margin, more richly-valued of the pair; Minda Corporation is the smaller, more mid-cap, faster-changing one. When you read a broker note, a headline or an order-win story, check the ticker — MINDACORP versus UNOMINDA — before you conclude anything. Everything below is strictly about MINDACORP.

03

What Minda Corporation actually makes

Four product families, reported today under two segments — decoded for a first-timer.

Minda's catalogue can look like a jumble of unrelated parts. It isn't. Everything it makes clusters into four product families, which the company now reports under two operating segments. Here is the whole portfolio in plain language, followed by how the revenue actually splits.

The four families

  • 1 · Mechatronics & security (vehicle access) — the founding business: lock-sets, ignition switches, keys, steering locks, immobilisers, and increasingly smart keys and keyless/passive-entry access. "Mechatronics" simply means mechanisms fused with electronics — a lock that is also a chip. As vehicles digitise, a dumb metal lock becomes a smart-access module, which is worth far more.
  • 2 · Wiring harness — the vehicle's electrical nervous system: the bundled loom of wires, connectors and terminals that carries power and signals to every light, sensor, motor and control. It is a high content-per-vehicle item, and one that grows as a vehicle gains features or goes electric. This is Minda's single largest product line (~31% of the product mix in Q3 FY26).
  • 3 · Information & connected systems — instrument clusters (the speedo/display pod), plus sensors, telematics and connected-vehicle electronics. This is the fastest-growing family, riding the shift from an analogue dial to a digital, connected screen that talks to a phone and the cloud.
  • 4 · Die-casting, plastics & aftermarket — high-pressure aluminium die-cast components (metal housings and structural parts, relevant to EV light-weighting), interior/exterior plastics, and a large independent aftermarket business selling spares under the Spark Minda brand.

The two reporting segments

  • Information & Connected Systems (ICS) — wiring harness + clusters + sensors/connected electronics. The larger and faster-growing segment: 9-month FY26 revenue of roughly ₹2,409 Cr, up ~26% (approx.), lifted by domestic and export wiring-harness order wins.
  • Mechatronics & Aftermarket — vehicle access/security + die-casting + the aftermarket. 9-month FY26 revenue of roughly ₹2,073 Cr (approx.), a steadier grower held back at times by soft ASEAN demand and die-casting capacity ramp-up.
Why the spread of products is the point, not a weakness

A one-product supplier lives and dies by one contract and one commodity. Minda deliberately spreads across access, wiring, clusters and metal so that (a) it can offer an OEM a basket of parts and win more content per platform, (b) a downturn in one product or one customer doesn't sink the whole business, and (c) it has multiple independent ways to raise content-per-vehicle. The trade-off is that being spread across many mid-value parts is harder to run at high margin than owning one dominant, high-value product — which is exactly why Minda's ~12% EBITDA margin sits below the mid-teens that more focused peers earn.

04

How Minda actually earns — the economics

OEM contracts, the aftermarket franchise, and the margin puzzle.

Minda has two engines that behave very differently. The OEM engine is big, sticky and lower-margin; the aftermarket engine is smaller, steadier and richer. Understanding how each works explains both the resilience and the margin ceiling of the business.

The OEM engine — designed in, and hard to remove

Winning an OEM part is a multi-year process. A supplier is chosen at the design stage of a new vehicle platform, engineers its part to fit, gets it validated and tooled, and then supplies it for the whole production life of that model — often 5–8 years. That makes revenue sticky once won: you don't get switched out mid-model. But the same process cuts the other way at negotiation time. OEMs are large, concentrated buyers with real power; they push suppliers on price every year, demand annual cost-downs, and pass raw-material swings only partly and with a lag. A Tier-1's margin is therefore permanently squeezed between what the OEM will pay and what copper, plastic, steel and electronics cost.

The aftermarket engine — the quiet high-margin franchise

Every vehicle Minda's parts go into eventually needs replacement locks, keys, wiring and spares. Minda sells those through an independent aftermarket channel under the Spark Minda brand — to distributors, mechanics and owners, not OEMs. This channel matters out of proportion to its size: it carries higher margins (no single powerful buyer dictating price), it is less cyclical (repairs happen whether or not new-vehicle sales are strong), and it turns Minda's installed base of parts on the road into a recurring annuity. A large, decades-old vehicle parc is a moat a new entrant simply cannot conjure.

The margin puzzle — and why it's the whole re-rating question

Minda's ~12% EBITDA margin is respectable for a diversified Tier-1 but modest next to the mid-to-high-teens that switch, driveline or premium-electronics specialists earn. The bet embedded in the share price is that margin rises — as the mix shifts toward higher-value electronics (connected clusters, smart access, EV systems), as exports and aftermarket grow, and as the higher-margin Flash Electronics business consolidates in. Whether that mid-teens margin actually arrives is the number that will make or break the re-rating (Section 10).

The mechanism in one line

Minda's revenue is defended by long, sticky, designed-in OEM contracts and topped up by a steady aftermarket annuity — but its profitability is capped by OEM pricing power and input costs, so the entire "compounder" thesis rests on shifting the product mix upward fast enough to drag the margin into the mid-teens.

05

The company and who controls it

A promoter-led group, four decades old, now mid-transformation.

Minda Corporation is a family-promoted business, tightly controlled by the Ashok Minda branch of the founding family. That gives it long-horizon, owner-operator decision-making — and it puts a great deal of weight on one family's strategy and succession.

Timeline (dates around the split are approximate)

  • ~1958

  • 1985

  • ~1990s–2000s
    The family enterprise divides into two independent groups: Ashok Minda's Spark Minda (Minda Corporation) and N.K. Minda's Minda Industries (today Uno Minda).
  • 2010s
    Minda Corp diversifies well beyond locks — into wiring harness, instrument clusters, die-casting and plastics, partly through acquisitions (including a European plastics business) and JVs; builds the Spark Minda aftermarket brand.
  • 2020s
    Pivot to connected and electric: smart access, telematics, sensors, EV-relevant components; sharpens the "content-per-vehicle" strategy.
  • Jan 2025

  • FY26
    Record revenue ₹6,185 Cr; signs a Toyodenso (Japan) switches JV with orders from Japanese OEMs; moves to consolidate Minda VAST; reaffirms a ₹17,500 Cr FY30 revenue ambition.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter group: the Minda family holds roughly ~65% (~64.8% at the June 2025 disclosure) — a firm controlling stake. Within that, Ashok Minda holds around 34% personally, with the balance across family holding entities (Minda Capital) and family members (Sarika Minda). High promoter ownership means strategy and capital allocation sit squarely with the family.
  • Leadership: Ashok Minda is Chairman and Group CEO — the architect of the diversification and the EV/electronics pivot. Owner-operator control is a strength for long-term bets like Flash, but concentrates key-person and succession risk.
  • Balance sheet: unlike a debt-free peer, Minda carries modest net debt and has stepped up capex sharply (FY26 capex ~₹413 Cr, a record) to fund the electronics and die-casting build-out and the Flash investment. It is investing ahead of the growth it is underwriting — a deliberate, and watchable, use of the balance sheet.
06

Flash, the JVs, and the EV pivot

The recent deals that are re-shaping what Minda will be — and how the market values it.

The last two years of corporate action tell you where management is steering. Every major move points the same way: toward higher-value electronics and the electric-vehicle powertrain, and toward exports. Three deals matter most.

Flash Electronics — the big one

Minda agreed to buy a 49% stake in Flash Electronics for ~₹1,370 Cr (an all-cash deal, struck at roughly ~25× FY26 earnings). Flash is a powertrain-electronics specialist — alternators, magnetos, electronic control units and, crucially, EV components — with strong export exposure and, importantly, much higher margins than Minda's base business: Flash generates on the order of ₹1,803 Cr revenue at a ~17% EBITDA margin (approx.). Two things follow. First, Flash roughly doubles the pool of EV-electronics content Minda can sell into a vehicle. Second, as Flash consolidates into the group accounts, it should lift Minda's blended margin toward the mid-teens the thesis needs. It is the clearest single lever in the entire re-rating.

The Toyodenso switches JV

Minda formed a joint venture with Toyodenso of Japan to make switches, and has already secured orders from leading Japanese OEMs, with production expected to start around Q4 FY27. Switches are a classic content item — every handlebar and dashboard has them — and a Japanese-OEM design win is a credibility marker and an export channel, not just a domestic order.

Minda VAST consolidation and die-casting

Minda is moving to consolidate Minda VAST (an access-systems associate) into its accounts following a change in the shareholder agreement — bringing more access/security revenue fully in-house. Alongside, it is scaling aluminium high-pressure die-casting capacity (partly organically, partly acquired) — a light-weighting play that matters more, not less, as EVs chase range by shedding weight, though the ramp has hit some capacity-timing bumps.

The pattern to notice: Minda is not waiting for electrification to lift its old products' content organically — it is buying and partnering its way straight into the high-value, high-margin electronic and EV parts of the vehicle (Flash's EV electronics, Toyodenso's switches, its own connected clusters and smart access). That accelerates the content-per-vehicle story, but it spends cash and adds integration and execution risk. The deals are the thesis made concrete — and the place where it could go wrong.

07

The content-per-vehicle engine

The one idea that could let Minda grow faster than the vehicles it supplies — built around a single, non-transferable number.

Here is the crux, and it is specific to Minda. A parts-maker tied only to volume grows as fast as the auto cycle and no faster. Minda's escape hatch is to raise the rupees of Minda content on each vehicle — and electrification hands it the biggest single jump in that number the industry has seen. On a two-wheeler, the same scooter, electrified, carries roughly three times the Minda kit of its petrol equivalent.

Why does an electric scooter carry so much more Minda content? Trace the swap. An EV deletes the engine, tank and mechanical drivetrain — parts Minda mostly didn't make anyway — and replaces them with an electrical powertrain that lives squarely in Minda's territory: heavier, higher-current wiring harnesses to move battery power; new sensors and battery-management connections; a digital, connected instrument cluster showing range and charge; new security and access architecture (a battery and a motor controller need protecting differently from a fuel tank); and EV-specific electronics of exactly the kind Flash supplies. The vehicle-maker still builds one scooter — but it now needs three times the Minda hardware to do it.

Why this is the number that actually matters

India's two-wheeler market might grow low-to-mid single digits in a good year and shrink in a bad one — a volume-only supplier is chained to that. But if Minda's content-per-vehicle climbs from ~₹11k toward ~₹35k as the fleet premiumises and electrifies, Minda's revenue can grow at a multiple of vehicle volumes, through the cycle. That is precisely why FY26 revenue rose ~22% while India's underlying vehicle production grew far less: Minda is winning more content per vehicle, not just riding more vehicles. The company's own ₹17,500 Cr-by-FY30 target — nearly a tripling from ₹6,185 Cr — only works if content-per-vehicle keeps compounding, because auto volumes alone cannot get there.

The catch, stated plainly: content-per-vehicle cuts both ways. Some of Minda's legacy content is mechanical — parts that shrink or vanish as vehicles electrify (an EV has no engine wiring, no fuel-system locks). The thesis is that Minda's new electronic content grows faster than its old mechanical content fades. It is a race between the two, not a free ride. The bull's whole claim is that Minda ends up with more rupees on the electric scooter than it ever had on the petrol one — which the ~₹35–40k figure says it can, if it wins that content rather than ceding it to a rival.

End markets and the auto cycle

Where the demand comes from — and why it swings.

Content-per-vehicle sets how much Minda can win per vehicle; the auto cycle sets how many vehicles there are to win it on. Minda's demand base spans three vehicle types and both the home and export markets, which spreads the risk but does not remove the cyclicality.

The more of these four families Minda wins on a single platform, the higher its content-per-vehicle — the strategic logic behind selling a basket rather than a part.

By end-market — a 2W and CV-heavy supplier

  • Two-wheelers — the largest end-market and the one where Minda's content-per-vehicle story is most potent (EV two-wheelers are the fastest-electrifying segment in India). Ties Minda to Hero, TVS, Bajaj, Honda and the new EV two-wheeler brands.
  • Commercial vehicles & off-road — trucks, buses, tractors and off-highway machines. Wiring harnesses and clusters carry high content per unit here, and CV cycles are distinct from 2W cycles, adding diversification.
  • Passenger vehicles — cars and SUVs; a growing but smaller slice, where Minda supplies access, clusters and select electronics.
  • Exports — a strategic priority and a key reason for the Flash and Toyodenso deals: export revenue diversifies away from the Indian cycle and typically earns better margins. Growing exports is central to the FY30 plan.
The cyclicality reality: when India's two-wheeler or CV production turns down — on a weak monsoon, a rate shock, a demand air-pocket — Minda's volumes fall with it, regardless of how good its content story is. Diversification across 2W/CV/PV, across products, and across geographies softens the blow but cannot cancel it. Any read of a single quarter has to separate cycle (volumes) from structure (content-per-vehicle). The structural story is the reason to own it; the cycle is the reason the ride is bumpy.

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, competitive Tier-1 landscape — including a company that shares its name.

Auto-components is a competitive, fragmented arena where every product line has strong specialists. Minda's diversification means it doesn't face one rival — it faces a different, capable competitor in each of its four families. Here is the field.

The competitive tension that decides the story

Minda's diversification is a double-edged sword against this field. On the upside, it can offer an OEM a multi-product basket — access + wiring + cluster on one platform — that a single-product specialist can't, which is the natural way to grow content-per-vehicle. On the downside, in each individual product it faces a rival that is more focused, and often larger, than Minda's slice of that product: Motherson dwarfs it in wiring, Uno Minda and Sandhar press it in access, Pricol in clusters, Bosch in electronics. So the strategic question is whether Minda's basket-selling and its Flash-powered EV electronics let it win rising content faster than these specialists defend their turf. That is a genuinely open contest, not a settled one.

How to read the multiple

At a market cap of roughly ₹16,700 Cr on FY26 profit of ₹358 Cr, Minda trades near ~47× headline earnings — and higher, around ~65–70× (approx.), on some normalised trailing measures that strip out one-off gains. Either way it is a full re-rating: auto-ancillaries historically changed hands in the low-to-mid teens, and Minda now carries a growth-electronics multiple. That price is discounting the transformation — mid-teens margins, EV content wins, Flash consolidating in, exports scaling — before most of it shows up in the reported margin. If the mix-shift arrives, today's multiple looks reasonable against tomorrow's earnings; if Minda stays a ~12%-margin cyclical, the multiple has borrowed from a future that hasn't been earned.

The one metric that settles it: watch the gap between Minda's revenue growth and India's 2W+CV production growth (its content-per-vehicle premium), together with the EBITDA margin marching toward the mid-teens as Flash consolidates and the electronic mix rises. If revenue keeps outrunning vehicle volumes and the margin climbs from ~11.7% toward ~14–15%, the compounder case is proving itself in the accounts. If revenue growth collapses back to the auto cycle, or the margin stalls near 12% while capex and debt keep rising, then this is a cyclical Tier-1 wearing a growth multiple — and the re-rating unwinds. That pair of numbers — content outgrowth and margin — is the scoreboard.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A real transformation underway, priced as if it is already done.

Minda Corporation is a credible attempt to turn a diversified, cyclical, mid-margin parts-maker into a content-per-vehicle electronics compounder — powered by a genuine structural tailwind (premiumisation + electrification) and concrete deals (Flash, Toyodenso, connected clusters). The debate is whether the execution and the margins arrive fast enough to justify a multiple that has run ahead of the current numbers.

The bull case

  • Content-per-vehicle tailwind — premiumisation and EVs push Minda's kit per vehicle from ~₹11k toward ~₹35–40k, letting revenue outgrow vehicle volumes for years.
  • Flash Electronics — bought-in EV-electronics business at ~17% margin, doubling EV content reach and set to lift blended margins as it consolidates.
  • Diversified basket — four product families across 2W/CV/PV and exports let Minda sell OEMs more content per platform and cushion single-product downturns.
  • New wins & JVs — Toyodenso switches for Japanese OEMs, wiring-harness order wins, connected clusters, Minda VAST consolidation — a stacking order book.
  • Aligned promoter — owner-operator control under Ashok Minda backing long-horizon EV/electronics bets.

The bear case

  • Rich multiple, thin margin — a ~45–70× stock on a ~12% EBITDA margin; the price assumes a mid-teens-margin future that hasn't yet shown up.
  • OEM cyclicality & pricing power — 2W/CV downturns hit volumes directly, and powerful OEMs squeeze Tier-1 pricing every year.
  • EV cuts both ways — some legacy mechanical content shrinks as vehicles electrify; the new electronic content has to more than replace it.
  • Execution & balance sheet — integrating Flash, ramping die-casting, record capex and rising debt all have to land on schedule.
  • Tough field — Motherson, Uno Minda, Sandhar, Pricol, Bosch and Sona each defend or attack a Minda product with more focus or scale. Input-cost (copper, plastics, electronics) and FX swings add noise.
The bottom line

Minda Corporation is a diversified Tier-1 mid-way through a deliberate climb from mechanical hardware to vehicle electronics, with the content-per-vehicle math and the Flash acquisition giving that climb real substance. The business is sound; the argument is about pace and price. The next concrete tests are on the calendar and countable: Flash Electronics landing in the consolidated accounts and dragging the group EBITDA margin up from ~11.7%, the Toyodenso JV starting production around Q4 FY27, and each quarter's revenue growth measured against India's two-wheeler and commercial-vehicle output. Watch whether the rupees of Minda on every vehicle keep rising and whether that mix finally shows up as a fatter margin — that, not the auto cycle, is what this share price is really betting on.
