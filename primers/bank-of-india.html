00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Bank of India is one of India's oldest and largest public-sector (government-majority) banks — founded in Mumbai in 1906, nationalised in 1969, and today 73.38% owned by the Government of India. It runs ~5,200 branches across India and — unusually for a PSU bank — a genuine international network spanning London, Paris, Singapore, Tokyo, Hong Kong, New York, Dubai and more, which contributes roughly 28% of its total business.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a large-balance-sheet turnaround story. Global business (deposits + advances) reached ₹16.99 lakh crore in FY26, up ~14.6%; the bank earned a record ₹10,527 Cr net profit (+14%), with return on assets around 0.93% and return on equity 16.4%. A few years ago this was a loss-making, bad-loan-choked bank; today it is comfortably profitable.
  • The asset-quality clean-up is the spine of the story. Gross NPAs fell from crisis-era double digits to 1.98% (Mar 2026), net NPAs to 0.56%, with a provision coverage ratio of 93.6% — meaning almost all recognised bad loans are already provided for. That is what turned losses into profit.
  • It's cheap because of who owns it — and that's the re-rating debate. The stock trades around 0.8× book value and ~6× earnings, a steep discount to private banks. The government's 73% stake means a dilution overhang (SEBI wants ≥25% public float), but it's also the classic PSU-bank re-rating setup: a cleaned-up, well-capitalised, profitable bank priced as if it were still broken.
In plain terms

Think of Bank of India as a giant, government-owned deposit-and-lending machine with a passport. It takes in savings from millions of Indians (and, unusually, from Indian businesses and diaspora abroad), lends that money out to companies, farmers and households, and pockets the spread. For years it lent badly, drowned in defaults, and lost money. The last several years have been a rescue: recognise the bad loans, provide for them, recover what you can, and rebuild profit. It has largely worked — profits and asset quality are the best in years. What holds the share price down is simpler: the government owns nearly three-quarters of it, and everyone knows a chunk of new shares must eventually be sold to the public, which caps how excited the market gets.

01

How a bank actually earns — the four levers

No finance degree required — just the handful of numbers that drive every bank.

A bank is, at heart, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (mostly your deposits) and lends it out more expensively (loans), keeping the difference. Everything an analyst studies about a bank is really about protecting and widening that spread while not losing the loaned money. Four ideas explain almost the whole business.

The banker's vocabulary, decoded (with Bank of India's FY26 number)
TermWhat it means — and BOI's figure
NII & NIMNet Interest Income = interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits (BOI: ₹25,172 Cr in FY26). Net Interest Margin expresses that as a % of loans/assets — the "spread" (BOI: global ~2.52%, domestic ~2.78%). Higher NIM = more profit per rupee lent.
CASACurrent Account & Savings Account deposits — the cheap, sticky money a bank pays little or no interest on. A high CASA % lowers the cost of funds and props up NIM. BOI's domestic CASA is ~37.6%.
GNPA / NNPAGross Non-Performing Assets = loans where the borrower has stopped paying (90+ days overdue), as a % of the loan book (BOI: 1.98%). Net NPA is what's left after the bank sets aside provisions against them (BOI: 0.56%). Lower is better; the gap between them is how much has been provided for.
PCR & credit costProvision Coverage Ratio = the share of bad loans already provided for (BOI: 93.6% — very high). Credit cost = new provisions in the year as a % of loans (BOI: ~0.48%, down sharply). Low credit cost = the clean-up is done and profits flow through.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: a bank's profit ≈ (interest spread × loans) + fees − operating costs − credit cost − tax. Two of those levers dominate. Net interest margin is the engine — a wider spread on a bigger book means more income. Credit cost is the brake — when loans go bad, the bank must set aside provisions, and those come straight out of profit. Bank of India's whole recovery is exactly this story: it widened and stabilised the spread, and — crucially — it slashed credit cost as the old bad loans were provided for and recovered. Once you stop bleeding provisions, a large deposit franchise becomes very profitable very quickly. That is "operating leverage" in banking.

Why capital is the fourth lever

Unlike an ordinary company, a bank is legally required to hold a cushion of equity capital against its loans — because it is lending out other people's deposits. Regulators measure this as the Capital Adequacy Ratio (CRAR) and the higher-quality CET-1 ratio (core equity). More capital = more capacity to grow the loan book and absorb shocks, but also more shares outstanding (which dilutes per-share earnings). BOI is well-capitalised — CRAR 18.01%, CET-1 15.05% — comfortably above the ~11.5% minimum, which matters greatly for the capital and dilution story in Section 07.

02

The company and who controls it

A 1906 Mumbai institution, nationalised in 1969, still majority-owned by the state.

Bank of India was founded in 1906 by a group of Mumbai businessmen — including Ramnarain Ruia and members of the Tata family — as a privately-owned commercial bank, one of the first Indian-owned banks of the colonial era. It was nationalised in July 1969 in the sweep that brought India's biggest banks under government control, and it has been a public-sector bank ever since.

Timeline

  • 1906
    Founded in Mumbai by Ramnarain Ruia, the Tatas and others — one 50-employee office, ₹50 lakh capital. A privately-owned Indian bank.
  • 1946
    Opens its first overseas branch in London — the start of a distinctively international footprint that still defines it.
  • 1969
    Nationalised alongside 13 other major banks; ownership passes to the Government of India.
  • 1997
    First public share issue — begins the long transition from wholly-state-owned to a listed PSU bank.
  • 2008
    Qualified Institutional Placement (QIP) raises capital — its last QIP to date, a fact that matters for the free-float story.
  • 2016–20
    The bad-loan crisis: gross NPAs balloon into double digits; BOI is placed under the RBI's Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework and posts heavy losses. The nadir of the story.
  • FY24–FY26
    The turnaround completes: GNPA falls to 1.98%, PCR to 93.6%, net profit to a record ₹10,527 Cr; the bank re-emerges as a clean, profitable, well-capitalised PSU.

Ownership & the government's role

  • Government of India — 73.38% (held via the President of India). This makes BOI a majority state-owned bank, but with a meaningfully larger public float than heavily-owned peers like Indian Overseas Bank (~96% government) or Central Bank of India. That matters: BOI's dilution need to reach the 25% public-float rule is smaller.
  • Institutions ~21%, with LIC alone holding ~8.5% and other insurers, plus foreign portfolio investors (~3.5%) and mutual funds (~4.3%). Retail/non-institutional holders make up the rest (~5.4%).
  • Sovereign backing is the invisible asset. As a government bank, BOI enjoys implicit sovereign support, deep trust with depositors, and access to government business — but it also carries the flip side: political direction of lending, bureaucratic pay scales, and the government's own priorities (fiscal, developmental) shaping strategy.
Why "public-sector bank" is a category of its own

A PSU bank is not just a bank that happens to be owned by the state — the ownership shapes the whole business. On the plus side: an enormous, low-cost, ultra-sticky deposit base (Indians trust government banks with their savings), a nationwide branch network reaching villages private banks ignore, and a sovereign safety net. On the minus side: historically weaker underwriting discipline (which produced the bad-loan crisis), thinner margins than private banks, government-set constraints on pay and hiring that make it hard to retain talent, and the ever-present dilution overhang from the government eventually selling shares. Understanding BOI means holding both sides at once — a franchise of real scale and trust, run inside government constraints.

03

The international book — BOI's defining difference

The one thing that sets Bank of India apart from almost every other PSU bank.

Most Indian public-sector banks are overwhelmingly domestic — their business is deposits from Indian savers and loans to Indian borrowers, full stop. Bank of India is different. Thanks to a foreign presence dating to its 1946 London branch, it runs one of the most internationally-present networks of any Indian PSU bank: branches, subsidiaries and offices across roughly 15–22 countries and five continents — Tokyo, Singapore, Hong Kong, London, Paris, New York, DIFC Dubai, GIFT City, and more. The overseas book is ~28% of the bank's total business, with overseas advances of about ₹1.17 lakh crore (+14% in FY26).

International business ≈ 27.8% of total. For comparison, most peer PSU banks run overseas books in the low single digits — this is a genuine structural differentiator.

How the overseas book is different from a domestic one

  • Different customers. Overseas branches serve Indian corporates operating abroad, trade-finance (import/export) flows, NRI (non-resident Indian) deposits, and lending to Indian and local businesses in those financial centres — not retail savings accounts in a Mumbai suburb.
  • Different currencies and margins. The overseas book is largely foreign-currency (USD-linked). It typically earns thinner spreads than the domestic book — dollar lending is competitive and low-margin — which is a big reason BOI's global NIM (~2.52%) sits well below its domestic NIM (~2.78%). The overseas book adds scale and diversification, but drags the blended margin down.
  • Different risks. It brings currency risk, foreign-regulator compliance (each country's rules), and exposure to global credit cycles — but also diversifies the bank away from a purely Indian economic cycle. When Indian corporate credit was weak, the overseas book was a different kind of exposure.
  • A trade-finance and treasury engine. The international network makes BOI a natural conduit for cross-border trade settlement and treasury operations — a franchise built over decades that a domestic-only bank simply cannot replicate quickly.
Why this matters for how you value BOI

The international book is a genuine, hard-to-copy asset — but it's a double-edged one. It gives Bank of India diversification and a trade-finance franchise most PSU peers lack, and it grew ~14% in FY26. But because dollar lending is low-margin, it structurally caps the bank's blended NIM below that of a purely domestic, retail-heavy bank. So when you compare BOI's ~2.5% global margin to a domestic-focused peer's, you're partly seeing this mix effect, not just underlying quality. The bull reading: as the domestic book (higher-margin RAM lending) grows faster, the blend improves. The realist's reading: the overseas book is a permanent feature that keeps BOI a lower-margin, higher-diversification bank than its domestic-only cousins.

04

Deposits & CASA — the raw material

A bank is only as good as the money it can gather cheaply.

A bank's competitive advantage starts on the liability side — how much money it can raise, and how cheaply. Bank of India's global deposits reached ₹9.27 lakh crore in FY26, up ~13.6%. The prize within that number is CASA — current and savings accounts — the low-cost, sticky deposits that a bank pays little interest on. BOI's domestic CASA ratio is ~37.6%, respectable for a PSU bank but the number every analyst watches, because it directly sets the cost of funds.

Why CASA is the quiet moat

Savings and current accounts are the closest thing a bank has to a moat. A depositor who keeps their salary account, auto-debits and family savings at BOI rarely switches — it's a hassle — and the bank pays them very little (often ~2.7–3.5% on savings, nothing on current accounts). Compare that with a term deposit, where the saver shops around for the best fixed rate and the bank must pay ~7%. So every extra point of CASA lowers the bank's cost of funds and widens its margin. The pressure BOI faces — like all PSU banks — is that in a competitive, digital, rate-hungry world, depositors are increasingly moving money into higher-yielding term deposits, which is why CASA ratios have drifted down industry-wide and why FY26 deposit growth was "mainly driven by term deposits." Defending CASA is a permanent battle.

05

The loan book — RAM vs corporate

Where the money goes determines both the yield and the risk.

On the asset side, BOI's global advances grew a strong ~15.8% to ₹7.71 lakh crore in FY26. What matters as much as the growth is the mix. Indian banks split their lending into two broad camps: RAM — Retail, Agriculture and MSME — the granular, higher-yielding, better-spread loans; and corporate/wholesale — big-ticket loans to large companies, lower-margin but easier to grow in chunks (and historically the source of most PSU bad loans). BOI's RAM share is ~58.7% of domestic advances — a deliberate tilt toward the safer, more profitable end.

RAM = Retail, Agriculture, MSME. Higher RAM share generally means better yields and more granular (less lumpy) risk — the opposite of the concentrated corporate exposures that sank PSU banks in the last cycle.

Why the RAM tilt is a post-crisis correction

  • Retail (home loans, vehicle loans, personal loans, gold loans) — small-ticket, spread across millions of borrowers, so one default barely dents the book. Higher yields than corporate, and a growth engine as Indian household credit deepens.
  • Agriculture — priority-sector lending PSU banks are mandated to do; lower-yielding and prone to political loan waivers, but part of the PSU mandate and rural reach.
  • MSME (micro, small & medium enterprises) — higher-yielding business lending, increasingly credit-scored and collateral-backed; a policy priority and a margin-friendly segment.
  • Corporate — large-company term loans and working capital. Necessary for scale, but the concentration here (a handful of big borrowers) is exactly what produced the last crisis, so banks now underwrite it far more carefully.
The lesson the bad-loan crisis taught every PSU bank

In the 2010s, PSU banks — BOI among them — lent aggressively to a few large corporates and infrastructure projects. When those projects soured (power, steel, telecom, infra), the losses were concentrated and enormous. The strategic answer the whole sector adopted is the RAM tilt: lend smaller amounts to far more borrowers, so no single default can blow a hole in the book. It lowers ticket size and lifts yields at the same time. BOI's ~59% RAM share is that lesson made concrete — a more granular, higher-spread, more resilient book than the corporate-heavy one that got it into trouble a decade ago.

06

The asset-quality clean-up — the heart of the story

How Bank of India went from bad-loan casualty to sub-2% gross NPAs.

If you understand one thing about Bank of India as an investment, understand this: the entire recovery is an asset-quality story. A decade ago BOI was one of the worst-hit victims of India's bad-loan crisis — gross NPAs in the double digits, heavy losses, and a spell under the RBI's Prompt Corrective Action regime (a regulatory "intensive care" that restricts a weak bank's lending). FY26 is the other end of that journey: GNPA 1.98%, NNPA 0.56%, PCR 93.6%, credit cost 0.48%, slippage ratio 0.83% — every number pointing the right way.

The improvement from FY25 to FY26 alone was ~129 basis points. Net NPA fell to 0.56% and PCR rose to 93.6% — meaning almost all recognised bad loans are already fully provided for.

The clean-up vocabulary, in plain English

  • GNPA vs NNPA. Gross NPA is the total pile of loans gone bad. The bank then sets aside provisions (money charged against profit as a cushion) against them; what's left after provisions is Net NPA. BOI's NNPA of just 0.56% means the residual, un-provided bad-loan risk is tiny.
  • PCR (provision coverage ratio) — 93.6%. The share of bad loans already provided for. At 93.6%, BOI has effectively pre-absorbed almost all the losses on its known bad loans. This is the "the bad news is behind us" number — and it's very high.
  • Slippages — 0.83%. The rate at which good loans turn bad in a year. Low and falling slippages mean the new lending is holding up — the clean-up isn't just fixing old wounds, the underwriting has improved.
  • Credit cost — 0.48%. The provisions charged this year as a % of loans. When this collapses (from 0.76% to 0.48% year-on-year here), the money that used to be swallowed by provisions instead drops to the bottom line. This single shift is what powered the profit recovery.
Why this mechanism turns losses into record profit

During the crisis, BOI had to charge enormous provisions every year against its mountain of bad loans — and those provisions are a cost that turned the P&L red. Once a bank has (a) recognised its bad loans, (b) provided heavily against them (high PCR), and (c) stopped generating many new ones (low slippage), the provisioning burden falls away. The same deposit-and-loan machine that was drowning in credit costs suddenly keeps most of its operating profit. That's precisely BOI's FY26: operating profit of ~₹17,049 Cr, but now only a modest slice is eaten by provisions, so ₹10,527 Cr reaches net profit. The clean-up didn't make the bank bigger — it made the profit it always generated finally stick.

The honest caveat: asset quality is cyclical. Today's sub-2% GNPA reflects a benign credit environment, aggressive recoveries (including via the bankruptcy code) and heavy write-offs. A future downturn — corporate stress, an unsecured-retail wobble, or an agri loan-waiver cycle — could lift slippages again. The clean balance sheet is real, but it is a snapshot in a good part of the cycle, not a permanent state.
07

Capital, the government stake & the free-float overhang

The single biggest thing keeping a cleaned-up bank cheap.

Bank of India is now well-capitalised — CRAR of 18.01% and core CET-1 of 15.05%, both comfortably above regulatory minimums, giving it plenty of room to grow the loan book. But its share price is shadowed by an ownership problem: the Government of India owns 73.38%, and India's market regulator (SEBI) requires listed companies to have at least 25% public shareholding (the MPS rule). PSU banks have long had special extensions, but the direction is clear — the government's stake must eventually come down.

Why the free-float overhang matters — and why it's less acute than at IOB

  • CET-1 & CRAR tell you the bank has enough capital — it doesn't need to raise equity to survive or grow. That's a strong position: any capital raise would be to meet the public-float rule, not out of weakness.
  • The MPS path — to get public shareholding to 25%, the government must dilute its stake via a QIP (selling new shares to institutions), an offer-for-sale, or divestment. Each of those adds shares to the market, which weighs on the price (an "overhang") until it's done. BOI's last QIP was way back in 2008.
  • BOI vs IOB. This is where BOI screens better than the most-owned PSU banks. Indian Overseas Bank is ~96% government-owned — it has a huge dilution gap to close, and has been launching QIPs specifically to chip away at it. BOI at 73% already has a ~27% public float; its distance to the 25%-public target is far smaller, so the dilution overhang is materially less severe.
The PSU-bank re-rating theme, explained

Here's the whole investment argument in one paragraph. PSU banks spent the last decade being priced as broken: bad loans, losses, government interference. Now many are cleaned up — profitable, well-capitalised, with sub-2% NPAs — yet they still trade at big discounts to private banks (BOI at ~0.8× book vs private banks at 2–3×). The bull thesis (the "re-rating") is that as the market gains confidence the clean-up is durable and returns are sustainable, that valuation gap narrows and the shares re-rate upward. The counter-argument: the discount is structural and permanent — lower margins, government control, the perpetual dilution overhang and weaker growth mean PSU banks deserve to trade below book. BOI sits squarely in this debate: cheap on every metric, cleaned up on every metric, but held back by who owns it.

08

Profitability recovery — RoA, RoE & the normalisation

From losses to a record ₹10,527 Cr — and what "normal" looks like now.

The clean-up and the loan growth show up, finally, in the return ratios — the numbers that tell you how efficiently a bank turns its assets and equity into profit. FY26 delivered net profit of ₹10,527 Cr (+14%), a return on assets (RoA) of ~0.93% and a return on equity (RoE) of ~16.4%. For a PSU bank that was loss-making a few years ago, this is a genuine normalisation.

Reading the return ratios

  • RoA ~0.93%. Return on assets — profit as a % of the bank's total assets. For Indian banks, ~1% RoA is the "good bank" threshold. BOI is now essentially at it. (The Q4 FY26 quarter touched ~1.01%; the full-year figure was ~0.93%.) Getting sustainably above 1% is the next milestone.
  • RoE ~16.4%. Return on equity — profit as a % of shareholders' capital. A mid-teens RoE is respectable and, crucially, above the bank's cost of equity, meaning it is creating value rather than destroying it. PSU banks' high leverage (lots of assets per unit of equity) flatters RoE relative to RoA.
  • The margin caveat. Notice NII grew only ~3% in FY26 even as the book grew ~15% — margins compressed as deposit costs rose and the low-margin overseas book grew. The profit growth came from lower provisions, not a wider spread. That's a subtle but important quality point: the easy gains from provision normalisation are largely banked; from here, growth must come from volume and margin, which is harder.
What to watch each quarter: the "provision tailwind" that drove profits is fading (credit cost is already low). Sustaining profit growth now depends on (1) holding NIM against deposit-cost pressure, (2) keeping slippages low, and (3) growing RAM advances profitably. If NIM slips and credit cost can't fall much further, profit growth naturally slows — watch NII growth and NIM as closely as the NPA numbers.
09

The PSU-bank landscape — where BOI sits

Big, old, international — and mid-pack among the state banks.

India's banking sector splits into three broad camps: the dominant private banks (HDFC, ICICI, Axis, Kotak) that earn the best margins and richest valuations; the public-sector banks led by giant SBI and a cluster of mid-sized nationalised banks (BOB, PNB, Canara, Union, Indian Bank, BOI, and smaller ones like IOB and Central); and the newer small-finance and niche banks. Bank of India is a mid-to-large nationalised bank — not the biggest, but distinguished by its age, scale and international reach.

Because both are being written up in this library, it's worth being precise. Both are asset-quality turnaround stories trading below book; but BOI is the larger, more diversified, less dilution-encumbered of the two, while IOB offers a more concentrated, higher-government-ownership profile. Different flavours of the same PSU re-rating trade.

10

Financials & valuation — why banks trade on book

The numbers, and the single most important idea in bank valuation.

Bank of India's FY26 financials read like a recovered bank: record profit, strong loan growth, clean asset quality, ample capital. Yet the stock trades at ~0.8× book value and ~6× earnings — cheap by any measure. To understand why that isn't a contradiction, you have to understand why banks are valued on book value (P/B), not earnings (P/E).

Why banks are valued on book, not on P/E

An ordinary company is valued on its earnings (P/E) because its profit is fairly stable and predictable. A bank's earnings are not — they swing violently with the credit cycle. In a good year, provisions are tiny and profit looks huge; in a bad year, a wave of defaults can wipe out years of profit in a single quarter (exactly what happened to BOI in the crisis). So a bank's P/E is misleading — a low P/E might just mean you're at the top of a credit cycle about to turn. Instead, analysts anchor on book value — the bank's net worth (assets minus liabilities) — and ask: how much am I paying per rupee of equity, and what return (RoE) does that equity earn? The rule of thumb: a bank sustainably earning a high RoE deserves to trade above book (P/B > 1); one earning a poor or volatile RoE trades below book (P/B < 1). BOI at ~0.8× book with a ~16% RoE is the market saying: "I don't yet fully trust that 16% RoE is durable — and there's a dilution overhang — so I'll pay less than book." The re-rating case is that if the RoE holds, that discount should shrink.

A cleaned-up bank trading below its own book value is the market pricing in doubt — about the durability of returns, and about the shares the government still has to sell.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely recovered bank — priced for the doubts, not the recovery.

Bank of India in FY26 is a bank that has done the hard part: it recognised and provided for its bad loans, rebuilt profit to a record, grew its book at healthy rates, and holds ample capital. The debate now is almost entirely about the durability of returns and the ownership overhang, not survival. Early FY27 momentum is encouraging — a Q1 FY27 business update showed global business up ~16.6% to ₹17.6 lakh crore.

The bull case

  • Clean balance sheet — GNPA 1.98%, NNPA 0.56%, PCR 93.6%; the provisioning burden that caused losses is largely gone.
  • Deep-discount valuation — ~0.8× book and ~6× earnings on a ~16% RoE; a classic PSU re-rating candidate.
  • Well-capitalised & growing — 18% CRAR, ~14–16% business growth, ~59% RAM tilt; capital to grow without distress-raising.
  • Smaller dilution overhang than peers — GoI at 73% (vs IOB's ~96%) means a shorter road to the 25% public float.
  • Unique international franchise — a ~28% overseas book and trade-finance network no domestic-only PSU peer can replicate.

The bear case

  • The dilution overhang is real — the government must eventually sell shares to meet the public-float rule, capping upside until it's done.
  • Margin pressure — NII grew only ~3%; the low-margin overseas book and rising deposit costs keep blended NIM (~2.5%) below private peers.
  • The easy gains are banked — the provision-normalisation tailwind is fading; future profit growth is harder to source.
  • Cyclical asset quality — today's sub-2% GNPA is a good-cycle snapshot; a downturn or agri-waiver cycle could lift slippages.
  • PSU structural discount — government control, pay/hiring constraints and lower margins may mean it deserves to trade below book indefinitely.
The bottom line

Bank of India is a large, old, internationally-networked PSU bank that has genuinely turned around — clean asset quality, record profit, strong capital, near-1% RoA. On the fundamentals it is a much better bank than the ~0.8× book valuation suggests. What holds it back is not the business but the structure: government majority ownership, a dilution overhang (albeit smaller than IOB's), thin margins weighed down by the overseas book, and the fading of the provision-writeback tailwind. The story from here is written in three numbers each quarter — NIM (is the margin holding?), slippages/credit cost (is asset quality staying clean?), and the government's stake (how, and how disruptively, does it get to 25% public float?). Get those right and a below-book PSU bank re-rates; disappoint on any, and the discount is exactly what a structurally-constrained state bank is worth.
