Reliance Industries, India's everything-company

A complete walk through India's largest company by revenue and market value — how one balance sheet holds an oil refinery, the country's biggest mobile network, its biggest retailer, an upstream gas field and a clean-energy moonshot; why the "consumer" half now out-earns the "energy" half; and what the looming Jio IPO and a $110-billion AI bet really mean.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Reliance Industries is India's largest company — by revenue, by profit, and by stock-market value. But it isn't one business; it's four very different ones stitched into a single holding company: a giant oil refinery and petrochemicals complex (the "O2C" business), India's biggest mobile network (Jio), India's biggest retailer (Reliance Retail), and an upstream oil-and-gas field — with a brand-new clean-energy and AI build-out on top. Founded as a textile trader by Dhirubhai Ambani, it is now controlled by his son Mukesh Ambani and family, who own about 50%.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Enormous scale, record FY26. Consolidated revenue reached ~₹11,75,919 Cr (~$124 bn) in FY26 (year ended Mar 2026), up ~9.8%; EBITDA was ~₹2,07,911 Cr (up 13.4%); net profit crossed ₹10 bn for the first time at ~₹95,610 Cr (up ~18%). That profit alone is bigger than most listed Indian companies' entire revenue.
  • The centre of gravity has shifted to the consumer. Jio and Reliance Retail together now generate more than half of the group's EBITDA and profit. The legacy O2C/energy engine still throws off the most cash, but the growth story — and increasingly the valuation story — is the consumer pivot.
  • Two big catalysts are live right now. Jio Platforms filed its IPO papers (DRHP) with SEBI in June 2026 — set to be India's largest-ever public offering — and management has committed to a multi-year, ~$110-billion AI-and-data-centre build plus a 20 GW solar / 40+ GWh battery "giga-complex" at Jamnagar. The whole story is whether these unlock value faster than they consume capital.
In plain terms

Think of Reliance as a country's economy in a single share. When an Indian fills up petrol, makes a phone call, buys groceries, or streams a film, there's a good chance Reliance earns from it. The company's founder built it on the simple idea of making at home what India used to import — first cloth, then plastics, then refined fuel, then mobile data, then retail. The current chapter is the hardest to value: Reliance is using the cash from its old, dependable oil business to fund two giant bets on the future — green energy and artificial intelligence — while preparing to list its crown-jewel telecom arm, Jio, on the stock market. The question for an investor isn't "is the business good?" — it plainly is — but "how do I price four businesses moving at four different speeds, plus two moonshots?"

01

Conglomerate economics 101: why this is hard to value

No finance degree required — just the one idea that governs everything: sum-of-the-parts.

Most companies do one thing, so you value them with one yardstick. Reliance does four things that don't even share a yardstick — you cannot price a mobile network the way you price an oil refinery. The professional approach is "sum-of-the-parts" (SOTP): value each business separately, as if it were its own listed company, then add them up and subtract the debt. Understanding SOTP is the single key that unlocks the whole Reliance investment case.

The "conglomerate discount" — and why Reliance attacks it

Markets often value a conglomerate at less than the sum of its parts — a "conglomerate discount" — because a sprawling group is hard to analyse and capital can get trapped in weak divisions. Reliance's answer is the reverse: it keeps unlocking the parts. It sold ~33% of Jio Platforms and ~10% of Reliance Retail to global investors (Meta, Google, KKR, Silver Lake, Mubadala and others) in 2020, which put a hard market price on those crown jewels — and the looming Jio IPO is the next, biggest unlock of all. Each time a piece is priced separately, the market can no longer pretend the consumer businesses are "just bolted onto an oil company." That is the structural reason the stock often trades on the parts, not the whole.

The two yardsticks you'll hear most

  • EBITDA — earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation; a rough proxy for the operating cash a business throws off before financing and accounting noise. It's the common currency for comparing divisions and the number Reliance reports per segment.
  • Net debt — total borrowings minus cash. A conglomerate borrows at the parent and deploys across divisions, so the group's net debt (and whether free cash flow covers the capex) is what tells you if the empire is over-stretching. Reliance ran ~₹1,24,717 Cr of net debt at FY26-end — large in rupees, but modest against ₹2.08 lakh Cr of annual EBITDA.

03

The company and who controls it

From a Mumbai textile trader to India's most valuable enterprise — under one family.

Reliance is a founder-family-controlled empire. Dhirubhai Ambani started it in the 1960s trading textiles, then pursued relentless backward integration — making the inputs to whatever he sold. After his death in 2002 and a famous split, his elder son Mukesh Ambani took the flagship and transformed it twice over: first into a refining-and-petrochem colossus, then into a consumer-internet-and-retail giant.

Timeline

  • 1966
    Dhirubhai Ambani founds Reliance Commercial Corporation, trading textiles; later builds the "Vimal" brand and a famously wide retail-shareholder base.
  • 1990s
    Backward-integrates into petrochemicals and then refining — building the foundations of the modern O2C business.
  • 1999–2008
    Commissions the Jamnagar refinery complex, eventually the world's largest single-site refinery; KG-D6 gas comes onstream (2009).
  • 2016
    Launches Jio with free-then-cheap 4G data — detonating India's data market and signing up 100m+ users in months.
  • 2020
    Raises ~₹2.6 lakh Cr selling stakes in Jio Platforms and Reliance Retail to Meta, Google, KKR, Silver Lake, Mubadala and others; briefly turns net-debt-free.
  • 2023
    Demerges the financial-services arm as Jio Financial Services; deepens the new-energy build at Jamnagar.
  • FY26
    Record revenue (₹11.76 L Cr) and profit (₹95,610 Cr); Jio IPO DRHP filed with SEBI (Jun '26); commits to a multi-year ~$110bn AI / data-centre build with partners including Meta.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter group: the Ambani family (through trusts, LLPs and investment companies) holds roughly 50% of RIL. Individual direct holdings are tiny; control sits with the family-trust structure.
  • Leadership & succession: Mukesh Ambani is Chairman & Managing Director. His three children now lead the future engines — Akash Ambani (Jio / digital), Isha Ambani (Retail), and Anant Ambani (new energy). The orderly grooming of the next generation across the three growth arms is itself a watched corporate-governance theme.
  • Strategic investors: the 2020 fundraises seated marquee names (Meta, Google, KKR, Silver Lake, Mubadala, PIF, ADIA) directly in Jio and Retail — a validation of the consumer businesses and a precursor to listing them.
04

O2C: the cash machine that funds everything

The oldest engine, the biggest revenue line — and the one with the toughest margins.

"O2C" means "oil to chemicals." Reliance buys crude oil, refines it at Jamnagar into transport fuels (petrol, diesel, jet fuel), and converts a large slice into petrochemicals — the plastics, polyester and intermediates that go into packaging, textiles and countless products. It is the world's largest single-site refining complex, and for decades it was Reliance. In FY26 it remained the group's largest revenue line at ~₹6,62,401 Cr (up 5.7%) with EBITDA of ~₹60,546 Cr (up 10.1%).

The number that rules O2C: the "crack spread"

A refiner doesn't really earn on the price of oil — it earns on the gap between what it pays for crude and what it sells the fuel for. That gap is the refining margin or "crack spread." Reliance's edge is being bigger, more complex and more flexible than rivals: a high-complexity refinery can process cheaper, heavier, "dirtier" crude (e.g. discounted Russian or heavy grades) and still squeeze out premium fuels — capturing a wider crack than a simple refinery can. That feedstock flexibility is why O2C "powered through" a turbulent FY26 energy market and still grew EBITDA while many global refiners struggled.

Why O2C is both a strength and a worry

  • The strength: it is a relentless cash generator. Even in a soft year it threw off ₹60,000 Cr+ of EBITDA — the war-chest that built Jio, Retail and now the clean-energy complex. It also gives Reliance an enviable feedstock position for downstream chemicals and (soon) green molecules.
  • The worry: margins are cyclical and globally set. A flood of new refining/petchem capacity (much of it in China) periodically crushes spreads; demand for transport fuels faces a long-run threat from electrification. And it's capital-heavy — the asset base is huge and slow to grow.
  • The pivot: Reliance's answer is to transform O2C itself — pushing the molecule mix toward higher-value chemicals, integrating new-energy feedstocks, and turning Jamnagar into a partly autonomous, lower-carbon complex over time.
How to read O2C each quarter: watch refining margins / GRMs and petrochemical spreads, not headline revenue (which mostly tracks oil prices). Strong cracks + cheap feedstock = a good O2C quarter; oversupplied spreads = a weak one, almost regardless of how much crude flows through.
05

Jio: the digital giant going public

The business that re-rated the whole company — and the catalyst of 2026.

Jio is India's largest mobile network and the centrepiece of Reliance's transformation. Launched in 2016 with free-then-ultra-cheap 4G, it triggered a data revolution, consolidated the telecom industry, and turned Reliance from "an oil company" into "a consumer-tech company that also refines oil." In FY26 Jio Platforms posted revenue of ~₹1,72,317 Cr with an EBITDA margin around 52% and profit of ~₹30,053 Cr — and it reached 524.4 million subscribers, including 268 million on 5G.

The one telecom number to know: ARPU

ARPU = "average revenue per user" per month. Telecom is a fixed-cost network business: once the towers and spectrum are paid for, every extra rupee of ARPU is almost pure profit. Jio's ~₹214 ARPU is rising on tariff hikes and a mix shift to data-heavy plans, fixed wireless (JioAirFiber), and digital services. The bull math is simple: 500m+ users × even modest ARPU increases × ~50% incremental margins = a powerful, recurring profit engine — the opposite of cyclical O2C. That predictability is exactly why a telecom-tech platform earns a far higher valuation multiple than a refinery.

Why the Jio IPO matters so much

In June 2026, Jio Platforms filed its DRHP with SEBI — the formal step toward what is expected to be India's largest-ever IPO. The listing does three things at once:

  • It puts a public price on the crown jewel. Today Jio's value is embedded inside RIL; once listed, the market values it directly — the ultimate "sum-of-the-parts" unlock (see §01).
  • It can re-rate the parent. If the market prices Jio richly, the implied value of RIL's Jio stake rises in plain sight — historically a catalyst for the RIL share price.
  • It funds the next leg. A listed Jio with its own currency and balance sheet can fund 5G/AI/fibre expansion independently, easing the load on the parent.

Jio is also more than a telco: it bundles JioCinema/streaming, JioMart, payments, devices and an expanding AI stack — the platform Reliance is wiring its $110bn AI ambition through (see §08).

06

Reliance Retail: India's biggest shopkeeper

The least-understood engine — and quietly one of the largest retailers in Asia.

Reliance Retail is India's largest retailer by revenue and store count, spanning groceries (Reliance Fresh, Smart Bazaar), electronics (Reliance Digital), fashion (Trends, AJIO, plus brands like Hamleys), and a fast-growing online and quick-commerce operation (JioMart). In FY26 it delivered gross revenue of ~₹3,70,026 Cr (up 11.8%) and EBITDA of ~₹27,033 Cr (up ~8%), serving a registered customer base of ~387 million across 20,160 stores and ~78m sq ft of retail space.

Why retail is a different kind of bet from O2C or Jio

Retail is a thin-margin, high-volume, execution-heavy business — you make a few percent on each sale and win by scale, supply-chain efficiency and footfall. Reliance's edge is reach and integration: thousands of stores feeding (and fed by) JioMart and quick commerce, a captive 500m-user Jio funnel to market to, and the buying power of the country's biggest purchaser. FY26 also showed retail's sensitivity to consumer demand — growth softened in places, and quick-commerce/hyperlocal became the new battleground (hyperlocal orders grew ~300%). The long bull case is the formalisation of Indian retail: organised chains steadily taking share from tens of millions of small unorganised shops, with Reliance the largest single beneficiary.

Like Jio, Retail took in marquee outside investors in 2020 (KKR, Silver Lake, Mubadala, GIC and others), seeding a future listing. A Reliance Retail IPO is widely expected to follow Jio's — the second great value-unlock in the SOTP story.

07

The consumer pivot: when the new half outgrew the old

The single most important structural shift in the company's modern history.

For most of its life, Reliance was energy — crude in, fuels and plastics out. The defining change of the last decade is that the consumer businesses (Jio + Retail) now generate more than half of the group's EBITDA and profit. This isn't a footnote; it is the reason the market increasingly values Reliance like a consumer-and-tech platform rather than an oil company — and it is the lens through which every result is now read.

Why this re-rates the stock

Different businesses earn different valuation multiples. A cyclical refiner trades cheaply (its profits are unpredictable and capital-hungry); a growing consumer-tech platform trades richly (recurring, scalable, defensible). As Jio and Retail come to dominate the profit mix, a larger share of Reliance's earnings deserves the higher multiple — which mechanically lifts the sum-of-the-parts value even if total profit grows only steadily. In other words, Reliance can re-rate not just by earning more, but by earning the same money in a more valuable form. The Jio (and eventually Retail) IPOs are the events that force the market to actually pay for that shift, rather than burying it inside an "oil company" multiple.

The honest tension: the pivot is real, but it isn't free. O2C still funds the group, so a deep refining/petchem downturn would still hurt — and the consumer arms are capital-hungry in their own way (5G, fibre, store roll-out, quick commerce). The pivot lowers cyclicality over time; it does not abolish it. Watch the consumer share of EBITDA each year — if it keeps climbing, the re-rating thesis is intact.
08

New energy & the AI bet: the third act

The most ambitious — and least proven — part of the whole story.

Reliance is building a third growth engine from scratch: clean energy and AI infrastructure. At Jamnagar, the Dhirubhai Ambani Green Energy Giga Complex (~5,000 acres) is being built to make solar modules and cells end-to-end (target ~20 GW/year), batteries (phase-1 ~40 GWh, scalable to ~120 GWh), plus electrolysers for green hydrogen. New energy is expected to start contributing revenue from FY27. On top, management has committed to a multi-year, ~$110-billion build of AI and data-centre capacity across India — including a flagship AI data centre at Jamnagar with partners such as Meta.

The integration logic: cheap captive green power is the input that makes giant AI data centres economic — so the energy build and the AI build are two halves of one bet.
Why this is brilliant — and risky — at the same time

The logic is elegant: AI data centres are colossal consumers of electricity, and Reliance can supply that power from its own solar-and-battery complex — so it turns clean energy from a cost centre into the fuel for a high-value AI business, all vertically integrated and located beside its existing Jamnagar infrastructure. If it works, Reliance owns the full stack from sunlight to compute. The risk is equally large: this is tens of billions of dollars of capex into businesses Reliance has never operated at scale, with uncertain returns and long payback, in fields (solar manufacturing, AI compute) that are intensely competitive and fast-moving. It is, in effect, a new "Jio-scale" gamble — and the market will spend years debating whether it is the next great compounding engine or an expensive distraction.

What to watch: first new-energy revenue from FY27, the pace and funding of the AI/data-centre capex, and whether free cash flow stays positive while these bets are built (see §09). Early proof points — module shipments, battery commissioning, data-centre go-lives — will matter more than the headline ambition.
09

Debt, capex and the cash question

A conglomerate's health lives or dies on whether cash covers the ambition.

With four businesses and two moonshots, the question that matters for Reliance isn't any single division — it's whether the group's cash flow can fund its enormous capex without the balance sheet getting stretched. The reassuring news from FY26: despite record capex of ~₹1,44,271 Cr ($15.2 bn), net debt stayed modest at ~₹1,24,717 Cr — comfortably below one year's EBITDA.

The deleveraging story, in one idea

Reliance has run this cycle before. The Jio build-out (2016–19) pushed net debt up sharply; the 2020 stake sales (~₹2.6 lakh Cr from Jio + Retail + a rights issue) then wiped it out, briefly making the company net-debt-free. The pattern repeats: spend heavily on the next engine, then deleverage by monetising it — via stake sales or IPOs. The Jio IPO (and a future Retail IPO) is the next deleveraging lever, expected to bring in fresh capital and unlock value precisely as the AI/new-energy capex peaks. The key tell of balance-sheet health is the ratio net debt ÷ EBITDA: at well under 1× even in a record-capex year, Reliance has room — but a much larger AI build, or a soft O2C year, would test it.

The bear watches this section closely: if capex outruns cash flow for several years and the IPOs slip or disappoint, net debt could climb and the "self-funding empire" narrative weakens. The bull points to the proven playbook — Reliance has always paired its biggest spends with its biggest monetisations.

How the market actually values Reliance

Analysts rarely value Reliance on a single P/E. Instead they run a sum-of-the-parts: O2C on a low EBITDA multiple, Jio on a high (telecom-tech) multiple, Retail on a premium consumer multiple, E&P on reserves, and new energy on an option value — then subtract net debt. At ~₹17.8 lakh Cr market cap, much of the headline value is the consumer parts; the O2C engine is valued relatively cheaply. That's why the Jio IPO is such a potential catalyst: a rich public price for Jio forces the market to mark RIL's stake up in the open, narrowing any conglomerate discount. The risk cuts both ways — if Jio (or Retail) lists below hopes, the SOTP that justifies today's price gets marked down.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely dominant business — priced for it to keep executing on three fronts at once.

Reliance is one of the most formidable enterprises in the world: a self-funding cash engine, the leader of two huge consumer markets, and a credible builder of a third (clean energy + AI). The debate is less about quality and more about capital intensity, execution across too many fronts, and how the parts get priced.

The bull case

  • Self-funding scale — a ₹2 lakh Cr+ EBITDA base that has repeatedly funded a new growth engine and then deleveraged via monetisation.
  • Consumer majority — Jio + Retail now >50% of EBITDA, earning a richer multiple and lowering cyclicality.
  • Value-unlock catalysts — the Jio IPO (and a likely Retail IPO) can crystallise sum-of-the-parts value in the open.
  • Optionality — new energy + a $110bn AI build give a credible third act, integrated with captive clean power.
  • Aligned, generational ownership — a ~50% family promoter with the next generation already running the growth arms.

The bear case

  • Capital intensity — record capex into AI + new energy could outrun cash flow and lift net debt if IPOs slip.
  • O2C cyclicality — global refining/petchem oversupply (China) and long-run fuel-demand risk can hit the cash core.
  • Execution sprawl — succeeding simultaneously in telecom, retail, refining, solar manufacturing and AI is a tall order.
  • Unproven moonshots — new energy and AI are huge bets in fields Reliance hasn't operated at scale; returns are uncertain.
  • Complexity & governance — conglomerate discount, related-party scrutiny, succession, and the sheer difficulty of valuing the whole.
The bottom line

Reliance is a structurally dominant, self-funding conglomerate mid-way through its boldest transformation yet — from an energy major into a consumer-tech-and-clean-energy platform. The business risk is low for the core and high for the moonshots; the valuation hinges on the sum-of-the-parts. The next chapters will be written in three numbers: the Jio IPO (does the crown jewel list richly?), the consumer share of EBITDA (does the pivot keep deepening?), and net debt vs capex (does the AI/new-energy bet stay self-funded?). Get those right and the re-rating compounds; stumble on capex or a soft O2C cycle, and a complex giant has a long way to be re-marked.
