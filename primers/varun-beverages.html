00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Varun Beverages (VBL) is the company that makes, bottles, sells and delivers Pepsi, Mountain Dew, 7Up, Mirinda, Sting, Tropicana Slice and Aquafina for PepsiCo across most of India and a widening slice of Africa. It does not own those brands — PepsiCo does. VBL is the franchise bottler: it buys concentrate from PepsiCo, turns it into finished drinks, and pushes them out to millions of shops. It is the largest PepsiCo bottler in the world outside the United States, handling roughly 90% of PepsiCo's beverage volume in India.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A volume compounder that just keeps scaling. Revenue grew from ~₹6,450 Cr (CY2020) to ~₹21,685 Cr (CY2025); profit rose from ~₹357 Cr to ~₹3,062 Cr over the same five years — a ~50% profit CAGR. It sold 1,213 million cases in CY2025 (+7.9%) at a ~23.3% EBITDA margin, and is essentially net-debt-free with a CRISIL AAA rating.
  • The mix is carbonated-first, but energy and premiumisation drive the profit. Carbonated soft drinks (Pepsi, Mountain Dew, Sting) are ~74% of volume, water (Aquafina) ~22%, and juices/dairy ~4%. The star is Sting, the ₹20 energy drink that has become a genuine phenomenon and pulls up the whole company's realisation and margin.
  • It's now two growth stories, not one. The mature India engine (~90% of Pepsi India volume, ~67% of revenue, ~26% margins) throws off cash; the newer Africa engine (Zimbabwe, Zambia, Morocco, DRC, South Africa via BevCo, plus the 2026 Twizza deal) is lower-margin today but growing fast and is where the acquisition firepower is going. The market pays ~54× earnings for the combination — a rich price that is the whole debate.
In plain terms

Think of PepsiCo as the recipe owner and Varun Beverages as the factory-and-delivery-truck owner. PepsiCo invents the drinks, funds the advertising and keeps the secret concentrate; VBL does the unglamorous, capital-heavy work of manufacturing, bottling, refrigerating and delivering — and gets exclusive rights to do it in defined territories. That's a lower-margin job than owning the brand, but it's a hard, sticky, scale-driven business: whoever has the most plants close to demand, the widest network of shops and the most fridges on the ground wins. VBL has spent 30 years quietly building exactly that, and is now copy-pasting the playbook across Africa.

01

The bottler business, decoded

No finance degree required — just the four levers that move a beverage business.

A soft-drink bottler is, financially, a volume-times-price business built on top of a fixed asset base (plants, lines, trucks, fridges). Almost everything about VBL reduces to four numbers, and if you understand these you understand the results.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and VBL's CY2025 number)
Cases (volume)The industry counts sales in "unit cases" — a standardised bundle of bottles/cans. VBL sold ~1,213 million cases in CY2025, up 7.9%. This is the single most-watched number.
Net realisation / caseAverage revenue earned per case after discounts and taxes — effectively the price mix. VBL's was ~₹178.8 in CY2025 (up ~0.5%). Rises when the mix shifts to premium (Sting, small cans) and falls when cheap water sells more.
EBITDA marginOperating profit as a share of revenue — how much of each rupee survives after making and selling the drink. VBL ran ~23.3% consolidated (India alone is nearer 26%).
Capex / capacityHow much it spends building plants and lines to make the cases. VBL spent ~₹4,500 Cr in CY2025 across new India and Africa plants — this is a capital-hungry business by design.
The simple revenue equation

Roughly: Revenue ≈ Cases × Realisation per case. So VBL grows in two ways — sell more cases (distribution reach, new territories, hotter summers, rising per-capita consumption) and lift realisation (more Sting and premium packs, fewer plain-water cases in the mix, price hikes). Profit then depends on keeping raw-material and logistics costs in check — which is exactly why VBL obsesses over building its own preforms, caps and boxes (see §06). Because plants and trucks are largely fixed costs, once a region's volumes cross break-even, extra cases are highly profitable — the same operating-leverage logic that makes Africa's ramp so important.

Why "cases" hide a lot of nuance

  • Not all cases are equal. A case of Sting or small-format cola earns far more per litre than a case of 1-litre Aquafina water. So volume growth and realisation can move in opposite directions — a big water summer can grow cases while realisation dips.
  • Concentrate is the hidden input. VBL buys the flavour concentrate from PepsiCo at a set price and adds sugar, water, CO₂, packaging and labour. The gap between what it sells the finished case for and what it costs to make is the bottler's margin — thinner than a brand owner's, but earned on enormous volume.
  • Refrigeration is destiny. A warm cola doesn't sell. VBL's ~1.1 million visi-coolers (branded fridges placed in shops) are a quiet but crucial asset — they secure shelf space and drive impulse purchases.
02

The PepsiCo franchise deal — who earns what

The single most important thing to understand: VBL is a franchisee, not the brand.

The entire investment case rests on one structural fact: Varun Beverages is a licensed franchise bottler of PepsiCo. It has exclusive rights to make and distribute PepsiCo's beverages in defined geographies, but PepsiCo owns the brands, sets the marketing and supplies the concentrate. Getting the economics of that relationship right is the whole game.

What a bottler actually earns vs the brand owner

A brand owner like PepsiCo earns a fat margin on a light asset — it sells concentrate and collects royalties without owning many factories. A bottler earns a thinner margin on a heavy asset — it sinks capital into plants, lines and trucks, and makes its money on throughput. The bottler's advantage is defensibility: you cannot serve a chilled fizzy drink to a village in Bihar over the internet. It takes local plants (so the product doesn't travel far and warm up), a delivery fleet, distributor relationships and fridges in every shop. That physical network, built over decades and exclusive by contract, is VBL's real moat — and it's why PepsiCo hands ever-larger territories to VBL rather than doing the job itself.

Why the franchise structure is powerful — and its catch

  • Exclusive territories. VBL holds franchise rights across 27 Indian states and 7 union territories, plus Nepal, Sri Lanka and a swathe of Africa. Within those, it is the only PepsiCo bottler — no internal competition.
  • PepsiCo funds the brand. The advertising, the celebrity endorsements, the innovation pipeline (Sting, new Tropicana variants) — those are PepsiCo's cost and risk, not VBL's. VBL rides a brand it doesn't have to build.
  • Aligned but dependent. The catch: VBL's fate is tied to PepsiCo's brand strength and to the terms of the franchise agreements. If PepsiCo's brands lost relevance, or renegotiated concentrate pricing hard, VBL would feel it directly. In practice the relationship has been deepening for 30 years — PepsiCo keeps expanding VBL's territory — but the dependence is real and permanent.
The consolidation VBL rode: PepsiCo has progressively handed its own directly-controlled Indian bottling regions to VBL (South & West India in 2019, and further territory since), plus international markets. Each handover instantly lifts VBL's volumes and lets it apply its efficiency playbook. This "acquire PepsiCo's own territories" pattern has been a repeatable growth lever — but it also means a big part of past growth was acquisitive, not purely organic.

The portfolio and the mix

Carbonated drinks are the base; water is the volume; energy is the profit kicker.

VBL sells three broad categories, and the balance between them explains its realisation and margin trends. Understanding the mix is understanding the P&L.

The three categories, and what each does for VBL

  • Carbonated soft drinks (CSD) — the profit base. Pepsi, Mountain Dew, 7Up, Mirinda and Sting. This is the highest-realisation, highest-margin category and the heart of the business. Within it, Mountain Dew is a giant in India and Sting is the fast-growing premium layer.
  • Packaged water (Aquafina) — the reach engine. Water is low-margin and dilutes realisation, but it drives footfall and frequency: it gets VBL's trucks into every shop daily, keeps the network busy, and is a cash-generative staple. Roughly a fifth of volume.
  • Juices & non-carbonated (Tropicana, Slice, Nimbooz, dairy) — the diversifier. Small today (~4%) but strategically useful: it broadens the portfolio into "healthier" perception and helps de-seasonalise (juices sell year-round more evenly than colas).
The premiumisation story hiding in the mix

A crucial trend: VBL keeps nudging the mix toward higher-value packs — small chilled cans, single-serve bottles, and especially Sting — which lifts realisation per case even when total volume growth is ordinary. It has also pushed hard into low-sugar / no-sugar variants, which now make up roughly 59% of consolidated volume. That matters for two reasons: it answers the health-and-regulation worry that hangs over all sugary-drink businesses, and (because sugar is a major raw-material cost) less sugar can actually help margins. Watch realisation per case each quarter: rising realisation on flat volume usually means the premium mix is working.

04

The Sting phenomenon

How a ₹20 energy drink became one of the most important products in the whole company.

If you want to understand VBL's margins over the last few years, look at one product: Sting, PepsiCo's energy drink. What Zudio is to Trent, Sting is to VBL — the disproportionate growth engine that changed the economics. It is now estimated to contribute around 15% of VBL's volumes, and far more of the incremental profit.

Why Sting worked when energy drinks were a niche

  • Price disruption. Global energy drinks (Red Bull, Monster) sold at ₹100+ in India — a treat for the affluent. Sting launched at roughly ₹20 for a 250ml bottle, collapsing the price point and opening the category to the mass market: students, drivers, gig-workers, small-town India.
  • Distribution already existed. VBL didn't need to build a route to market — it slotted Sting into the same trucks, fridges and 4-million-shop network that already carried Pepsi. Near-zero incremental distribution cost, instant national reach.
  • Higher realisation per case. Sting sells at a premium realisation versus mainstream cola, so every case that shifts toward Sting lifts the blended number — a mix-driven margin tailwind that doesn't require a single price hike on the base portfolio.
Why this is the textbook franchisee win

Sting is the perfect illustration of the bottler model's upside. PepsiCo took the brand and product risk — inventing and positioning an energy drink for India. VBL supplied the thing that actually made it a blockbuster: a distribution network so dense that a new SKU can reach millions of shops overnight at almost no extra cost. Because Sting's realisation and margin are richer than plain cola, and because it rides existing infrastructure, incremental Sting volume drops through to profit at an unusually high rate. It's the clearest evidence that VBL's real asset isn't any single drink — it's the reach that can monetise whatever PepsiCo invents next.

The maturing question: Sting grew explosively off a small base; as it gets big, its growth rate naturally cools and competitors (including other energy brands) crowd in. The next act is whether VBL can repeat the trick with the next premium innovation — new Sting variants, Gatorade, or reformulated low-sugar lines — to keep lifting realisation. A slowdown in the energy-drink mix is one of the subtler risks to the margin story.
05

The distribution machine

The real moat isn't the drink — it's the ability to get it cold into millions of shops, daily.

Ask an analyst what VBL actually owns that a rival can't buy, and the answer is its go-to-market network. This is the physical infrastructure that turns a franchise licence into a near-unassailable position — and the thing VBL invests in most relentlessly.

The scale here is the point. VBL reaches roughly 4 million retail outlets through more than 2,800 primary distributors and an owned fleet of about 12,000 delivery vehicles, and it has placed over 1.1 million visi-coolers to keep its drinks chilled at the point of sale. Management talks openly about pushing distribution far wider — deeper rural penetration is the multi-year organic growth lever.

Why density beats everything in beverages

Soft drinks are an impulse, low-ticket, high-frequency purchase. Nobody plans a Pepsi; they see a cold one in a fridge and buy it. That means the winner is whoever is physically present, cold, and available in the most shops at the moment of thirst. Building that presence requires plants near demand (so product is fresh and cheap to move), a fleet that visits shops often, distributor trust, and a fridge in the corner store. None of it can be leapfrogged with a clever app or a marketing budget — it has to be built, shop by shop, over years. This is why VBL's ever-expanding outlet count and cooler base are the metrics that quietly compound the business, and why widening the network into under-served rural India is the single largest domestic growth runway.

06

Backward integration and the capex machine

Owning the boring inputs — bottles, caps, boxes — is quietly how VBL protects its margin.

A bottler's margin is under constant pressure from raw-material costs: PET resin (for bottles), sugar, aluminium (for cans), and packaging. VBL's answer is backward integration — making these inputs itself rather than buying them — combined with a steady drumbeat of new-plant construction. This is the least glamorous part of the story and one of the most important.

What "backward integration" means here

  • PET preforms — the little test-tube-shaped blanks that get blown into bottles. Making them in-house cuts cost and insulates VBL from resin-price swings and supplier margins.
  • Crowns & closures — bottle caps, produced internally.
  • Corrugated boxes — the cartons that carry the cases, also made in-house.

By producing these itself, VBL captures the supplier's margin, reduces input-cost volatility, and gains supply-chain control. It's a big reason India-standalone EBITDA margins sit near 26% — high for a bottler.

Why VBL keeps 48+ plants — and keeps building

Chilled beverages are heavy, cheap and perishable in appeal — a bottle of water is mostly water, and shipping it far is uneconomic. So a bottler needs plants near where the drinks are consumed. Every new greenfield plant does three things at once: it adds capacity for volume growth, it lowers freight cost by shortening the distance to market, and it lets VBL enter under-served regions (like the North-East) properly. Combine that with making its own bottles, caps and boxes, and you get a business that can grow volumes while defending margins against cost inflation. The trade-off is that this is genuinely capital-intensive — ~₹4,500 Cr of capex a year — which is why the market watches whether returns on that capital (ROCE ~20%) hold up as the base gets bigger.

07

The Africa expansion

The second growth story — higher risk, currently lower margin, and where the M&A firepower is going.

For most of its life VBL was an India story. Over the past few years it has built a second engine across Africa and the Indian subcontinent, and this is now where the incremental growth and the acquisition activity are concentrated. International markets contribute roughly a third of revenue and are growing faster than India.

The margin and currency dynamic — read this carefully

Africa is a lower-margin business than India today, but with a clear path to improve. Newly-acquired operations often sell through modern-trade channels and buy inputs from third parties — both margin-dilutive. VBL's playbook is to apply its India recipe: shift the sales mix toward higher-margin general trade (small shops), build backward integration (make bottles, caps, cans locally) and add its own distribution. Management guides that international EBITDA margins should converge toward Indian levels over ~2–3 years as this plays out. The extra wrinkle is currency: revenues and profits earned in rand, kwacha or Moroccan dirham must be translated back to rupees, so African currency weakness can dent reported earnings even when local volumes grow. The bull sees a repeat of the India margin-expansion story on a fresh continent; the bear sees integration risk, FX volatility and richly-priced acquisitions.

Why Africa, and why now: Africa offers what India offered VBL two decades ago — low per-capita soft-drink consumption, a young population, and fragmented, under-invested bottling. PepsiCo needs a capable, well-capitalised partner to develop those markets, and VBL — flush with India cash flow and a AAA rating — is the natural acquirer. The strategy is essentially "buy under-managed PepsiCo bottlers, plug them into VBL's efficiency and backward-integration playbook, and re-rate their margins." It has worked before; the question is execution across many currencies and jurisdictions at once.
08

Seasonality and the snacks pivot

The weather is a shareholder — and the answer to it is diversification into food.

A crucial feature of VBL that catches newcomers off guard: it is highly seasonal, and calendar-reporting makes that obvious. Unlike most Indian companies (April–March fiscal years), VBL reports on a January–December calendar year, which lines up its financials with the beverage cycle — and puts the peak Indian summer squarely in the first half.

Why the calendar and the monsoon matter

  • Summer is everything. The bulk of Indian volume is sold in the hot months (roughly March–June). A strong, dry, hot summer is a bumper year; an early or heavy monsoon can dent volumes materially.
  • CY2025 was a live example. An unusually early monsoon hit the peak summer, subduing domestic volumes — yet VBL still grew full-year volume 7.9% and held realisation and margins, then bounced to +10.2% cases in Q4 and +16.3% in Q1 CY2026. Weather creates quarter-to-quarter noise; the trend line stays up.
  • The reporting quirk to remember. Because H1 carries the summer, VBL's June-half is far bigger than its December-half. Judge it year-on-year, not quarter-on-quarter.
Why VBL is moving into snacks — the de-seasonalisation play

Here's the strategic logic of the food pivot. Beverages are seasonal and weather-dependent; snacks (chips, Kurkure-style puffs) sell evenly all year and through the same shops, trucks and distributors. So by adding PepsiCo snacks, VBL can (1) smooth its seasonality, (2) sweat its distribution network harder — more products down the same pipe means more revenue per truck-visit — and (3) diversify beyond fizzy drinks into a category with fewer health-regulation clouds. VBL already co-manufactures Kurkure Puffcorn for PepsiCo in India, distributes Lay's/Doritos/Cheetos in Morocco, and runs snack plants in Zimbabwe. The snacks business is small today (~₹340 Cr) but it's a structurally clever adjacency: same logistics, complementary seasonality, additional PepsiCo brands.

The trucks that carry cold drinks in summer can carry chips in winter — that's the whole idea behind the snacks foray.
09

The company and the Jaipuria family

A three-decade franchise relationship, run by one of India's great beverage families.

VBL is the flagship of the Jaipuria family's RJ Corp — a business empire spanning beverages (VBL), quick-service restaurants (KFC, Pizza Hut, Costa Coffee via Devyani International), dairy (Cream Bell) and healthcare. Patriarch Ravi Kant Jaipuria has spent over three decades building PepsiCo bottling in South Asia and Africa.

How to think about the ~54× multiple

At ~₹1.72 lakh crore market cap on ~₹3,062 Cr of CY2025 profit (EPS ~₹8.98), VBL trades around 54× trailing earnings — a premium even by Indian consumer-staples standards. That price is paying for a specific belief: that VBL can keep compounding volumes at high-single/low-double digits in India, expand distribution into rural white space, lift realisation via Sting and premiumisation, and turn Africa into a second India-style margin-expansion engine — for many years. Bulls argue a debt-free, AAA-rated, ~20% ROCE franchise with a 30-year PepsiCo relationship and a proven acquisition playbook deserves scarcity pricing. Bears argue the multiple leaves no room for error: a weak-summer year, an Africa integration stumble, currency drag, sugar-tax/health regulation, or simply the law of large numbers slowing growth — and a 54× stock can de-rate hard. Both can be right at different points; watch volume growth and international margins each quarter.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality franchise — priced for it to keep executing on two continents.

VBL is one of the cleaner compounding stories in Indian consumption: a dominant, debt-free PepsiCo bottler with a widening distribution moat, a premiumisation tailwind and a fresh African growth leg. As with the best businesses, the debate is about price and execution, not quality.

The bull case

  • Distribution moat — ~4M outlets, 1.1M coolers, 12,000 trucks; near-impossible to replicate, and still widening into rural India.
  • Premiumisation tailwind — Sting, small packs and low-sugar variants lifting realisation and margin.
  • Africa optionality — a second India-style growth-and-margin engine, funded by a AAA, net-cash balance sheet.
  • Backward integration — in-house bottles, caps, boxes protecting margins against input inflation.
  • Aligned promoter & deepening PepsiCo tie — 30-year relationship; PepsiCo keeps handing VBL more territory.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~54× earnings leaves little margin for error; vulnerable to de-rating on any disappointment.
  • Franchise dependence — VBL doesn't own the brands; it's tied to PepsiCo's relevance and concentrate terms.
  • Weather & seasonality — an early monsoon or weak summer can dent a whole year's volumes.
  • Africa & FX risk — integration across many currencies/jurisdictions; rand/kwacha weakness can hit reported earnings.
  • Regulation & health — sugar taxes and anti-sugary-drink sentiment are a permanent overhang on the core category.
The bottom line

Varun Beverages is a structurally advantaged, high-quality franchise — the physical backbone that monetises PepsiCo's brands across India and Africa, with a distribution moat that compounds quietly and a balance sheet that lets it keep acquiring. The business risk is modest; the valuation risk is real. The story over the next few years will be written in two numbers — India volume growth (does the distribution engine keep widening?) and international margins (does Africa converge toward Indian profitability?). Get those right and the compounding justifies the premium; stumble on either, and a 54× multiple has a long way to fall.
