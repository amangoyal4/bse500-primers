00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Mphasis is a mid-sized Indian IT-services company — it writes software, runs applications, modernises technology and processes back-office work for large enterprises, mostly in the United States. What makes it different from every other IT name in the index is how concentrated it is in one industry: banks, financial firms and insurers (BFSI), which make up roughly two-thirds of revenue. Mphasis is, in effect, a leveraged play on how much global — especially American — banks and mortgage lenders spend on technology.

Four facts frame everything:

  • A BFSI specialist, not a generalist. Where TCS, Infosys or LTIMindtree spread across banking, retail, manufacturing, healthcare and telecom, Mphasis pours ~62% of revenue into banking & financial services and another ~8% into insurance (~67% BFSI combined). That deep domain focus is its moat — and its single biggest risk.
  • Private-equity giant Blackstone controls it. The world's largest alternative-asset manager bought control of Mphasis from Hewlett-Packard Enterprise in 2016 and has been the promoter ever since. That ownership reshaped the whole strategy — pivoting the business from serving HP/DXC as a captive vendor to winning direct enterprise clients. In FY26 Blackstone began selling down, cutting its stake to ~30.6%.
  • The number that matters is the deal pipeline. Analysts watch "net-new TCV" — the total contract value of fresh deals won — because it predicts future revenue. FY26 was a record: ~$2.1 billion of new-deal TCV, up 68%, of which about 60% was "AI-led". After a two-year growth stall (US banks froze tech budgets during the rate-hike cycle), that deal momentum is the recovery signal bulls point to.
  • Solid but not spectacular financials. FY26 revenue was ~$1.79 bn / ₹15,880 Cr, EBIT margin ~15.3%, net profit ~₹1,863 Cr, and it pays a chunky dividend (₹62/share, ~2.7% yield). It trades at ~23× earnings — a discount to pricier mid-cap peers like Persistent and Coforge, a premium to the sector average.
In plain terms

Think of Mphasis as a highly specialised outsourced engineering-and-operations team for banks. When a large US bank or mortgage company needs to build a new digital lending platform, modernise a decades-old core system, or run the machinery behind millions of loan applications, it hires firms like Mphasis to do it — cheaper and faster than doing it in-house. Because it went so deep into one customer industry, Mphasis knows banking technology better than a generalist — but it also rises and falls with the fortunes of that industry. When banks are flush and interest rates are supportive, Mphasis grows nicely; when banks freeze budgets or the US mortgage market seizes up, Mphasis feels it fast and hard. And sitting on top of it all is Blackstone, the PE owner that bought it, reshaped it, and is now gradually cashing out.

01

IT services 101: how a company like Mphasis earns

No engineering degree required — just how the money actually flows.

An IT-services company sells, at bottom, skilled people and the software they build and run. A client (say a bank) has a technology job to do — build an app, migrate to the cloud, process loan documents — and instead of hiring and managing a large in-house team, it pays Mphasis to deliver the outcome. Mphasis earns the difference between what it charges the client and what it costs to employ and deploy its engineers. A few core concepts explain the whole economic engine.

The vocabulary you actually need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for Mphasis)
Headcount & utilisationThe number of billable employees, and the share of their time that's actually charged to clients. Higher utilisation = more revenue on the same payroll. Mphasis runs ~31,000+ employees. Utilisation is a key margin lever.
Billing rateWhat Mphasis charges per hour/day of an engineer's time. Higher-value work (digital transformation, AI, complex banking cores) commands premium rates vs. commodity coding.
AttritionThe rate at which employees quit. High attrition means constant re-hiring and training cost, and risk of losing client knowledge. It spiked industry-wide in 2021–22 and has since cooled.
TCV (Total Contract Value)The full value of a deal over its life. "Net-new" TCV — fresh wins, not renewals — is the leading indicator of future revenue. Mphasis won ~$2.1 bn of new TCV in FY26.
EBIT marginOperating profit as a share of revenue. For Mphasis this runs ~15% — driven by utilisation, billing rates, wage inflation, the pyramid of junior-to-senior staff, and the currency.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Revenue ≈ Billable people × Utilisation × Billing rate, and Profit ≈ Revenue − (wages + delivery cost). So an IT-services firm grows in three ways — win more work (which is why TCV matters), charge more per hour (move up to higher-value services), and run staff more efficiently (utilisation, automation, the "pyramid"). The permanent tension is wages vs. billing rates: salaries rise every year, so the firm must keep lifting productivity and mix to protect margins. This is exactly why the AI question (Section 07) is existential — AI is the biggest change to that productivity equation in a generation.

Why "domain expertise" is the real product

A generalist coder can write software for any industry. But a bank doesn't just want code — it wants a vendor who understands mortgage-origination workflows, regulatory capital rules, payments rails and core-banking systems before the project starts. That domain knowledge lets Mphasis charge more, deliver faster, and — crucially — become sticky: once it's embedded in a bank's mortgage platform, ripping it out is costly and risky. Mphasis's entire strategy is to be the deepest BFSI specialist in the mid-cap pack, and to mine each large bank account for more and more work over time.

02

The BFSI concentration — the defining feature

Two-thirds of the business is banking. This one fact explains most of the story.

Every IT-services firm has some banking exposure — it's the biggest tech-spending industry on earth. But Mphasis has taken concentration to an extreme: Banking & Financial Services is ~54% of revenue, and Insurance adds ~8%, so BFSI together is roughly 62–67% of the company. No other listed Indian IT name of comparable size is this levered to one vertical. This is a deliberate strategy — and understanding its double-edge is the master key to Mphasis.

The concentration double-edge

The upside: deep specialisation is a genuine competitive advantage. Mphasis has decades of banking and (especially) mortgage domain knowledge, marquee US financial-services logos, and proprietary tools built specifically for lending and payments. That earns premium pricing, high client stickiness, and referrals — a generalist can't easily muscle in. The downside: when your revenue is two-thirds one industry, you inherit that industry's cycle. If US banks tighten technology budgets — as they did sharply during the 2022–24 interest-rate shock — Mphasis has fewer other verticals to cushion the blow. Diversified peers can lean on retail, manufacturing or healthcare when banking wobbles; Mphasis largely can't. That is why Mphasis is structurally more cyclical than its diversified peers — it's a feature, not a bug, and it cuts both ways.

The mechanism to remember: BFSI concentration turns Mphasis into a high-beta bet on global financial-services tech spending. In a banking up-cycle it can outgrow diversified peers; in a banking down-cycle it can underperform them. Everything else in this primer — the mortgage sensitivity, the growth stall, the recovery — flows from this single design choice.
03

The company, and how Blackstone came to own it

From a 1990s Indian software firm, to an HP captive, to a Blackstone portfolio company.

Mphasis has an unusual corporate history for an Indian IT company: it has been passed between very different owners, and each owner reshaped it. Understanding that lineage explains both its BFSI focus and its current strategy.

Timeline

  • 1992–2000
    Founded and built as an Indian IT-services and BPO firm; Mphasis emerges from the merger of Mphasis Corporation and BFL Software.
  • 2006
    EDS (Electronic Data Systems) acquires a controlling stake — anchoring Mphasis as a captive delivery arm for a global IT-outsourcing giant, and deepening its banking/BPO roots.
  • 2008
    Hewlett-Packard buys EDS for ~$13.9 bn — and with it, control of Mphasis. Mphasis becomes an HP captive, doing large volumes of work for HP (the "HP/DXC channel").
  • Sep 2016
    Blackstone buys control from Hewlett-Packard Enterprise for ~$1.1 bn — then India's largest PE buyout. HP had spun off the enterprise-services arm that became DXC Technology; the legacy "HP/DXC channel" begins its long wind-down.
  • 2016–2020
    Under Blackstone, Mphasis pivots hard to the "Direct" business — winning its own enterprise clients rather than depending on the HP/DXC channel. Blackstone-portfolio-company synergies (selling to Blackstone's own investee firms) add a new demand source.
  • Apr 2021
    Blackstone (via a new fund, with ADIA, UC Investments and others co-investing) commits up to $2.8 bn to buy control again — effectively rolling its stake into a fresh vehicle, BCP Topco IX, at the top of the post-COVID IT boom.
  • FY22–FY24
    Post-boom hangover: US banks and mortgage lenders slash discretionary tech spend as rates spike; Mphasis's revenue declines ~3.8% in INR in FY24 — a multi-year growth stall.
  • FY26
    Recovery: record $2.1 bn new-deal TCV, AI-led repositioning (NeoIP, Tria platforms). Blackstone begins selling down its stake via large block deals, cutting it to ~30.6%.

Ownership & management today

  • Promoter: Blackstone, through BCP Topco IX Pte. Ltd., holds ~30.55% (Jun 2026) — down from ~55% at the 2021 buyout and ~40% entering FY26, after two large block sales during the year (~15% and ~9.5%). It remains the controlling promoter and has confirmed no encumbrance on its shares.
  • Co-investors: sovereign and institutional names (ADIA, UC Investments and others) came in alongside Blackstone in the 2021 deal — a pattern typical of large PE consortia.
  • Balance sheet: essentially net-cash / lightly levered, healthy ROE (~18–19%) and ROCE (~22%), and a generous dividend policy — a mature, cash-generative services business rather than a capital-hungry one.
Why "Blackstone owns it" actually matters

A PE owner is not a passive shareholder. Blackstone's control shaped three things. (1) Strategy: it drove the shift from being an HP captive to a direct-selling enterprise services firm — the "Direct" business is now essentially the whole company. (2) Demand: Blackstone's vast portfolio of investee companies is itself a source of IT work and warm introductions. (3) The exit clock: PE firms buy to eventually sell. Blackstone's FY26 stake sales are the start of that monetisation — which is good (it broadens the free float and adds index eligibility) but also creates a supply overhang: the market knows more stock is likely to come. We return to this in Section 10.

04

The "Direct" pivot — what the company actually is now

The story of Mphasis is the story of one channel dying and another taking over.

For years Mphasis was two businesses stapled together: a "Direct" business (its own enterprise clients) and a "HP/DXC channel" (work funnelled through its former parent). Under Blackstone, the strategy was explicit — grow Direct, let the legacy channel run off. That transition is now essentially complete: Direct is ~98–99% of revenue. Understanding this explains why Mphasis's headline growth looked weak for years even when its core was growing.

Because the shrinking HP/DXC channel dragged on total revenue for years, "Direct" growth was consistently faster than the reported number — a headwind that has now largely cleared.

Account mining vs. new logos — two ways to grow

  • Account mining — selling more to existing clients. Cheaper, lower-risk, higher-margin: Mphasis is already trusted and embedded, so expanding within a top-20 bank is the path of least resistance. Most services growth comes this way.
  • New logos — winning brand-new clients. Slower and costlier to land, but essential to avoid over-dependence on a few accounts. New-logo wins are a closely watched health signal.
  • Client concentration risk — the flip side of account mining: a services firm can become uncomfortably dependent on its largest one or two clients. If a top bank cuts back, it hits fast — a real risk given Mphasis's BFSI tilt.
Why the "Direct" completion is quietly bullish

For most of the last decade, every rupee of Direct growth was partly cancelled out by the melting HP/DXC channel — so Mphasis's reported growth understated its true underlying momentum. Now that the legacy drag has essentially gone, reported growth and core growth finally converge. That's why FY26's headline numbers (revenue +6.7% USD, record TCV) look cleaner than the muddy comparisons of earlier years — the company you see today is the Direct business, with no hidden anchor pulling it down.

05

The mortgage cycle — Mphasis's hidden macro engine

The structural force unique to a BFSI-heavy, US-facing services firm — and the one that stalled its growth.

Here is the fact that separates Mphasis from every other Indian IT name: a meaningful chunk of its BFSI business is tied to US mortgage lending — the origination, processing and servicing of home loans. Mortgage volumes are violently sensitive to interest rates. When rates are low, Americans refinance and buy homes in huge numbers, and lenders drown in loan-processing work they outsource to firms like Mphasis. When rates spike, mortgage origination collapses — and so does the tech-and-process spending that rides on it. This is the demand-side equivalent of a commodity company's price cycle, and Mphasis sits right in it.

The mechanism, plainly

Mortgage-linked work is volume-driven and discretionary. When the US Federal Reserve raised rates aggressively in 2022–23, refinancing dried up almost overnight, home sales slowed, and lenders shed the very processing volumes Mphasis was paid to handle. At the same time, nervous banks froze broader "discretionary" tech projects (new builds, transformations) to protect their own margins — hitting the discretionary side of Mphasis's book too. The result was FY24's outright revenue decline. The same mechanism runs in reverse: as rates ease and banking confidence returns, mortgage volumes and discretionary projects both come back — which is exactly the recovery Mphasis has been reporting. So watching US interest rates and mortgage-origination trends is genuinely part of watching Mphasis.

The honest tension: Mphasis's mortgage and banking domain depth is a real, hard-to-replicate asset — but it comes bundled with genuine macro sensitivity. A diversified peer can shrug off a US banking air-pocket; Mphasis can't fully. Bulls argue the domain depth wins share through the cycle and that rate-easing is a multi-year tailwind; bears argue the cyclicality caps the multiple and that any fresh US banking stress hits Mphasis first and hardest. Watch BFSI growth, mortgage commentary and US rate direction together.

TCV and the deal engine — the number analysts live by

Revenue tells you the past; TCV tells you the future.

Because a services firm's revenue is delivered after deals are signed, the single most forward-looking metric is new-deal TCV (Total Contract Value) — the aggregate value of fresh contracts won in a period. It's the order book that will convert into revenue over the coming quarters and years. FY26 was Mphasis's strongest deal year in memory, and it's the core evidence for the recovery thesis.

Why TCV can mislead if you read it naively

TCV is powerful but slippery. A big number is only as good as (a) how fast it converts to revenue (long deals drip in slowly), (b) the margin in the deal (a mega-deal won on price can dilute profitability), and (c) whether it's genuinely new work or a re-badged renewal. Mphasis's FY26 TCV surge is encouraging precisely because it's net-new and pipeline-backed — but the market's "show-me" stance reflects a fair question: does the record book convert into the double-digit revenue growth it implies? The gap between "deals won" and "revenue delivered" is where the next few quarters' debate lives.

07

The AI question — threat, opportunity, or both

The single biggest uncertainty hanging over every IT-services company on earth.

Generative and "agentic" AI is the most important force in IT services since offshoring itself — and it cuts two ways at once. The threat: if AI can write, test and maintain code, a big slice of the labour-arbitrage model (billing armies of engineers by the hour) could deflate — clients may need fewer people for the same output, pressuring revenue and pricing. The opportunity: enterprises need help adopting AI — building it into their systems, governing it, rewiring legacy cores — and that transformation work is exactly what firms like Mphasis sell. Whether AI is net-positive or net-negative for Mphasis is the defining question of the next five years.

How Mphasis is positioning

  • AI-led deals dominate the pipeline. Mphasis reports that ~60% of FY26's new-deal TCV and ~69% of its pipeline is "AI-led" — i.e. clients are buying AI transformation, not resisting it. That's the single most reassuring data point on the opportunity side.
  • Proprietary platforms. It has launched enterprise-AI platforms — NeoIP (a unified AI platform spanning modernisation, app development and IT operations) and Tria (an "enterprise agency" / agentic-AI platform) — plus its Mphasis.ai unit. The aim: sell outcomes and platforms, not just hours, so AI-driven productivity accrues partly to Mphasis rather than only to the client.
  • Efficiency claims. Early engagements are cited as delivering up to ~60% improvement in development/modernisation efficiency via AI-led code generation and testing — the double-edged proof that AI genuinely compresses effort.
  • The margin management challenge. As AI compresses the hours in a project, Mphasis must protect margins through utilisation discipline, higher-value mix, and platform/outcome pricing rather than pure time-and-materials.
The honest read on AI

The bull framing: AI is a demand accelerant. Every bank now has to modernise decades-old "legacy cores" to make them AI-ready, govern AI risk, and re-engineer workflows — a wave of transformation spending that a deep BFSI specialist is well placed to capture, as the AI-led pipeline suggests. The bear framing: AI is a deflationary force on the core labour-arbitrage model — if a project that once took 100 engineers now takes 40, revenue per project falls unless volume rises enough to offset it, and clients may capture most of the savings. The realistic answer is probably both, in sequence: a multi-year build-out boom (good for revenue) followed by a longer-term efficiency squeeze on the commodity end (pressure on pricing). Mphasis's platform bet (NeoIP, Tria) is an attempt to be on the right side of that shift rather than a victim of it — but it is a bet, not a settled outcome.

08

The industry and the rivals

Where a BFSI-focused mid-cap sits in the Indian IT pecking order.

Indian IT is a global export machine — hundreds of billions of dollars of software and back-office work delivered from India for clients in the US and Europe. It splits into the large-caps (TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCLTech — huge, diversified), and a fast-growing pack of mid-caps (Mphasis, Persistent, Coforge, LTIMindtree, Hexaware) that often grow faster and trade at richer multiples. Mphasis's distinctive place is as the BFSI-concentrated specialist of that mid-cap cohort.

The strategic question for the mid-caps

The mid-cap IT names have out-grown the large-caps for years by being nimble, specialised and willing to win share deal-by-deal. But that premium rests on sustained above-average growth — the moment growth converges with the large-caps, the multiple premium is at risk. For Mphasis specifically, the debate is whether its BFSI depth is a durable edge (winning ever more banking work as budgets recover and AI transformation ramps) or a cyclical liability (leaving it over-exposed when banking rolls over). Its FY26 deal wins argue for the former; its FY24 stall is the reminder of the latter.

The structural backdrop: Indian IT faces three cross-currents at once — AI (reshaping delivery economics), US visa & onshore-cost politics (periodic pressure on the offshore model), and the global rate/macro cycle (client budgets). A BFSI-heavy, US-facing firm like Mphasis is exposed to all three, which is part of why the sector re-rates and de-rates in sharp cycles.

Financials & valuation

Steady, cash-generative, well-paid — priced at a discount to the flashier mid-caps.

Mphasis's financials are those of a mature, profitable, cash-returning services business: high-teens EBIT margins, strong cash conversion, a light balance sheet and a generous dividend. It is not a hyper-growth story; it is a quality-cyclical recovery story. The valuation debate is whether the recovery justifies re-rating it toward its pricier peers, or whether the cyclicality keeps it at a discount.

How to think about the ~23× multiple

At roughly ₹43,800 Cr market cap on ~₹1,863 Cr of FY26 profit, Mphasis trades near 23× trailing earnings. That's a premium to the broad market but a discount to the flashier mid-cap peers — Persistent, Coforge and Info Edge all carry much richer multiples (often 30–45×). The bull reads that as value: a recovering, cash-returning BFSI specialist at a reasonable price, with a big AI-led order book and rate-cut tailwinds. The bear reads the discount as deserved: higher cyclicality, single-vertical concentration, an AI threat to the labour model, and a Blackstone stake overhang all justify paying less than for a more diversified, faster-growing peer. Brokers have flagged a "show-me" phase — the multiple re-rates only if the record TCV actually converts into sustained double-digit growth.

10

The Blackstone overhang

A PE owner selling down is both a technical headwind and a structural positive — here's the nuance.

Private-equity firms buy companies to eventually sell them, and Blackstone's clock on Mphasis has been ticking since the 2016/2021 buyouts. In FY26 that monetisation began in earnest: Blackstone (via BCP Topco IX) sold two large blocks — around 15% in one tranche and about 9.5% in another — cutting its stake from ~40% to ~30.6%. This "promoter selldown" is a genuine, ongoing factor in the stock.

The mechanism to watch

Overhangs are a timing-and-flow phenomenon, not a business-quality one. The fundamental company is unchanged whether Blackstone owns 40% or 20%; what changes is the supply of shares the market must absorb and the uncertainty about when the next block lands. In practice these situations often follow a pattern: choppy while the selling is in progress (each block a mini-headwind), then a cleaner setup once the stake is low enough that further sales are immaterial. For Mphasis, the key questions are the pace of Blackstone's remaining selldown and who — if anyone — steps in as a long-term anchor. Until that resolves, the overhang is a real, if temporary, weight on sentiment layered on top of the operating story.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A quality BFSI specialist in recovery — with real cyclicality and a PE seller on the register.

Mphasis enters FY27 as a recovering, deeply BFSI-focused mid-cap with a record order book, an AI-led repositioning, a healthy balance sheet and a generous dividend — but also with genuine cyclicality, single-vertical concentration, an unresolved AI question, and a controlling PE shareholder in selldown mode. The investment debate is unusually balanced.

The bull case

  • BFSI domain moat — deepest banking/mortgage expertise in the mid-cap pack; sticky, premium-priced, referral-generating work.
  • Record deal engine — ~$2.1 bn new-deal TCV (+68%), ~60% AI-led, +38% pipeline — the leading indicators point up.
  • Rate-cut tailwind — easing US rates could re-open mortgage volumes and unfreeze bank tech budgets, hitting Mphasis's exact sweet spot.
  • Reasonable valuation — ~23× earnings, a clear discount to Persistent/Coforge, with a ~2.7% dividend yield while you wait.
  • Clean Direct business — the legacy HP/DXC drag is essentially gone; reported and core growth now converge.

The bear case

  • Cyclical concentration — ~67% BFSI and heavy US-mortgage exposure make it high-beta to banking budgets and rates; FY24 shrank.
  • AI threat — GenAI could deflate the labour-arbitrage core faster than platform/outcome revenue replaces it.
  • Blackstone overhang — further stake sales likely; block deals pressure the stock and cap sentiment until resolved.
  • "Show-me" conversion risk — record TCV must actually turn into sustained double-digit revenue growth; brokers are cautious.
  • Client & vertical concentration — few large banking accounts; a top-client pullback or a US banking air-pocket bites quickly.
The bottom line

Mphasis is a high-quality, cash-generative BFSI-services specialist caught at an interesting inflection: a genuine recovery in its order book, an AI repositioning that could go either way, and a PE owner heading for the exit. It is more cyclical than its diversified peers by design, which is why it trades cheaper — the discount is partly earned, partly opportunity. The story over the coming quarters will be written in a handful of numbers: BFSI/revenue growth (is the recovery real?), TCV conversion (does the record book become revenue?), AI-led mix and margins (is AI a tailwind or a margin drag?), and the pace of Blackstone's selldown (is the overhang clearing?). Get those right and a ~23× cyclical re-rates toward its peers; get them wrong and the same concentration that is its moat becomes its cage.
