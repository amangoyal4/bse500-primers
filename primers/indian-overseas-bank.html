00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Indian Overseas Bank is a Chennai-headquartered, government-majority public-sector bank (PSB) — a mid-tier lender with a strong South-India franchise and a historical overseas / NRI heritage baked into its very name. It takes deposits from savers, lends to households and businesses, and earns the spread. What makes IOB genuinely distinctive isn't its size (it is mid-pack among India's dozen public-sector banks) but its story and its structure.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A textbook turnaround. IOB spent six years (2015–2021) under the RBI's Prompt Corrective Action (PCA) framework — regulatory "intensive care" for a bank whose bad loans and losses had spiralled out of control (gross bad loans peaked near 25% of the book; it lost ~₹6,300 Cr in FY18). It exited PCA in September 2021 and has since strung together record profits — FY26 net profit of ₹5,208 Cr, up 56% — with gross NPAs now down to 1.42% and a near-total 97.5% provision coverage.
  • Almost no free float. The Government of India (through the President of India) owns roughly 92.4% of IOB as of March 2026 — one of the highest state stakes among listed PSBs. That leaves barely ~7–8% in public hands, and SEBI rules require at least 25% public shareholding. IOB must dilute the government's stake — a structural supply overhang that hangs over the stock.
  • Priced like a re-rating, not a value trap. Despite being a PSU bank, IOB trades at roughly 2× book value (P/B) and ~11× earnings on a ~₹68,000 Cr market cap — a rich multiple for the sector, reflecting both the clean-up and the scarcity of stock. The debate is whether the quality now justifies the price, or whether the coming dilution and a normalising credit cycle pull it back to earth.
In plain terms

Think of IOB as a state-owned bank that nearly went off a cliff, was rescued by the government, and has clawed its way back to health. The government pumped in over ₹24,000 Cr of capital to keep it alive, cleaned out the bad loans, and now owns almost the entire company. The bank today is profitable, well-provisioned and growing loans fast — but it carries two peculiar burdens: it must eventually sell a chunk of government stock into the market (which can cap the price), and after a huge share-price run it is no longer cheap. The whole investment question is: turnaround quality vs. dilution overhang vs. a full valuation.

01

How a bank earns: the only mechanics you need

No finance degree required — just the handful of levers that drive every bank.

A bank is, at heart, a spread business built on trust and leverage. It borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors), lends it out at a higher rate, and pockets the difference — while holding only a thin sliver of its own capital against a very large balance sheet. Five ideas explain almost everything IOB does.

The banking vocabulary, decoded (with IOB's FY26 number)

The simple profit equation

Roughly: Bank profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) + fees − operating costs − loan losses (provisions) − tax. The single biggest swing factor is that last-but-one term. A bank can have a great spread and still collapse if too many loans go bad — because it is running huge leverage (₹100 of loans on maybe ₹8–10 of its own capital). That's why asset quality — how many loans go sour — matters more than almost anything else, and why the whole IOB story is really an asset-quality story. Get the lending discipline wrong, as IOB did in 2010–2015, and the losses can wipe out years of profit and much of the capital.

Why "CASA" is the holy grail

Not all deposits are equal. CASA — Current Accounts and Savings Accounts — are the sticky, low-cost deposits people leave in everyday accounts, paying little or no interest. Term deposits (fixed deposits) cost the bank much more. A high CASA ratio means cheaper funding, a wider margin and a more resilient franchise. IOB's domestic CASA ratio of ~41.5% is among the best in the public-sector pack — a genuine competitive asset and a legacy of its deep South-India retail branch network.

02

The company and who controls it

A 1937 Chennai institution, nationalised in 1969, majority-owned by the state.

Indian Overseas Bank was founded in Chennai (then Madras) on 10 February 1937 by M. Ct. M. Chidambaram Chettiar, a businessman of the Nattukottai Chettiar community, with a deliberate international ambition — the bank opened branches in Rangoon, Penang and Singapore essentially on day one, to serve the trade and remittance needs of the Tamil merchant diaspora across South-East Asia. That founding mission is why "Overseas" sits in the name.

Ownership — the defining structural fact

  • The Government of India — acting through the President of India — is the promoter and holds ~92.4% of equity (as of 31 March 2026), down from ~96.4% in late 2024 after two rounds of share sales. This is one of the highest state stakes and lowest free-floats among listed public-sector banks.
  • Public shareholders — retail investors, mutual funds, insurers and FIIs — hold the remaining ~7.6%. That tiny float is central to both the stock's volatility and its coming dilution (see §08).
  • Governance — as a nationalised bank, IOB is run by a government-appointed MD & CEO and board, and answers to the RBI as regulator and the Ministry of Finance as majority owner. Leadership continuity and the government's recapitalisation support have been central to the turnaround.
Why "the government owns 92%" is a double-edged sword

On the upside, a majority government owner is the ultimate backstop: when IOB was bleeding, the state injected over ₹24,000 Cr of capital to keep it solvent and Basel-compliant — no private bank gets that. Depositors treat PSBs as effectively sovereign-guaranteed, which keeps funding cheap and sticky. On the downside, the same ownership brings political/policy influence over lending and appointments, historically weaker underwriting discipline, and — crucially here — a legal obligation to eventually sell stock down to 25% public float, creating a known future supply of shares that weighs on the price. The government is both IOB's saviour and, structurally, an overhang.

03

The "Overseas" heritage — small today, symbolic always

Why a mid-tier PSB carries an international name — and what's left of it.

Unlike most public-sector banks, IOB was built for cross-border banking from birth. It was the first Indian bank to open in Singapore (1941), followed by branches in Hong Kong (1955), Colombo and Bangkok. For decades its foreign-exchange and NRI-remittance expertise was a real differentiator, serving the Tamil and South-Indian diaspora across South-East Asia and Sri Lanka.

What the overseas footprint is now

  • Four foreign branches today — Singapore, Hong Kong, Bangkok and Colombo — plus a remittance centre at Serangoon, Singapore. These handle trade finance, remittances and correspondent banking, but are a small fraction of the balance sheet; IOB is overwhelmingly a domestic bank now.
  • An NRI / remittance franchise — the historical diaspora relationships still feed NRI deposits and inward remittances, a modest but sticky, low-cost funding source that complements the domestic CASA base.
  • A South-India retail spine — the enduring legacy is not the foreign branches but the dense branch network across Tamil Nadu and the South, which is the real engine of IOB's strong CASA and its deposit franchise.
Read the name correctly: "Overseas" is heritage and brand, not a large earnings driver today. An analyst treats IOB as a domestic South-India PSU bank with a legacy international footprint — the international operations are a rounding error on profits, but the remittance/NRI relationships and the trade-finance expertise remain a genuine, if minor, differentiator versus a purely domestic peer.
04

The near-death experience: PCA, decoded

The six years in regulatory intensive care that define the entire IOB story.

You cannot understand IOB without understanding what happened between roughly 2011 and 2021. Aggressive corporate and infrastructure lending in the boom years soured en masse; when the RBI forced honest recognition of bad loans (the 2015–16 Asset Quality Review), IOB's true condition was exposed — and it was dire. In October 2015 the RBI placed IOB under Prompt Corrective Action (PCA), and it stayed there for nearly six years.

What is PCA? (Prompt Corrective Action)

PCA is the RBI's early-intervention regime for weak banks — a kind of regulatory ICU. When a bank breaches thresholds on three vital signs — bad loans (Net NPA), profitability (negative Return on Assets), and capital adequacy — the RBI switches on PCA and imposes restrictions: curbs on fresh lending (especially risky corporate loans), limits on branch expansion, dividend bans, and tighter oversight. The point is to stop the bleeding and force the bank to shrink risk and rebuild capital before it fails. It protects depositors, but it also freezes growth — a bank in PCA essentially stops competing while it heals. IOB was one of a cluster of PSBs put under PCA in that era; getting out became the whole management objective.

How bad did it get?

  • Gross NPAs peaked around 25% of the loan book — meaning roughly one rupee in four that IOB had lent was not being repaid. For context, a healthy bank runs low single digits.
  • Years of heavy losses — as the RBI's tougher recognition and provisioning norms bit, IOB booked large net losses, including roughly ₹6,299 Cr in FY18, driven by provisions against soured corporate accounts and fraud-flagged loans. Losses ran through the late 2010s.
  • Government life-support — the state injected over ₹24,000 Cr of recapitalisation capital across FY18–FY22 to keep IOB above Basel III minimums and solvent while it worked through the mess.

The turning point came in September 2021, when the RBI removed IOB from the PCA framework, citing improved capital, profitability and asset quality. That exit is the hinge of the entire modern IOB story: it re-opened the taps on lending and growth, and set up the profit ramp of FY22–FY26.

Why this history still matters to an investor: a bank that has been through a full bad-loan cycle is a different proposition than one that hasn't. Bulls say IOB's book has been stress-tested, cleaned and re-underwritten with discipline learned the hard way. Bears say PSU credit culture is cyclical, and the true test of the "new" IOB is how its recently originated, fast-growing loan book behaves through the next downturn — not how the cleaned-up legacy book looks today.

Asset quality, fully decoded

GNPA, NNPA, PCR, slippages, credit cost — the five words that run a bank.

Asset quality is the language in which bank health is spoken. For a PSU bank in recovery, these five terms are the investment case. Here's each one in plain English, with IOB's FY26 number and why it matters.

Why GNPA vs NNPA vs PCR is the whole trick

Picture ₹100 of bad loans (that's the gross problem, GNPA). If the bank has already set aside ₹97.5 against them (that's PCR), then only ₹2.5 is still "live" and could hit future profit (that's net, NNPA). IOB's numbers — GNPA 1.42%, NNPA 0.21%, PCR ~97.5% — say something striking: the bad loans are both small and almost fully absorbed already. There is very little unrecognised pain left in the recognised book. That is exactly what a fully-cleaned, post-crisis balance sheet looks like — and it's why the market re-rated the stock. The remaining question is slippages: not the old bad loans, but whether the new ₹3.1 lakh Cr book (growing 24% a year) stays clean.

The turnaround, in numbers

From record losses to record profits — the four-year profit ramp.

Once the bad loans were recognised and provided for, and PCA lifted the growth handcuffs, IOB's profit-and-loss statement swung hard. Lower credit costs (fewer new provisions), a growing loan book and a stable margin combined to drive net profit from losses to a series of record highs, culminating in FY26 net profit of ₹5,208 Cr, up 56% year-on-year.

Where the profit growth actually came from

Three engines, in order of importance. (1) Falling credit cost. The single biggest lever: as the legacy bad loans were resolved and new slippages stayed tiny, the huge provisioning charge that once created losses simply went away — that alone flips a P&L. (2) Loan growth. Freed from PCA, advances grew ~24% in FY26, expanding net interest income (+15%). (3) Recoveries. Cash recovered from previously written-off accounts drops almost entirely to profit. The caution for the future: (1) is a one-time normalisation — you can only clean the book once. Sustaining ~20% RoE from here relies increasingly on (2) and (3), i.e. genuine, well-underwritten growth rather than the tailwind of a shrinking bad-loan pile.

Deposits, CASA, advances & the margin

The franchise engine — where a PSU bank actually competes.

Below the asset-quality drama sits the everyday business: gathering deposits cheaply and lending them profitably. Here IOB is a genuinely solid operator — its South-India retail deposit franchise gives it one of the best CASA ratios in the public-sector pack, which underpins a respectable margin.

Why the CASA edge is IOB's quiet strength

A bank's margin is a tug-of-war between the yield it earns on loans and the rate it pays on deposits. IOB's ~41.5% CASA means a large share of its funding is near-free savings and current-account money — so even in a competitive market it can hold a decent spread without over-paying for term deposits. That's a structural advantage rooted in its dense South-India branch network and retail relationships, and it's precisely the kind of durable franchise asset that survives a credit cycle. The pressure point ahead: system-wide, deposits are getting more expensive and harder to gather, and IOB's advances are outgrowing its deposits — so funding growth and margin defence are the metrics to watch each quarter.

08

The free-float overhang: the government must sell

The structural quirk that makes IOB unlike almost any private bank — and shapes the stock.

Here is the force an analyst leads with for IOB specifically. Because the Government of India owns ~92.4% of the bank, the public float is only ~7.6% — and that is a problem the law requires IOB to fix. SEBI's Minimum Public Shareholding (MPS) rule mandates that every listed company (public-sector banks included, after successive extensions) have at least 25% of shares in public hands. IOB is nowhere near it.

What the MPS gap means, mechanically

To go from ~7.6% public float to 25%, the government must sell down (or the bank must issue) a large block of shares — via a Qualified Institutional Placement (QIP), an Offer for Sale (OFS), or similar. That means a known future supply of stock hitting the market. Basic supply-and-demand: a big pending sale caps the upside and can pressure the price, because everyone knows more shares are coming. This is the "overhang." IOB has already begun — earlier QIPs (raising several thousand crore) trimmed the state stake from ~96% toward ~92% — but closing the full gap to 25% is a multi-year, multi-round exercise, and the bank has flagged it may miss the original MPS deadline and seek an extension. Until it's done, the overhang is a permanent feature of the investment case.

The two-sided nature of the overhang

  • The negative: a steady drip of share supply from dilution can cap the multiple — why pay up when you know cheaper stock is coming? It also mildly dilutes existing holders' per-share economics with each fresh issue.
  • The positive (the counter-intuitive part): the same tiny free float that creates the overhang also creates scarcity — with so few shares trading, index inclusion and institutional demand can push the price up sharply (and volatility is high in both directions). And each dilution round improves liquidity and can broaden the shareholder base, a long-run governance positive.
  • The wildcard: how the government sells matters. QIPs to institutions at a small discount are absorbed cleanly; a large, poorly-timed OFS can hit the price. Watch the pace and pricing of each tranche.
The honest tension: IOB's ownership is simultaneously its safety net and its ceiling. The near-total government stake made the rescue possible and keeps the deposit franchise trusted — but the legal requirement to unwind that stake down to 75% government / 25% public creates years of supply that a private bank simply doesn't carry. An investor in IOB is, in part, taking a view on how and when ~₹40,000+ Cr of eventual dilution lands.
09

Capital adequacy & the PSU re-rating theme

Why the whole PSU-bank basket got re-rated — and where IOB sits in it.

IOB's story doesn't sit in isolation; it rode a powerful sector-wide wave. After a decade in the wilderness, India's public-sector banks collectively re-rated hard from 2021 onward as the bad-loan cycle turned, government recapitalisation restored capital, and profits recovered. Understanding IOB's valuation means understanding both its capital position and this "PSU re-rating."

Capital — from fragile to comfortable

  • CRAR (Capital Adequacy Ratio) ~18% — total regulatory capital as a % of risk-weighted assets, comfortably above the ~11.5% minimum. This is the bank's overall shock-absorber.
  • CET-1 ~15.5% — "Common Equity Tier 1," the highest-quality core capital (mostly equity and reserves). A strong CET-1 means IOB can absorb losses and fund loan growth without urgently raising capital.
  • The transformation: during the crisis, thin capital was a existential threat that only ₹24,000+ Cr of government injections fixed. Today, capital is a source of strength — and, helpfully, the MPS-driven equity raises (§08) will add fresh CET-1, funding further growth.
The PSU re-rating, in one paragraph

For years, PSU banks traded at deep discounts to book value (often well below 1× P/B) because the market didn't trust their stated asset quality or their ability to earn a return. From 2021, three things changed at once: the bad-loan cycle turned decisively (record-low NPAs across the sector), profitability normalised (RoAs crossing 1%), and the government's cleaner-balance-sheet, growth-focused stance gave investors confidence. The result was a multi-year re-rating of the entire PSU-bank basket — many names multiplying several-fold. IOB was among the sharpest movers, precisely because a cleaned-up, high-provision, high-CASA bank with a tiny float is a very leveraged bet on that theme. The flip side: much of the re-rating has now happened. From ~0.5× book to ~2× book is a completed journey, not a starting point — which is why valuation (§10) is now the crux.

10

Financials & valuation

A healthy bank at a full price — why P/B, not P/E, is the lens.

IOB's financial profile today is genuinely healthy: record profits, pristine reported asset quality, strong capital and good CASA. The entire debate is the valuation — and for a bank, the right lens is Price-to-Book (P/B), not Price-to-Earnings.

Why banks are valued on P/B, not P/E

A bank's "product" is money, and its balance sheet — its book value (net worth) — is the business. What matters is how much profit (return) it can squeeze from each rupee of equity, i.e. Return on Equity (RoE). So the market pays a multiple of book value: a bank earning a high, sustainable RoE deserves a higher P/B. P/E is treacherous for banks because earnings can be flattered by low provisioning in good years (exactly IOB's current tailwind) — a bank can look cheap on P/E right before credit costs normalise. P/B, cross-checked against RoE and asset quality, is the honest gauge. IOB trades around 2× book — rich for a PSB, and pricing in that the ~20% RoE and low credit cost persist.

The rivals and the industry backdrop

Where a mid-tier PSU bank sits in a consolidating, competitive market.

IOB competes in one of the world's most crowded banking markets: a dozen public-sector banks led by the giant SBI, a set of large aggressive private banks, and a rising tier of small-finance and regional players. IOB is mid-tier among PSBs — smaller than SBI, PNB, Bank of Baroda, Canara and Union Bank, but a solid regional franchise in its own right.

The structural forces shaping the sector

Three backdrop themes matter for IOB. (1) The credit cycle. The whole PSU-bank recovery rode a benign phase of low slippages; the next up-tick in bad loans — whenever it comes — will test which banks truly re-learned discipline. (2) The deposit war. System-wide, deposit growth has lagged loan growth, making cheap funding scarce and prized — which is exactly where IOB's CASA edge helps. (3) Consolidation and privatisation talk. The government has periodically floated consolidating or privatising smaller PSBs; any such move involving IOB (or its non-inclusion) is a recurring speculative catalyst. IOB's defensible ground is its deposit franchise and regional density; its vulnerability is the generic PSU-bank weakness on underwriting sophistication and technology versus private peers.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cleaned-up bank — with a valuation and a dilution to justify.

IOB in 2026 is a fundamentally different animal from the crippled bank of 2018: profitable, well-provisioned, well-capitalised, growing. The investment debate is no longer about survival — it's about whether a ~2× book, low-float PSU bank facing years of dilution can keep compounding from here.

The bull case

  • Genuine turnaround — GNPA 1.42%, NNPA 0.21%, PCR ~97.5%: the book is clean and almost fully provided.
  • Record, growing profits — FY26 PAT ₹5,208 Cr (+56%), RoA past 1%, RoE ~20%.
  • Best-in-class CASA — ~41.5% domestic CASA underpins a durable, low-cost deposit franchise.
  • Strong capital — CRAR ~18%, CET-1 ~15.5%; MPS raises will add growth capital.
  • Scarcity & sovereign backing — tiny float can amplify moves; government ownership keeps funding cheap and trusted.

The bear case

  • Full valuation — ~2× book is rich for a PSB; much of the re-rating has already happened.
  • Dilution overhang — must sell government stock down to a 25% float; years of supply cap the upside.
  • Credit-cost normalisation — today's profits are flattered by ultra-low provisioning that can't repeat; the new fast-growing book is untested through a downturn.
  • PSU governance — policy influence, weaker underwriting/tech vs private peers, appointment risk.
  • Cyclicality — bank earnings and PSU multiples are cyclical; a rate/credit turn hits margins and slippages together.
The bottom line

Indian Overseas Bank is a credible, well-executed PSU turnaround — a bank that came back from the brink with a cleaned-up book, strong capital and one of the better deposit franchises in the public-sector pack. The business risk is far lower than it was five years ago. What remains is a two-part market risk: a valuation that has already priced in a lot of good news at ~2× book, and a structural dilution that must play out as the government unwinds its ~92% stake toward a 25% public float. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter — slippages (does the new book stay clean?), NIM and CASA (does the funding edge hold?), and the pace and pricing of the MPS dilution (how gently does the supply land?). Get those right and a healthy bank keeps compounding; stumble on any, and a richly-valued, low-float PSU stock has room to give back.
