00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

SBI Cards and Payment Services is a pure-play credit-card company — it issues credit cards, lends on them, and earns fees on the spending they generate. It is India's only listed stand-alone credit-card business and the country's #2 issuer (behind HDFC Bank), with ~2.15 crore cards in force and an 18.6% share of all cards in the market. Its parent and promoter is State Bank of India, the country's largest bank, which owns ~68.6% and feeds it customers through the widest branch network in India.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a lender, not a bank. SBI Cards is a Non-Banking Financial Company (NBFC). It has no deposits — it borrows money in the market and lends it to cardholders, earning the spread. In FY26 it earned ₹20,708 Cr of total income and ₹2,167 Cr of net profit (up 13%), on a receivables (loan) book of ₹56,926 Cr.
  • Two engines drive it. (1) Interest income from cardholders who "revolve" — carry a balance instead of paying in full — and from EMI conversions; and (2) fee income — interchange on every swipe, plus annual fees and late fees. Total card spends hit ₹4.30 lakh crore in FY26 (+29%), but the loan book grew only ~2% — a gap that is the whole story of the current cycle.
  • The story is asset quality. Because card lending is unsecured (no collateral), the swing factor is bad debts. A post-COVID delinquency spike plus the RBI's Nov-2023 crackdown on unsecured credit squeezed returns hard. FY26 is the recovery: gross NPAs fell to 2.41% (from 3.08%), credit cost eased to 8.6%, and management is steering return-on-assets back from 3.2% toward a 4–4.5% medium-term goal.
In plain terms

Think of SBI Cards as a machine that rents out money at ~16% and borrows it at ~7%, wrapped in a rewards-and-convenience product that also collects a small toll every time you swipe. The genius is the parent: State Bank of India hands it a firehose of pre-screened customers from 22,000+ branches, so it barely has to hunt for business. The danger is the flip side of lending without collateral — when the economy wobbles or too many risky customers are onboarded, some simply stop paying, and those losses (called "credit costs") can wipe out a big chunk of profit in a bad year. Everything an analyst watches here comes down to one tension: grow the loan book fast enough to matter, but not so fast that the bad debts blow up.

01

Credit-card economics 101: how a card actually earns

No finance degree required — just the handful of terms that drive everything.

A credit card is two products fused into one plastic rectangle: a payment tool (you swipe, the issuer pays the merchant, you settle later) and a short-term loan (if you don't settle in full, the issuer charges you interest). SBI Cards earns from both. To follow the business you only need six words.

The vocabulary that runs the whole business
TermWhat it means (and SBI Cards' number)

ReceivablesThe outstanding loan book — money cardholders owe that hasn't been repaid. FY26: ₹56,926 Cr (+2%). Drives interest income.

The simple profit equation

Strip it down and SBI Cards' profit is roughly: (interest earned on the loan book) + (fees on spends and cards) − (cost of the money it borrows) − (operating costs) − (bad-debt losses). Two of those five levers are the swing factors that decide a good year from a bad one: the cost of funds (which rises and falls with interest rates — SBI Cards borrows to lend) and the bad-debt losses (which explode in a downturn and shrink in a recovery). Everything else — spends, cards, fees — grows fairly steadily. So when you hear the stock is "cheap" or "expensive," it's almost always a bet on where those two levers are heading.

Why "unsecured" is the word that matters most

A home loan has a house behind it; a car loan has a car. A credit-card loan has nothing — if the borrower defaults, the issuer can't repossess the dinners and flight tickets they bought. That's why card interest rates are so high (SBI Cards' yield is ~16%): the price compensates for the risk. It's also why the RBI treats card lending as the riskiest consumer credit, and why the entire investment case rests on one discipline — lending to the right people, and pricing the losses in.

02

The two revenue engines: interest and fees

Read this once and the entire income statement clicks into place.

SBI Cards' income comes from two distinct engines that behave very differently. Interest income is the bigger, higher-margin engine, but it only turns when customers borrow. Fee income is steadier and grows with every swipe, whether or not the customer ever pays a rupee of interest. Understanding which engine is firing tells you where the business is in its cycle.

Engine 1 — interest income (the receivables book)

  • The revolvers. When a cardholder pays only the minimum due and carries the rest, that balance "revolves" and earns interest at ~3.5% a month (~40%+ annualised). This is the single most profitable slice of the book — but it's only ~22% of receivables today, down from pre-COVID highs as customers turned cautious.
  • EMIs & term loans. Big-ticket purchases converted to instalments, plus loans-on-cards, now form a growing share of the book. Lower yield than revolving, but lower risk and more predictable — management is deliberately tilting toward this "transactor-plus-EMI" mix.
  • Why it's cyclical. Revolving balances shrink when consumers are nervous (they pay down debt) and swell when they're confident. So interest income is tied to the consumer credit cycle — and in FY26, with the book flat, this engine grew only modestly.

Engine 2 — fee income (interchange, annual & late fees)

  • Interchange / MDR. Every time you swipe, the merchant pays a Merchant Discount Rate; a large chunk of it — the "interchange" — flows to the card issuer. More spends = more interchange, regardless of whether the customer revolves. This is why spends growth (+29%) matters even when the loan book is flat.
  • Annual & membership fees. Yearly card fees (often waived above a spend threshold) and premium-card charges.
  • Instalment, late & other fees. Late-payment fees, over-limit fees, EMI processing, forex mark-ups and reward-redemption economics.
Why the fee engine is quietly the resilient one

Here's the subtle point most people miss: a customer who pays their bill in full every month (a "transactor") earns SBI Cards no interest — but still generates interchange on every swipe. So even in a cautious credit cycle, when revolving balances shrink and the interest engine sputters, the fee engine keeps humming as long as people keep spending. That's exactly what FY26 showed: spends up 29%, book up just 2%. The trade-off is that fee income is lower-margin and exposed to regulatory risk on interchange/MDR (see §08). A healthy card business wants both engines firing — plenty of spends and a healthy, well-underwritten revolving book.

03

The company and who controls it

A bank's card arm, spun into India's only listed pure-play issuer.

SBI Cards began as a joint venture between State Bank of India and GE Capital in 1998 — one of the first companies to bring modern credit cards to India at scale. SBI later bought out GE's stake, and in March 2020 listed the business, giving public investors the only clean way to own a stand-alone Indian credit-card franchise. State Bank of India remains firmly in control as promoter.

Timeline

  • 2017

  • Mar 2020
    IPO — a ₹10,340 Cr issue, one of India's largest; lists on NSE & BSE just as COVID hit, making it the only listed pure-play card company.
  • FY21–22

  • Nov 2023
    RBI hikes risk weights on unsecured consumer credit — credit-card receivables to 150% (banks) / 125% (NBFCs). Directly raises SBI Cards' capital cost and cools the whole sector.
  • FY24–25
    Delinquencies climb as post-COVID vintages season; credit cost peaks (~9%) and RoA compresses — the toughest stretch of the cycle.
  • FY26
    Recovery underway: GNPA falls to 2.41%, credit cost eases to 8.6%, PAT +13% to ₹2,167 Cr; cost of funds drops 71 bps as rates ease. Management targets RoA back to 4–4.5%.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter: State Bank of India holds ~68.6% — the controlling shareholder, brand licensor and customer-sourcing partner all in one. This is the single most important fact about the company: the parent is the moat.
  • Public float: the ~31% free float is held by mutual funds (~10%), foreign institutions (~10%), insurers (~7%) and retail. A liquid, widely-tracked large-cap.
  • Balance sheet as an NBFC: unlike a bank, it has no deposits. It funds its ₹57,000 Cr book with ~₹44,000 Cr of market borrowings (bonds, bank lines, commercial paper). Capital adequacy is strong at 25.5% (Tier-1 20.0%) — a cushion the RBI's risk-weight hike made more precious.
04

The SBI distribution edge — and the cost-of-funds catch

The parentage that makes SBI Cards special — and the sensitivity it can't escape.

Ask an analyst why SBI Cards deserves to exist as a stand-alone franchise and the answer is one word: distribution. Acquiring a credit-card customer is expensive and risky — you have to find them, verify them, and bet they'll repay. SBI Cards gets that almost for free, because its parent has already done it.

Steps 1–2 are the parent's gift: cheap, low-risk customer acquisition that no independent issuer can match. Step 4 is the catch — with no deposits, funding costs move with market rates.

The "open market" vs "banca" split

SBI Cards sources customers through two channels. "Banca" — through SBI's own branch network, tapping existing bank customers — is the low-cost, low-risk channel and the structural advantage. "Open market" — salespeople signing up non-SBI customers on the street or online — is faster-growing but riskier, and is exactly where post-COVID delinquencies concentrated. Part of the FY24–25 asset-quality pain came from open-market vintages, and management has since tilted the mix back toward the safer banca channel.

The cost-of-funds sensitivity, explained

Because SBI Cards is an NBFC with no deposits, it must borrow every rupee it lends — around ₹44,000 Cr of bonds and bank lines. Its cost of funds was ~6.7% in FY26, down 71 basis points from the prior year as the RBI cut rates. That fall is pure profit tailwind: it lends at ~16% and borrows cheaper, so the spread widens. But the same lever cuts both ways — if rates rise, funding costs climb and margins compress, and SBI Cards can't fully re-price its card rates as fast. A bank with sticky low-cost deposits is insulated from this; a card NBFC is not. So watch the rate cycle: falling rates are a quiet tailwind for SBI Cards, rising rates a headwind.

05

Spends vs receivables — the gap that defines FY26

Why the loan book barely grew even as swiping surged — and why that's deliberate.

The most striking number in FY26 is the 29% jump in spends against just 2% growth in the loan book. To a newcomer that looks contradictory. It isn't — it's the signature of a card business that is choosing to grow transactions while deliberately restraining lending, because the last credit cycle burned it.

New account additions fell to 3.59 million (from 4.09 million) as the company throttled open-market sourcing after the delinquency spike. Fewer, better-quality accounts means slower book growth but healthier asset quality.

Three forces explain the divergence:

  • Fewer, safer new customers. New-account additions fell to 3.59 million from 4.09 million as management dialled back the riskier open-market channel. Slower onboarding directly slows book growth.
  • Customers borrowing less. The revolver rate — the share of the book carrying interest — sits at ~22%, well below pre-COVID levels. Cautious consumers pay their bills down rather than carry them, so spends convert into less lending.
  • A mix shift toward EMIs and transactors. The company is deliberately favouring lower-risk instalment lending and full-payers over high-risk revolvers, trading yield for safety while the cycle heals.
Why this is the right call — but also the tension in the stock

Restraining book growth in a recovering credit cycle is prudent risk management: it lets asset quality heal and rebuilds the underwriting muscle. But it comes at a cost — the high-margin interest engine (§02) stays idle, so profit growth leans on fees and on falling credit costs rather than on a growing loan book. The bull says this is a coiled spring: once confidence returns and the company re-accelerates lending into a clean book at lower funding costs, the interest engine roars back and RoA jumps toward the 4–4.5% target. The bear says muted book growth plus interchange/regulatory risk caps how exciting this can be. Both are watching the same two numbers each quarter: receivables growth and the revolver rate.

06

The credit-cost cycle — the swing factor that defines everything

For an unsecured lender, this is the section. Nothing else moves the profit as much.

A credit-card company can grow spends, add cards and expand fees for years — and still see a single bad year in credit costs erase most of the progress. Because the lending is unsecured, bad debts are the master variable. The last three years have been a textbook cycle: a delinquency spike, a regulatory tightening, a returns squeeze, and now a recovery. Understanding this arc explains both the recent numbers and the current valuation.

The three terms that decide a card lender's fate

  • GNPA (Gross Non-Performing Assets). The share of the loan book that has gone bad (broadly, 90+ days unpaid). FY26: 2.41%, sharply better than 3.08% a year earlier. Falling GNPA = the storm is passing.
  • Credit cost. The provisions the company sets aside for expected losses, as a % of the book — the actual hit to the P&L. FY26: ~8.6%, easing from ~9.0%. This is the number that directly eats profit.
  • RoA (Return on Assets). Profit as a % of the loan book — the master scorecard for a lender. FY26: ~3.2%, with Q4 running at ~3.6% and management targeting 4–4.5% once credit costs normalise fully.

Gross NPA fell 67 bps year-on-year; the Stage-3 (bad-loan) stock shrank ~₹268 Cr in Q4 alone. The company still carries a ~₹220 Cr management overlay as a buffer — a sign it's healing cautiously, not declaring victory.

What caused the stress — and why it's fading

The pain had two roots. First, post-COVID normalisation: a wave of new cards issued during the 2021–23 boom — many through the riskier open-market channel — seasoned into higher-than-expected defaults as revenge-spending gave way to repayment stress. Second, the RBI's November 2023 crackdown on unsecured credit, which raised risk weights on card receivables and signalled the whole industry to slow down and tighten. SBI Cards, being 100% unsecured, felt this more than any diversified peer.

FY26 is the other side of that cycle. The bad vintages have largely worked through the book; new sourcing skews safer (banca over open-market); collections and underwriting have tightened; and falling interest rates are cutting the cost of funds. The result: GNPA down, credit cost easing, and RoA climbing back toward target.

Why the RBI risk-weight hike still matters: raising the risk weight on card receivables (to 150% for banks, 125% for NBFCs) means every rupee lent now consumes more regulatory capital. For SBI Cards that had two effects: it made capital adequacy (25.5%) something to guard carefully, and it structurally raised the return-on-equity hurdle the business must clear. It's a permanent tax on growth-by-lending — and a big reason management now prizes profitable, well-underwritten growth over sheer book expansion.
The single mechanism to internalise

Here is the whole investment case in one sentence: SBI Cards' profit is a lever pivoting on credit costs. When credit cost falls from ~9% toward the ~6% the company earned in calmer years, an enormous amount of income stops being set aside for losses and drops straight to profit — RoA and RoE jump, and the stock re-rates. When credit cost spikes, the reverse happens fast and brutally. Growth in cards and spends sets the size of the lever; the credit-cost cycle sets which way it swings. That is why, for this company more than almost any other in the index, the quarterly GNPA and credit-cost prints matter more than the revenue line.

07

The industry and the rivals

A fast-growing market dominated by banks — with SBI Cards the lone pure-play.

India's credit-card market is large, under-penetrated and growing fast — roughly 11 crore cards for 140 crore people, versus near-universal penetration in developed markets. But it's dominated by banks, which can cross-sell cards to deposit customers and fund them with cheap deposits. SBI Cards is the odd one out — the only major issuer that is a stand-alone company, not a bank division.

The structural handicap — and the offsetting edge

Every major rival is a bank, and that matters for one reason: deposits. HDFC, ICICI and Axis fund their card books with cheap current- and savings-account money, giving them a permanent cost-of-funds advantage over SBI Cards, which must borrow in the market. In a rising-rate world, that gap widens against SBI Cards. The offset is SBI's distribution — no rival has 22,000+ branches funnelling pre-screened customers at near-zero acquisition cost. So the competitive story is a standing trade-off: SBI Cards wins on customer sourcing and loses on funding cost. The pure-play structure also cuts both ways — it gives investors a clean, focused bet on the card cycle, but denies the company the diversification and deposit base that cushion a bank through a downturn.

08

The UPI-on-credit & MDR question — the regulatory wildcard

The structural threat unique to Indian card issuers — and it aims straight at the fee engine.

The most-watched risk hanging over every Indian card issuer isn't a competitor — it's a policy question about who pays for digital payments. India built UPI as a near-free, real-time payment rail. Credit cards, by contrast, charge merchants a Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) that funds the whole rewards-and-interchange model. Two forces now put that model under pressure.

Force 1 — RuPay credit cards on UPI

Since 2022 the RBI has allowed RuPay credit cards to be linked to UPI, so you can scan a QR code and pay from a credit card. This is genuinely useful — it merges India's most popular payment rail with credit — and RuPay has grabbed a rising share of new-card issuance on the back of it. But it's a double-edged sword for issuers like SBI Cards:

  • Opportunity: it massively expands where a credit card can be used (every UPI QR, including tiny merchants who never accepted cards), potentially lifting spends.
  • Threat: for small-ticket UPI-credit transactions (below ₹2,000), MDR is effectively zero — so the issuer earns interchange on bigger tickets but little or nothing on the flood of small ones. If credit-card usage migrates to low-MDR UPI rails, the fee engine's economics erode.

Force 2 — the MDR policy overhang

There is persistent pressure — political and regulatory — to reduce or zero out MDR to make digital payments cheaper for merchants. Debit-card and standard UPI MDR is already effectively nil. If a future mandate were to cap or eliminate credit-card MDR, it would strike directly at interchange — one of SBI Cards' two engines. The industry has pushed back hard precisely because, as banks have warned, zeroing MDR "may impact the entire credit card business model."

Why this is the genuine structural wildcard: unlike the credit-cost cycle (which swings and heals), an adverse MDR ruling would be a permanent repricing of the fee engine. It wouldn't end the business — interest income on revolving balances would remain — but it would compress the economics of the payment side and force issuers to lean even harder on lending (the riskier engine) and on annual fees. There is no confirmed mandate as of mid-2026; the current framework keeps meaningful MDR on higher-ticket credit transactions. But it is the single risk most likely to structurally reset the sector's earning power, so it belongs at the top of any watch-list for this stock.
How to hold both the opportunity and the threat in your head

The honest read is that UPI-on-credit is expansionary for volumes but dilutive to per-transaction economics. It brings credit to hundreds of millions of new merchant touchpoints — great for spends and for keeping the card relevant against pure-UPI habits. But it does so at thinner (sometimes zero) interchange on small tickets, and it hands the network narrative to RuPay/NPCI, which the government can regulate more directly. For SBI Cards, the practical response is to keep pushing higher-ticket spends, EMIs, premium cards and annual fees — parts of the model less exposed to small-ticket MDR erosion. Watch two things: how fast credit spends migrate to UPI rails, and any RBI/government signal on credit-card MDR.

How to think about the valuation

SBI Cards trades around ₹57,000 Cr market cap, roughly 26× trailing earnings and ~3.6× book — a long way below the ~40×+ it once commanded, and the stock is down sharply from its 52-week high. Card lenders are usually valued on P/E and P/B, cross-checked against RoA/RoE: a business earning ~3% RoA and ~15% RoE, with a clear path to 4–4.5% RoA as credit costs normalise, can support a mid-20s P/E and a ~3.5× book multiple. The bull case is straightforward: if the credit-cost lever swings back toward the ~6% of calmer years, earnings can grow well above the income line and the multiple re-rates. The bear case is that muted book growth, interchange/MDR risk and the deposit-funded competition cap the upside, leaving a fairly-valued franchise rather than a cheap one. The number that resolves the debate is RoA — watch it each quarter.

The growth runway

Under-penetrated cards, premiumisation, and a book waiting to re-accelerate.

Even after a cautious year, SBI Cards sits on a long runway. India has among the lowest credit-card penetration of any large economy, spending is digitising fast, and the company has both the distribution and the balance sheet to grow — once the cycle gives it the confidence to lend harder again.

The next chapter is less about issuing more cards and more about the credit cycle giving SBI Cards permission to lend confidently again — that's where the operating leverage lives.
The two-speed growth model

SBI Cards can grow two ways, and they have very different risk profiles. The safe way is more spends, more cards, more fees, more premium mix — steady, low-risk, but lower-margin. The powerful way is a bigger, healthy receivables book with more revolvers — high-margin, but only prudent once underwriting and the macro are sound. FY26 was almost entirely the safe way. The upside case for the stock is that FY27–28 lets the company add back the powerful way on top of the safe way — spends and fees still compounding, and the interest engine re-firing into a clean book at cheap funding. Get that combination and RoA and earnings both step up.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A quality franchise coming off a hard cycle — the debate is where the cycle goes next.

SBI Cards is a strong franchise with a genuine moat (SBI's distribution) that has just endured the toughest stretch of its credit cycle. The business quality isn't seriously in question; the debate is entirely about the trajectory of credit costs, funding costs and regulation — and whether the modest valuation already prices the recovery.

The bull case

  • Cycle turning up — GNPA falling (2.41%), credit cost easing (8.6%), RoA climbing toward the 4–4.5% target; profit grows faster than income.
  • Unmatched distribution — SBI's 22,000+ branches source pre-screened customers at near-zero acquisition cost; the parent is the moat.
  • Funding tailwind — cost of funds down 71 bps as rates ease; NIM widening.
  • Structural growth — deeply under-penetrated cards market; spends +29%; digitisation and premiumisation compounding.
  • Modest valuation — ~26× earnings, well below IPO-era levels; a clean pure-play on the recovery with a strong 25.5% capital buffer.

The bear case

  • 100% unsecured — no collateral; any fresh delinquency wave (macro shock, over-onboarding) hits profit hard and fast.
  • MDR / UPI-on-credit — a permanent regulatory reset of interchange would strike the fee engine directly.
  • Muted book growth — receivables +2% and revolver rate at ~22%; the high-margin interest engine is idling.
  • No deposits — market-funded NBFC at a structural cost-of-funds disadvantage to bank rivals if rates rise.
  • Regulatory tax on growth — RBI risk-weights raised the capital cost of lending; RoE hurdle permanently higher.
The bottom line

SBI Cards is India's only clean, listed bet on the structural rise of the credit card — backed by the country's biggest bank, throwing off ~3% RoA and ~15% RoE even in a recovering cycle, at a valuation the market has de-rated to sober levels. The business risk is real and specific: it lends without collateral, so its fate rides on the credit-cost cycle, and its fee economics carry a genuine MDR/UPI regulatory tail-risk. But the same cyclicality is the opportunity — if credit costs normalise toward calmer-year levels and the company re-accelerates a clean loan book at falling funding costs, RoA marches to 4–4.5% and earnings compound well above the top line. The story over the next few years is written in three numbers each quarter: credit cost / GNPA (is the cycle healing?), receivables growth & revolver rate (is the interest engine re-firing?), and any MDR/interchange signal (is the fee engine safe?). Read those right and this is a compounder bought at a discount; read them wrong and it's a fairly-priced lender exposed to the next unsecured-credit downturn.
