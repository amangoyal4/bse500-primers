00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Viyash Scientific Limited is an integrated pharmaceutical company that makes the active molecules inside medicines — active pharmaceutical ingredients, or APIs — plus finished-dose formulations for regulated export markets and a growing contract development & manufacturing (CDMO) business that makes molecules to order for other drug companies. It spans both human pharma (the bulk of the business) and animal health (its heritage, under the Alivira brand). If the name is unfamiliar, the history is the reason: until January 2026 this exact listed company was called SeQuent Scientific, and it was known mainly as one of the world's larger animal-health players.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The listed shell and the business inside it are two different stories. The stock has been publicly traded for years as SeQuent — a veterinary API-and-formulations company. But in December 2025 a private, Carlyle-backed human-pharma roll-up called Viyash Life Sciences was amalgamated into SeQuent, its owners took majority control, and the company was renamed. So today's Viyash Scientific is mostly a new business wearing an old listing — and its famous animal-health franchise is now the small minority segment.
  • It sells molecules, not brands. Viyash is a business-to-business supplier: its customers are other pharmaceutical companies (generic and innovator), not patients at a chemist. It earns on APIs (bulk active ingredient), on regulated-market formulations, and increasingly on CDMO contracts where an innovator pays it to develop and manufacture a molecule. That is a fundamentally different economic animal from a branded-drug company — explained in section 01.
  • FY26 was a dramatic first year — on a very rich valuation. Revenue rose ~14% to ~₹3,420 Cr, EBITDA jumped ~57% to ~₹653–690 Cr (margin up ~560 bps to ~19–20%), and net profit swung to ~₹225 Cr from almost nothing — a genuine turnaround powered by merger synergies. Yet the market caps the company near ~₹12,000 Cr, which on that profit is a steep multiple. The debate is entirely about whether the turnaround compounds from here.
In plain terms

Strip away the corporate history and Viyash is a factory-and-laboratory business that supplies the raw active chemistry of medicines to the companies that sell the finished pills. It does not own famous drug brands; it owns approvals, plants and chemistry know-how, and it rents those out — as bulk API, as export formulations, and as contract manufacturing. What makes it unusual right now is that it is barely a year old in its current shape: a private equity firm spent four years buying up API factories, then folded the whole pile into a long-listed animal-health company to take it public in a single step. FY26 shows the combined machine running well. The price already assumes it keeps accelerating — which is the whole question.

01

API & CDMO 101: selling the molecule, not the medicine

No chemistry needed — just what an active ingredient is and how you earn from it.

A medicine is really two things bolted together: the active molecule that does the healing, and the pill or vial that delivers it. The active molecule is the API — active pharmaceutical ingredient. A paracetamol tablet is mostly filler; the few hundred milligrams of paracetamol compound is the API. Viyash lives on that active-molecule side of the industry. To follow the company you need five terms.

The only pharma terms you need here
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for Viyash)
APIActive Pharmaceutical Ingredient — the actual drug molecule (e.g. an oncology or heart-drug compound) in bulk chemical form, before it is turned into a pill. Viyash's core heritage. Sold by the kilo to drug-makers, not to patients.
KSM / intermediateKey Starting Material and intermediates — the earlier chemical building blocks you react together to build the API. India imports many of these, heavily from China. Owning this step is "backward integration" (section 06).
Formulation (FDF)Finished Dosage Form — the actual tablet, capsule or injectable a patient takes. Higher-value than raw API. Viyash makes formulations for regulated export markets (Europe, US, India).
CDMO / CMOContract Development & Manufacturing Organisation — you make an API or drug to order for another company (often an innovator), under contract. Stickier and better-margin than commodity API. Viyash's stated growth engine.
DMF / regulated marketA Drug Master File is the dossier you file with a regulator (USFDA, EU) proving your API is made to standard. Filing DMFs and holding GMP-approved plants is the licence to sell into rich "regulated" markets — the entry barrier.
The distinction that defines the whole business: molecule-maker, not brand-owner

A branded-drug company like a Sun Pharma or a Cipla owns the medicine — the box on the shelf, the prescription, the marketing, the pricing power. An API/CDMO company like Viyash owns the molecule and sells it to those brand-owners. That has two consequences a first-timer must internalise. First, Viyash's revenue is lumpy and relationship-driven: a handful of large pharma customers, long qualification cycles, orders that ramp and fade with a customer's own product. Second, its moat is not a brand — it is process chemistry and regulatory approvals. The prize is being the qualified, audited, cost-competitive maker of a hard molecule that a big drug company would rather buy than build. Get onto enough of those approved-supplier lists and the business is durable; fail an inspection or lose a molecule to a cheaper rival and a revenue line simply disappears.

Who Viyash sells to, and how the money is earned

  • Generic drug companies — buy Viyash's APIs to make their own off-patent tablets. Price-competitive, volume-driven, but a huge global market. This is the traditional bulk-API trade.
  • Innovator (patent-holding) companies — the CDMO customers. They pay Viyash to develop and manufacture a proprietary molecule they do not want to make in-house. Higher value, stickier, and the direction management is steering toward.
  • Its own formulation channel — Viyash also converts some API into finished export formulations, capturing more of the value chain rather than selling only the raw ingredient.
  • Animal-health customers — via Alivira, veterinary APIs and formulations for livestock and companion animals, sold into 100-plus countries. A smaller slice now, but a real, differentiated franchise (section 03).
02

The reverse-merger: how a roll-up took over a listing

The single most important structural fact about this company — and it fits no other Indian pharma name.

Most companies grow into their listing over years. Viyash Scientific arrived at its current form almost overnight, through a corporate manoeuvre that is worth understanding in full, because it explains the ownership, the balance sheet and the entire "why now" of the stock. In short: a private roll-up was poured into a public shell, and the shell took the roll-up's name.

Timeline

  • 1985
    SeQuent Scientific incorporated; over time it builds a global animal-health business (the Alivira brand) plus a human-API arm, listed on NSE (symbol SEQUENT) and BSE (code 512529).
  • 2019
    Viyash Life Sciences founded by Hari Babu Bodepudi (with co-founder Srihari Raju Kalidindi) — a new, private venture with an explicit plan to consolidate API, intermediate and formulation assets into one integrated supplier for large generic customers.
  • 2021
    Private-equity giant Carlyle partners with Viyash to build a generics platform; from June 2021 the roll-up accelerates — acquiring Symed Labs (niche APIs, backward integration), and later Vandana Lifesciences, certain Mylan units, Appcure Labs and a controlling stake in US formulations player Appco Pharma.
  • 2022–24
    Viyash assembles ~10 manufacturing sites in India (plus a US formulation facility), becoming one of India's larger mid-tier integrated API-and-formulation players — but unlisted.
  • 2025
    SeQuent agrees to absorb Viyash Life Sciences (deal valued around ₹3,800 Cr). Shareholders approve (~99.99% vote, Dec 2025); the NCLT clears the scheme; the amalgamation becomes effective ~16 December 2025.
  • Jan 2026
    The listed company is renamed Viyash Scientific Limited (effective 8 Jan 2026); NSE ticker changes from SEQUENT to VIYASH. BSE code 512529 and ISIN INE807F01027 stay the same.
  • FY26
    First full year as one company: revenue ~₹3,420 Cr (+14%), EBITDA ~₹653–690 Cr (+57%, ~19–20% margin), PAT ~₹225 Cr; synergy realisation begins; Carlyle emerges as ~61% promoter.
The device that fits no other primer: the tail became the whole animal

Here is the manoeuvre in one line. Carlyle spent four years privately buying up API factories, then instead of running a fresh IPO it folded that entire pile into an already-listed animal-health company — and in doing so, took the listing, the majority stake and the name. The famous business the market had tracked for a decade, SeQuent's animal-health franchise, did not disappear; it was simply demoted, and now sits as roughly an eighth of the company that carries a version of its own ticker. A reader who remembers "SeQuent, the animal-health stock" is looking at a company where animal health is the side dish. That inversion — a listed brand becoming a minority passenger inside the private roll-up it appeared to acquire — is specific to this exact situation and to nothing else in Indian pharma.

Who controls it now

  • Carlyle is the ~61% promoter. Absorbing the private Viyash into the listed SeQuent meant issuing a large slug of new shares to Viyash's owners — the share count roughly rose from ~25 crore to ~43.5 crore (a ~74% increase). Existing SeQuent public holders were diluted; Carlyle-led Viyash ownership became the controlling block.
  • A financial sponsor, not a strategic parent. This matters: the controlling owner is a global buyout fund, whose horizon is a return, not a permanent industrial home. That can be a positive (professional capital allocation, appetite to scale and eventually exit at a premium) and a risk (an eventual large block for sale, and objectives set by fund timelines rather than by minority holders).
  • Founder-operators run it. The Viyash founders who built the roll-up carry the operating logic — consolidate, integrate, cross-sell to big generic and innovator customers — into the listed company.
Why the structure colours everything that follows: the ownership explains the balance sheet (a roll-up funded partly with debt, now being paid down), the strategy (integration synergies as the near-term profit lever), and the valuation puzzle (a young, PE-controlled combination priced for the growth it promises rather than the profit it has yet booked). Every later section is, in part, a test of whether the merger math turns into durable earnings.

The genuine surprise a SeQuent-watcher should sit with

For years the investment case for this ticker was animal health — Alivira, veterinary APIs, a defensive global vet franchise. Why each line earns differently

  • Human API — the volume base. Competitive and price-sensitive at the commodity end, but higher-value for complex oncology and cardiovascular molecules where few players qualify. FY26 API revenue grew a modest ~8%.
  • Formulations — finished dosages for regulated markets grew faster, ~18% in FY26 (Europe, India and the US leading), because selling the finished tablet captures margin that raw API leaves on the table.
  • CDMO — the strategic prize. Management has stated an aim to roughly double the CDMO business over about three years. Contract work for innovators is stickier and richer than commodity API, and it is the clearest path to the margins the valuation assumes.
  • Animal health (Alivira) — smaller now, but genuinely differentiated: a global veterinary footprint, a growing companion-animal portfolio, and end-markets that do not move in lockstep with human generics. A quiet diversifier rather than the headline.
04

The regulated-market moat: approvals are the barrier

Why a wall of paperwork and inspections is the real asset in this business.

In a business that sells molecules, the competitive edge is not a secret formula — off-patent chemistry is, by definition, known. The edge is the right to sell into the markets that pay well, and that right is earned only through regulatory qualification. This is the moat, and it is worth understanding why it is so hard to cross.

To supply a regulated market — the United States (USFDA), the European Union (EU GMP/EDQM), Japan, Australia (TGA), Brazil (ANVISA) — a maker must file dossiers (DMFs) proving each API is made to a validated, reproducible standard, and must run plants that pass unannounced Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) inspections. That takes years, capital, and a clean audit history. Once achieved, it is genuinely defensible: a big drug company will not casually switch away from a supplier it has qualified, because re-qualifying a new source is slow and costly. Viyash carries approvals across USFDA, EU GMP, WHO, TGA, ANVISA and EDQM, and the merger's own headline benefit was scale here — management framed the combination as delivering roughly a nine-fold expansion in USFDA-approved manufacturing capacity and about a five-fold increase in R&D talent versus the standalone animal-health company.

The double edge of a moat made of inspections

Approvals cut both ways, and a first-timer should hold both edges at once. On the upside, a qualified plant is a toll-booth: once you are the audited, approved supplier of a hard molecule, customers stay and pricing is steadier than raw commodity chemistry. On the downside, the very same regulator that granted the approval can suspend it. A single adverse USFDA inspection — a warning letter or an import alert on a key plant — can freeze a chunk of revenue overnight and take quarters to resolve. So the moat is real but conditional: it protects you right up until an audit goes wrong. For a company that just multiplied its regulated footprint through a rapid roll-up of many sites, maintaining a spotless inspection record across all of them is not a footnote — it is the core operational discipline the whole thesis rests on.

05

The CDMO engine: why contract work is the whole bet

The lever that decides whether this is a re-rated commodity API maker or a premium partner.

Every expensive pharma-supplier stock in India is, at heart, one wager: that the company can shift its earnings from commodity API — where price competition is brutal and margins are thin — toward contract manufacturing for innovators, where a customer pays for reliability and chemistry rather than the lowest kilo price. Viyash's valuation is that wager made explicit. This section is the crux of the case.

Commodity API is a hard way to earn. You compete on cost against Chinese and Indian rivals for a known molecule; a customer can re-source on price; margins live in the low double digits. CDMO work is different: an innovator hands you its molecule to develop and manufacture under a long relationship, values a clean audit trail and supply security over the last rupee of cost, and does not switch easily once you are embedded in its supply chain. That stickiness is why CDMO revenue commands both higher margins and a higher market multiple. Viyash has said plainly that it intends to roughly double its CDMO business over about three years — and the FY26 margin jump to ~19–20% is the first evidence the mix is starting to move the right way.

The one number that settles the thesis: CDMO-plus-formulation growth holding a ~20% EBITDA margin

Forget the headline revenue line. The figure that proves or breaks Viyash is the growth of the CDMO and regulated-formulation revenue, read together with a sustained EBITDA margin near or above 20%. Here is why that specific pairing is decisive. If CDMO and formulations keep outgrowing commodity API (FY26 already showed formulations +18% versus API +8%) and the ~19–20% margin holds or climbs as synergies land, then the mix is genuinely shifting toward the premium end, the turnaround is structural, and the rich multiple is defensible. If instead margin stalls back toward the mid-teens while API stays the bulk of revenue, then FY26's leap was mostly a one-time synergy sugar-rush on a commodity base — and the price has run far ahead of the business. Two lines on two clocks: the mix moving up the ladder, and the margin refusing to slide back down it. Watch them every quarter; they are the scoreboard.

06

The roll-up logic: integrating ten factories into one supplier

What buying a dozen API assets is actually meant to achieve — and the China question underneath it.

Viyash was not built; it was assembled. The private company that now controls the listing spent 2019–2024 acquiring API, intermediate and formulation businesses and stitching them together. That roll-up history is the source of both the opportunity and the execution risk, so it is worth being precise about what consolidation is supposed to buy.

What integrating many sites is meant to achieve

  • Backward integration — owning the earlier chemical steps (intermediates, KSMs) rather than importing them. The Symed Labs acquisition was explicitly about this. Every step you make in-house is a cost you control instead of a price you pay, and a supply risk you remove.
  • Scale and cross-selling — a big generic or innovator customer would rather deal with one qualified partner that can supply many molecules than juggle a dozen small vendors. Bolting the assets together lets Viyash present a broad, integrated catalogue to the same buyers.
  • Fixed-cost absorption and synergy — shared R&D, procurement, quality systems and regulatory machinery spread across more revenue. Management has guided to ~₹125–150 Cr of synergies over 12–18 months, and said it was already annualising at roughly ₹50–60 Cr — the visible mechanism behind FY26's margin jump.
  • A one-step path to public markets — merging into a listed shell gave the private roll-up scale, a stock currency and an exit runway in a single move, without the timing risk of a fresh IPO.
The dependency underneath every Indian API maker: China

There is a structural fact no Indian API company escapes, and it belongs in this section because integration is partly a response to it. India's pharma industry — Viyash included — depends heavily on China for key starting materials and intermediates, the early chemical building blocks that feed API synthesis. When Chinese supply tightens or prices spike, an Indian maker's cost base moves and its margins wobble. The whole logic of backward integration is to claw back some of that control: make more of your own intermediates, qualify alternative sources, and reduce the number of critical inputs that arrive on a boat from a single country. Viyash's roll-up — buying an intermediates specialist like Symed, adding captive chemistry — is, in part, a bet that owning more of the chain is worth the capital in a world where that one dependency periodically bites. It softens the exposure; it does not erase it.

The honest risk in a roll-up: a company assembled from a dozen acquisitions in four years has not yet proven it runs as one company. Integrating disparate plants, quality cultures, IT systems and teams is exactly where roll-ups stumble — synergies slip, an inherited site fails an audit, or the promised cross-selling never quite materialises. FY26's numbers say the early integration is going well. But "assembled recently" is a genuine, not theoretical, execution risk, and it is the reason a chunk of the investment debate is about management delivery rather than end-market demand.

What the balance sheet quietly tells you

The most under-appreciated line in FY26 is not the profit — it is the deleveraging. A roll-up funded through acquisitions typically carries debt; the worry is always that integration costs and working capital swallow the cash. Instead, Viyash generated roughly ₹323 Cr of operating cash and ~₹225 Cr of free cash flow, and cut net debt from ~₹309 Cr to ~₹166 Cr in a single year. That is the signature of a business whose earnings are turning into actual cash rather than accounting gains — the single best early evidence that the merged machine is real. It does not settle the valuation question (section 10), but it removes the crudest bear case: this is not a leveraged story limping to service its debt.

08

Geography and therapy: where the molecules go

A footprint on four continents, tilted toward regulated markets and complex therapies.

Viyash sells into more than 100 countries from manufacturing bases across several countries, and its therapy focus sits in areas where qualification is hard and value is higher. Two lenses — where it sells and what it makes — round out the picture.

The geographic footprint

  • Manufacturing across India and abroad — Indian API and formulation sites (Panvel, Visakhapatnam, Sangareddy, Ambernath and others), plus operations linked to Spain, Turkey and Brazil, and a US formulation presence (via the Appco acquisition). The merger's stated benefit was a large step-up in USFDA-approved capacity.
  • Regulated markets are the prize — momentum in FY26 formulations came notably from Europe, India and the USA. These are the markets where GMP approval limits competition and pricing is steadier than in the commodity RoW trade.
  • Emerging markets add volume and are improving — the company noted better profitability in emerging markets in FY26. They broaden the base and, for animal health especially, reach farming economies worldwide.
  • Animal health spans 100-plus countries — Alivira's veterinary reach is genuinely global, a distinct distribution asset that human-pharma peers do not carry.

The therapy tilt

  • Oncology — cancer-drug APIs are complex, potent and tightly regulated; few makers qualify, so the margins and stickiness are better than commodity chemistry. A core focus of the human-API book.
  • Cardiovascular — heart-disease molecules are large, chronic-therapy markets with durable generic demand — a steady volume complement to the higher-complexity oncology work.
  • Regulated formulations across therapies — the finished-dosage business converts some of this API into higher-value exports rather than selling only bulk active.
  • Animal-health therapeutics — nutrition, pain management, parasite control, antibiotics and gut health for livestock, poultry and companion animals, with a stated push into the growing companion-animal (pet) segment.
Why the therapy mix is a quiet quality signal

A bulk-chemical maker's earnings quality is set less by how much it sells than by how hard what it sells is to make. A company leaning into oncology APIs and USFDA-grade formulations is choosing molecules where a handful of qualified players share the market, rather than commodity actives where the whole world competes on price. That choice is deliberate: complex, regulated, hard-to-copy chemistry is where a supplier earns a premium and keeps a customer. The therapy tilt toward oncology and cardiovascular, and the push up into regulated formulations and CDMO, is the same strategy seen from a different angle as sections 04 and 05 — trade breadth of easy molecules for depth in hard ones.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, capable Indian API/CDMO field — with a separate set of animal-health peers.

Viyash competes in one of the most contested corners of Indian pharma: the API and CDMO supply business, where a cluster of well-run, deeply-integrated companies chase the same regulated-market and innovator customers. It also carries an animal-health franchise with its own distinct competitors. Knowing the neighbours frames what "good" looks like.

The competitive truth to hold in mind

This is not a market where a newcomer wins by being cheap — the incumbents are already cost-competitive, deeply integrated and USFDA-seasoned. Viyash wins, or fails to, on two things it must prove over years, not quarters: keeping a spotless regulatory record across a freshly-assembled set of plants, and actually converting API relationships into stickier CDMO and formulation contracts. The likes of Divi's and Laurus set the bar for what a premium Indian molecule-maker looks like. Viyash is priced as if it will join that club; the field it is racing is exactly the set of companies that got there first and are not standing still.

10

Financials & valuation

A great year — priced as if many more are guaranteed.

Here is where the two halves of the story collide. The business, on FY26 evidence, is executing: margins up, debt down, cash real. But the stock is not priced as a cyclical or a turnaround-in-progress — it is priced as a proven premium compounder. At roughly ₹12,000 Cr of market value on ~₹225 Cr of net profit, the trailing earnings multiple is well above fifty times. That is not a number you pay for what a company has done; it is a number you pay for what you are convinced it will do.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A young, well-executing combination carrying a price that demands it keeps executing.

Viyash Scientific enters FY27 with real momentum: a first clean year of rising margins and falling debt, a stated plan to double CDMO, a broad regulated footprint, and professional capital behind it. What it must still prove is that a company assembled from a dozen acquisitions barely a year ago can compound like the premium peers its price already assumes. Both cases flow from that one tension.

The bull case

  • The turnaround is banking cash — margin up ~560 bps to ~19–20%, net debt roughly halved, ~₹225 Cr free cash flow: integration is working, not just promised.
  • A real mix-shift lever — CDMO targeted to double in ~3 years and regulated formulations already outgrowing API; the path toward premium, stickier earnings is visible.
  • A widened regulated moat — ~9× USFDA-approved capacity and ~5× R&D talent from the merger; approvals across USFDA, EU GMP, TGA, ANVISA open the markets that pay.
  • Backward integration & scale — captive intermediates (Symed) and a consolidated catalogue let Viyash cross-sell to big generic and innovator customers as one partner.
  • An overlooked animal-health optionality — Alivira's global vet reach and companion-animal push is a differentiated, uncorrelated franchise the market barely credits.

The bear case

  • Priced for perfection it hasn't proven — a ~50×+ trailing multiple on a one-year-old combination leaves no cushion if growth disappoints.
  • Integration risk is live, not theoretical — a roll-up of ~10 sites must yet run as one company; a slipped synergy or an inherited weak plant would bite.
  • Regulatory single-points-of-failure — one adverse USFDA inspection on a key site can freeze revenue for quarters; the moat inverts on a bad audit.
  • China & input dependence — reliance on Chinese KSMs/intermediates leaves the cost base exposed to supply and price shocks integration only partly offsets.
  • A PE-controlled float — Carlyle's ~61% is an eventual seller; fund timelines, a possible large block overhang, and customer-concentration risk sit over the shares.
The bottom line

Viyash Scientific is a genuinely improving business dressed in a confusing costume — a Carlyle-built human-pharma API and CDMO roll-up that took over a listed animal-health name, and in FY26 showed it can lift margins, generate cash and pay down debt as one company. The strategy is coherent and the early execution is good. The catch is entirely in the price: the market already pays for the premium compounder Viyash intends to become, not the mid-tier molecule-maker it currently is. The next test is concrete and datable — whether, through FY27, the EBITDA margin holds at or above ~20% while CDMO and regulated formulations keep outgrowing commodity API, and whether the ~₹125–150 Cr synergy run-rate is delivered on schedule with a clean inspection record across the merged plants. Clear those bars and the roll-up graduates into the company its ticker now implies; miss them and FY26 will read as the year the re-rating outran the chemistry.
