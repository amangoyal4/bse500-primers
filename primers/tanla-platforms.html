Tanla Platforms, software margins inside a messaging pipe

A complete walk through India's largest CPaaS company — the invisible plumbing behind almost every OTP, delivery alert and bank SMS you receive — how it earns pennies routing billions of messages, why a thin sliver of "platform" revenue quietly carries a third of its gross profit, and why the number that matters is not the ₹4,400 Cr headline but the ₹1,175 Cr the company actually keeps.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Tanla Platforms is the company you have never heard of but interact with dozens of times a week. When your bank texts you a one-time password, when a food-delivery app pings your order status, when an airline sends a boarding reminder, there is a good chance the message physically passed through Tanla's systems on its way from the business to your phone. Tanla is India's largest CPaaS — Communications-Platform-as-a-Service — and the biggest handler of A2P (application-to-person) messaging traffic in the country. In FY26 it moved enough messages to generate ~₹4,418 Cr of revenue, and it did so with no meaningful debt and a fat pile of cash.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • The revenue is mostly not Tanla's to keep. Enterprise messaging is a pass-through business: a business pays Tanla to deliver an SMS, and Tanla pays the telecom operator a per-message "termination" charge to actually put it on the network. Of ₹4,418 Cr billed in FY26, roughly ₹3,240 Cr flowed straight back out to telcos and routing partners. What Tanla keeps — its gross profit of ~₹1,175 Cr — is the number that describes the real business. Revenue here is closer to a volume gauge than an earnings gauge.
  • A tiny "platform" leg carries an outsized share of the profit. Tanla runs two very different businesses under one roof: a high-volume, low-margin enterprise-messaging leg (Karix, ValueFirst — routing SMS, voice, WhatsApp) and a high-margin platforms leg (Trubloq, the anti-spam blockchain rail on Indian telcos; Wisely, a private enterprise-grade CPaaS). Platforms are only about a tenth of revenue but, at near-software gross margins, contribute close to a third of gross profit. The whole equity story turns on whether that mix keeps shifting.
  • FY26 was steady, not spectacular. Revenue grew ~9.7% and gross profit ~11.8%, but PAT was essentially flat (+0.4% to ~₹509 Cr) as pricing pressure in the messaging pipe and the withdrawal of Meta's WhatsApp incentives squeezed margins. The stock trades on a modest ~14–15× earnings, a rating that reflects genuine doubt about whether a business this exposed to price wars and channel shifts can keep growing profit.
In plain terms

Two businesses share one balance sheet. The first is a toll road: billions of enterprise messages pass through, and Tanla clips a wafer-thin fee on each, most of which it immediately hands to the telecom operator whose network carries the message. High volume, low margin, brutal competition. The second is a small software franchise sitting beside the road — Trubloq, which every Indian telco is effectively required to use to filter spam, and Wisely, which sells fraud-proof messaging to enterprises and telcos abroad. That software leg is a rounding error on revenue but a large slice of profit. The investment question is whether Tanla can keep the toll road busy while growing the software beside it faster than the toll erodes.

01

CPaaS & A2P 101: the plumbing behind every OTP

No telecom background needed — just who sends the message, who carries it, and who sits in the middle.

Every automated message a business sends you — an OTP, a shipment update, a promotional offer — is an application-to-person (A2P) message: it originates from a company's software, not a human, and lands on your handset. The businesses that want to send these messages do not connect directly to the telecom networks; the plumbing is too fragmented, too regulated, and too fiddly. Instead they hire an aggregator / CPaaS provider like Tanla to do it for them. To follow the company, you need six terms.

The only messaging terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
CPaaSCommunications-Platform-as-a-Service — software (usually via APIs) that lets any business plug messaging, OTP, voice and chat into its own app without building telecom integrations. Tanla's core identity, and its rename from "Tanla Solutions" in 2020 signalled the shift toward it.
A2P messagingApplication-to-person: automated, business-originated messages (vs person-to-person chat). OTPs, alerts, reminders, marketing. The high-volume traffic Tanla routes — and the pipe that global peers call "the SMS firehose."
AggregatorThe middleman between thousands of enterprises and the handful of telecom operators. It holds the operator contracts, the delivery tech and the compliance machinery. Tanla's enterprise leg (Karix, ValueFirst) is an aggregator at scale.
Termination / interconnect feeThe per-message charge the telecom operator levies to actually deliver a message on its network. This is Tanla's single largest cost — the reason enterprise messaging is a low-margin pass-through, not a fat software business.
OTT channels"Over-the-top" messaging that rides the internet rather than the SMS network — WhatsApp, RCS, Truecaller, Viber. Richer, cheaper for the enterprise, and the fastest-growing slice of Tanla's traffic. Both a threat to SMS and an opportunity.
ILDInternational long-distance messaging — cross-border A2P traffic (e.g. a foreign OTP terminating on an Indian number, or vice-versa). Higher-priced, higher-margin, but volatile — a recurring swing factor in Tanla's results.
The dependency that shapes the whole sector: the operator owns the last mile

An aggregator does not own a network. The message it sells has to travel the last mile on a telecom operator's infrastructure, and the operator charges for that mile. So a messaging company's cost of goods is set, in large part, by the very operators it depends on — and those operators can, and periodically do, raise the termination price. That single structural fact explains why enterprise A2P is a low-margin business, why scale and routing intelligence matter (a smarter route shaves cost), and why the real prize sits above the pipe: software the operator cannot re-price at will. Everything Tanla does to escape commodity economics — Trubloq, Wisely, the push into OTT and international routes — is an attempt to earn money the operator does not control.

Who is on each side of Tanla

  • Enterprises — banks, e-commerce, logistics, airlines, government, fintechs. They need OTPs and alerts delivered reliably and compliantly, at the lowest price, across whatever channel the customer prefers. They are demanding on price and quick to multi-source.
  • Telecom operators — Jio, Airtel, Vodafone Idea in India, plus operators abroad. They own the last mile, set termination charges, and — crucially — are also Tanla's customers for platform products like Trubloq and Wisely. Tanla sits on both sides of the operator relationship.
  • The regulator (TRAI) — the Telecom Regulatory Authority of India, whose 2020 anti-spam mandate created the market for Trubloq. In this business the regulator is not background noise; it can conjure or kill an entire revenue line with a rule.
02

Two speeds, one share: the pipe and the platform

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is invisible in the revenue line.

Tanla is not one business valued as one business; it is two businesses fused into one share, with wildly different economics. Miss this and you will misread every number the company reports. On one side is the enterprise-messaging pipe — huge volume, thin margin, fierce price competition. On the other is the platforms leg — small, software-like, high margin. The revenue line flatters the pipe and hides the platform; the gross-profit line does the opposite. Read the second, not the first.

The reason the two pictures diverge is margin. A rupee of platform revenue retains something like 90 paise of gross profit — near-software economics — while a rupee of enterprise-messaging revenue retains barely 20 paise after the telecom operator is paid, dragging the blended company gross margin to around 26.6%. In gross-profit terms a rupee of platform sales is worth roughly four-and-a-half rupees of pipe sales. That single ratio is why management steers relentlessly toward platforms and richer OTT channels — and why "revenue growth" on its own tells you almost nothing about earnings.

The non-transferable arithmetic: a ₹4,418 Cr company that is really a ₹1,175 Cr company

Here is the sentence that only fits Tanla. It reports ₹4,418 Cr of revenue, but of that, roughly ₹3,240 Cr is termination and routing cost that never belonged to Tanla — it is collected from enterprises and passed to telcos, a courier holding cash it must hand over at the next stop. What Tanla actually keeps is ₹1,175 Cr of gross profit, and within that keep, the small platforms leg supplies close to ₹400 Cr. So the "true size" of Tanla is not the ₹4,400 Cr coat it wears in the headlines; it is the ₹1,175 Cr body underneath, one-third of which is software. Value the coat and you overpay for a pass-through; value the body and you are pricing a real, partly-software business. This is the lens for the rest of the primer.

The strategic consequence is simple to state and hard to execute: Tanla's job is to grow the ₹1,175 Cr number faster than the ₹4,418 Cr number, by tilting mix toward platforms and toward richer OTT channels (WhatsApp, RCS) where it retains more per message. In FY26 it managed exactly that — gross profit grew ~11.8% while revenue grew ~9.7%, and the OTT share of enterprise traffic climbed sharply. The pipe keeps the lights on and the volumes huge; the platform is where the equity value is minted.

03

The take-rate: how a message becomes revenue

Follow a single message through the value chain and the economics become obvious.

To see why gross profit is the whole story, follow one enterprise SMS from the business that sends it to the phone that receives it. At each hop, money changes hands. Tanla is the coordinator in the middle, and its earnings are the small slice left after the operator is paid. This is the take-rate — the share of the billed amount the aggregator actually retains — and for plain SMS it is thin.

Blended across all channels the company keeps ~27 paise of every rupee billed (its FY26 gross margin); on plain domestic SMS the take-rate is thinner still, on platform and premium OTT routes it is far fatter. The mix within this ₹100 is what moves earnings.

Two things move the take-rate, and both are worth watching every quarter. Channel mix: plain domestic SMS is the thinnest take-rate; premium routes — international (ILD), certain OTT channels, and platform products — retain far more per message. Pricing power: because enterprises multi-source and operators periodically raise termination charges, Tanla is squeezed from both ends on commodity SMS. Its defence is to route smarter, to sell the enterprise a richer channel (WhatsApp instead of SMS), and to sell the operator a platform (Trubloq, Wisely) the enterprise never sees. Every one of those moves lifts the take-rate on the same underlying traffic.

Why "growth" and "profit" can point in opposite directions here

A subtle trap: Tanla's revenue can jump because a big client pushed a wave of low-margin promotional SMS, while gross profit barely moves — lots of traffic, almost no take-rate. Equally, revenue can stall while gross profit rises, because mix shifted toward platforms and premium OTT. This is why the market watches gross profit and gross margin, not the top line, and why a headline like "revenue up 10%" is close to meaningless until you know which messages drove it. In FY26 the good news was in the second line: gross profit outgrew revenue, evidence the mix was moving the right way even as the pipe stayed competitive.

04

Trubloq: the anti-spam rail the whole country runs on

The genuine surprise — a private listed company operates national messaging infrastructure by regulatory mandate.

Here is the fact that startles almost everyone who looks at Tanla for the first time. In 2020, TRAI mandated that every commercial SMS in India be checked against a blockchain-based registry to stamp out spam and fraud — every business, every template, every consent, logged on a distributed ledger. Tanla built one of the platforms that does this scrubbing, called Trubloq, and it ended up processing a very large share of the country's entire commercial-SMS flow. A private, listed company effectively runs a slice of national anti-spam infrastructure — and gets paid a tiny fee on an enormous number of messages.

The economics are the mirror image of the messaging pipe. Trubloq charges a minuscule per-transaction fee, but it applies to a colossal, non-discretionary volume: a business that wants to send commercial SMS in India has no choice but to be scrubbed. There is no telco termination cost to hand over, no raw material — it is software running at scale. That is why a business that is a sliver of revenue throws off software-grade gross margins and anchors the high-margin platforms leg.

The double edge of a regulator-created business

Trubloq is a gift and a hostage at the same time. It exists because a regulator decreed it, which gave Tanla a high-margin, high-volume annuity almost overnight — the kind of moat you cannot buy. But the same origin is its vulnerability: what a regulator creates, a regulator can reprice, reallocate or reopen to more competitors. The DLT market is shared with other providers, and TRAI periodically revisits the framework, the fees and the operating rules. So Trubloq is best understood as a durable but policy-contingent annuity — wonderfully profitable while the rules hold, and worth watching closely every time the regulator issues a new consultation paper. It is the most valuable thing Tanla owns that it did not, strictly, create for itself.

05

Wisely & the AI turn: selling fraud-proofing to telcos

The growth bet that reframes Tanla from Indian aggregator to global platform vendor.

If Trubloq is the annuity, Wisely is the ambition. Wisely is a private, enterprise-grade CPaaS that Tanla built — originally in collaboration with Microsoft — using blockchain and end-to-end encryption to guarantee that a message is authentic, untampered and spam-free. It is aimed not just at enterprises but at telecom operators themselves, especially outside India, who want to clean up and monetise their own messaging traffic. This is a different kind of customer and a different kind of sale: not "route my OTPs cheaply," but "run my network's messaging trust layer."

The FY26 proof point is worth stating plainly, because it is the clearest evidence the model travels beyond India. Tanla deployed Wisely AI with Indosat in Indonesia, and within about six months the platform reportedly protected 100 million subscribers from over 2 billion spam and scam communications — and, tellingly, contributed to roughly 10% ARPU growth for the operator. That last number is the pitch in a nutshell: Wisely does not just cut spam, it helps the operator make more money per subscriber, which turns messaging security from a cost centre into a revenue-share conversation.

Why the Indosat deal changes the shape of the story

For most of its life Tanla has been an Indian business with Indian regulatory tailwinds — a great franchise, but a geographically capped one. Wisely, if it replicates the Indosat template across other international operators, converts Tanla from a domestic aggregator into a platform vendor selling to telcos worldwide, earning software-margin revenue on other operators' traffic without carrying their termination costs. That is a far larger, far higher-quality addressable market than routing India's OTPs. The catch is execution and proof: one lighthouse deal is a signal, not a trend. The question the next few years must answer is whether Indosat was the first of many or a hard-won one-off. Watch the count of live operator deployments — that number, not any margin ratio, is the leading indicator for Wisely.

The strategic logic ties the whole company together. The enterprise pipe gives Tanla scale, relationships and traffic data; Trubloq turns regulatory compliance into an annuity; Wisely tries to package that accumulated trust-and-routing capability into a product it can sell globally at software margins. Each leg feeds the next. The bear worry is that Wisely stays small and India-bound; the bull hope is that it becomes the reason to own the stock at all.

06

The company, the founder, and who controls it

A founder-run, Hyderabad-headquartered platform company with an unusually consistent hand at the top.

Tanla is very much one person's company. D Uday Kumar Reddy founded it, built it through several reinventions, and remains Founder Chairman and CEO decades on — a rare continuity in Indian tech. Understanding the arc of the business, and who owns it, explains both its restlessness (it keeps remaking itself) and the key-person risk that comes with a founder this central.

  • 2023

  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter / founder: the Reddy family holds roughly 46% (approx.) of the company — with Uday Kumar Reddy himself around 23% and family member D Tanuja Reddy a further ~16%. This is a genuine founder-owner, aligned with minorities but also firmly in control. Confirm the exact figure in the latest shareholding filing.
  • Headquarters & footprint: based in Hyderabad, with the enterprise business (Karix, ValueFirst) serving thousands of Indian enterprises and a growing international book, and the platforms business (Trubloq, Wisely) selling to telecom operators in India and abroad.
  • Capital-return culture: the company is debt-free, generates strong free cash flow, and has repeatedly returned cash through dividends and buybacks (section 08) — a founder deploying surplus cash to shareholders rather than empire-building, which is unusual and worth crediting.
The founder factor cuts two ways: Uday Reddy's long tenure has given Tanla a coherent, repeatedly-reinvented strategy — from VAS to enterprise aggregation to regulatory platform to global software — that few professionally-managed peers have matched, and his family's large stake keeps incentives aligned. But a company this identified with one person carries real key-person and governance concentration; the reinventions that are a strength in good years are also a reminder that this management is willing to bet the model on the next platform. Minorities are along for a founder's ride.
07

The SMS-substitution question: threat, or the next pipe?

The defining industry force — will WhatsApp and RCS kill the SMS that pays Tanla's bills?

Every serious debate about a messaging company eventually arrives here. SMS is old, expensive per useful character, and increasingly bypassed by internet-based (OTT) channels — WhatsApp, Google's RCS (Rich Communication Services), Truecaller, Viber. If enterprises migrate their OTPs and alerts off SMS, does the pipe Tanla depends on shrink? The honest answer is nuanced: SMS volumes are under pressure, but the migration lands right back on Tanla's doorstep — because it routes the new channels too.

In two years OTT roughly doubled its share of enterprise revenue — a fast, visible channel shift. Tanla is not being disintermediated by it; it is carrying it. The risk is the economics of the new channel, not the loss of the traffic.

So the substitution threat is real but double-sided. On one hand, SMS is the highest-take-rate mass channel Tanla has, and if it structurally shrinks, some of the best margin in the pipe erodes. On the other, WhatsApp and RCS are richer, higher-value channels that enterprises will pay more for — and Tanla is a leading Indian aggregator for exactly those. The channel changes; the intermediary can remain. The unresolved question is whether the new channels are as profitable for Tanla as the old one, or whether their owners — chiefly Meta and Google — capture the value the aggregator used to keep.

The dependency a first-timer should sit with: Meta and Google now sit upstream

SMS had one gatekeeper Tanla had to pay: the telecom operator. WhatsApp and RCS add a second gatekeeper above the operator — Meta and Google — who set the rules and the pricing of the new channels. When Meta withdrew certain WhatsApp incentives, it hit Tanla's margins directly, a live demonstration of who holds the whip. So the migration to OTT does not free Tanla from platform dependency; it swaps a telco-shaped dependency for a big-tech-shaped one, and adds a powerful new counterparty to the value chain. The bull reads the OTT surge as Tanla successfully riding the next wave; the bear reads it as trading a familiar squeeze for a less predictable one. Both are watching the same rising line and drawing opposite conclusions — the FY26 margin softness is the first data point in that argument.

There is also the ILD wildcard. International long-distance traffic is higher-margin but genuinely volatile — Tanla has been through a rough patch of ILD "clean-up," shedding low-quality volumes and re-focusing on more profitable international routes and customers. Handled well, ILD is a margin sweetener; handled badly, or hit by a route disruption, it is a source of ugly quarters. It is one more reason a single quarter's numbers can mislead, and why the trend in gross profit matters more than any one print.

08

Cash, buybacks and capital allocation

The quiet strength — a debt-free, cash-generative business that keeps returning money.

For all the debate about margins and channel shift, one thing about Tanla is unambiguously good: it turns profit into cash and gives a lot of it back. This is not a capital-hungry manufacturer; it is an asset-light software-and-services business whose main "investment" is people and technology. That shows up as strong free cash flow, a debt-free balance sheet, and a persistent habit of dividends and buybacks — capital discipline that is rarer than it should be among founder-run Indian tech companies.

Free cash flow of ~₹477 Cr was roughly 94% of PAT — high cash conversion for the sector — leaving over ₹1,140 Cr of cash against negligible debt even after money went back to shareholders. In a business whose reported profit can wobble quarter to quarter, that near-one-to-one translation of profit into cash is the reassurance the income statement cannot give on its own.

The capital-return record is concrete. In July 2025 the board approved a ~₹175 Cr buyback (about 2 million shares, ~1.5% of equity), one of several buybacks the company has run over the years, alongside a regular dividend (a ~2% yield and a payout ratio around 31%). For a business generating cash it cannot fully reinvest at high returns, buying back stock at a modest ~14–15× multiple is a sensible use of capital — it quietly lifts per-share value while the operating debate plays out.

What the cash tells you that the P&L doesn't

A flat-profit year like FY26 can read as stagnation on the income statement, but the cash flow tells a steadier story: the business still converted almost all of its profit into real cash and handed a good chunk back. That matters for two reasons. First, it means the "flat PAT" is not a mirage propped up by aggressive accounting — the cash is genuinely there. Second, it gives Tanla optionality: a debt-free, cash-rich company can fund the Wisely international push, absorb a bad ILD quarter, or buy another asset (as it did with ValueFirst) without stress. In a business whose earnings can wobble quarter to quarter, a fortress balance sheet is not a footnote — it is what lets the founder keep reinventing the model without betting the company each time.

09

The industry and the rivals

A global CPaaS market with a handful of scaled players — and Tanla's peculiar profitability.

CPaaS is a global business, and Tanla competes in a field of much larger and much smaller names. What sets it apart is not size — several rivals are bigger by revenue — but profitability and cash generation. Many global CPaaS players have chased revenue at the expense of margin, or bled cash; Tanla has stayed stubbornly profitable and debt-free. That is the frame for comparing it to peers.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two features define this contest. First, the enterprise pipe is a near-commodity where the winners compete on cost, routing intelligence, compliance and reach — not on a differentiated product a customer falls in love with. That caps how much any aggregator can charge for plain SMS, which is why every serious player is scrambling upmarket into platforms, OTT and operator software. Second, Tanla's differentiator is not scale but discipline: while Twilio and others prioritised growth, Tanla ran the business for profit and cash. Whether that discipline is a durable edge or simply a smaller ambition is the crux — the same trait that keeps it debt-free could also keep it from ever becoming a global platform of consequence, unless Wisely breaks it out.

10

Financials & valuation

A cash-rich, profitable business on a modest multiple — priced for the doubt about growth.

FY26 was a year of steady growth and flat profit: revenue up ~9.7% to ~₹4,418 Cr, gross profit up ~11.8% to ~₹1,175 Cr, EBITDA up ~4.8% to ~₹724 Cr, but PAT essentially flat at ~₹509 Cr as pricing pressure and the WhatsApp-incentive withdrawal squeezed the pipe. The market rates the stock at roughly 14–15× earnings — cheap for a debt-free, high-cash-conversion, partly-software business, and a clear signal that investors doubt the durability of profit growth rather than the quality of the balance sheet.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A profitable, cash-rich aggregator trying to become a global platform — asked whether the mix can outrun the pipe.

Tanla enters FY27 with real assets: the country's largest A2P franchise, a regulator-anchored annuity in Trubloq, a genuine international proof point for Wisely, a fortress balance sheet, and a founder with a track record of reinvention. What it must prove is that the high-margin platform leg can grow fast enough — and the OTT transition can be monetised richly enough — to overpower the relentless price deflation in the commodity SMS pipe. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Mix is already working — gross profit outgrew revenue in FY26 and OTT roughly doubled its share in two years; the shift toward higher take-rate is visible, not hypothetical.
  • Trubloq annuity — a high-margin, high-volume, regulation-anchored platform processing a large share of India's commercial SMS; hard to replicate, hard to dislodge.
  • Wisely goes global — the Indosat deployment (100M users, 2bn+ threats blocked, ~10% ARPU uplift) is a template that, if repeated, reprices Tanla as a global operator-software vendor.
  • Fortress balance sheet — debt-free, ~94% cash conversion, consistent buybacks and dividends at a modest ~14–15× multiple; downside is cushioned by cash and capital returns.
  • Founder discipline — Uday Reddy's long tenure and large stake have delivered profitable reinvention where global peers burned cash chasing revenue.

The bear case

  • The pipe is deflationary — commodity SMS is a price war between multi-sourcing enterprises and operators who raise termination charges; the take-rate is structurally squeezed.
  • New gatekeepers upstream — Meta (WhatsApp) and Google (RCS) now sit above the aggregator and set OTT economics; Meta's incentive withdrawal already dented FY26 margins.
  • Regulatory dependence — Trubloq exists by TRAI mandate; a change to the DLT framework, fees or competitive structure could reprice the best annuity Tanla owns.
  • Wisely unproven at scale — one lighthouse operator deal is a signal, not a trend; if international replication stalls, the growth engine stays small and India-bound.
  • ILD & concentration volatility — international routes and large-client dependence make individual quarters lumpy; flat FY26 PAT may prove the new normal, not a blip.
The bottom line

Tanla Platforms is the profitable, disciplined exception in a global CPaaS field littered with cash-burning growth stories — a company that quietly carries billions of India's messages, owns a regulator-blessed anti-spam rail, throws off cash, and returns it. Its problem is not quality; it is trajectory. The pipe that gives it scale is a price war it can only manage, not win, and the platform that gives it margin is still small and — in Wisely's case — early. The next test is concrete and datable: whether gross profit keeps outgrowing revenue through FY27 as the platform-and-OTT share climbs, and whether the Indosat template turns into a second and third live Wisely operator abroad. Deliver both and a debt-free compounder on ~15× looks mispriced; deliver neither, and FY26's flat profit is the shape of things to come. For this company the argument is not settled in the revenue line the headlines quote — it is settled in the ₹1,175 Cr the business actually keeps, and where the next hundred crore of it comes from.
