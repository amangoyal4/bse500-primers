KRBL, the world's basmati landlord

A complete walk through the company behind India Gate — the world's largest basmati-rice miller and the owner of the country's number-one rice brand. How a business turns a monsoon-grown paddy crop into a premium, aged, branded pack; why its warehouses full of slowly-maturing rice are the strategy rather than a burden; how it splits its life between the Indian kitchen and the Gulf dinner table; and why an old money-laundering case still hangs quietly over the share.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

KRBL Limited is the company that puts India Gate basmati rice on kitchen shelves in India and across the Gulf. Founded in 1889, it is the world's largest basmati-rice miller and marketer, running the biggest single rice-processing plant on Earth at Dhuri in Punjab. It is fully integrated — from seed development and farmer procurement, through drying, ageing and milling, to the branded 1kg and 5kg packs a shopper actually buys. Alongside India Gate it sells Unity, Nur Jahan, Doon, Bemisal and a growing basket of non-basmati rice, edible oils and health grains.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Its main raw material has to sit still for a year or more before it can be sold. Premium basmati is aged — stored 12 to 24 months so the grain matures, elongates on cooking and commands a higher price. That single agronomic fact makes KRBL, unusually for a consumer-goods company, an inventory business: it held ~324 days of inventory in FY25 and carried roughly ₹3,900 Cr of paddy and rice on its balance sheet in late FY26. The warehouse is the factory floor.
  • It owns the brand that dominates a duopoly. In branded basmati, India Gate holds about a 42% market share; its only real rival, LT Foods' Daawat, holds roughly 30%. Between them the two control the vast majority of India's branded basmati shelf — a genuine consumer-goods moat built on decades of trust, not a commodity trade. KRBL has publicly set a target of 60% share by 2028.
  • FY26 was a record home year with a lumpy export year. Domestic revenue hit an all-time high of ₹4,444 Cr (+10%) and total revenue reached ~₹6,098 Cr, lifting net profit ~36% to ~₹648 Cr. But exports — heavily skewed to the Middle East — wobbled as Red Sea shipping disruption raised freight and stretched transit times. The stock trades on a low multiple (~11× earnings) with an almost debt-free, cash-rich balance sheet.
In plain terms

Most FMCG companies want to hold as little stock as possible — inventory is dead cash. KRBL is the rare consumer-goods business where the opposite is true: its product is designed to be locked in a warehouse for a year or more, getting better and more valuable while it waits. So the company runs two engines at once. One is a slow, capital-hungry commodity engine — buy a whole year's paddy in a three-month harvest window, finance it, and store it while it ages. The other is a fast, high-trust branding engine — the India Gate name, which lets it sell that aged rice at a premium and take the biggest slice of a two-player market. The investment question is whether the branding engine keeps earning enough premium to justify all the cash the ageing engine ties up.

01

Why aged basmati is a different product

No cooking degree needed — just what makes premium rice premium, and where the money hides.

Rice is not one thing. At the cheap end it is a bulk commodity sold by the tonne on price alone. At the premium end sits basmati — a long-grain, aromatic rice grown only in a specific belt of north-west India and Pakistan, prized for the way each grain elongates and stays separate when cooked. KRBL lives almost entirely at that premium end, and its whole model rests on one property of basmati that ordinary rice does not share: it improves with age.

The only rice terms you need for KRBL
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
BasmatiThe aromatic, long-grain premium rice from a protected north-Indian geography. It sells on brand, grain length and aroma — not on price per kilo. KRBL's core.
AgeingStoring milled or paddy rice for 12–24 months so moisture stabilises and the grain matures. Aged basmati cooks longer, fluffier and separates better — and fetches a clear price premium. This is why KRBL's warehouses are full for a reason.
PaddyThe raw, un-milled rice as harvested from the field, still in its husk. Basmati paddy is available only in a short October–December window each year, so a full year's needs must be bought in one burst.
Non-basmatiOrdinary rice varieties (and value brands). Lower margin, but a large volume market KRBL is now pushing into hard to grow its branded footprint beyond premium basmati.
RealisationThe average price KRBL actually gets per kilo. For a brand like India Gate this sits well above the commodity price — the gap is the reward for ageing, milling quality and the brand.
The single quirk that shapes the whole business: basmati is bought once a year but sold every day

Basmati paddy is harvested in a narrow window from roughly October to December. But households buy rice all year round. So a basmati company has to purchase almost its entire annual raw-material requirement in one short, cash-intensive season, then hold it and release finished rice steadily over the following twelve to twenty-four months. That mismatch — a once-a-year buy against an every-day sell, made longer still by the need to age the grain — is the source of both KRBL's biggest cost (working capital) and its biggest edge (a huge, ageing stockpile no small rival can afford to build). Understand that timing mismatch and most of the balance sheet stops looking strange.

Where basmati is grown, and who buys it

  • The growing belt — basmati is a geographically-protected crop (GI-tagged) grown across Punjab, Haryana, western UP, Uttarakhand, J&K and parts of Pakistan. That scarcity of origin is part of the premium.
  • The Indian household — the domestic buyer treats branded basmati as an everyday-premium staple: bought for biryanis, pulao and daily meals, increasingly by brand name off a supermarket or kirana shelf.
  • The export buyer — the Middle East is the world's great basmati consumer, especially Saudi Arabia and the Gulf, alongside a historically large but politically fraught Iran market. India is the dominant global basmati exporter, and KRBL is its single largest branded name abroad.
02

The India Gate machine: a brand, not a bag of rice

The most valuable thing KRBL owns doesn't sit in a warehouse — it sits in the shopper's head.

Strip away the mills and the ageing sheds and the real asset is a name. India Gate is the largest-selling packaged basmati brand in India and one of the most recognised basmati brands in the world. In a category where the underlying grain is broadly similar between serious players, a trusted brand is what converts a commodity into a premium, repeat-purchase consumer good — and it is what lets KRBL take the largest single slice of a two-horse market.

KRBL runs a stable of brands rather than one, so it can price across the pyramid without diluting the flagship. India Gate is the premium mother-brand; Unity, Nur Jahan, Doon and Bemisal reach mid-market and regional buyers; and a growing line of branded non-basmati rice extends the name into the far larger everyday-rice market. In FY26 that non-basmati push was the fastest-growing part of the company — up around 38% year on year — a deliberate attempt to sell the KRBL distribution and brand machine more product per shelf.

Why the brand is worth more than the mill

Milling capacity can be bought; a 137-year-old consumer relationship cannot. The reason India Gate matters is that it lets KRBL charge a realisation above the raw grain and be chosen by name at the shelf, season after season, which turns a weather-driven agri-commodity into something closer to a packaged-foods business. That is also why management is willing to invest in new packaging (refreshed in FY26 for the first time in 25 years), regional brands and non-basmati extensions: every point of branded share is worth more, and stickier, than an extra tonne of undifferentiated rice. The brand is the part of KRBL that behaves like an FMCG stock; the inventory is the part that behaves like a commodity trader.

03

Seed to shelf: the integrated value chain

KRBL touches the grain at every stage from the field to the pack — and that is deliberate.

KRBL calls itself India's first fully integrated rice company, and the phrase is doing real work. Rather than simply buying rice and slapping a label on it, it participates across the whole chain — developing seeds, engaging farmers, procuring paddy, drying, ageing, milling, grading, branding and distributing. Each step it owns is a step where quality is controlled and a bit of margin is captured rather than paid away.

Owning the middle two stages — ageing and processing at scale — is what lets KRBL guarantee a consistent premium grain that smaller traders cannot, and is where the working capital lives.

The manufacturing backbone

  • Dhuri, Punjab — acquired in 2003 and expanded into what is described as the largest single rice-processing plant in the world. It is the physical heart of the company: procurement, ageing, milling and packing at a scale no domestic rival matches.
  • Ghaziabad / other units — additional processing and packaging capacity closer to markets, supporting the branded and export basket.
  • Ageing warehouses — the less glamorous but decisive asset: the covered storage that lets KRBL hold a full year's crop and mature it. This capacity is a barrier to entry in itself; you cannot sell aged rice you did not have the space and capital to store two years ago.
The moat hiding in the boring parts of the chain

It is tempting to see the brand as the whole moat. But the brand is only credible because the chain behind it delivers a consistently aged, consistently graded grain at national scale — something that requires owning ageing capacity, milling capacity and procurement reach that took decades and a lot of capital to build. A new entrant with money can buy advertising; it cannot instantly buy two years of aged inventory, the warehouses to hold it, or the farmer relationships to source a full crop in a three-month window. The integration is the defensibility. It is also, unavoidably, the reason the balance sheet is so heavy.

04

The ageing trade: when inventory is the asset

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a premium-basmati house.

At a soap or biscuit company, a warehouse full of stock is a warning sign — cash frozen, product going stale. At KRBL it is the opposite: the rice in the shed is appreciating while it sits there, and the size of that stockpile is a measure of the company's staying power. To read KRBL you have to invert the normal instinct about inventory and see the warehouse as a maturing cellar rather than a cost.

KRBL held roughly 324 days of inventory in FY25 — nearly a full year. In almost any other consumer business that would signal distress. Here it is the product doing its job: ageing. The rough FMCG/processor figures are illustrative benchmarks.

Peel that stockpile apart and it is two different things at two different stages. As of late FY26 (December 2025), total inventory of about ₹3,941 Cr split into roughly ₹1,322 Cr of paddy — the raw crop just bought and waiting to be processed — and ₹2,450 Cr of rice, largely finished or semi-finished basmati sitting through its ageing window. Paddy is bought in the harvest burst; rice is released steadily over the year that follows, so the paddy line swells after each October–December season and drains through the year. The whole balance sheet breathes to that annual rhythm.

The device that only fits a premium-basmati house: the warehouse is a wine cellar, not a stockroom

The number that runs KRBL is not sales per store or price per kilo — it is the spread between what a tonne of paddy costs at harvest and what the same rice fetches as aged, branded India Gate two years later, minus the cost of financing and storing it in between. That is a carry trade dressed as a grocery business: buy the crop cheap in a glut season, hold it while it literally gets better, and sell it dear under a trusted name. It works because three things line up — basmati genuinely improves with age (so time adds value), the harvest is seasonal (so patient buyers get a better entry price), and the brand lets KRBL capture that added value at the till. Break any one link — a bad crop-price call, a cash crunch that forces early selling, a brand that stops commanding its premium — and the carry turns into exactly the dead-inventory problem it looks like from the outside.

Here is the counter-intuitive pay-off: because that inventory holds and gains value rather than spoiling, KRBL can carry an enormous stockpile without a big permanent debt load. Working-capital borrowings fell to about ₹334 Cr in FY25 and average utilisation of its credit lines ran near 8% over the following year — remarkably light for a business sitting on ₹3,900 Cr of stock. The company has been using its own cash flow, not the bank, to finance the crop. That is the difference between an ageing pile that is a strength and one that is a millstone.

05

The company, its owners, and the ED shadow

A promoter-run, cash-rich institution — with one legacy legal cloud that will not fully clear.

KRBL is a founder-family business of long standing, controlled by the promoter Gupta family with a substantial public and institutional float alongside. Its ownership is straightforward; its governance history is not entirely, because of a legacy money-laundering case that continues to sit over the name. Both halves of that picture matter to an investor.

Timeline

  • 1889
    Origins as a rice-trading business — one of the oldest continuously operating names in Indian rice, giving KRBL a genuinely rare heritage in a category built on trust.
  • 1993
    The India Gate brand is launched, moving KRBL from commodity trading toward a branded, premium consumer-goods model.
  • 2003
    Acquires the Dhuri, Punjab plant at a court auction and expands it into what becomes the largest rice-processing facility in the world.
  • 2000s–2010s
    Builds out ageing and milling scale, wind and biomass power, and a dominant export position in the Gulf; India Gate becomes the category leader at home.
  • 2021
    Joint MD Anoop Kumar Gupta is arrested by the ED under PMLA in connection with the AgustaWestland VVIP-helicopter case; granted bail within months. The matter remains under investigation.
  • FY24–FY25
    Basmati export headwinds ease as the government removes the Minimum Export Price (MEP) on basmati (Sept 2024); KRBL leans into branded non-basmati and diversification.
  • FY26
    Record domestic year: revenue ~₹6,098 Cr, domestic ₹4,444 Cr (+10%), PAT ~₹648 Cr (+36%); final dividend ₹4.50/share; near-debt-free, cash-rich balance sheet.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Gupta family holds roughly 60% of the company — a high, aligned promoter stake typical of a founder-run consumer business. The balance is public and institutional float.
  • Institutions: foreign investors hold around 7%, and — a detail that surprises many — government/LIC-linked holders own roughly 6%, a reminder that this is a widely-held, index-relevant consumer name, not a thinly-traded family shop.
  • Capital returns: with little debt and strong cash generation, KRBL pays a steady dividend (₹4.50/share final for FY26) and has periodically bought back stock — the pay-off of an inventory model financed largely from internal cash rather than borrowing.
The ED / PMLA overhang — stated factually: KRBL's joint managing director was arrested by the Enforcement Directorate in 2021 under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act in connection with the long-running AgustaWestland VVIP-helicopter investigation, and was granted bail. The case concerns historical transactions alleged to involve overseas entities linked to the promoter; it remains under investigation and has not, to date, disrupted the operating business. It is nonetheless a live governance and headline risk that a prospective investor must weigh and monitor through primary legal and disclosure sources — nothing here is a conclusion about guilt or outcome.
06

Two markets: the Indian kitchen and the Gulf table

One company, two very different demand engines — one steady, one geopolitical.

KRBL's revenue lives a double life. About three-quarters of it is domestic — the steady, brand-driven Indian retail business built on India Gate. The rest is export, overwhelmingly to the Middle East, where basmati is a staple and India is the dominant supplier but where politics, sanctions and shipping lanes can move the numbers around from quarter to quarter. Reading KRBL means holding both engines at once.

The home market is the ballast — record and growing steadily; exports are the swing factor. Roughly a 74/26 domestic-export split in FY26.

The domestic engine is the one an FMCG investor recognises: brand-led, distribution-heavy, growing volume and value together. FY26 was its best year yet, with a standout Q4 that grew domestic revenue ~22% on volume plus price, and the fast-growing branded non-basmati line adding a second leg. This is the part of KRBL that justifies thinking of it as a consumer-staples company at all.

The export engine is where the geopolitics live. The Middle East accounts for the large majority of KRBL's exports — around 61% of export sales in FY25, with Saudi Arabia the anchor market. That concentration is a strength in normal times (deep, loyal demand for premium Indian basmati) and a liability in disrupted ones. In Q4 FY26, exports fell sharply — to about ₹279 Cr from ₹450 Cr a year earlier — as Red Sea shipping disruption raised freight costs and lengthened transit times, stranding shipments and denting realisations. Management framed it as temporary, even flagging that food-security stockpiling could rebound once lanes normalise.

The uncomfortable history behind the export line: Iran

The export book carries a memory that a first-timer should know. For years Iran was one of the largest buyers of Indian basmati, and Indian exporters — KRBL among them — built up sizeable receivables there. When international sanctions on Iran tightened, getting paid became genuinely difficult: money could not move freely, and exporters were left carrying balances they struggled to collect. The lesson embedded in that episode still applies: KRBL's export margins can look attractive, but a chunk of the customer base sits in markets where payment and settlement are hostage to geopolitics. The Gulf is more reliable than Iran was, but the concentration risk — one region, exposed to sanctions, shipping and diplomacy — never fully goes away.

07

The rice mill that quietly generates 149 MW

The part of KRBL almost nobody expects — and what it says about how the company thinks.

Here is the fact that surprises people who assume KRBL is "just a rice company": it also runs a sizeable power business. Across wind, solar and biomass, the group operates roughly 149 MW of generation capacity — more than some listed mid-size power producers — and it turns its own rice husk, a waste product of milling, into energy. The rice company is also, in effect, a small renewables company bolted onto a food business.

Why does a rice miller own wind farms? Two reasons, both revealing. First, cost and reliability: milling and drying are energy-intensive, and self-generation shields margins from grid tariffs and outages while monetising husk that would otherwise be waste. Second, it reflects a conservative, self-reliant temperament that runs through the whole company — the same instinct that keeps the balance sheet nearly debt-free while carrying ₹3,900 Cr of stock, and that builds ageing capacity years ahead of need. KRBL tends to own its dependencies rather than rent them.

The diversification push beyond basmati

  • Branded non-basmati rice — the biggest growth lever, up ~38% in FY26; it stretches the India Gate distribution machine into the far larger everyday-rice market.
  • Edible oils — a small but growing FMCG adjacency, using the same brand trust and retail shelf.
  • Health & specialty grains — quinoa, chia, flax, brown rice and ready-to-cook lines aimed at premium, health-conscious urban buyers — a natural extension of a trusted staples brand.
What the power plants really tell you

The 149 MW is not, on its own, a big earnings driver. Its value is as a tell: it shows a management that prefers to own the awkward, capital-heavy links in its chain — energy, ageing capacity, milling scale — rather than depend on outsiders. That temperament is why KRBL can sit on a mountain of ageing inventory without leverage, and why it survives the bad basmati years that periodically thin out smaller traders. The same conservatism that some investors read as under-geared and slow is exactly what lets the ageing model work without blowing up. The power business is a small window onto a big habit.

08

KRBL vs Daawat: the basmati duopoly

Two brands, two strategies, one shelf — and a real difference in how each grows.

Branded basmati in India is, to a first approximation, a two-company market: KRBL's India Gate and LT Foods' Daawat. They look similar from the checkout, but they are built and run differently, and the contrast is the clearest way to understand what kind of company KRBL actually is.

The tell is in the market caps. Despite leading the domestic branded market, KRBL carries a smaller market value than LT Foods — the market pays up for Daawat's broader geographic spread, faster diversification and growth cadence, while pricing India Gate as the steadier, cheaper, more India-anchored franchise. Both moved together when the government scrapped basmati's Minimum Export Price in 2024, and both wobble on the same Gulf-shipping news — proof they share one weather system even as they play it differently.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

This is not a price war. With two entrenched brands owning most of the shelf and a long tail of small players below, the contest is fought on brand strength, distribution reach, product mix and the discipline of the ageing/procurement engine — not on discounting a near-identical grain. That structure is good for both incumbents: it makes share durable and premiums defensible. Where they diverge is ambition. LT Foods is trying to become a global convenience-foods company that started in rice; KRBL is trying to own more of the Indian rice shelf, more deeply, from a fortress balance sheet. An investor is really choosing between those two temperaments.

09

Financials & valuation

A record home year and a fortress balance sheet, on a distinctly un-FMCG multiple.

FY26 was a strong year led by the domestic brand: revenue up ~9% to ~₹6,098 Cr, net profit up ~36% to ~₹648 Cr, on record domestic sales and improving realisations, partly offset by a soft export quarter. What stands out against a typical branded-foods peer is not the growth but the capital structure: KRBL is essentially debt-free, cash-generative, and yet trades on a low-teens earnings multiple — the market treating it more like a commodity processor than the branded consumer leader it also is.

Two features of the balance sheet deserve emphasis because they explain the low multiple and the resilience at once. First, debt is minimal (of the order of ₹170 Cr) against a large cash and treasury position — unusual for a business carrying ₹3,900 Cr of inventory, and the reason KRBL rides out bad basmati cycles that break smaller, borrowed traders. Second, the returns are respectable but not spectacular — ROE in the low teens, ROCE mid-teens — held down precisely by all that inventory and cash sitting on the books. The market, in effect, discounts the company for being over-capitalised and commodity-exposed, even as it leads a branded duopoly.

The one number that settles the thesis: blended realisation, read with domestic branded volume

KRBL is cheap enough that the case doesn't need a re-rating — it needs proof that the brand keeps converting aged inventory into premium price. So the metric that proves or breaks it is blended realisation per kilo (is India Gate still commanding its premium, or is the aged rice being sold into a soft market?), read alongside domestic branded volume growth and the march of India Gate's market share toward the stated 60% goal. If realisation holds and domestic branded volume compounds while non-basmati scales, the low multiple looks like the market mistaking a branded leader for a rice trader. If realisation slips — a weak crop-price call, a soft export market forcing aged stock out at thinner spreads — then the heavy balance sheet stops being a fortress and starts being trapped cash. Price per kilo times branded volume, financed from own cash — that is the scoreboard.

10

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, cash-rich category leader — asked whether it can keep converting patience into premium.

KRBL enters FY27 in a strong position: record domestic sales, a fortress balance sheet, a fast-growing non-basmati leg, an eased export-policy backdrop after the MEP removal, and a stated ambition to push India Gate toward 60% branded share. What it cannot fully control is the weather over its crop, the politics over its export lanes, and the legal cloud over its promoter. That trio frames both cases.

The bull case

  • Category leader on a value multiple — ~42% branded basmati share and a record domestic year, yet only ~11× earnings; little optimism is priced in.
  • Fortress balance sheet — near debt-free and cash-rich while self-financing ₹3,900 Cr of ageing inventory; it can out-wait weaker rivals through a bad crop cycle.
  • A real second growth leg — branded non-basmati up ~38%, plus edible oils and health grains, all riding the existing India Gate distribution.
  • Share-gain ambition — a stated 60%-by-2028 target, new packaging after 25 years, and a duopoly structure that makes premium and share durable.
  • Policy tailwind & structural demand — MEP on basmati removed; India remains the dominant global basmati exporter into a loyal Gulf market.

The bear case

  • Export concentration & geopolitics — ~61% of exports to one region; Red Sea disruption already hit a quarter, and the Iran history shows how payment risk bites.
  • Crop & price-timing risk — buying a full year of paddy in one window is a bet; a bad price call or weak crop compresses the ageing spread.
  • Heavy, cash-trapping inventory — the ageing model ties up enormous working capital and holds ROE in the low teens; over-capitalisation is the flip side of the fortress.
  • The ED / PMLA overhang — an unresolved money-laundering investigation touching the promoter is a live governance and headline risk.
  • Commodity perception cap — the market keeps rating it like a rice trader, not an FMCG brand; the re-rating may simply not come.
The bottom line

KRBL is the closest thing Indian rice has to a fortress: the leading brand in a two-player premium category, financing a mountain of appreciating inventory almost entirely from its own cash, and priced as if it were a commodity processor rather than the consumer leader it also is. The self-help is genuine — a fast-growing non-basmati leg, a packaging refresh, a share-gain target — and the balance sheet gives it the patience the ageing model demands. But the three forces that shape any given year sit outside its walls: the crop and the price it pays for it, the shipping lanes and politics of the Gulf, and an unresolved legal cloud over the promoter. The next concrete test is datable and specific: whether blended realisation and domestic branded volume keep climbing through the FY27 kharif crop and the next export season while India Gate's share pushes toward its 60% goal. Hold the premium and keep gaining share, and a cash-rich category leader on 11× looks mispriced; lose the premium to a soft crop or a stranded export book, and the fortress balance sheet becomes a warehouse of trapped cash.
