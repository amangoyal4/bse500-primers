Neogen Chemicals, bromine, lithium, and a battery bet

A full walk through a three-decade-old specialty-chemistry house — India's quiet pioneer of bromine and lithium compounds sold to global pharma and agrochem — that is now pouring nearly ₹1,800 crore into electrolytes and lithium salts for the batteries India cannot yet make at scale. How the steady old business actually earns, why the new one could be five times its size, and why the balance sheet is the thing to watch.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Neogen Chemicals Limited makes specialty chemicals built on two elements: bromine and lithium. For thirty-odd years it has quietly been one of India's most established makers of bromine-based compounds (organic and inorganic bromides used as building blocks in pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, flavours & fragrances and electronics) and lithium-based inorganic salts (lithium carbonate and bromide derivatives). It runs plants at Mahape (Navi Mumbai), Karakhadi (near Vadodara) and Dahej SEZ (Gujarat), sells a basket of a couple of hundred products, and exports a large share to the USA, Europe and Japan. This is a high-value niche chemistry business — not a bulk commodity maker.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • The old business is small, specialised and steady. In FY26 Neogen turned ~₹862 Cr of revenue into ~₹137 Cr of EBITDA — a mid-teens operating margin that reflects custom, made-to-spec chemistry rather than tonnage. Organic (bromine) chemicals are the larger leg at ~₹734 Cr; inorganic (lithium) salts add ~₹128 Cr. It has compounded sales at roughly 21–24% a year over a decade — respectable, unglamorous, cash-generative-when-not-investing.
  • The whole equity story is a bet on a business barely born. Neogen is building capacity for lithium-ion battery electrolytes and electrolyte salts through a subsidiary, Neogen Ionics, and a JV with Japan's Morita. That business earned about ₹36 Cr in FY26 — but management guides it toward ₹2,400–2,900 Cr by FY29. The market is not pricing the ₹29 Cr of current profit; it is pricing that ramp.
  • The bet is financed with debt, mid-build. Committed capex on the battery platform is about ₹1,795 Cr; net debt at FY26-end was already about ₹1,295 Cr against just ₹137 Cr of EBITDA. That mismatch — a small, steady chemicals cash flow underwriting a large, unproven energy-materials plant — is the tension in one line.
In plain terms

Neogen has spent three decades becoming very good at difficult small-batch chemistry — the bromine and lithium building blocks that a drug or a crop-protection molecule needs a few tonnes of, made to exacting spec. That business is real, profitable and growing steadily, but it is not why the stock trades where it does. The company has decided to use that platform — its lithium know-how, its Gujarat land, its chemical-engineering bench — to attack a completely different, far larger prize: making the electrolyte and the lithium salt that go inside every lithium-ion cell, so that India's coming battery factories don't have to import them from China. The catch is that India barely makes cells yet, the plants aren't finished, and the money to build them is borrowed. Everything about the next three years turns on whether that new business shows up on schedule and at scale.

01

Bromine & lithium 101: two elements, two franchises

No chemistry degree needed — just what these elements do and why a niche maker of them is valuable.

Neogen's whole identity sits on two spots in the periodic table. Bromine (Br) is a heavy, reactive halogen — a chemical building block that lets drug-makers and crop-scientists bolt a bromine atom onto a molecule as a stepping-stone in a synthesis. Lithium (Li) is the lightest metal — the same one that stores charge in a battery, but which Neogen has historically sold as salts for entirely non-battery uses. The company converts both into specialised, high-purity compounds that other manufacturers buy by the tonne, not the shipload. To follow it you need five terms.

The only chemistry terms you need

The genuine surprise: Neogen was a lithium company long before batteries

It is tempting to read "lithium" and assume Neogen rode the EV wave. It did the opposite — it has been India's largest importer of lithium carbonate and lithium hydroxide for roughly three decades, and one of its very first products, back in the mid-1980s, was lithium bromide. Its classic use was not power storage at all: lithium bromide is the working fluid in vapour-absorption air-conditioning and industrial chilling — the salt that lets a large building be cooled by waste heat instead of a compressor. So Neogen spent thirty years mastering lithium handling, purification and supply chains for reasons that had nothing to do with cars. The battery pivot is not a company chasing a fashion; it is a lithium specialist finally pointing its oldest competence at the largest market it has ever seen.

Who buys, and why the chemistry is defensible

  • Pharmaceutical companies — buy bromine intermediates and Grignard reagents as steps in a drug synthesis. Once a molecule is designed into a regulated process, the supplier is hard to switch out — a source of stickiness.
  • Agrochemical & specialty makers — use brominated and lithium building blocks in crop-protection actives, catalysts, flavours and fragrances, and electronic chemicals.
  • Export markets — USA, Europe, Japan — a large share of the organic book ships abroad, which makes Neogen a forex earner exposed to the rupee, and a beneficiary of global buyers wanting a non-China source of niche chemistry.
02

The custom-synthesis engine: how a niche chemist earns

Why a couple of hundred low-volume products can be a better business than one high-volume commodity.

Neogen's legacy economics are the opposite of a bulk-chemicals plant. It does not win by making the cheapest tonne of a single molecule; it wins by making many difficult molecules in small quantities to tight specification, where the barrier is process know-how, regulatory qualification and safety — not scale. That is why a business doing under ₹900 Cr of revenue can hold a mid-teens EBITDA margin and compound sales at 20%-plus for years.

The strategic direction is a tilt from catalogue products (a standard compound anyone can order) toward custom synthesis and CDMO work (a molecule made to a single customer's process, usually under contract). The difference matters for quality of earnings: a catalogue product competes on price and can be second-sourced; a custom molecule that a pharma customer has written into its regulatory filing is far stickier, carries better margins, and grows with the customer's own product. Neogen has been steadily adding this kind of work, alongside two dedicated R&D centres at Vadodara and Mahape that develop new processes and, crucially, developed the indigenous electrolyte-salt chemistry that underpins the battery bet.

Why small-batch chemistry resists competition

A commodity plant's moat is its cost curve; a specialty chemist's moat is its process library and its qualifications. Every difficult molecule Neogen has learned to make safely — a reactive Grignard, a high-purity brominated intermediate — is a process that took years to perfect and a customer relationship that took as long to qualify. A rival cannot simply undercut it, because the customer's regulator has already approved this supplier's this process. The trade-off is that such a business grows one hard-won molecule at a time; it does not scale in leaps. That patient, additive quality is exactly why Neogen's management wanted a second engine that could scale in leaps — and found it in batteries.

04

The company, the founder, and who controls it

A founder-run specialty chemist, promoter-controlled, now spending like a growth company.

Neogen is a first-generation, founder-built enterprise — which shapes both its patient chemistry culture and the boldness of its current bet. It was started by Dr. Haridas Kanani, a chemical engineer, who built it from a tiny lithium-bromide operation into a listed specialty-chemicals company, with the next generation now active in the business.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter (the Kanani family): holds roughly 53% (as of mid-2026) — a clear controlling stake that keeps the founder's long-horizon, chemistry-first culture in charge of a capital-intensive bet. Investors should confirm the exact figure in the latest shareholding filing.
  • Institutional interest: domestic institutions (DIIs) hold roughly 19–20% and foreign investors (FIIs) about 4% — meaningful institutional presence for a company this size, reflecting the battery-materials narrative.
  • Subsidiary & JV structure: the battery platform sits in Neogen Ionics (the subsidiary that books electrolyte and salt sales) with the NML Morita JV (≥80% Neogen / ≤20% Morita Investment) housing the LiPF₆ salt plant. Ring-fencing the new, capital-heavy activity from the steady core is deliberate.
Why the structure matters: a founder-controlled company can make a decade-long, balance-sheet-stretching bet without being second-guessed each quarter — an advantage when the payoff is years out. The flip side is concentration: the same control that enables patience also means minority investors are, in effect, backing the founder's judgement that India's battery ecosystem arrives before the debt taken on to serve it becomes a burden. The Morita JV and MUIS licence are the external validation that the chemistry is real; the leverage is the price of moving first.
05

The battery bet: electrolytes, salts, and a Japanese licence

The single reason this stock is interesting — and the single hardest thing to pull off.

Inside every lithium-ion cell is a liquid, the electrolyte, and dissolved in it a conducting salt, LiPF₆. Together they carry lithium ions between the electrodes every time the battery charges or discharges. Today India makes almost none of either — both are overwhelmingly imported, largely from China. Neogen's bet is to become the domestic supplier of both, using its lithium heritage plus licensed Japanese technology, just as India's own cell factories come online.

The plan has two plants and two technology sources:

  • Dahej — the electrolyte + salt facility. A greenfield plant in the Dahej PCPIR (Gujarat) with nameplate capacity for 30,000 MT of electrolytes and 5,500 MT of electrolyte salts and additives. The electrolyte formulation uses a manufacturing licence from MUIS — itself a JV of Japan's Mitsubishi Chemical and UBE — while the salts run on Neogen's own indigenous chemistry.
  • Pakhajan — the LiPF₆ salt plant (the Morita JV). Housed in Neogen Morita New Materials (NML), this makes solid LiPF₆, the hardest and most import-dependent piece of the electrolyte. Morita brings decades of Japanese salt-making expertise; Neogen holds the majority.

The 1-to-5 ratio that defines the bet: master the salt to sell the blend

Look at the Dahej nameplate: 5,500 MT of salts and additives feeding 30,000 MT of electrolyte — a deliberate roughly one-to-five ratio. The electrolyte is the large, visible volume; the salt is the small, difficult ingredient that makes it work. Anyone can, in principle, blend an electrolyte if they can buy the salt; almost no one outside China and a handful of Japanese and Korean firms can make the salt. Neogen has chosen to plant its flag on the hard link — LiPF₆ via the Morita JV — precisely because that is where the scarcity, the pricing power and the import-substitution value sit. It is building the difficult 5,500 tonnes so it can credibly sell the easy 30,000, rather than being a blender at the mercy of a Chinese salt supplier. That choice is the whole strategy in one ratio.

06

The 70× question: re-rating thesis vs execution risk

The most important idea in the case — and one that could belong to no other company in the sector.

Here is the number that governs everything. Neogen's battery business earned about ₹36 Cr in FY26. Management's stated ambition is ₹2,400–2,900 Cr by FY29. That is not growth in the ordinary sense — it is a roughly 70-fold scale-up in three years, on plants that are not yet finished, into a domestic market that barely exists yet. The entire debate about the stock is whether that arc is a credible ramp or a hopeful projection.

The visual is deliberately brutal: FY26 is a sliver, FY29 is the whole bar. The credibility of the case rests on the middle bar — whether FY27 actually clears ~₹300 Cr is the first hard, datable test of the ramp.

Two things make the target more than a slide. First, the demand backdrop: India has committed, through the ACC PLI scheme and private plans, to build tens of gigawatt-hours of domestic cell capacity — and every one of those cells needs electrolyte and salt that is currently imported. Second, validation: three US-based electrolyte makers have completed audits of Neogen's facilities, and the Morita JV and MUIS licence are external endorsements that the chemistry meets global standards. The FY29 capacity is framed as enough to serve on the order of 30 GWh of electrolyte and 40 GWh of salt demand — a scale that only makes sense if India's cell build genuinely lands.

Why this is an execution problem, not a demand-forecasting one

The bull and bear here are not really arguing about whether India will eventually need domestic electrolyte — it will. They are arguing about timing and delivery. The plants must be commissioned on schedule (Dahej supply from around mid-2026, Pakhajan salt in H2 FY27); each product must be qualified by fussy cell-makers, a slow process; and the cell factories that buy the output must themselves come up on time. Any of those slipping by a year turns a ₹1,795 Cr asset into idle capacity carrying full interest cost while it waits for customers. This is the rare Indian specialty name whose risk is not the chemistry — that is licensed and partnered — but the synchronisation of three build-outs at once: Neogen's plant, its customers' qualification cycles, and India's gigafactories. Get them to line up and ₹36 Cr becomes ₹2,500 Cr; miss the alignment and the ramp simply arrives later, on borrowed money.

This is why the standalone valuation looks absurd on today's numbers and reasonable on tomorrow's. At a market capitalisation near ₹5,835 Cr against ₹29 Cr of profit, the trailing multiple is meaningless — you are not buying FY26 earnings, you are pre-paying for the FY29 battery business. The sensible way to hold the stock is to ignore the current P/E entirely and instead track the ramp milestones one by one, because each cleared milestone converts a slice of that ₹2,400–2,900 Cr ambition from hope into revenue.

07

The debt, the fire, and the balance-sheet stretch

The unglamorous reality that decides whether the bet is survivable.

A growth story financed with equity is patient; one financed with debt is on a clock. Neogen's battery build is largely the latter, which makes its balance sheet the most important page in its accounts right now. At FY26-end the company carried roughly ₹1,330 Cr of gross debt and ~₹1,295 Cr of net debt — against EBITDA of just ₹137 Cr. That is a business borrowing many multiples of its annual cash generation to build something that does not yet earn.

Committed capex and net debt each dwarf annual EBITDA roughly ten-fold. Until the battery plants earn, that gap is bridged by borrowing — which is why every quarter of ramp delay is expensive.

Then there is the fire. In FY25 a blaze at the Dahej plant damaged equipment and inventory, knocking an exceptional loss of about ₹14 Cr off profit and forcing management to cut FY26 revenue guidance from ~₹950–1,000 Cr toward ~₹775–850 Cr. It was an unwelcome reminder that this is physical, hazardous chemistry — Grignard reagents and lithium salts are not forgiving materials. The company has been recovering through insurance (on-account receipts of ~₹140 Cr, a net claim receivable of ~₹203 Cr, salvage of ~₹7 Cr) and expected to recommission around mid-2026, but the episode delayed the core business precisely as the battery capex was accelerating.

The counter-intuitive read on the balance sheet

It is worth sitting with an uncomfortable fact: Neogen's profit fell in FY26 (PAT down ~17% to ₹29 Cr) even as its stock treated it as a growth company, and its debt rose even as earnings shrank. Both are consistent — because the market is not valuing the profit, it is valuing the plants the profit is being spent to build. The danger in that arrangement is specific and mechanical: interest on ₹1,300-plus crore of debt is a fixed, monthly bill, while the battery revenue that is meant to service it is back-end-loaded to FY28–FY29. If the ramp lands on time, the leverage was simply the fuel that got Neogen to market first. If it slips, the same leverage becomes a headwind that compounds every quarter the plants sit under-utilised. Read the interest line and the commissioning dates together; they, not the P/E, tell you whether the bet is on track.

What to watch each quarter: net debt trend, interest cost, Neogen Ionics revenue against the >₹300 Cr FY27 marker, and confirmation that Dahej and Pakhajan commission on their stated timelines. A single delayed plant or a stalled customer qualification is the kind of thing that turns a re-rating candidate back into a leveraged chemicals company waiting for its market to arrive.
08

India's cell ecosystem and the China wall

The demand Neogen is building for — real in ambition, nascent in fact.

Neogen's battery revenue can only be as big as India's battery-cell manufacturing. And that is the honest weak point of the thesis: India today makes very few lithium-ion cells. The vast majority of cells in Indian EVs and storage are imported, and the electrolyte inside them is overwhelmingly Chinese. Neogen is, in effect, building a supplier for factories that are themselves still being built.

The bet rests on a structural shift the government is actively pushing:

  • The gigafactory build-out — under the ACC PLI scheme and private investment, several players (from established battery and auto names to new entrants) have announced large domestic cell plants. As these come up, they need electrolyte and salt near at hand — importing a flammable liquid across oceans is costly and slow.
  • Import substitution as the wedge — India imports essentially all of its LiPF₆ and electrolyte. A credible domestic maker with Japanese technology is exactly what a policy environment trying to localise the battery supply chain wants to encourage.
  • An export option too — the interest from US-based electrolyte makers hints that the play is not purely domestic: global buyers seeking a non-China source of salt and electrolyte are a second, optional demand pool.
The China reality to price in: China dominates global electrolyte and LiPF₆ manufacturing — with enormous installed capacity, deep cost advantages and, at times, aggressive pricing. A domestic Indian maker must clear that bar on cost and quality, not just on patriotism or logistics. Import-substitution economics help — as can any tariff or localisation preference — but Neogen will be selling into a world where the incumbent supplier is large, entrenched and cheap. That is the competitive weather the battery business must survive.
The dependency a first-timer should internalise

Neogen does not control the pace of its own biggest opportunity. Its battery revenue is downstream of decisions made in gigafactory boardrooms and in government policy: how fast Indian cell capacity is actually commissioned, whether localisation incentives hold, and how cell-makers weigh a home supplier against a cheaper Chinese import. Neogen can control its own plants, its chemistry and its qualifications — and it has moved early to control the hard salt link. What it cannot control is whether India's cell factories arrive on the timetable its ₹1,795 Cr of capex is underwritten against. That external dependency, more than any chemistry risk, is the real variable in the case.

09

The rivals: specialty chemist, not commodity miner

Why Neogen is best understood against two different competitive maps.

Neogen sits at the seam of two industries, so it has two competitive sets. In its legacy bromine-and-lithium chemistry it competes with other specialty and adjacent-halogen chemists; in its new electrolyte business it will square up against the global — largely Chinese — battery-materials industry. The most important distinction to hold is what Neogen is not.

The distinction that reframes the whole company

The easiest mistake is to file Neogen next to a commodity bromine or salt producer like Archean and value it on the same cyclical logic. That gets it backwards. Archean's edge is a low-cost resource at the base of the chain — it sells the raw halogen. Neogen's edge is process knowledge at the top of the chain — it buys the halogen and turns it into molecules a regulated pharma customer will pay a premium for, and now into a battery salt only a handful of firms worldwide can make. One competes on cost of extraction; the other on difficulty of chemistry. Judge Neogen by commodity-producer yardsticks and you will both misprice the steady legacy business and completely miss the point of the battery bet, which is a specialty-chemistry play on a scarce, defensible link — not a bulk-materials play on tonnage.

Financials & valuation

A modestly profitable core carrying a very large option — priced almost entirely on the option.

Neogen's accounts tell two stories at once. The legacy business is a steady, mid-teens-margin specialty chemist that grew revenue ~11% in FY26 to ~₹862 Cr. This is not a company you value on trailing profit; it is one you value on the probability and scale of a ramp that has barely begun.

On any trailing measure the stock looks extreme — a market capitalisation near ₹5,835 Cr on ₹29 Cr of profit is a P/E well into the triple digits, and enterprise value against ₹137 Cr of EBITDA is similarly stretched once ₹1,295 Cr of net debt is added. Those numbers are not the point; they are simply what happens when a company is mid-build. The Q1 FY27 print — consolidated revenue up 34% to ~₹250 Cr and EBITDA up ~53% — is the first small evidence that the ramp is real. But one quarter is not a trend.

The one number that settles the thesis: Neogen Ionics revenue, and the ROCE behind it

Forget the P/E. The metric that will prove or break this specific case is Neogen Ionics revenue — and the return on capital it earns. The ambition is stark and datable: from ~₹36 Cr in FY26, past ₹300 Cr in FY27, toward ₹2,400–2,900 Cr by FY29 at a target 18–20% ROCE. That last clause matters as much as the revenue — a battery business that hits ₹2,500 Cr of sales but only single-digit returns (because Chinese pricing crushes margins) would validate the demand thesis and still destroy value. So the scoreboard has two columns: does the top line clear the ~₹300 Cr FY27 gate and keep climbing, and does it do so at a return that justifies the ₹1,795 Cr and the debt behind it. If both hold, the current valuation is a down-payment on a much larger company; if the revenue ramps but the returns don't, Neogen will have built India's electrolyte plant and handed the profits to price competition.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A steady chemist making a large, levered leap — asked whether the leap lands on time.

Neogen enters FY27 as two companies bolted together: a dependable, export-earning specialty chemist crossing ~₹1,000 Cr of core revenue, and a barely-born battery-materials venture underwritten by ~₹1,795 Cr of capex and a Japanese salt partner. The first is proven; the second is the reason anyone is watching. Both cases below turn on the same pivot — the timing of India's battery demand against the clock on Neogen's debt.

The bull case

  • A scarce, defensible link — the Morita LiPF₆ JV puts Neogen on the hardest, most import-dependent piece of the electrolyte, where few firms outside China can compete.
  • A steady core underneath — a three-decade bromine/lithium specialty book, export-heavy and sticky, crossing ~₹1,000 Cr and funding part of the bet.
  • Genuine validation — MUIS licence, Morita partnership and audits by US electrolyte makers say the chemistry meets global standards, not just Indian ones.
  • Structural tailwind — India must localise its battery supply chain; every domestic gigawatt-hour is electrolyte and salt Neogen could supply instead of China.
  • Early proof — Q1 FY27 revenue up 34% and the FY27 >₹300 Cr Ionics guidance suggest the ramp has actually started.

The bear case

  • Demand-timing risk — India's cell ecosystem is nascent; if gigafactories slip, ₹1,795 Cr of plant sits idle carrying full interest cost.
  • Leverage — ~₹1,295 Cr net debt against ₹137 Cr EBITDA; a levered balance sheet servicing back-loaded revenue is unforgiving of delay.
  • The China wall — incumbent Chinese electrolyte/LiPF₆ makers are vast and cheap; Neogen must win on cost and quality, not just location.
  • Execution & safety — three build-outs must synchronise; and the FY25 Dahej fire showed how physical hazard can derail even the core.
  • Valuation air — priced almost entirely on FY29 ambition; any faltering in the ramp removes the only justification for the multiple.
The bottom line

Neogen Chemicals is a patient, founder-built specialty chemist that has chosen to make an impatient, capital-hungry bet: to hand India a domestic source of the electrolyte and lithium salt its batteries will need. The strategy is coherent and the chemistry is credible — it is aiming its oldest competence, lithium, at the biggest market it will ever address, and it has moved early to own the scarce link rather than blend around it. What it cannot manufacture is time. The next twelve months are unusually concrete: Dahej beginning supply around mid-2026, Pakhajan's LiPF₆ salt commercialising in H2 FY27, and Neogen Ionics revenue clearing the ~₹300 Cr FY27 marker on its way toward FY29. Hit those and the levered balance sheet reads as the price of a first-mover; miss them and it reads as a bill arriving before the customer does. This is one of the few Indian chemicals stories where the chemistry is the easy part and the calendar is the hard one — watch the commissioning dates and the Ionics top line, in that order.
