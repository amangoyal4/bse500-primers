00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Poly Medicure — "Polymed" — makes the single-use plastic-and-metal devices that flow through a hospital by the millions: intravenous (IV) cannulae, infusion and blood-transfusion sets, dialysis bloodlines, urology catheters, surgical drains, and anaesthesia and respiratory disposables. These are the humble essentials a nurse opens, uses on one patient, and discards. Polymed is India's largest home-grown medical-device maker of this kind, and it is a genuinely global one — a top-three player worldwide in IV cannulae, selling into 125+ countries.

Three facts frame everything:

  • An export machine, not just an Indian one. FY26 consolidated revenue was ~₹1,875 Cr (+12.3% YoY), and roughly 69% of it was exported — Europe (~₹597 Cr) and the Rest of the World (~₹683 Cr) together out-earn the Indian home market (~₹582 Cr). Polymed is India's low-cost, high-IP device factory selling to the world, competing head-on with the global majors on price and patents.
  • Recurring by design. Because every procedure consumes and discards the device, demand refreshes with each patient — a razor-and-blade dynamic where the "blade" (the disposable) is the whole business. Volumes track the sheer number of hospital procedures worldwide, not a replacement cycle.
  • Profit dipped in a growth year — on purpose. Even as revenue rose 12%, FY26 PAT slipped ~5% to ~₹321 Cr and return on capital employed fell from ~24% toward ~14%. Nothing broke. Polymed raised a ₹1,000 Cr QIP, bought three European companies, and is pouring new plant capacity — capital that sits idle before it earns. The whole investment debate is whether that build-out pays off.
In plain terms

Polymed is the Indian company that quietly supplies the world's hospitals with the disposable plumbing of modern medicine. Each item is cheap and unglamorous — a cannula, a drip set, a dialysis line — but a hospital burns through them endlessly, and someone has to make billions of them to exacting sterile-quality standards at a price the world can afford. Polymed does that from Indian factories, protects it with an unusually deep patent wall for safety-engineered devices, and ships most of the output abroad. Right now it is spending heavily to make more, and to move into pricier products — which is why today's profit looks flat while tomorrow's capacity gets built.

01

Single-use medical devices 101

No clinical background needed — just why a two-rupee tube is a real business.

Medicine runs on disposables. Every time a patient gets a drip, a blood transfusion, a dialysis session, a surgery drain or an anaesthetic, one or more sterile, single-use devices are opened, used once, and thrown away — never reused, because reuse risks infection. That "use once, discard" rule is the entire economic engine: it turns a manufactured object into a consumable, bought again and again for as long as the procedure keeps happening.

The vocabulary you actually need
TermWhat it means (in Polymed's world)
IV cannulaThe thin tube inserted into a vein to deliver fluids or drugs — the single most-used device in any hospital. Polymed is a global top-3 maker; "safety" versions shield the needle to prevent needlestick injuries.
Infusion / IV setThe drip line and chamber connecting the fluid bag to the cannula. Sold with every IV therapy. Polymed's largest single product family.
Single-use / disposableUsed on one patient, then discarded. This is what makes demand recurring rather than one-off.
ConsumableA product consumed in use and repurchased continuously — the razor-and-blade "blade". A hospital's device spend is mostly consumables.
Renal / dialysis lineThe bloodlines and sets used in every dialysis session; a chronic patient needs thrice-weekly sessions for life — deeply recurring demand.
CE / US-FDA approvalThe regulatory passports to sell in Europe and the USA. Winning and keeping them is a core competitive barrier — and a permanent operating risk.
Why "cheap and boring" is the point

An individual cannula sells for a trivial sum, and no patient ever asks for it by brand. But zoom out and the picture inverts: a mid-size hospital consumes these devices by the tens of thousands every month, and the global count runs into billions of units a year. At that scale, the winner is not whoever has the flashiest product — it is whoever can manufacture enormous volumes to sterile-grade quality at the lowest cost per unit, hold the regulatory approvals to sell everywhere, and defend a few clever patented features. Low price per item plus vast, repeating volume plus a hard-to-clear quality bar is precisely the combination that keeps new entrants out and lets an efficient maker earn steady 24–27% operating margins on a "two-rupee tube".

Where the money comes from — and why it is sticky

  • Procedure-linked, not cycle-linked. Demand rises with the number of drips, surgeries and dialysis sessions performed worldwide — a slow, structural growth driver tied to healthcare access, ageing and chronic disease, not to any boom-bust replacement cycle.
  • Switching friction. Once a hospital's procurement, nurses and clinical protocols are built around a supplier's devices — and that supplier is on approved-vendor and tender lists — swapping to save a few paise per unit is rarely worth the disruption.
  • Regulatory moat. Selling into Europe, the USA and Japan requires clearing CE, FDA and similar regimes plus factory audits — a multi-year, expensive barrier that keeps the field of credible global suppliers small.

The product portfolio, broken into parts

Infusion therapy is the trunk; a fast-growing set of higher-value branches is the story.

Polymed sells across a wide catalogue, but the shape is simple: a large, mature Infusion Therapy core, and a diverse, faster-growing bucket of "Others" — renal, oncology, surgery, urology, gastroenterology, anaesthesia and blood management. The core pays the bills and funds the plant; the "Others" leg is where the mix is shifting up-market.

Why the "Others" leg matters more than its size suggests

At roughly a third of revenue, "Others" is not yet the biggest number — but it is the most important one to watch. It grows faster (~36% in FY26), it sells at higher prices per unit, and it is where Polymed's newest patents and its European acquisitions land. A company that is still 60-odd-percent basic infusion sets is a commodity manufacturer with a good cost position; a company steadily rotating toward renal, oncology and safety devices is climbing out of the commodity and into differentiated ground. The rotation, not the current split, is the thing.

03

The company and who controls it

A Faridabad promoter family that turned a disposable-devices workshop into a global exporter.

Poly Medicure is a promoter-led business run by the Baid family, headquartered near Delhi in Faridabad, Haryana. Founded in 1995, it grew from a domestic disposable-devices maker into one of India's few genuinely global medical-device brands, listing in India and building an export franchise that now spans more than a hundred countries. The family holds a commanding stake, aligning ownership tightly with the long capacity build now under way.

Timeline

  • 1995
    Poly Medicure founded by the Baid family to make disposable medical devices in India, starting with infusion products.
  • 2000s
    Scales IV cannulae and infusion sets, wins CE and international approvals, and builds an export business as a low-cost, quality-credible supplier to Europe and emerging markets.
  • 2010s
    Invests heavily in R&D and patents, especially safety-engineered cannulae; broadens into renal, surgery, urology and blood management; adds manufacturing capacity across multiple Indian plants.
  • Early 2020s
    Enters Europe by acquisition — Pendracare (Netherlands, cardiology/interventional) and Citieffe (Italy, orthopaedics/trauma) — plus Medyneo, moving up the value chain beyond disposables.
  • FY25–FY26
    Raises a ~₹1,000 Cr QIP and launches a large capacity expansion; FY26 revenue reaches ~₹1,875 Cr (+12.3%) while PAT dips ~5% and ROCE compresses — the deliberate cost of the build-out.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters: the Baid family holds ~62.42% — a controlling stake that keeps strategy, capital allocation and the aggressive expansion firmly in founder hands. High promoter ownership means the family's own capital rides on whether the current build-out pays off.
  • Institutional & public float: the remaining ~38% sits with domestic and foreign institutions and the public; the recent ₹1,000 Cr QIP brought fresh institutional capital onto the register to fund growth.
  • Balance sheet posture: after the QIP the company is well-capitalised for its capex and M&A programme; the strategic choice has been to fund expansion and buy European specialists rather than simply harvest the cash-generative core.
A promoter-driven expansion bet: with ~62% in family hands, this is not a widely-held professional-management story where capital discipline is enforced by outside owners. It is a founder family choosing to reinvest hard — raising equity, buying abroad, and building plants — in the belief that a bigger, higher-value Polymed a few years out beats a smaller, higher-return one today. That alignment is a strength if the thesis works and a concentration if it doesn't.
04

Manufacturing scale and the patent moat

The genuine surprise: a "boring tube maker" that holds 375+ patents.

The instinctive read on a disposable-devices company is "low-tech commodity — the moat is just cheap labour." Polymed quietly refutes that. Its edge rests on two legs that reinforce each other: manufacturing scale at an Indian cost base, and — the part that surprises people — an unusually deep patent portfolio of 375+ patents for an Indian device maker, concentrated in the areas that matter most, like safety-engineered cannulae.

The genuine surprise, stated plainly

Most people would guess an IV-tube business competes on price alone. In fact Polymed out-innovates on safety devices — cannulae and needles engineered so the sharp tip retracts or shields after use, protecting nurses from needlestick injuries and blood-borne infection. These features are patented, they command higher prices, and they are exactly where regulation in rich countries is pushing hospitals. An Indian maker holding 375+ patents and setting the pace on safety engineering — against giants like BD and B.Braun — is not what the "cheap commodity" caricature predicts. That IP is the difference between selling an undifferentiated tube and selling a protected, premium device.

Why the two legs together make a moat

  • Scale + cost base. Large, automated Indian plants let Polymed make enormous volumes at a landed cost the Western majors struggle to match — the classic "India as the world's low-cost factory" advantage, here applied to a sterile, regulated product.
  • Patents + safety features. IP on safety-engineered and specialty devices lets it charge more than a bare commodity and blocks straight copying — turning cost leadership into differentiated cost leadership.
  • Approvals + audits. A stack of CE, FDA and other clearances, backed by audited plants, is a barrier that takes years and money to clear — keeping the roster of credible global suppliers short.
  • Vertical integration. Making its own components, moulds and tooling supports quality control and margin, and keeps proprietary designs in-house.

Put together, the combination is hard to attack from either side. A cheap commodity rival can match the price of a plain infusion set but not the patented safety cannula or the regulatory approvals; a premium Western major can match the technology but not the Indian cost base. Polymed sits in the seam between them — and that seam is where its ~24–27% operating margins live.

05

The export engine

The single most surprising fact about Polymed — most of it isn't sold in India.

For a company most Indians have never heard of, the geography of its revenue is striking: roughly 69% of FY26 sales were exports. The domestic market is the smallest of Polymed's three big geographies. This is fundamentally an Indian export champion — a manufacturer using India as the world's low-cost device factory and selling into more than 125 countries against the global majors.

Why the export tilt is a strength — and a exposure

The export tilt is the reason Polymed can grow far faster than India's hospital build-out alone would allow: the addressable market is every hospital on earth, not just Indian ones, and India's cost advantage travels well in a price-sensitive consumable. It also diversifies demand across dozens of health systems. The flip side is that the same tilt loads the business with currency, freight, tariff and country-regulation risk — a stronger rupee, a spike in ocean freight, a new import tariff or a regulatory re-classification in a big market all land straight on results. An exporter earns the world's demand and imports the world's frictions.

What Polymed is really competing on abroad

  • Cost, first. Indian manufacturing lets it undercut the Western majors on plain and mid-tier consumables while still earning a healthy margin.
  • IP, second. Patented safety and specialty devices let it compete on features, not just price, in regulated rich-world markets — the harder, higher-value game.
  • Approvals & reliability, third. A long track record of CE/FDA compliance and audited plants makes it a credible, low-risk vendor for hospital procurement and distributors worldwide.
06

Up the value chain: renal, safety and the European buys

The deliberate climb from cheap disposables toward pricier, patent-protected medical technology.

Polymed's strategic through-line is a steady move up the value chain — out of the most commoditised infusion products and into higher-priced, harder-to-copy categories: renal/dialysis, oncology and safety devices, and now, via acquisitions, orthopaedics/trauma and interventional cardiology. The ₹507 Cr "Others" leg growing ~36% is the organic side of this; three European acquisitions are the inorganic accelerant.

The logic — and the catch — of buying Europe

Organically climbing into cardiology or orthopaedic trauma from an infusion-set base would take a decade of R&D and approvals. Buying established European specialists compresses that: instant products, patents, CE clearances, clinical credibility and local manufacturing. It also gives Polymed a European base from which to sell — useful as trade and "local content" pressures grow. The catch is equally clear. Three cross-border integrations at once is execution-heavy: different cost structures, cultures, regulatory regimes and margins to fold in. Acquisitions this transformative are where returns are made or quietly lost — and, alongside the plant capex, they are the direct reason ROCE has halved while the assets are digested.

The "Make in India" import-substitution tailwind

India has historically imported the large majority of the medical devices it uses, from multinationals. Government policy — production-linked incentives, device parks, and a broad "Make in India" push — is aimed squarely at reversing that, and Polymed is the natural domestic champion for the disposable-consumables slice of it. As Indian hospitals and public tenders lean toward domestic supply, Polymed's home leg (~₹582 Cr today) has a policy tailwind on top of its export engine. It is one of the few Indian names positioned to be both the world's low-cost exporter and the home-team beneficiary of import substitution.

07

The industry and the rivals

Squeezed between global majors above and commodity makers below — and thriving in the gap.

Polymed competes in two directions at once. Above it sit the global medical-device majors — BD (Becton Dickinson), B.Braun, Terumo, Smiths Medical, Nipro — with vast scale, brand trust and deep clinical relationships. Below it sit commodity disposable makers, including cheaper Indian and Asian producers, competing purely on price. Polymed's whole position is the seam between them: cheaper than the majors, more differentiated (patents, safety, approvals) than the commodity crowd.

The competitive tension to watch

Two pressures define the field. From above, the majors can defend share with brand, bundling and clinical lock-in, and they are not standing still on cost. From below, commodity makers keep the plain-vanilla end of the market brutally price-competitive, and government tenders — in India and abroad — routinely squeeze prices on basic consumables. Polymed's answer to both is the same: keep moving up-market, where patents, safety features and specialty categories widen the gap from the commodity crowd and let it compete on more than price with the majors. The strategic question for the next five years is whether it can climb into renal, oncology, ortho and cardiology fast enough to stay out of the commodity price war it started in.

The input and tender backdrop: Polymed's raw material is largely polymer/resin (a petrochemical derivative), so input costs move with crude and plastics prices. Meanwhile a big share of demand flows through government and institutional tenders that compete hard on price. Cheap resin and a rich product mix widen margins; a resin spike plus aggressive tender pricing squeezes them. It is a permanent feature of the business, not a one-off.
08

The build-out and the ROCE dip

The one number that unsettles a first look — and why it is deliberate.

Here is the fact a careful reader trips over. In FY26 Polymed's factories produced 12% more revenue, yet the company reported about 5% less profit, and the return it earns on its capital nearly halved — from ~24% to ~14%. No demand collapsed. No product failed. No margin quietly rotted. Understand that gap and you understand the whole investment case.

The device that explains it (and fits no other company)

The entire gap is money spent early. Polymed raised a ₹1,000 Cr QIP, bought three European companies — Citieffe, Pendracare, Medyneo — and is pouring new plant capacity to lift manufacturing well beyond today's output. All of that lands on the balance sheet as capital now, while the revenue and profit it will generate arrive later. When you divide roughly-flat profit by a suddenly much-larger capital base, the return per rupee mechanically falls — even though nothing is wrong with the operating business. Polymed is a company deliberately running its own returns down for a few years to buy a bigger, higher-value decade. The ROCE dip is not the disease; it is the invoice for the growth.

That reframes the risk precisely. The danger is not that Polymed's core is failing — it plainly isn't. The danger is timing and execution: that the new plants take longer to fill, or the European acquisitions take longer to integrate and earn their keep, than the market's patience allows. An investment year is only justified if the investment eventually earns its cost of capital back. Which is why one number, above all others, will settle this thesis.

The question is not "is the core good?" — it is "does the capital they just deployed climb back to the returns the old capital earned?"

Financials & valuation

A high-quality compounder mid-investment — priced as if the investment already worked.

Polymed's long record is one of steady, profitable growth at strong margins. FY26 is the anomaly year — revenue up, profit down — for the reasons in §08. The valuation debate is whether to price the business on the returns it earned before the build-out, or the depressed ones it shows during it.

The metric that settles the thesis

Strip everything else away and one number decides whether today's ~53× multiple is justified: return on capital employed (ROCE) climbing back from ~14% toward the mid-20s it used to earn. That single figure is the scoreboard for the entire build-out, because it can only recover if the other things the bull case needs are actually happening — new plant capacity filling with orders, the high-value "Others"/renal/safety mix rising, the European acquisitions earning their keep, and margins holding. If ROCE grinds back up quarter by quarter, the investment year was exactly that, and the multiple is paying for a bigger, better-mix business. If it stalls near the mid-teens, the market has capitalised a return the company no longer earns.

How to hold the ~53× multiple in your head

At roughly ₹17,461 Cr of market value on ~₹321 Cr of FY26 profit, Polymed trades near 53× trailing earnings — a full price for a consumables maker whose profit just fell and whose returns just halved. The bull reads it as a durable, patent-backed, ~24–27%-margin export compounder in a genuine investment phase, where trailing profit understates the earning power the new capacity will unlock. The bear reads it as a richly-valued disposable manufacturer being asked to prove that a ₹1,000 Cr equity raise and three cross-border deals turn into returns, not just revenue. The multiple assumes the ROCE recovery; the recovery has to be delivered.

The growth runway

More capacity, a richer mix, a global market, and a home-team tailwind.

Polymed's growth rests on four stacked engines — each independent, all pointing the same way. The company's task is less about finding demand than about building the capacity and mix to serve it while earning an adequate return.

What has to go right

  • Fill the new capacity. The plants and lines being built have to convert into orders and utilisation — idle capacity is the fastest way to keep ROCE depressed.
  • Integrate Europe. Citieffe, Pendracare and Medyneo have to be folded in, their margins lifted toward group levels, and their up-market products cross-sold through Polymed's global distribution.
  • Keep the mix climbing. The "Others"/renal/safety leg must keep out-growing the commodity core so the blend moves up, not sideways.
  • Hold margins through the resin and tender cycle. Input-cost discipline and pricing power on differentiated lines have to offset commodity-tender pressure.
The next chapter is less about winning demand — the world always needs the next billion cannulae — and more about turning a QIP and three acquisitions into returns, not just revenue.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely good business in a genuinely expensive, mid-investment moment.

Polymed is a rare thing — an Indian medical-device maker with global scale, real IP, and a durable recurring-consumption model. The debate is not whether the business is good; it is whether a company deliberately depressing its own returns to build for the future is being priced for the future it has to deliver.

The bull case

  • Durable, recurring demand — single-use devices consumed and re-bought with every procedure, worldwide, structurally growing.
  • Real moat — Indian cost base + 375+ patents + CE/FDA approvals; global top-3 in IV cannulae, out-innovating on safety devices.
  • Export engine — ~69% of sales across 125+ countries; the world's hospitals, not just India's, are the market.
  • Mix climbing — the ~36%-growing "Others"/renal/safety leg and the European buys move it up the value chain and off the commodity floor.
  • Aligned owners + policy tailwind — 62%+ promoter skin in the game and a "Make in India" import-substitution wind at its back.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~53× earnings on a business whose PAT just fell 5% and whose ROCE just halved.
  • Investment-drag risk — a ₹1,000 Cr QIP, three cross-border acquisitions and new plants all have to earn their cost of capital, on schedule.
  • Export frictions — currency, freight, tariff and country-regulation risk land straight on results.
  • Squeezed on both sides — global majors (BD, B.Braun, Terumo, Nipro) above; commodity makers and price tenders below; resin-cost swings throughout.
  • Quality & recall risk — a single serious FDA/CE compliance failure or product recall would hit a global, approvals-dependent franchise hard.
The bottom line

Polymed is a high-quality, patent-backed, cash-generative export compounder that has chosen to spend a few years looking less profitable in order to be much bigger and higher-mix on the other side. The business risk is modest; the risk lives entirely in the price and the execution of the build-out. Watch one number above all — ROCE grinding back from ~14% toward the mid-20s — because it can only recover if the new plants fill, the "Others"/renal mix keeps rising, and Citieffe and Pendracare are digested and earning. The concrete milestone to mark on the calendar is the point where those new manufacturing lines are commissioned and running, the three European units are fully integrated into the group's margins, and the return on capital has climbed back through the high-teens on its way home. Reach that, and the invoice for this investment year will have been worth paying.
