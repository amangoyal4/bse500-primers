Lloyds Engineering Works, the firm that builds the machinery for India's heavy industry

A complete walk through a small-cap heavy-engineering maker — the company that fabricates the pressure vessels, columns and process equipment that go inside refineries, the machinery that outfits steel plants and power stations, and now steering gears for Navy warships. It is a bet on India's industrial capex, read through three levers only: how fast orders come in, whether they are executed at a decent margin, and whether the cash comes back before the next order has to be funded.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Lloyds Engineering Works makes heavy process equipment and industrial machinery — the large steel-and-alloy objects that live inside a refinery, a petrochemical plant, a steel mill or a power station. Its wares are the plumbing and organs of industry: pressure vessels, heat exchangers, reactors, columns, waste-heat boilers, marine loading arms, and steel-plant and power equipment, built to exacting international codes (ASME and the like). It does not sell to consumers; it sells to the capital-expenditure budgets of steel, oil-and-gas and power companies — and, increasingly, to the Indian Navy. It is a small-cap order-book business: it wins contracts, fabricates to order, installs, and gets paid against milestones.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The order book is the fuel gauge, and it just jumped. Lloyds exited FY26 with a consolidated order book of ~₹2,643 Cr, up ~91% year-on-year — roughly two years of revenue already booked. For a made-to-order engineering firm, that backlog is the single best forward indicator: it is work already won that now has to be executed at a profit.
  • It is growing fast off a small base. FY26 consolidated revenue rose ~54% to ~₹1,301 Cr and net profit nearly doubled to ~₹198 Cr. Rewind two years and revenue was under ₹650 Cr — this is a company scaling quickly as India's industrial capex cycle turns, and as it bolts on acquisitions.
  • It is re-inventing itself beyond its steel-and-oil roots. Formerly Lloyds Steels Industries, it has pushed into defence and naval equipment (a dedicated subsidiary, orders from the Navy and Cochin Shipyard), elevators/escalators (the Techno Industries buy) and, via an MoU for Bhilai Engineering's engineering division, deeper into metallurgy, mining and railways. The market has noticed: the stock trades near ~68× earnings on a ~₹12,970 Cr market cap — a rich multiple for a project-engineering company.
In plain terms

Almost nothing Lloyds makes is something you would ever see. When a company builds a refinery, it needs giant steel vessels to hold reactions under pressure, columns to separate chemicals, exchangers to move heat, and arms to load fuel onto ships. Lloyds is one of the workshops that fabricates those parts to spec and installs them. Its revenue therefore tracks not the economy you shop in, but the one that gets built — every new steel plant, refinery expansion or Navy vessel is a potential order. That is the appeal and the catch at once: the demand is real and lumpy, arriving as large contracts rather than a steady sales counter, so the whole business turns on winning orders, executing them without blowing the budget, and getting paid before the cash runs thin.

01

Heavy engineering 101: what "process equipment" and "EPC" actually mean

No engineering degree needed — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

To follow Lloyds you need to picture what its customers are building. A refinery, a fertiliser plant or a steel mill is essentially a maze of tanks, towers, pipes and heat-moving gear through which raw materials flow and get transformed. Someone has to design and fabricate each of those big metal objects, ship them to site, and bolt the plant together. That "someone" is a heavy-engineering / process-equipment maker. Five terms unlock the whole industry.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Process equipmentThe purpose-built metal hardware that a plant runs on — pressure vessels, heat exchangers, reactors, columns, dryers, boilers. Custom-fabricated in thick steel or special alloys, welded and tested to strict codes. Lloyds's core product line.
Pressure vesselA sealed container built to safely hold gas or liquid at high pressure/temperature — the workhorse item of any refinery or chemical plant. Getting the welding and testing right (ASME code) is the whole skill; a failure is catastrophic, so buyers pay for proven fabricators.
EPC / turnkey"Engineering, Procurement & Construction" — taking on a whole scope: design it, buy the materials, build and install it, hand over a working system. Lloyds does both stand-alone equipment supply and larger turnkey packages.
Order book / backlogThe rupee value of contracts won but not yet executed. For a made-to-order firm this is the forward fuel gauge — revenue for the next 1–2 years is largely already sitting here (Lloyds: ~₹2,643 Cr at April 2026).
Order inflowThe value of new orders won in a period. Because the backlog is constantly being burned into revenue, a company must keep replacing it — inflow above revenue (a book-to-bill above 1) means the backlog is still growing.
The mechanics that decide everything: fixed-price fabrication

Almost all of this work is fixed-price: Lloyds quotes a price to build a vessel or a plant package, wins the order against rivals, and is then locked into that price for months or years of fabrication. Its profit is the gap between that agreed price and what the steel, alloys, welding, machining and labour actually cost to deliver. Two things can shrink or vaporise that gap — input-cost inflation (steel and special alloys are a big part of the bill) and execution slips (rework, delays, penalties). So the whole game is estimating a job accurately, buying materials well, and building it on time. Win the order at a sensible price and deliver to plan, and the margin is healthy; misjudge the steel price or the schedule, and a won order can be executed at a loss.

Who buys this equipment

  • Oil & gas / hydrocarbon — refineries, petrochemical and fertiliser plants buying pressure vessels, columns, exchangers, dryers and marine loading/unloading arms. A traditional core market for Lloyds.
  • Steel & metals — integrated steel plants and mills buying capital equipment and structural fabrication. Lloyds's original heritage (it was "Lloyds Steels").
  • Power & nuclear — thermal and nuclear power stations buying boilers, heat-recovery equipment and specialised vessels.
  • Defence & marine — the Navy and Coast Guard buying steering gears, stabilisers and deck machinery; shipyards buying marine equipment. The newest, fastest-growing buyer class.
02

What Lloyds Engineering actually builds

The product lines and end-markets — read this once and the company clicks into place.

Lloyds reports in two broad segments — a dominant Engineering business and a smaller Electrical one — but underneath sits a spread of end-markets that matters more than the accounting split. The Engineering segment is where the heavy fabrication lives; the Electrical segment (pumps, motors, and now elevators/escalators) is the newer, product-led leg it is building alongside.

The end-markets, decoded

  • Hydrocarbon / oil & gas equipment — the flagship line: pressure vessels, heat exchangers, columns, reactors, waste-heat-recovery boilers, air/gas/liquid dryers and marine and truck/wagon loading-unloading arms for refineries, petrochemicals, fertiliser plants and ports/jetties. High-spec, code-stamped work where a proven track record is the moat.
  • Steel-plant & power equipment — capital equipment and structural fabrication for steel mills and thermal/nuclear power stations. The heritage business from its Lloyds Steels days, now one leg among several.
  • Marine, naval & defence — steering gears, stabiliser systems and deck machinery for the Indian Navy and Coast Guard, plus marine equipment for shipyards such as Cochin Shipyard. Housed increasingly under a dedicated defence subsidiary. Small today, but the highest-growth ambition.
  • Electrical & product businesses — pumps and motors, and — via the acquired Techno Industries — elevators and escalators, a more standardised, product-style line that diversifies away from pure project lumpiness.
Why the product mix is worth explaining

These lines behave very differently. A refinery pressure vessel is a big, one-off, engineered-to-order job — high value, lumpy, and won on reputation for quality welding. An elevator, by contrast, is closer to a repeatable product with an installed-base service tail. Naval equipment sits in between: technically demanding, but attached to a multi-year defence-procurement pipeline that, once you are qualified as a supplier, can recur. Lloyds is deliberately assembling a portfolio that mixes the lumpy-but-high-value fabrication it knows with steadier product and defence lines — so that a slow quarter in refinery orders is cushioned by lifts, pumps or a naval contract rather than leaving the whole company idle.

A genuine surprise: a company most investors would file under "makes tanks for refineries" is now on the Indian Navy's supplier list — building steering gears, stabilisers and deck machinery for warships, and standing up a separate defence arm with Polish and Italian technology tie-ups for drones and marine systems. The equipment fabricator quietly became a defence-indigenisation play.
03

The company, the Lloyds group, and who controls it

A Gupta-family Lloyds Group company, reshaped by a demerger and a rebrand.

Lloyds Engineering Works belongs to the Lloyds Group, the industrial house founded by the Gupta family (chairman Mukesh R. Gupta), with roots across steel, engineering, power and finance. The company in this primer is the group's engineering & capital-equipment arm — and it is not the same entity as the better-known iron-ore-and-steel producer Lloyds Metals & Energy that shares the Lloyds name. Keeping the group map straight matters.

  • 2011

  • Jul 2023

  • Dec 2023

  • FY24–FY25

  • 2025

  • Dec 2025

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Gupta family / Lloyds Group control the company, with Mukesh R. Gupta as chairman. Notably, promoter holding has fallen — to roughly ~42% (Jun 2026) from ~60% in late 2023 — as rights issues and equity-funded acquisitions brought in outside capital. Investors should track the promoter stake and any pledge directly in the shareholding filings.
  • Don't confuse the Lloyds entities: Lloyds Engineering Works (LLOYDSENGG) is this equipment/EPC maker. Lloyds Metals & Energy is a separate listed iron-ore and steel company; Lloyds Enterprises is another Lloyds-branded listco. They share brand heritage, not financials — a common source of investor confusion.
  • Subsidiaries built the FY26 leap: Metalfab Hightech and Techno Industries (elevators/escalators) are now consolidated in, and Lloyds Advance Defence Systems is the new defence vehicle. This is why consolidated numbers run well ahead of standalone ones — read the two carefully, they are not the same company.
Why the standalone-vs-consolidated gap matters: FY26 consolidated revenue was ~₹1,301 Cr but standalone (the parent alone) was ~₹1,052 Cr; net profit was ~₹198 Cr consolidated vs ~₹118 Cr standalone. The difference is the acquired businesses. When you read a headline number for Lloyds, always check which basis it is on — a chunk of the growth is bought, not just organic.
04

How an order-book business actually earns

The three levers — order inflow, execution margin, and working capital — and how they connect.

A made-to-order engineering firm does not have "sales" in the retail sense; it has an order-to-cash cycle. Understanding that cycle is understanding the company, because every risk and every reward lives at one of its four stages. Follow a single order through the machine.

The order-to-cash cycle — one contract, four stages
Stage 1 · Win
Order inflow
Bid and win a fixed-price contract vs rivals. Adds to the backlog.
→
Stage 2 · Build
Fabricate & execute
Buy steel/alloys, weld, machine, test, install. Costs are incurred here.
→
Stage 3 · Bill
Recognise revenue
Book revenue as milestones are met — the backlog converts to sales.
→
Stage 4 · Collect
Get paid
Customer pays against milestones/retention. Cash finally returns.
The gap between Stage 2 (spend cash to build) and Stage 4 (collect cash) is the working-capital cycle — the money tied up in half-built vessels and unpaid invoices. It is the quiet killer of engineering companies.

The three levers that decide the outcome

  • Order inflow (the top of the funnel). No new orders, no future revenue. Because the backlog is constantly burning into sales, Lloyds must keep winning — ideally more than it delivers, so the backlog grows. That is why book-to-bill (inflow ÷ revenue) above 1 is a health signal, and why its ~91% order-book jump matters more than any single quarter's revenue.
  • Execution margin (the middle). Winning an order at a bad price, or building it late and over budget, converts revenue into losses. The EBITDA margin — running around the high-teens for Lloyds — is the scoreboard for how well it estimates and executes fixed-price work. A stable or rising margin as revenue scales is the sign of a firm not "buying" growth by underpricing bids.
  • Working capital (the tail). Fabrication ties up cash for months before the customer pays. If receivables balloon or inventory piles up, a "profitable" order book can still starve the company of cash. Debtor days and net working capital are the vital signs — Lloyds's debtor days reportedly improved from ~117 to ~67, which is exactly the direction that lets it grow without choking.
Why all three must work together

Any one lever alone is a trap. A huge order book won at thin prices produces revenue without profit. Fat margins on a shrinking backlog produce a good year and then a cliff. And even a well-priced, growing backlog can sink the company if the cash is trapped in unbilled work and slow-paying customers while the next order still needs funding. The reason engineering small-caps blow up is almost never a lack of orders — it is executing them at a margin that doesn't cover the cost of the working capital they consume. For Lloyds, the investment question is whether it can keep all three spinning at once as it scales and bolts on acquisitions, when each is hardest to control.

05

The order book: inflow, margin, and the working-capital test

The single most important idea in the whole case — and the metric that settles the thesis.

Everything about Lloyds routes back to one number and its quality: the order book. A rising backlog is the reason the stock re-rated; but a backlog is only a promise of revenue, and that promise is worth what its execution margin and cash conversion make it worth. Watch the backlog grow — then interrogate whether it converts profitably.

The consolidated order book rose ~91% year-on-year to ~₹2,643 Cr — roughly two years of FY26 revenue already contracted. Management indicates the major portion executes within ~15 months, which is what underpins near-term growth expectations.

Set that backlog against the revenue it feeds. The device that only fits Lloyds: one backlog, read three ways

The order book is not one number but three questions stacked on top of each other. How big? — ~₹2,643 Cr, up 91%, is the growth case in a single figure. At what margin? — the backlog is only worth its execution margin, and Lloyds's ~18% EBITDA margin is what turns that ₹2,643 Cr from a revenue promise into a profit promise; a margin that holds as the book scales is the tell that it is not buying orders cheap. At what cash cost? — every rupee of that backlog will tie up steel and receivables before it pays, so the improving debtor days (~117 → ~67) decide whether the growth is self-funding or cash-hungry. A bigger book at a thinner margin funded by ballooning working capital would be a worse business than a smaller book executed cleanly. Size is the headline; margin and cash are the verdict.

06

The diversification push: defence, railways, and lifts

Bolting new engines onto a hydrocarbon-and-steel core.

A pure refinery-and-steel equipment maker is hostage to two capex cycles. Lloyds's answer has been to widen the base — through a burst of acquisitions and new subsidiaries that push it into defence, elevators, and (via an MoU) railways and mining. Whether these become real profit engines or expensive distractions is a live question, but the direction is unmistakable.

The moves, decoded

  • Defence & naval (Lloyds Advance Defence Systems). A wholly-owned subsidiary formed in late 2025 to chase India's defence-indigenisation spend. Beyond the Navy/Coast Guard steering gears, stabilisers and deck machinery it already supplies, it has signed technology tie-ups with FlyFocus (Poland) for tactical drones/UAVs and Cemi (Italy) for industrial automation, targeting marine systems and radar. High-ambition, early-stage.
  • Elevators & escalators (Techno Industries). Now a 100% subsidiary. This adds a more product-like, service-tailed business to a portfolio otherwise dominated by lumpy fabrication — a deliberate smoothing of the revenue profile, and the main driver of the Electrical segment's jump.
  • Metallurgy, mining & railways (Bhilai Engineering / BECL). An MoU to acquire the engineering division of Bhilai Engineering Corporation would deepen Lloyds's reach into steel-plant equipment, mining and railway engineering — extending the industrial-capex footprint. Still at MoU stage, so treat as intent, not done.
  • High-tech fabrication (Metalfab Hightech). A 76% stake (₹130/share, May 2025) adding specialised fabrication capacity to the core.
Why diversification is a double-edged move here

The logic is sound: defence and railway spending run on procurement cycles that don't move in lockstep with refinery capex, and an elevator business brings repeatable revenue. Spread across those, Lloyds is less at the mercy of any one industry's budget. But an acquisition-led sprint into unfamiliar markets carries its own bill — integration risk, management bandwidth, and equity dilution to pay for it all (the promoter stake fell from ~60% to ~42% partly for this reason). Defence in particular is a slow, credential-heavy market where orders take years to mature. The diversification is the right instinct; the risk is paying up front, in cash and in shares, for engines that take time to actually turn.

07

The industrial-capex tailwind Lloyds rides

Why the order book is growing — the structural demand behind the numbers.

Lloyds does not create its own demand; it harvests other industries' spending decisions. The reason its backlog nearly doubled is that several of India's capital-spending cycles are running hot at once — and each one lands as orders for exactly the equipment Lloyds makes.

What's driving the order flow
DriverWhat it isWhy it helps Lloyds
Steel-capacity expansionIndia's push to grow crude-steel capacity materially this decade — new mills, expansions, modernisation.Steel plants buy capital equipment and structural fabrication — Lloyds's heritage line — turning each expansion into potential orders.
Oil & gas / refining capexRefinery and petrochemical expansions, plus fertiliser and gas infrastructure build-out.Directly demands pressure vessels, columns, exchangers, dryers and loading arms — the flagship product line.
Defence indigenisation"Make in India" in defence — sourcing warship and platform equipment domestically rather than importing.Opens the Navy/Coast Guard and shipyards as a new buyer class for Lloyds's marine and naval equipment.
Power & infrastructureThermal, nuclear and broader infrastructure investment.Boilers, heat-recovery equipment and heavy fabrication feed off power-plant and infra build-out.
The insight that frames the whole tailwind

Lloyds is a second-order bet on India's industrialisation. It does not build refineries or steel plants or warships; it builds the equipment that goes into them. That is a subtle but important distinction. It means Lloyds can prosper without picking which end-industry wins — as long as someone is expanding heavy-industrial capacity, orders flow. It also means Lloyds is one step removed from the end-demand: when the capex cycle is strong, order books swell; when large projects are deferred or cancelled, the equipment orders dry up first, because they are the discretionary, deferrable line item in someone else's budget. The tailwind is real and broad-based right now — the discipline is remembering it is a cycle, not a permanent state.

The breadth is the point. A refinery slowdown alone would not sink the order book if steel and defence are still spending; that is precisely the resilience diversification is meant to buy. But all of these are capital-spending cycles, and capital-spending cycles turn. The order book tells you the tailwind is blowing today; it does not promise the wind won't drop.

08

The balance sheet and the equity raise

Funding the growth — mostly with fresh equity, keeping debt low.

Growing a working-capital-hungry engineering business fast takes capital, and Lloyds has chosen to raise most of it as equity rather than debt. That keeps the balance sheet clean but comes at the price of dilution — the trade-off at the heart of how this expansion is being financed.

Why the equity-first choice is the sane one here — with a catch

For a fixed-price fabricator, debt is dangerous: if a project slips or a customer pays late, interest keeps compounding while the cash is trapped in half-built steel. Funding growth with equity keeps the company from that trap — a near-debt-free balance sheet can absorb a bad project or a slow payer without a lender forcing its hand. The catch is dilution: every rights issue and every equity-funded acquisition shrinks existing holders' slice, and the promoter stake sliding under ~42% shows how much has been issued. So the balance sheet is a genuine strength — but the growth is being partly bought with new shares, and per-share value only compounds if the capital raised earns a return above what the dilution cost. Watch return on capital, not just headline growth.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded field of Indian process-equipment and heavy-engineering names.

Heavy process equipment is a competitive, credential-driven business. Buyers care about proven quality (a failed pressure vessel is catastrophic), on-time delivery and price — and there is a deep bench of established Indian makers, many far larger than Lloyds. Lloyds competes as a fast-growing small-cap against a set of bigger, more diversified engineering companies.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two realities frame the rivalry. First, Lloyds is a minnow among established whales — ISGEC, Thermax and Praj carry decades of reference projects and far bigger balance sheets, which matters when a refinery is choosing who to trust with a critical vessel. Lloyds's edge is agility, price and a hungry growth posture, not scale or pedigree. Second, because the underlying capex cycle is broad, a rising tide can lift several of these names at once — this is less a market-share war than a question of who executes cleanly while demand is strong. Lloyds's job is to keep winning mid-sized packages at sensible prices, deliver them on time, and build the reference list that lets it graduate into the bigger, higher-margin jobs its larger rivals dominate today.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A real growth story on a small base — asked to prove the quality behind the quantity.

Lloyds Engineering enters FY27 with genuine momentum: a backlog up ~91%, revenue and profit compounding, margins holding, a clean balance sheet, and new legs in defence and lifts. The debate is not whether it is growing — it plainly is — but whether the quality of that growth (margin, cash conversion, returns on the freshly-raised capital) matches the price the market has put on it.

The bull case

  • Broad industrial-capex tailwind — steel expansion, oil & gas capex, defence indigenisation and power/infra all feeding the order book at once.
  • Order book up ~91% to ~₹2,643 Cr — roughly two years of visible revenue, growing faster than it burns (book-to-bill above 1).
  • Margins holding in the high-teens even as revenue scaled — evidence of disciplined bidding, not bought growth.
  • Clean balance sheet — near debt-free after an equity-funded ramp; improving debtor days free cash for the next contract.
  • Optionality from diversification — defence/naval, elevators and the BECL railway/metallurgy MoU could add engines beyond the hydrocarbon-and-steel core.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~68× earnings on a small, partly-acquired base prices in years of flawless compounding; little cushion for a miss.
  • Cyclicality — order flow depends on other industries' capex, which is deferrable and turns; a capex slowdown hits equipment orders first.
  • Execution & input-cost risk — fixed-price fabrication means steel/alloy inflation or project slips can erode the margin the whole thesis rests on.
  • Dilution & acquisition risk — growth part-funded by new equity (promoter stake ~60%→~42%); integrating Metalfab/Techno/defence into returns above the dilution cost is unproven.
  • Small-cap & concentration — lumpy contracts, client/sector concentration, and a competitive field of far larger, better-credentialed rivals.
The bottom line

Lloyds Engineering has done the hard, unglamorous work of turning a steel-era fabricator into a fast-growing, near-debt-free engineering company riding a broad industrial-capex wave — and it has done it while holding margins and improving cash collection. That is a genuinely good business getting better. What it has not yet proven is that the acquisition-and-equity-fuelled diversification into defence, lifts and railways will earn returns above the shares it issued to buy them, and that a ~68× multiple can be grown into by execution rather than hope. The next milestones are concrete and checkable: the BECL engineering-division deal moving from MoU to close, the first meaningful revenue and margin out of Lloyds Advance Defence Systems, and — every quarter — whether order inflow stays above revenue at a steady ~18% margin while debtor days hold near ~67. Those prints, not the backlog headline, will tell you whether the quantity of growth came with the quality to justify its price.
