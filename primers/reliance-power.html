Reliance Power, the record IPO that became a cautionary tale

A complete walk through India's most famous wealth-destruction story in listed equities — how a company that raised the largest IPO in Indian history in January 2008 on a promise of tens of thousands of megawatts ended up building a fraction of them, what it actually owns today, and whether the debt cleanup plus a genuine push into solar-plus-battery storage adds up to a second act.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Reliance Power generates electricity and sells it, mostly under very long contracts, to state distribution companies. It is the power-generation arm of the Reliance Group (ADAG) controlled by Anil Ambani — a separate house from Mukesh Ambani's Reliance Industries, split from the original Reliance empire in 2005. Today it runs roughly 5,300 MW (approx.) of operating capacity, but that number hides the real shape of the business: one enormous coal plant, Sasan (3,960 MW), does most of the work, with Rosa (1,200 MW) in Uttar Pradesh a distant second and a handful of small solar and wind assets rounding it out. In FY26 it turned over ₹7,620 Cr and lost ₹337 Cr after a ₹382 Cr impairment, while generating ₹2,824 Cr of cash from operations.

Four facts frame everything that follows:

  • It raised the biggest IPO India had ever seen — and then didn't build what it sold. The January 2008 issue raised roughly ₹11,563 Cr, drew millions of retail applications and was oversubscribed many times over, on a prospectus pipeline of around 28,200 MW (approx.) across thirteen projects. Eighteen years later, operating capacity sits near 5,300 MW. That arithmetic — what was promised against what was poured in concrete — is the single most useful lens on this company, and it is the one the market applies to every new announcement.
  • Nearly all the cash comes from one asset. Sasan is not just the biggest plant; it is a pit-head plant sitting on its own captive coal mines in Madhya Pradesh, which means it burns fuel it digs itself, avoids long rail hauls, and runs at a very low cost. It ran at roughly 91% PLF in FY26. Remove Sasan and there is very little business left.
  • The balance sheet has been cleaned, not erased. Reliance Power has spent recent years settling with lenders, selling assets and clearing standalone borrowings — the reason the equity came back to life at all. But at the consolidated level, where the project debt of Sasan and Rosa sits, leverage is still real: a debt-to-equity ratio near 0.87 and a debt-to-EBITDA ratio around 10.25× against a net worth of roughly ₹16,516 Cr. "Debt-free" is a standalone claim, not a group one.
  • The growth story has changed fuel. Through subsidiary Reliance NU Suntech, the company won SECI's largest single award in its Tranche XVII auction — 930 MW of solar paired with a 465 MW / 1,860 MWh battery energy storage system, at a fixed tariff of ₹3.53 per kWh on a 25-year contract, with a stated capital outlay of up to ₹10,000 Cr. A sister entity, Reliance NU Energies, added a 350 MW solar award from SJVN. This is a coal builder attempting to become a storage developer.
In plain terms

Two companies live inside this ticker. The first is a working utility: a giant, low-cost coal plant with its own mine, tied to twenty-five-year contracts, throwing off a couple of thousand crore of operating cash a year. That part is real and it functions. The second is a reconstruction project: a promoter group with a long record of defaults, ongoing investigations by India's Enforcement Directorate and CBI, and a new renewables pipeline that exists so far mostly as letters of award and power-purchase agreements. Buying the share means buying both — an operating asset that works and a corporate history that has repeatedly failed to convert paper into plants.

01

January 2008: the IPO that broke every record

You cannot understand the discount on this stock without understanding the frenzy that preceded it.

In the winter of 2007–08 the Indian market was at a peak it would not see again for years, and the hottest thing in it was power. India was short of electricity, the government had launched a programme of gigantic Ultra Mega Power Projects (UMPPs) — 4,000 MW coal plants awarded by competitive bidding — and the Reliance name still carried the aura of the undivided empire. Into that moment came Reliance Power's initial public offering.

The scale of it was unlike anything the country had processed. The issue raised roughly ₹11,563 Cr, the largest IPO in Indian history at the time. It attracted an extraordinary number of retail applications — several million, a record for the era — and was oversubscribed many times over within the first minute of opening. Bank branches queued out onto pavements. It was, for a very large slice of India's first-generation equity investors, their first ever share purchase.

What they were buying was almost entirely a plan. Reliance Power at the time of listing had negligible operating capacity and negligible revenue. The prospectus set out a development pipeline of roughly 28,200 MW (approx.) across thirteen projects — coal, gas and hydro — spread across the country. The valuation was underwritten by that pipeline, not by output.

The unwind began immediately. The stock listed on 11 February 2008 above its issue price, then collapsed within the session and closed sharply below it. The broader market cracked weeks later. To calm retail anger, the promoters issued bonus shares in a 3:5 ratio to non-promoter shareholders, which lowered the effective cost of the IPO allotment to roughly ₹269 per share (approx.). That gesture is a useful benchmark: against a share price of about ₹24.5 in July 2026, an investor who bought at the IPO and held through the bonus is still down on the order of 90%+ — before eighteen years of inflation.

Why the 2008 story still matters in 2026

Markets forgive operational stumbles far more readily than they forgive a pattern. Reliance Power's founding event was not a bad quarter; it was a company being valued on a pipeline that then failed to materialise on a scale of tens of thousands of megawatts. That single fact rewired how Indian investors read announcements from this group. It is also why a genuine, contracted, 930 MW solar plus 1,860 MWh storage award — an award that would materially re-rate almost any other mid-cap developer — is met here with a shrug and a demand for photographs of commissioned panels. The scepticism is priced in because it was earned.

02

How a power plant actually earns money

Six ideas, no engineering required — after this the financials read themselves.

An Indian power generator is not really in the business of selling electricity on a spot market. It is in the business of signing very long contracts to be available, and then being available. Once you understand that distinction, the whole sector's economics — and the specific reason Sasan is valuable — become obvious.

The vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
PPA"Power Purchase Agreement" — a contract, typically 25 years, under which a distribution company agrees to buy a plant's output at a set tariff. It is what converts a pile of concrete into a bond-like cash stream. Sasan and Rosa both sell under long PPAs; the new solar-plus-storage project has a 25-year PPA with SECI at ₹3.53/kWh.
TariffThe price per unit (kWh) of electricity. Indian coal PPAs usually split into a capacity charge (paid for being available, covering fixed costs and return on capital) and an energy charge (paid for fuel actually burnt). The split is why availability matters more than raw output.
PLF"Plant Load Factor" — the share of theoretical maximum output a plant actually produced over a period. Sasan ran at ~91% in FY26, which is high; a plant with an uncompetitive tariff might sit at 40% because the grid simply doesn't call on it.
AvailabilityThe share of time the plant was ready to run, whether or not it was asked to. Rosa reported ~97% in FY26. Under Indian regulation, capacity charges are usually earned on availability — so a plant can be paid handsomely while running far below full load.
Merit orderThe grid dispatches the cheapest plants first. A plant with a very low variable cost is called almost always; an expensive one is called only at peak. This is the entire reason a pit-head plant with a captive mine is a structurally different animal from one buying imported coal.
BESS"Battery Energy Storage System" — a bank of batteries that stores solar output generated at midday and releases it in the evening peak. Sized in two ways: MW (how fast it can discharge) and MWh (how much it holds). Reliance's SECI project is 465 MW / 1,860 MWh — four hours of storage.
The one mechanism worth internalising: pit-head economics

Coal is heavy, bulky and cheap per tonne, which means transporting it is a large share of what it costs to burn. A plant built at the mine mouth — "pit-head" — moves coal a few kilometres on a conveyor instead of a thousand kilometres by rail. If it also owns the mine, it pays extraction cost rather than market price. Both effects push its variable cost far below a coastal plant importing coal or an inland plant railing it from Odisha. Under merit-order dispatch, the low-cost plant runs first and runs longest. This is not a management skill; it is a piece of geography that was locked in at the moment the project was sited, and it cannot be competed away.

03

The collapse, project by project

Not one failure but a sequence of them — and each has a distinct, instructive cause.

It is tempting to describe what happened to Reliance Power as simple over-promising. The record is more specific and more interesting than that: three or four separate mechanisms broke the pipeline, most of them structural to Indian power in that decade, some of them self-inflicted. Walking through them is the fastest way to understand why so little got built — and which of those mechanisms could break the new pipeline.

The gas that never arrived

The group planned large gas-fired capacity — most prominently at Samalkot in Andhra Pradesh, a project of roughly 2,400 MW (approx.) — on the assumption of domestic gas from the Krishna-Godavari basin. Output from those fields fell dramatically short of projections. A gas plant without gas is an extraordinarily expensive ornament: the turbines were bought, the plant substantially built, and it could not run economically. Equipment from the project was ultimately redeployed, including toward a power venture in Bangladesh (approx.). India's entire gas-power fleet suffered the same fate; Reliance Power simply had more of it.

The imported-coal shock

The Krishnapatnam UMPP in Andhra Pradesh — another 3,960 MW project — was bid on the basis of coal imported from Indonesia at then-prevailing prices. Indonesia then changed its coal-pricing regulations to benchmark exports to international rates. The economics inverted: the tariff had been bid low and fixed, while fuel cost jumped. The project was not built. The same shock hit other Indian developers with imported-coal exposure and produced years of litigation over whether such a change constituted grounds for tariff relief.

The land that could not be assembled

The Tilaiya UMPP in Jharkhand, a third 3,960 MW project, foundered on land acquisition and resettlement. After years without progress, the project was surrendered in 2015 and the development rights returned. Nothing about this was unique to Reliance Power — Indian mega-projects have been repeatedly defeated by land — but it removed another 4 GW from the promised pipeline.

Coal-block cancellation and the debt spiral

In 2014 the Supreme Court cancelled a large tranche of coal blocks allocated to private parties, upending the fuel assumptions of numerous projects across the industry. Meanwhile the group's borrowings — raised against a pipeline that was not producing revenue — kept compounding. What followed was a long decade of asset sales, defaults, restructurings and, for several ADAG entities, insolvency proceedings. Butibori, a 600 MW plant in Maharashtra, passed to Vidarbha Industries Power and ended up in the bankruptcy process. Hydro projects in the north-east never advanced past study stage.

The pattern, stated plainly

Of the four huge coal projects at the centre of the 2008 story, exactly one — the one that happened to sit on its own coal — was completed. The three that depended on somebody else supplying the input (Indonesian miners, KG-basin gas, a state government assembling land) all failed. That is worth carrying forward: this company's single success came where it controlled its own fuel chain end to end, and its failures clustered wherever an external party had to deliver. The new solar-plus-storage build depends on module supply, battery imports, land, transmission connectivity and a construction timetable — a great many external parties.

What Reliance Power actually owns today

Strip away the history and the asset list is short, concentrated and — in one place — genuinely good.

For all the noise, the operating business is easy to describe. Two coal plants do essentially all the work. A scattering of small renewable assets contributes at the margin. Everything else is either sold, surrendered, or still on paper.

Sasan Ultra Mega Power Project — Madhya Pradesh, 3,960 MW

Six supercritical units of 660 MW each, sited next to the Moher and Moher-Amlohri coal blocks, which the company mines itself and conveys directly to the boilers. Output is committed under long-term power purchase agreements to a consortium of state distribution companies across several states, at a tariff bid in the mid-2000s that was, at the time, considered startlingly aggressive. In FY26 the plant ran at roughly 91% PLF — the sort of number that puts it among the better-utilised coal stations in the country.

Rosa Power — Uttar Pradesh, 1,200 MW

Four units of 300 MW in Shahjahanpur district, selling essentially all output to the Uttar Pradesh distribution utility under a long-term PPA. Rosa is not pit-head; it buys and rails its coal, so its variable cost is higher and its dispatch lower. But because Indian coal PPAs pay a capacity charge on availability, and Rosa reported roughly 97% availability in FY26, it earns its fixed-cost recovery regardless of how heavily the grid calls on it. It is the steadier, smaller annuity behind Sasan's larger one.

The renewables tail — and the renewables future

The legacy renewable assets are small: a wind farm in Maharashtra of roughly 45 MW and solar assets in Rajasthan of roughly 140 MW, including one of India's early concentrated-solar-power installations at Dhursar. These are rounding errors in the P&L. The renewables that matter are not yet built — they sit in Reliance NU Suntech and Reliance NU Energies, and they are the subject of section 07.

A concentration you should sit with

Most listed utilities of this size own a dozen assets across several states and fuels, so that any single outage, coal disruption, tariff order or discom dispute is absorbed by the rest of the portfolio. Reliance Power does not have that cushion. A prolonged forced outage at Sasan, an adverse regulatory ruling on its tariff, a problem in its captive mine, or a payment freeze from the Sasan offtakers would flow almost undiluted into consolidated results. When a business has one engine, engine risk is business risk — and no amount of narrative about the solar pipeline changes that until the solar is actually running.

05

Sasan: the aggressive bid that turned out to be right

The genuine surprise inside this company — and the reason it survived at all.

In the mid-2000s bidding round for the Sasan UMPP, Reliance Power won with a levelised tariff quoted at roughly ₹1.196 per unit (approx.). At the time this was widely regarded as reckless — a number so low that observers doubted it could ever be delivered profitably, and one that became a case study in aggressive bidding. Two decades later, that same tariff is the reason the plant runs nearly flat out.

The mechanism is merit-order dispatch. India's grid calls the cheapest available generation first. A plant whose all-in variable cost is a fraction of the fleet average is called before almost everything else, every day, in almost every season. What was condemned as an unsustainable bid became a structural moat: Sasan is difficult to displace from the dispatch stack because there is very little cheaper thermal generation in the country. And because its coal comes from its own adjacent mines, its cost position does not deteriorate when Coal India revises prices or when rail freight rises.

The bid that was supposed to prove the company reckless is the single asset that kept it alive. Every other project built on someone else's fuel promise failed; the one built on its own coal became the cash engine.

This produces an unusual internal contradiction. Reliance Power's reputation rests on over-promising. Yet its one completed mega-project delivered exactly the thing its critics said was impossible — a 3,960 MW supercritical plant with an integrated captive mine, running at ~91% PLF and generating enough cash to fund a debt cleanup. Both statements are true simultaneously, and holding them together is the beginning of an honest reading of the company.

Why the captive mine is the whole thing

Consider two identical 4 GW coal plants. Plant A buys coal at a market-linked notified price and pays rail freight over 800 km. Plant B digs its own coal a few kilometres away and belts it in. Their fixed costs are the same; their construction costs are similar; but Plant B's variable cost per unit can be dramatically lower. In a market where the grid ranks generators by variable cost every hour of every day, that gap decides which plant runs at 90% and which one idles at 50%. Sasan is Plant B. That is not a management achievement renewable each year — it is a permanent feature of where the project was sited and what came bundled with it, and it is the reason a company with this history still generates ₹2,824 Cr of operating cash.

The corollary risk: a permanent advantage that lives in one location is also a permanent concentration. Coal quality degradation at Moher, a mining permit or environmental clearance issue, a major overhaul at a 660 MW unit, or a Central Electricity Regulatory Commission ruling that reopens tariff assumptions — any of these hits the group's entire cash generation at once. Sasan is both the strongest argument for owning the stock and the largest single-point risk in it.
06

The debt cleanup: what was actually settled

The real driver of the equity's revival — and the precise limits of the "debt-free" claim.

Between roughly 2020 and 2025 Reliance Power did something genuinely difficult: it negotiated its way out of a defaulted balance sheet. Lender settlements, one-time payments at a discount, asset disposals, guarantee releases and fresh equity raises were used to extinguish borrowings at the parent level. This is the substantive change beneath the story — a company that could not access capital markets at any price became one that could raise equity again.

What "debt-free" means here — read carefully

  • Standalone versus consolidated. The company's deleveraging claims are principally at the standalone level — the parent entity's own borrowings. The operating plants are held in subsidiaries (Sasan Power, Rosa Power), and those subsidiaries carry their own project debt, which is normal for infrastructure but does not vanish because the parent's books are clean.
  • The consolidated picture as reported. Group net worth is around ₹16,516 Cr, with a debt-to-equity ratio near 0.87 and a debt-to-EBITDA ratio around 10.25×. A leverage multiple in double digits is not the profile of a debt-free business; it is the profile of a leveraged utility that has stopped defaulting.
  • Contingent liabilities and legacy claims. Years of defaults, invoked guarantees, disputed dues and arbitration leave a long tail of contingent items and litigation. These are disclosed in the notes to accounts rather than on the face of the balance sheet, and any serious assessment of the equity has to read them.
  • Fresh capital is being raised, not retired. The board approved an enabling resolution for a capital raise of up to ₹9,000 Cr, alongside authority to raise up to US$600 million via foreign currency convertible bonds. This is the funding for the renewables build — and it means the equity base will likely expand.

Deleveraging and re-leveraging in the same breath

The sequence here is unusual enough to name. Most turnarounds clean the balance sheet and then harvest cash. Reliance Power is cleaning the balance sheet and immediately committing to a capital programme several times larger than its annual cash generation — funded by new equity, converts and project debt. The deleveraging is real; the durable low-debt state it implies may be brief. An investor buying "the deleveraging story" should be clear that what is on offer is a company swapping defaulted legacy debt for new project finance and a larger share count. That can be an excellent trade if the new assets earn their cost of capital. It is not the same thing as a company that has simply become less risky.

07

The solar-plus-battery pivot

The growth story — and the first genuinely new thing this company has done in fifteen years.

India's power capex is no longer flowing into coal. It is flowing into solar, and increasingly into solar with storage, because the country's problem has stopped being daytime generation and become evening supply. Reliance Power has moved into exactly that gap, and the size of its first award is not trivial.

The SECI award, in detail

  • What was won. In December 2024, subsidiary Reliance NU Suntech secured the largest allocation in SECI's Tranche XVII auction: 930 MW of solar integrated with a 465 MW / 1,860 MWh battery energy storage system.
  • The contract. A 25-year power purchase agreement with the Solar Energy Corporation of India was signed in May 2025, at a fixed tariff of ₹3.53 per kWh. SECI as counterparty is a materially better credit than a typical state distribution company — a meaningful structural improvement over the discom exposure that has plagued Indian generators.
  • The build. Stated capital investment of up to ₹10,000 Cr, with the project described as Asia's largest single-location integrated solar-and-BESS installation, targeted for commissioning within roughly 24 months of the PPA.
  • The second award. In May 2025, Reliance NU Energies won a 350 MW solar project from SJVN, broadening the renewable pipeline beyond a single counterparty.

Why storage, and why now

A solar farm produces its peak output around noon and nothing after sunset. India's demand peak is in the evening. As solar penetration rises, that mismatch stops being a curiosity and becomes the binding constraint on the grid: midday power gets curtailed as surplus while evening peaks still need thermal generation. A four-hour battery bank — 465 MW able to discharge for four hours, hence 1,860 MWh — absorbs the midday surplus and releases it into the evening. That converts intermittent solar into something closer to dispatchable power, which is why tenders of this shape now command premium tariffs and why SECI is running them.

What is genuinely different about this pipeline

Two things distinguish the renewables push from the 2008 pipeline, and they deserve credit. First, the offtaker is SECI, a central intermediary, rather than a stressed state distribution company — the payment risk that hollowed out a generation of Indian renewable developers is materially lower here. Second, the tariff is fixed and already contracted for 25 years, and — unlike the Krishnapatnam disaster — solar has no fuel cost, so there is no Indonesian regulator or KG-basin geology that can invert the economics after the bid. The input risk that destroyed the last pipeline genuinely does not apply to this one. What remains is capital and construction: can it be funded, and can it be built on time. Those are the two things this company has historically been worst at.

A detail worth flagging: battery storage at this scale is a young business in India, and cell supply is overwhelmingly imported. Project cost, delivery schedule and warranty terms therefore depend on international battery supply chains and on the evolving policy on domestic content and duties. A four-hour, 1,860 MWh system is a large procurement commitment, and the delta between a battery price assumed at bid and one paid at order can meaningfully move project returns on a tariff that is fixed for twenty-five years.
08

The promise-versus-delivery ledger

The one framework that makes this specific company legible — and it works for no other stock in the sector.

Every developer announces capacity. For most of them, an announcement is reasonable evidence about the future, because their historical conversion rate from announced to commissioned is high enough that you can extrapolate. Reliance Power is the rare case where that inference is demonstrably invalid, because the company itself has published the counter-example: a 28,200 MW pipeline that produced 5,300 MW of running plant. The correct way to underwrite this equity is therefore not to build a discounted cash-flow model on the announced pipeline. It is to keep a ledger.

The ledger has two columns, and only one of them is allowed to move the valuation.

Two disciplines follow from keeping this ledger, and they are worth stating as rules.

  • Rule one: an award is not an asset. A letter of award and even a signed PPA are options to spend ₹10,000 Cr, not claims on future cash. In 2008 the market capitalised the announcement; the mistake cost investors ~90% of their money. Any valuation built on the 1,745 MW column repeats the original error in a new fuel.
  • Rule two: measure the conversion rate, not the pipeline. The number that will settle this thesis is the ratio of commissioned megawatts to announced megawatts on the post-2024 pipeline, tracked quarter by quarter against the 24-month clock that started with the May 2025 SECI PPA. If, by roughly mid-2027, a material share of 930 MW of solar and 1,860 MWh of storage is energised and selling at ₹3.53/kWh, the company will have done something it has not done since Sasan — and the historical discount becomes arguable. If that date passes with the project substantially incomplete, the ledger's left column will have grown while its right column stayed at zero, and the market's scepticism will have been validated a second time.
The arithmetic that makes the ledger concrete

Put the two columns in rupees. Sasan and Rosa together produced ₹2,824 Cr of operating cash in FY26 — that is the entire right-hand column, the proven part, and it comes from assets commissioned more than a decade ago. The left-hand column asks for ₹10,000 Cr of capex for the SECI project alone, before the SJVN award. So the proven business must be levered roughly 3.5:1 by external capital to deliver the unproven one. Every quarter, one question answers itself: did commissioned megawatts on the right go up, or did announced megawatts on the left go up? For eighteen years, the answer to that question at this company has overwhelmingly been the second.

09

The ADAG overhang: governance, regulators and the promoter

The part of the analysis that cannot be modelled — and cannot be ignored.

Reliance Power does not trade in isolation from its promoter group. It carries the reputation, the litigation and, at times, the direct regulatory exposure of the wider Reliance Group under Anil Ambani. Any assessment of this stock that stops at plant economics is incomplete, because a material part of the discount attached to the share is about the shareholder above the company, not the assets below it.

The group's record

Several ADAG entities have been through insolvency or default over the past decade — Reliance Communications, Reliance Capital, Reliance Home Finance and Reliance Commercial Finance among them. Lenders, bondholders and in some cases retail debenture-holders took losses. This history is why the market applies a governance discount to every entity carrying the name, regardless of the individual company's operating performance.

Live regulatory matters

  • Enforcement Directorate. The ED has pursued a money-laundering investigation into the group, alleging that public funds raised by the group's finance companies — a figure of around ₹15,548 Cr has been cited in relation to RHFL and RCFL — were diverted through connected entities. Attachment orders in 2026 have reached assets linked to Reliance Power, including equity shares of Reliance Power held by Reliance Infrastructure and certain receivables from Sasan Power and Reliance Power; a provisional order covering roughly ₹1,021 Cr of assets and a separate attachment of about ₹258 Cr in July 2026 have been reported.
  • CBI. A search and seizure action was reported at Reliance Power premises in July 2026, following earlier cases registered against the promoter.
  • SEBI. The market regulator rejected settlement applications from Reliance Infrastructure and Anil Ambani in a case concerning the alleged routing of approximately ₹6,526 Cr from Reliance Infrastructure to promoter-linked entities. A rejected settlement means the proceedings continue.
Read this carefully: these are allegations and ongoing proceedings, not concluded findings against Reliance Power as an operating company, and the company has contested actions affecting it. But for an equity investor, the distinction between "alleged" and "proven" matters less than the distinction between "resolved" and "live". Attachments on assets, searches at company premises and a regulator refusing to settle are all live. They constrain the company's ability to raise capital cheaply — which is precisely the resource the ₹10,000 Cr renewables build requires.

Ownership

As of June 2026, promoters held approximately 25% of Reliance Power, with foreign institutional investors around 13.6%, domestic institutions around 2.8%, and the public — overwhelmingly retail — around 58.6%. Two features of that table deserve attention. First, a promoter stake of a quarter is low for an Indian infrastructure company, and the group's holdings have historically been subject to pledges and, more recently, to regulatory attachment; current encumbrance levels must be read from the latest shareholding disclosure rather than assumed. Second, a public float approaching 60% held largely by retail investors is what makes this one of the most heavily traded and most volatile names on the exchange — the price moves on narrative flow far more than on quarterly operating data.

Why the discount is structural, not a mispricing

A recurring temptation with this stock is to compute what Sasan and Rosa would be worth as standalone infrastructure assets, compare that to a ₹10,058 Cr market capitalisation, and conclude the share is cheap. The gap is real — but it is not free money sitting on the floor. It is the market pricing the probability that cash generated inside the group does not reach the minority shareholder intact: through related-party dealings, through capital raised at prices that dilute, through assets attached by regulators, or through capital allocated into projects that never commission. That probability is not modelled with a discount rate; it is a judgement about people. Investors who are unwilling to underwrite that judgement should not attempt to arbitrage the asset value, because the discount will only close if the governance record changes.

How to value it — and what not to do

Conventional earnings multiples do not work here. FY26 earnings are negative; FY25 earnings were flattered by one-offs; and quarterly profit swings on impairments and settlements rather than on operations. Book value looks attractive — a market capitalisation of about ₹10,058 Cr against net worth of about ₹16,516 Cr, roughly 0.6× book — but book value at a company with this impairment history is a soft number, and the FY26 charge is a reminder that carrying values get revised.

The more defensible frame is cash. The operating fleet generated ₹2,824 Cr of operating cash in FY26. Against an enterprise value that includes substantial subsidiary project debt, that cash stream is what a buyer of the whole company would actually be acquiring — and it belongs almost entirely to two ageing coal plants with finite PPA lives and a long-term transition risk. Anything above that is a claim on the renewables build, which as of this writing has commissioned nothing.

The metric that settles the thesis

One number decides this case, and it is not a multiple. It is commissioned megawatts on the post-2024 renewables pipeline, measured against the ~24-month clock that began with the May 2025 SECI power purchase agreement — with two supporting readings alongside it: the quantum of net debt actually extinguished at the consolidated level (not the standalone claim), and Sasan's PLF holding at or near 90%. Those three together are checkable from public disclosures every quarter. Everything else about this company — the narrative, the history, the group headlines — resolves into them.

The single comparison that frames the valuation

Reliance Power's entire market capitalisation of roughly ₹10,058 Cr is approximately the same as the stated capital cost of the one solar-plus-storage project it has not yet built. The market is, in effect, valuing eighteen years of accumulated operating assets — a 3,960 MW pit-head coal plant with captive mines running at 91% PLF, a 1,200 MW plant with 97% availability, and ₹2,824 Cr of annual operating cash — at about the price tag of a single announced project. That is either an extraordinary mispricing of proven assets, or a precise measure of how little the market believes any rupee generated inside this group reaches its minority shareholders. Which of those it is will not be settled by a spreadsheet.

11

Future, risks, and the two sides of the argument

Where this goes from here, and what would prove each side right.

Reliance Power in 2026 is an operating utility with a functioning cash engine, a repaired parent balance sheet, a large contracted renewables pipeline, and a promoter group under active investigation. Those four facts pull in different directions, which is why the share is as volatile as it is.

The competitive set

It is worth being clear about the company Reliance Power keeps. In thermal generation it sits beside NTPC (state-owned, enormous, the sector's anchor), Adani Power, Tata Power, JSW Energy and Torrent Power. In the solar-plus-storage tenders it is now bidding against Adani Green, ReNew, NTPC Green, Tata Power Renewables, JSW Neo and a deep field of international developers. Those competitors generally have investment-grade credit, cheaper capital and unbroken execution records. In a business where the winning bid is determined largely by cost of capital, a governance discount is not merely a stock-market inconvenience — it raises the tariff at which this company can afford to bid, and therefore how often it wins.

Timeline

  • 2005
    The Reliance empire is divided; the power, telecom, financial-services and infrastructure businesses pass to Anil Ambani's Reliance Group (ADAG).
  • 2007
    Reliance Power wins the Sasan UMPP with a levelised tariff bid of roughly ₹1.196/unit — widely criticised as unsustainably aggressive.
  • Jan–Feb 2008
    India's largest-ever IPO raises ~₹11,563 Cr on a ~28,200 MW pipeline. Lists 11 Feb, collapses within the session. Bonus shares (3:5) are later issued to non-promoter holders.
  • 2010–2014
    Sasan and Rosa are commissioned. Meanwhile Samalkot is stranded by missing KG-basin gas and Krishnapatnam is abandoned after Indonesia's coal-pricing rule change.
  • 2015
    Tilaiya UMPP surrendered after land acquisition fails — another ~3,960 MW leaves the pipeline.
  • 2016–2021
    The long unwind: asset sales, defaults, restructuring; several ADAG group entities enter insolvency.
  • 2022–2024
    Lender settlements and one-time payments clear the bulk of standalone borrowings; the equity begins to recover from distressed levels.
  • Dec 2024
    Reliance NU Suntech wins SECI Tranche XVII — 930 MW solar + 465 MW / 1,860 MWh BESS, the auction's largest award.
  • May 2025
    25-year PPA signed with SECI at ₹3.53/kWh; ~₹10,000 Cr capex committed. Reliance NU Energies separately wins 350 MW from SJVN.
  • FY26
    Revenue ₹7,620 Cr; net loss ₹337 Cr after a ₹382 Cr impairment; operating cash ₹2,824 Cr. Sasan PLF ~91%, Rosa availability ~97%. Board approves an enabling resolution for a ~₹9,000 Cr raise and up to $600m of FCCBs.
  • Jun–Jul 2026
    SEBI rejects the promoter's settlement bid in the ₹6,526 Cr Reliance Infrastructure matter; ED attachments touch Reliance Power shares and receivables; a CBI search is reported at company premises on 18 July. The share sits near ₹24.5, down ~62% over twelve months.

The case for

  • Sasan is a genuinely good asset. A 3,960 MW pit-head supercritical plant with captive mines, running at ~91% PLF on a tariff so low the grid cannot afford not to dispatch it. That cost position is geographic and permanent.
  • The cash is real. ₹2,824 Cr from operations in FY26 — the FY26 loss is a non-cash impairment, not an operating failure.
  • Standalone debt has been cleared. A company that could not raise a rupee five years ago now has board authority for a ₹9,000 Cr equity raise and $600m of convertibles.
  • The renewables pipeline is contracted, not speculative. 930 MW solar + 1,860 MWh storage on a signed 25-year PPA at ₹3.53/kWh with SECI — a central counterparty, not a stressed discom — plus 350 MW from SJVN.
  • The old failure mode does not apply. Solar has no fuel cost; no foreign regulator can invert these economics the way Indonesia did to Krishnapatnam.
  • Assets are carried below book. ~₹10,058 Cr of market value against ~₹16,516 Cr of net worth.

The case against

  • An 18% lifetime conversion rate. ~28,200 MW announced in 2008 produced ~5,300 MW of plant. The new 1,745 MW pipeline has commissioned zero.
  • One plant carries the company. Sasan is ~75% of capacity and more of the cash. An outage, mining issue, tariff review or offtaker payment freeze there is not diversifiable.
  • "Debt-free" is a standalone claim. Consolidated debt/EBITDA is around 10.25× and D/E ~0.87. Project debt at Sasan and Rosa did not disappear.
  • Live regulatory action. ED attachments touching Reliance Power shares and receivables, a CBI search in July 2026, and a SEBI settlement refused in a ₹6,526 Cr matter — all unresolved.
  • Dilution is coming. ₹10,000 Cr of capex against ₹2,824 Cr of operating cash means new equity, converts and project debt. The share count and the leverage both go up.
  • It bids against cheaper capital. NTPC Green, Adani Green, ReNew and Tata Power fund at rates this group cannot match — which decides who wins the next auction.
  • Coal transition risk. The cash engine is a coal plant with a finite PPA horizon in a decarbonising grid.

What to watch, in order

  • Commissioned MW on the SECI project. The 24-month clock from the May 2025 PPA points to roughly mid-2027. Quarterly progress disclosures on solar energised and battery capacity installed are the primary evidence.
  • Consolidated net debt, not standalone. Track total borrowings across subsidiaries and the debt/EBITDA ratio, and compare each period against the deleveraging narrative.
  • Sasan's PLF and receivable days. If PLF drifts below the high-80s, or receivables from the offtaking discoms stretch, the cash engine is weakening before anything else shows.
  • The terms of the ₹9,000 Cr raise. Price, instrument, and who subscribes — this determines how much of any future upside accrues to existing shareholders.
  • Regulatory resolution. Outcomes in the ED, CBI and SEBI matters, and whether attachments affecting Reliance Power assets are lifted or extended.
The bottom line

Reliance Power is the rare company whose own prospectus is the best available argument against buying it. In 2008 it sold India roughly 28,200 megawatts of intention and delivered about 5,300 — and the one project it completed succeeded precisely because it depended on nobody else's fuel. Today it owns a coal plant good enough to fund a comeback and a renewables award large enough to be one, and it is asking the market to fund ₹10,000 Cr of construction on the strength of a signed PPA. There is exactly one way to test that request, and it does not involve a valuation multiple: the SECI clock started in May 2025 and runs to roughly mid-2027. Somewhere out in Rajasthan, 930 megawatts of panels and 1,860 megawatt-hours of batteries either get energised inside that window, or they don't. This company has spent eighteen years teaching investors to wait for the switch to be thrown.
