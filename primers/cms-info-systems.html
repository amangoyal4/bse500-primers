00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

CMS Info Systems is the largest cash-management company in India — the outsourced logistics network that physically moves and manages the country's banknotes. When an ATM is refilled overnight, when a bank's daily takings are collected in an armoured van, when a supermarket chain's cash is picked up and banked, when a currency chest is replenished — much of that plumbing, for much of the banking system, runs on CMS's fleet of 4,500-plus cash vans and its network of roughly 1.53 lakh business touchpoints across 97% of India's districts. It also runs a fast-growing second business: deploying and remotely managing ATMs and banking-automation technology for banks on an end-to-end outsourced basis.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It sells recurring services, not a product. CMS does not lend, take deposits or own the money it carries — it is a services company paid a small, contracted fee for each ATM it stocks, each cash point it services, each machine it monitors. Revenue is annuity-like and sticky: multi-year B2B contracts with banks, renewed at high rates, billed month after month. FY26 revenue was ~₹2,487 Cr, split roughly 60% cash management / 40% managed services & technology.
  • The economics are unusually good for a "logistics" business. CMS runs an EBITDA margin near 32% and a return on capital employed around 17-18% — numbers a trucking company could never post. The reason is route density: the same van, driver and guard serving more points per route turns a fixed-cost run into pure incremental margin. Scale and coverage, not price, are the moat.
  • FY26 was a rare stumble. Net profit fell ~18.6% to ~₹303 Cr even as revenue edged up — hit by a delayed State Bank of India cash-outsourcing rollout (a ~₹150 Cr revenue drag), a shrinking off-site ATM base, a competitor's disruptive exit, and larger-than-usual wage hikes. The stock trades on a modest ~13× earnings, reflecting a quality franchise caught in an off year rather than a broken one.
In plain terms

India runs on cash far more than its digital-payments headlines suggest, and that cash does not move itself. Somebody has to drive it, count it, guard it, insure it and load it into tens of thousands of machines every night. CMS is that somebody, at national scale — a business-to-business services firm, not a bank and not an IT company, that gets paid a modest recurring fee to keep the physical money supply circulating reliably and securely. Its profit does not come from charging more per run; it comes from squeezing more stops onto each route and each vault, so the fixed cost of a van and a crew is spread thinner. FY26 tripped over a big contract's timing and a bruising cost year. The underlying machine — dense routes, sticky bank contracts, and a growing pile of cash to move — is intact.

01

Cash logistics 101: the plumbing of physical money

No finance background needed — just the journey a banknote takes, and who is paid to carry it.

Every rupee note in your wallet has a physical supply chain behind it, as real as the one for petrol or parcels. Notes are printed by the RBI's presses, stored in currency chests at banks, dispatched to branches and ATMs, spent by you, collected as takings by shops and banks, sorted, and recirculated. Moving cash through that loop safely — under armed guard, fully insured, reconciled to the rupee — is a specialist job most banks would rather not do themselves. That outsourced job is the cash-management industry, and CMS is its largest Indian player. To follow the company you need five ideas.

The only cash-logistics terms you need

The one dependency that shapes the sector: trust, licences and a fleet you cannot rent overnight

Anyone can buy a van. Almost nobody can be trusted, overnight, to move a bank's cash. This industry runs on things that take years and regulatory clearance to assemble: armoured vehicles built to RBI/Ministry-of-Home-Affairs specification, armed and vetted guards, GPS-tracked routes, cash-in-transit insurance underwritten on your track record, currency-chest access, and a spotless security record — because a single major heist or fraud can end a contract and a reputation. A bank outsources cash to a CMS precisely because assembling all of that in-house is expensive and risky. That web of licences, assets, insurance and trust is the real barrier to entry, and it is why a handful of national players — not thousands of local couriers — carry the country's cash.

Who pays CMS, and for what

  • Banks — the core customers. Public-sector and private banks outsource ATM replenishment, cash-in-transit and increasingly the whole managed-ATM operation. This is where customer concentration sits: a handful of large banks drive a large share of volume, so a single big contract (like SBI's) can move the numbers materially.
  • Retailers, e-commerce & enterprises — organised retail chains, fuel stations, quick-commerce and logistics firms pay CMS to collect and bank their daily cash. A faster-growing, more fragmented, higher-frequency customer base than banks.
  • White-label / ATM operators & OEMs — CMS also deploys and services machines for ATM network operators and, through its technology arm, sells banking-automation hardware and software. The customer here is the ATM ecosystem itself.
02

The two engines: cash management and managed services

One mature, cash-generative core; one younger, faster-growing, technology-led leg.

CMS is best understood as two businesses under one roof, sharing a fleet, a network and a set of bank relationships. The larger, older engine is cash management — the vans, refills, pick-ups and vaults. The smaller, faster engine is managed services & technology — running ATMs end-to-end and selling banking automation. Roughly 60% of FY26 revenue came from the first, 40% from the second, and the strategic story is the second growing faster than the first.

Engine 1 — Cash Management Services

This is the network business. It bundles four related activities that share the same vans, guards and vaults:

  • ATM cash management — the biggest single activity: forecasting demand, loading and reconciling cash for tens of thousands of bank ATMs. Sticky, recurring, priced per ATM per month.
  • Cash-in-transit — armoured movement of cash between the RBI, banks, chests and large customers. High security, high barrier, steady.
  • Retail cash management — daily collection and banking of retail/e-commerce takings; the fastest-growing sub-line as organised retail expands.
  • Cash processing / vaulting — counting, authentication and sorting in secure facilities; the efficiency hub behind the fleet.

Engine 2 — Managed Services & Technology

This is the operate-and-automate business — CMS taking over more of the ATM value chain than just the cash inside it:

  • End-to-end / brown-label ATM management — CMS deploys the machine, maintains it, keeps it running and takes a per-transaction or per-ATM fee. It effectively runs the ATM channel for the bank.
  • Banking automation & self-service — supplying and servicing self-service kiosks, cash recyclers, note-sorting machines and other branch-automation hardware and software.
  • AIoT remote monitoring — sensors and software that watch ATM health, uptime, cash levels and security remotely, cutting downtime and reducing needless van trips. This is the digital layer that makes the physical network smarter.

Why the two engines belong together

The vans are the reason the technology sells, and the technology is the reason the vans run efficiently. A bank that already trusts CMS to carry its cash is the natural buyer when it wants to outsource the whole ATM operation — the relationship, the coverage and the security clearance are already in place. And AIoT monitoring feeds straight back into the cash business: knowing in real time which machines are low, or busy, or idle lets CMS route vans more intelligently and skip wasted trips. Cash management gives the company reach and trust; managed services gives it growth and a richer margin — and each makes the other harder for a rival to dislodge.

03

The cash paradox: why a UPI nation still needs more vans

The counter-intuitive fact that decides whether this whole business has a future.

Here is the objection every first-timer raises, and it deserves a straight answer: "Isn't India going cashless? UPI is everywhere — surely moving physical cash is a dying trade?" The intuition is reasonable and the data flatly contradicts it. Digital payments and physical cash are both booming at the same time. The amount of cash in the Indian economy is not shrinking — it keeps hitting new records.

Why does cash keep growing when UPI keeps setting records? Several forces pull in the same direction. Cash is still the default in rural and semi-urban India, among small businesses, older people and lower-income households. It carries no transaction trail, so the informal economy prefers it. It is held as a precautionary store of value — people keep notes for emergencies regardless of how they pay day to day. And the population and nominal economy keep growing, so even a falling cash-to-GDP ratio can sit atop a rising cash pile. India has around 176 billion banknotes in circulation — roughly three times the number of US dollar bills — and every one of them has to be printed, distributed, dispensed, collected and recirculated.

The surprise to sit with

The headline "India is going cashless" and the fact "India's cash in circulation just grew ~12% to an all-time high" are both true at once, and reconciling them is the whole investment case. UPI has replaced cash for a huge share of transactions — the ₹200 auto fare, the chai, the kirana bill — but it has not shrunk the stock of cash the economy holds and moves. CMS is paid on that stock and its circulation, not on the count of small digital payments. So the digital wave that looks like an existential threat has, so far, coincided with the very thing CMS needs — more physical currency to handle. The risk is real but it is slow and it is structural, not the cliff the intuition imagines. That gap between perception and data is exactly why a high-quality, high-return network can trade at a modest multiple.

The honest caveat: "resilient" is not "immortal." Over a long enough horizon, rising digital adoption, RBI's push on the Digital Rupee (CBDC), and any structural fall in cash intensity could slow ATM-transaction and cash-handling volumes. The bet is not that cash lasts forever — it is that the decline, if it comes, is gradual enough that CMS can migrate its network toward managed services, retail cash and technology faster than its cash-carrying volumes fade. Watch cash-in-circulation growth and ATM-transaction trends each year; they are the sector's vital signs.
04

The company, its history, and who owns it

A private-equity-built national champion, listed in 2021 and steadily broadening its ownership.

CMS is unusual among Indian listed companies in having been assembled and scaled largely under private-equity ownership before its 2021 IPO, rather than as a family promoter business. That history explains both its professional, roll-up-driven build and the steady reduction of its founding shareholder's stake since listing.

  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Founding shareholder: the company was controlled at IPO by Sion Investment Holdings, an affiliate of Baring Private Equity Asia (now part of the global EQT platform). Since listing, this promoter has steadily sold down its holding, progressively moving CMS toward a widely-held, professionally-managed, board-run company rather than a promoter-dominated one. Investors should confirm the exact current promoter/founder stake in the latest shareholding filing — it has changed materially post-IPO.
  • Professional management: with the PE promoter reducing, CMS increasingly resembles an ownerless, institutionally-held business — a profile that puts extra weight on the board, capital-allocation discipline and management incentives.
  • Capital returns: the FY26 combination of a ~3% share buyback at ₹340 and dividends signals a management comfortable returning cash — consistent with an asset-medium, cash-generative services model that does not need to reinvest every rupee.
Why the ownership arc matters: a departing private-equity seller can cap a stock for a while — an "overhang" of shares steadily coming to market can weigh on the price regardless of how the business performs. But the flip side is a genuinely professional, roll-up-built franchise with no single family calling the shots, and a management team returning cash via buybacks. Read the promoter-selldown pace and the buyback together: one is a headwind on the shares, the other a support.
05

The route-density moat: where the profit actually hides

The single most important idea in the case — and it is specific to a national cash-logistics network.

Most people assume a cash-logistics company makes money by charging a lot per trip. It doesn't — pricing per ATM or per pick-up is competitive and roughly known across the industry. CMS makes money by packing more stops onto each route. A van, a driver, two armed guards, fuel and insurance cost almost the same whether they service 6 points on a run or 12. The first cluster of stops on a route pays the fixed cost; every additional stop the network can add to that same run is nearly pure margin. Density, not price, is the entire game.

The fixed cost of a run barely moves with the number of stops; revenue roughly doubles when the stops do. That gap is why the market leader with the densest network earns the widest margin on the identical service. Illustrative.

This is why scale and coverage compound in cash logistics in a way they don't in ordinary trucking. Being present in 97% of India's districts with 4,500-plus vans means that when a new bank contract or a new retail chain comes along, most of its stops fall on routes CMS already runs. The incremental cost of adding those stops is small; the incremental revenue is not. A sub-scale rival, by contrast, has to add whole new routes — new vans, new crews, new insurance — to win the same customer, so its cost of that customer is far higher. The leader's network is therefore both cheaper to grow and more profitable to run. That is the moat, and it shows up directly in a ~32% EBITDA margin and ~17-18% ROCE that a pure haulage business could never reach.

The device that only fits a national cash network: profit = stops-per-route, not price-per-stop

Forget "how much does CMS charge to fill an ATM." The number that runs this company is how many revenue-generating stops it can hang on each fixed run — and how much of the country it already blankets so that the next customer's stops land on existing routes. CMS's vans cover roughly 4 lakh kilometres every single day — the equivalent of driving around the Earth about ten times, daily — carrying money for an economy that is supposedly going cashless. The profit is not in those kilometres; it is in how many paying stops share them. Double the stops on a route and the run's margin can more than double, because the diesel and the guards were already paid for. A challenger can match CMS's price on any single ATM; what it cannot cheaply match is a route that is already dense. Density is the one thing money alone cannot buy quickly — it has to be built, district by district, over years.

The same logic governs the vaults. A cash-processing centre has a fixed cost — building, machines, staff, security — and grows more profitable the more notes flow through it. So the network has two density levers working together: denser routes for the vans and denser throughput for the vaults. Both reward the player with the most volume, which is why this industry naturally consolidates toward a few large operators rather than fragmenting into many small ones.

06

The annuity contract: sticky, recurring, and concentrated

Why the revenue is dependable — and where its one structural fragility sits.

CMS's revenue has a quality that investors prize: it is recurring and sticky. Banks sign multi-year contracts to have their ATMs filled and their cash carried, and they renew them at high rates, because switching a cash vendor is disruptive, security-sensitive and rarely worth the risk for a small price saving. That gives CMS an annuity-like income stream billed month after month. But the same customer base carries a specific fragility: it is concentrated among a relatively small number of large banks, so the timing of one big contract can swing a year — as FY26 proved.

The pricing dynamic is worth understanding plainly. Because the service is broadly standardised and several credible national players exist, CMS competes for each contract on cost position and reliability, not on a premium price. Its edge is that its dense network lets it serve the contract cheaply and therefore bid competitively while still earning a good margin — the density moat again. But it means CMS is a price-taker at the contract level: it cannot simply raise fees to offset, say, a wage-inflation year. It has to claw margin back through efficiency, mix and volume instead.

The trade-off inside a "boring, recurring" revenue stream

Recurring revenue is not the same as effortless revenue. CMS's income is dependable in aggregate — thousands of ATMs and touchpoints billed every month smooth out any single wobble — but it is dependable the way a subscription base is: it must be constantly renewed, defended on price, and grown by winning the next tender. The concentration among big banks means the business is steadier than a project company but lumpier than a mass-consumer one: most years the annuity just compounds, and then occasionally a single large contract's timing (a delay, a win, a loss) makes a whole year look good or bad regardless of the underlying network's health. That is the correct lens on FY26 — a timing dent in a sticky base, not an erosion of the base itself.

07

The managed-services pivot: buying growth and margin diversity

The deliberate tilt from carrying cash toward operating and automating the ATM channel.

If cash management is the dependable, slow-growing core, managed services and technology is where CMS is placing its growth bet. Rather than simply filling a bank's ATMs, CMS increasingly wants to run them end-to-end — deploy the machine, maintain it, monitor it, and take a broader, longer, stickier fee — while selling the banking-automation and remote-monitoring technology that makes the whole network smarter. This leg grows faster than cash carrying, diversifies the revenue away from pure cash volumes, and deepens the bank relationship.

Management is guiding to a sharp reacceleration on the back of this leg. FY27 total revenue is targeted at ~₹2,800-2,900 Cr (13-17% growth) and, tellingly, services revenue at ~₹2,700-2,800 Cr (17-21% growth) — the recovery from FY26's contract-timing dent, with deferred SBI volumes and the enlarged managed-ATM portfolio ramping. It is guidance, not a guarantee, but it makes explicit where management expects the growth to come from: services, not cash carrying.

Three reasons the pivot matters strategically. First, growth: outsourced managed ATMs and automation are penetrating faster than raw cash volumes, as banks push non-core operations to specialists. Second, margin diversity: technology and end-to-end management add fee streams that don't rise and fall purely with cash-in-transit volumes, cushioning the day cash intensity eventually softens. Third, stickiness: a bank that has handed CMS its entire ATM operation and integrated its monitoring technology is far harder to dislodge than one that merely hires it to fill machines. The pivot is, in effect, CMS using the trust and reach earned by the vans to climb into a higher, more defensible layer of the banking-outsourcing stack.

Why this is the right hedge against the cash question

Section 03 argued that cash is resilient, not immortal. The managed-services pivot is precisely the insurance policy against the "not immortal" part. If, one day, cash-handling volumes do plateau or slowly fade, CMS wants to have already migrated a large chunk of its revenue into ATM operations, automation and monitoring — activities that persist, and even grow, as banks keep outsourcing and digitising their branch and self-service networks. The company is deliberately turning its physical, cash-era moat into a broader banking-technology-services franchise while the cash business is still healthy and funding the transition. Doing it now, from strength, is far easier than doing it later, under pressure.

08

The rivals and the barrier to entry

A consolidated field of national specialists, plus the banks' own in-house option.

Indian cash management is a consolidated business of a few serious national players, because — as section 01 explained — the licences, armoured fleet, insurance and trust needed to carry a bank's cash take years and regulatory clearance to assemble. CMS is the largest by ATM points and touchpoints; the rest of the field competes on the same density logic, and the banks themselves are always a latent alternative.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, this is not a price war of the ordinary kind — the licences, insurance and security clearances mean only a handful of firms can credibly bid for a national bank's cash at all, and among them the one with the densest existing routes can serve the contract cheapest. Coverage is the competition: the leader wins the widest margin on the same standardised service. Second, the real ceiling on the whole industry is not any single rival but the banks' in-house option — a bank can always choose to run its own cash and ATMs. The industry's pitch is that outsourcing to a trusted specialist with a national network is cheaper and safer than doing it yourself, and CMS's density is what keeps that pitch true. As long as CMS is the low-cost, high-reliability operator, insourcing rarely pays.

09

The FY26 wobble: what actually went wrong

A rare down year — and why its causes look like timing, not decay.

FY26 broke a long run of steady growth: revenue inched up ~2.6% to ~₹2,487 Cr, but net profit fell ~18.6% to ~₹303 Cr. For a business prized for its dependability, that is a jarring result, and it deserves a clear-eyed unpacking — because whether FY26 was a pothole or a sign of erosion is the crux of the current investment debate.

Walk through the causes — a handful of identifiable, largely one-off or timing-related items that took PAT from ~₹372 Cr to ~₹303 Cr, rather than any collapse in the core network's economics:

  • The SBI contract delay — a large State Bank of India cash-outsourcing rollout slipped, costing CMS an estimated ~₹150 Cr of expected revenue in FY26. This is pure timing: the contract is real, the volume deferred rather than lost, and it is expected to ramp in FY27 — which is why services guidance jumps.
  • Off-site ATM shrinkage — banks rationalised some off-site (standalone) ATMs, trimming the fee base CMS serves. A genuine structural nibble, though a modest one against the total network.
  • A competitor's disruptive exit & weaker H1 — market dislocation and softer first-half consumption weighed on volumes early in the year.
  • Larger-than-usual wage hikes & Labour Codes — CMS is a people-heavy business (drivers, guards, processors); a bigger wage increase and new labour-law provisions lifted the cost base, with an incremental employee-benefit charge recognised as an exceptional item. Because CMS is a contract-level price-taker (section 06), it could not simply pass this straight through in-year.

Crucially, the underlying network kept working: EBITDA held near ~₹800 Cr and the margin stayed around 32%, and Q4 PAT rose ~38% sequentially as the year closed stronger. The damage was concentrated in revenue timing and a cost spike, not in the route-density economics that make the business what it is.

The distinction that decides the thesis

There are two very different stories that can produce a falling profit: the business is decaying, or the business hit a bad year. FY26's causes point firmly at the second. A deferred (not lost) marquee contract, a modest ATM-count trim, a one-time labour-cost step, and a soft half — these are the texture of an off year in a sticky franchise, not the signature of a network losing its density or its customers. The tell is that margins and returns barely moved: a genuinely eroding business would show its damage in the margin, not just the timing of revenue. FY27's guided rebound — services up 17-21% as SBI ramps — is management's wager that the wobble was exactly that. It is the number to hold them to.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality network leader, in a dip year, betting it can outrun the slow cash question.

CMS enters FY27 with the machine intact: the densest cash network in India, a sticky bank-contract base, a growing managed-services leg, a deferred SBI contract set to ramp, and a management returning cash via buyback. What it cannot promise is the one thing every sceptic points at — that physical cash keeps circulating long enough for the pivot to higher-value services to complete. That tension frames both cases.

The bull case

  • Cheap for the quality — ~13× earnings for a market leader with ~32% EBITDA margins and ~17-18% ROCE, priced as if FY26's dip is permanent.
  • Cash is resilient — currency in circulation grew ~12% in FY26 to a record; the "cashless" fear runs ahead of the data, and CMS is paid on the cash stock.
  • Density moat — a national network in 97% of districts serves the next customer at low incremental cost; sub-scale rivals cannot match its economics.
  • Services pivot & SBI ramp — managed ATMs toward ~39,000, deferred SBI volumes, and FY27 services guidance of 17-21% growth diversify and re-accelerate revenue.
  • Cash returns — an asset-medium model funding buybacks and dividends; growth doesn't consume every rupee.

The bear case

  • The long cash question — over a decade, rising digital adoption, CBDC and falling cash intensity could slowly erode ATM-transaction and cash-handling volumes.
  • Customer concentration — a few large banks drive the numbers; one delayed, insourced or lost contract swings a year, as FY26's SBI slip showed.
  • Price-taker on cost inflation — a people-heavy business that cannot instantly pass through wage and labour-law cost spikes; margins can compress in a bad cost year.
  • PE overhang — the founding private-equity holder selling down can cap the shares regardless of operating performance.
  • Security tail-risk — a major heist, fraud or reputational lapse in a trust-based business could threaten contracts well beyond the incident itself.
The bottom line

CMS Info Systems is the company that keeps India's physical money moving — a market-leading, high-return, cash-generative services network trading at a modest multiple because the market is fixated on a "cashless India" that the data has not yet delivered and a single off year that looks like timing. The moat is real and unusually durable: density built district by district over fifteen years, sticky bank contracts, and licences and trust that money cannot buy quickly. The genuine risk is not a sudden collapse but a slow one — and the entire strategy is a race to convert a cash-era network into a broader banking-technology-services franchise before that slow risk bites. The next test is concrete and datable: whether FY27 services revenue lands in management's guided ~₹2,700-2,800 Cr range as the delayed SBI contract ramps and the managed-ATM portfolio scales to ~39,000 machines, with ROCE holding near 17-18%. Hit that, and FY26 reads as the pothole it appears to be; miss it, and the sceptics get their first real evidence that the network's best years were behind the wheel.
