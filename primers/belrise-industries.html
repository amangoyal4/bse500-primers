Industry & Company Primer NSE: BELRISE · BSE: 544405 Built for a first-time reader

Belrise Industries, the press shop that stamps India's two-wheelers

A complete walk through one of India's largest makers of the structural metal in a vehicle — chassis systems, body panels, fuel tanks, suspension and sheet-metal pressings — built quietly over three decades in Aurangabad as Badve Engineering, listed in May 2025, and now trying to grow out of its two-wheeler roots into cars, trucks and, improbably, aerospace. Here is how the presses work, who buys the parts, and what the market is really paying for.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Belrise Industries makes the structural metal of a vehicle — the parts you never see but that hold everything else together. Its presses stamp and its lines weld chassis systems, body-in-white panels, sheet-metal pressings, fuel tanks, exhaust systems, suspension and swing-arm parts, battery containers, steering columns, mirrors and a range of polymer components. More than 1,000 distinct products, sold overwhelmingly to two-wheeler makers — Bajaj, Hero, Honda, TVS, Royal Enfield — and, increasingly, to passenger-car, commercial-vehicle and agri-vehicle plants. It was founded in Aurangabad (now Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar), Maharashtra, as Badve Engineering, renamed Belrise in 2022, and floated a ₹2,150-crore IPO in May 2025.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a metal-bashing scale business, not a technology one. FY26 consolidated revenue was ~₹9,509 Cr (up ~14.7%), but the EBITDA margin is only ~12% overall (~14% on the core manufacturing line) and net profit ~₹502 Cr. This is the economics of stamping steel close to a customer's factory: modest margins, heavy fixed assets, and returns that live or die on capacity utilisation and how fast steel-price moves can be passed through.
  • Two wheels carry almost the whole company. Roughly 81% of manufacturing revenue is two-wheeler parts, and Belrise holds about a 24% share of India's two-wheeler metal-components market. That is genuine leadership — but it means the profit-and-loss statement is, in effect, a leveraged bet on how many scooters and motorcycles India buys in a given year, and on the health of a handful of large customers.
  • The whole story is about escaping that box. The IPO cash went mostly to repay ₹1,618 Cr of debt; the acquisitions (H-One India, and two European aerospace firms) are about winning more content per vehicle and stepping outside two-wheelers altogether. At a P/E of ~42×, the market is not paying for a steady press shop — it is paying for the diversification to work.
In plain terms

Every vehicle has a skeleton — the pressed and welded steel frame that everything bolts onto. Belrise is one of India's biggest independent makers of that skeleton, and of the fuel tanks, exhausts and body panels around it. It grew up making these for motorcycles and scooters next door to Bajaj's plants, and it is very good at it. The catch is that "very good at motorcycle frames" is a narrow, cyclical, thin-margin place to live. So the company is spending its new listed-market money trying to become three things at once: a bigger supplier of cars-and-trucks metal, a maker of parts that do not care whether the engine is petrol or electric, and — the genuinely odd part — a precision aerospace supplier in Europe. You are buying a solid metal-forming franchise plus a bet that it can broaden faster than its two-wheeler base can wobble.

01

What Belrise actually makes

No engineering degree required — just the difference between the parts you see and the parts that hold them up.

Split a vehicle into what it is made of, and most of the weight and structure is formed metal: sheets of steel pressed into shapes, then welded and bolted into assemblies. Belrise sits squarely in that world. It is not an engine, electronics or tyre company — it is a metal-and-polymer structures company, supplying the load-bearing and safety-relevant hardware that other people's badges get stuck on.

The Belrise product shelf — decoded
Product familyWhat it is (and why it matters)
Chassis systemsThe structural frame of a two-wheeler or the sub-frame of a larger vehicle — the "spine" the engine, wheels and body hang from. Safety-critical: it takes the load and the crash energy.
Body-in-white (BIW)The welded metal shell of a vehicle before paint and trim — panels, brackets and reinforcements joined into the body structure. High-tensile steel here is what makes a car both light and strong.

Why "boring structural metal" is a decent place to sit

Three features make this niche more attractive than it first looks. First, it is heavy and bulky — a fuel tank or chassis is expensive to ship far, so parts are made close to the OEM's assembly line, which locks a supplier geographically to its customer. Second, it is designed-in per model — you cannot swap in a generic chassis; each part is engineered to one vehicle's tooling, so once Belrise wins a platform, it holds that content for the model's whole life. Third, a lot of it is powertrain-neutral — a scooter still needs a frame, a fuel tank gives way to a battery box, and the presses do not care which. Those three — logistics, tooling lock-in, and engine-agnosticism — are the moat around an otherwise commodity-flavoured trade.

02

The metal-forming business, from coil to component

How a roll of steel becomes a welded assembly on an OEM's line — and where the money leaks out.

Belrise's factory is, at heart, a chain of heavy processes that turn a coil of steel into a finished, painted, ready-to-bolt assembly. Understanding that chain is understanding the business, because each step adds a little value, consumes a lot of capital, and is squeezed from both ends — by steel prices going in and by demanding automakers on the way out.

The further right a part travels down this chain before it ships, the more value Belrise captures — which is why sub-assembly and finishing, not raw stamping, are where it wants to grow.

Where the economics bite

  • Steel is roughly two-thirds of the cost. When steel prices jump, Belrise's costs jump the same day; recovering that from OEMs happens on a lag, through quarterly price negotiations. A sharp steel spike can therefore dent margins for a quarter or two even when volumes are perfectly healthy — the thin ~12% margin leaves little cushion.
  • It is capital-hungry and working-capital-hungry. Presses, weld lines and paint shops are expensive and must run near full to earn their keep; and a metal-parts maker carries steel inventory and OEM receivables, tying up cash. Utilisation and working-capital discipline matter more here than any clever product.
  • Proximity is the relationship. Belrise builds plants beside its customers' factories and feeds them just-in-time. That closeness is a moat (a rival cannot easily undercut a supplier already welded into the OEM's logistics) but also a shackle — the plant is committed to that customer's fortunes.
The one press line, two futures device

Here is the mechanical fact that explains Belrise's whole strategy in a sentence. A stamping press does not know what it is making. The same high-tensile blank, off the same tool, becomes the chassis of a petrol motorcycle or the enclosure of an electric scooter's battery pack — the tonnage of pressed steel is identical, only the drawing changes. That indifference is why Belrise can claim 73.8% of its manufacturing revenue comes from powertrain-neutral parts, and why it can chase cars, trucks, EVs and even aircraft with fundamentally the same shop-floor skill: forming and joining metal to a tolerance. The equipment is agnostic; the challenge is winning the drawings.

03

The two-wheeler stronghold — strength and dependency

One end-market carries the company, and one customer looms largest within it.

To read Belrise correctly you must sit with a single lop-sided fact: this is, first and last, a two-wheeler parts company. Around 81% of manufacturing revenue comes from scooters, motorcycles and three-wheelers; four-wheelers and commercial vehicles are still the minority. Belrise's ~24% share of India's two-wheeler metal-components market makes it a leader, but leadership of one cyclical end-market is also its central vulnerability.

The customer-concentration wrinkle

Belrise supplies 27 OEMs, including Bajaj Auto, Hero MotoCorp, Honda Motorcycle & Scooter India, TVS, Royal Enfield and Jaguar Land Rover. Impressive breadth — but the depth is uneven. Belrise is Bajaj Auto's largest supplier of sheet-metal and fabricated parts, and the two grew up together in the same Aurangabad–Waluj industrial belt. For Honda it makes the complete chassis system for one of the two-wheelers and supplies both metal and plastic. That kind of anchor relationship is wonderfully sticky — and quietly concentrating.

Why this matters: a company that is "market leader in two-wheeler metal parts" reads as diversified across dozens of models. But strip it back and a large slice of revenue leans on the health of a few big two-wheeler OEMs — with Bajaj the keystone. If two-wheeler demand softens, or one anchor customer in-sources a line or shifts a platform, Belrise feels it directly. The 27-customer list is real; so is the fact that the biggest few carry most of the weight.
The hidden dependency a generalist should notice

Belrise's fate is welded to a number it does not control: how many two-wheelers India stamps out this year. Roughly four-fifths of what its presses form ends up on a scooter or a motorcycle, and Indian two-wheeler demand is one of the most sentiment-driven cycles in the economy — swinging with rural incomes, the monsoon, fuel prices and financing. A brilliant quarter of factory execution can be undone by a weak festive season for Bajaj and Hero. That is the single force a first-time reader should weigh above every clever bit of the diversification strategy, because in the near term it overrides all of it.

04

The company and the Badve family

A three-decade Aurangabad engineering house that only recently met the public market.

Belrise is not a new company — it is a newly listed one. It was incorporated in 1996 as Badve Engineering Private Limited in Aurangabad, Maharashtra, built by the Badve family into one of the region's largest auto-component manufacturers, and renamed Belrise Industries in 2022 ahead of its market debut. Its founder-chairman Shrikant Badve and co-founder executive director Supriya Badve remain at the helm, and the family controls roughly two-thirds of the company.

Ownership & control

  • Promoters: the Badve family, holding roughly 66.5% after the IPO (the float was created by a fresh issue, so the family diluted rather than sold out — no offer-for-sale). A controlling, founder-run block.
  • Institutions: foreign investors ~8.9% and domestic institutions ~1.2% as of mid-2026, with the public holding the balance — an ownership base still finding its footing on a stock barely a year old.
  • Home base: Chhatrapati Sambhajinagar (Aurangabad), Maharashtra, with a manufacturing footprint that had grown to ~22 facilities across India and Europe after the FY26 acquisitions.
05

The IPO and the debt it was built to kill

Why the May-2025 float was, more than anything, a balance-sheet event.

Belrise's IPO was unusual in a useful way: it was entirely a fresh issue of ₹2,150 crore, with no promoter selling a single share. That structure tells you the purpose. This was not the family cashing out — it was the company raising primary capital to fix its balance sheet. About ₹1,618 crore of the proceeds were earmarked to repay debt, and that single act is the hinge on which the FY26 numbers turn.

Before the listing, Belrise carried the leverage typical of a fast-growing, capex-heavy metal-forming business that had funded its expansion with borrowings — net debt-to-equity had run close to 1.0×. Using the bulk of the IPO to retire that debt pulled net debt-to-equity down toward ~0.2× and, crucially, slashed the interest bill. This is why the FY26 result shows a striking gap between the top and the bottom line.

The surprise inside the FY26 numbers

Revenue grew ~15% in FY26. Profit grew ~41%. A generalist would assume some margin miracle — but the manufacturing margin barely moved (in fact overall EBITDA margin compressed slightly). The reason profit ran nearly three times faster than sales lives below the operating line: the ₹1,618-crore debt repayment gutted the interest expense, so far more of each rupee of EBITDA now drops to net profit. In other words, the headline earnings growth that supports the ~42× multiple is, for now, substantially a deleveraging story, not an operating one. The presses did not suddenly become more profitable; the loans went away. That distinction matters enormously for what happens next: interest can only be saved once, after which profit growth has to come from the factories and the diversification actually delivering.

06

Growing out of the box: 2W → PV, CV and content per vehicle

The core growth thesis — sell more metal to bigger vehicles, and more of it per vehicle.

Belrise knows its two-wheeler concentration is both its strength and its ceiling. The strategic project of the next few years is to diversify the end-market and deepen the content per vehicle: win passenger-vehicle and commercial-vehicle platforms, and sell each existing customer more of the vehicle rather than a single bracket. Management has spoken of aiming to roughly double four-wheeler revenue over about two to two-and-a-half years.

Two levers, pulled together

  • End-market diversification. Four-wheelers were only ~9% of manufacturing revenue in FY25 and commercial vehicles less; both are growing off a small base. Bigger vehicles carry far more metal per unit than a scooter, so even modest share gains in PV and CV move the revenue needle disproportionately. The H-One acquisition (below) was bought partly to accelerate exactly this — its clientele includes four-wheeler OEMs.
  • Content per vehicle. The higher-value game is to supply not just a stamped part but a finished sub-assembly — a complete chassis, a painted BIW module, a fuel-and-exhaust system — so Belrise captures more of the value chain from Section 02 on each vehicle. More content per vehicle also makes the supplier stickier: an OEM that buys a whole system is harder to unbundle than one that buys a bracket.

The catch in the growth plan

Winning four-wheeler content is harder than the arithmetic makes it look. PV and CV OEMs run demanding validation, need proven quality systems, and often have entrenched suppliers or their own in-house press shops. Belrise is credible — it already supplies BIW to marquee names and picked up H-One's four-wheeler know-how — but it is a challenger in cars, not the incumbent it is in two-wheelers. The diversification is the right strategy and the necessary one; it is also the part most exposed to execution risk, because it means competing away from home turf against larger, more diversified rivals.

07

The EV-agnostic bet

Why the electric transition is closer to a tailwind than a threat for structural-metal makers.

Much of India's auto-component base lives in quiet dread of electrification — engine, transmission, fuel-system and exhaust makers face a genuine existential question. Belrise mostly does not, and it says so with a number: 73.8% of its manufacturing revenue comes from powertrain-neutral products. The logic is structural, in both senses of the word.

Powertrain exposure of manufacturing revenue (FY26)

Powertrain-neutralchassis, BIW, panels, battery boxes
73.8%
Powertrain-linkedfuel tanks, exhausts, engine parts
~26%
Powertrain-neutral parts are needed whether the vehicle is petrol, hybrid or electric; the ~26% that is powertrain-linked (fuel systems, exhausts) is the slice with EV exposure.

Why the metal is engine-agnostic

  • A chassis is a chassis. An electric scooter or car still has wheels, weight, bumps and crash loads — it still needs a frame, a body shell, sub-frames, brackets and panels. None of that vanishes when the engine does.
  • EVs can need more structural metal. Battery packs are heavy and must be protected, so EVs demand robust battery enclosures and reinforced floor structures — a product line Belrise already makes. Heavier vehicles put more, not less, load through the structure.
  • Only the fuel-and-exhaust slice is at risk. The genuine EV casualty in Belrise's shelf is the ~26% that is powertrain-linked — fuel tanks and exhaust systems. Even there, India's transition is gradual and two-wheeler electrification uneven, so the erosion is slow rather than sudden.
The nuance: "EV-agnostic" is not "EV-immune." Belrise still has a real fuel-tank-and-exhaust business that a fully electric future shrinks, and a scooter EV carries less metal content overall than a car. But relative to an engine-parts maker staring at obsolescence, a structural-metal company sees the same transition as broadly neutral-to-positive — which is precisely why the powertrain-neutral percentage is a number management quotes at every results call. It is the answer to the question every auto-parts investor now asks first.
08

The acquisitions — and the improbable aerospace turn

Three deals that redraw what Belrise is: a domestic bolt-on, and a leap into European precision.

The listed Belrise has been an acquirer from day one, and its deals point in two directions. One strengthens the core two-and-four-wheeler metal business at home. The other two are a genuine surprise — a sheet-metal house from Aurangabad buying its way into aerospace and defence precision manufacturing in Europe.

Reading the aerospace pivot honestly

The aerospace move is the boldest and the least proven part of the Belrise story. The appeal is obvious: aerospace precision work earns fatter, stickier margins than auto stamping, runs on multi-year programme cycles insulated from the two-wheeler monsoon, and plugs Belrise into the supply chains of Airbus-scale commercial makers and a French fighter OEM. A dedicated defence, space and aerospace subsidiary has been incorporated to house the ambition. But the integration is early and imperfect — management flagged a one-time operational loss tied to the French SDM unit and guided that the aerospace business should turn profitable only in the following financial year.

The genuine surprise, stated plainly

A company whose day job is stamping motorcycle frames next to Bajaj's plants in Aurangabad now owns European workshops that make mission-critical parts feeding into the supply chains of the world's largest commercial-aircraft maker and a French fighter-jet programme. That is not a natural adjacency — it is a deliberate leap from high-volume, low-margin auto metal into low-volume, high-margin aerospace precision, funded by fresh listed-market capital. If it works, Belrise stops being purely a play on Indian two-wheeler volumes and becomes something genuinely rarer. If it does not, it is a distraction of management attention and capital from the core it still depends on. This is the part of the story with the widest range of outcomes.

09

The industry and the rivals

A cyclical, fragmented auto-metal market — with the OEMs themselves as a competitor.

Belrise competes in the Indian auto-components industry, whose fortunes track vehicle production and whose suppliers are perpetually caught between demanding automakers and volatile steel. In structural-metal specifically, the field is a mix of listed Indian champions, forging specialists, diversified giants — and, unusually, the customers themselves, who often run their own press shops.

The competitive logic behind the whole strategy

Line the rivals up and Belrise's plan explains itself. As a two-wheeler stamping specialist it is narrower and more cyclical than JBM, Endurance or Motherson, and it competes on cost against customers who can stamp parts themselves. Its three moves — diversify into higher-content four-wheelers, deepen content per vehicle, and buy into higher-margin aerospace — are all attempts to climb out of the most commoditised, most concentrated corner of the market toward stickier, richer, less cyclical revenue. Whether it closes the gap on the diversified majors or simply adds complexity to a two-wheeler house is the question the next few years answer.

10

Financials & valuation

Solid growth and returns that are only just respectable — priced as if the transformation is already working.

Belrise's financials describe a business growing steadily, deleveraging fast, and earning returns that are decent-but-not-dazzling for its sector. The debate is not the record — it is that the share, up sharply from its ₹90 IPO price to well above ₹230, already prices in a diversification and margin story that is only beginning to show up.

Two tensions sit inside that table. First, the leverage picture is two-faced: net debt-to-equity looks conservative at ~0.2× (because the IPO topped up equity), yet net debt-to-EBITDA is a more sobering ~2.3× and the acquisitions add borrowing — so "deleveraged" is true on one ratio and only half-true on the other. Second, the returns are still ordinary — an RoE around 9% and RoCE around 13% are respectable for a capital-heavy press shop but well short of the best auto-ancillaries, which is exactly why a ~42× multiple demands they climb.

The metric that settles the thesis

Cut through everything and one number decides whether Belrise deserves its multiple: the share of revenue that is not two-wheelers. Today roughly four-fifths of manufacturing revenue is two-wheeler metal. The entire bull case — richer four-wheeler content, stickier system sales, higher-margin aerospace, less exposure to the scooter cycle — resolves into that single figure rising, quarter after quarter, while the manufacturing margin holds near ~14% and net debt-to-EBITDA falls back below ~2×. If the non-two-wheeler share climbs and margins hold, the ~42× re-rates into a diversified components platform. If it stalls near where it is, Belrise remains a well-run but cyclical two-wheeler stamping house wearing a premium price tag. Watch the mix; the mix is the story.

11

Future, risks, and the bull/bear ledger

What has to go right, what could go wrong, and where the evidence will show up.

Belrise arrives at the public market as a proven metal-forming operator trying to become something broader before its two-wheeler cycle catches up with it. The operating base is real; the premium is entirely about the pivot. Here is the ledger.

The bull case

  • Leadership in a real niche — ~24% of India's two-wheeler metal parts, anchor relationships with Bajaj, Hero and Honda, and plants welded into customers' logistics.
  • Powertrain-neutral by design — 73.8% of manufacturing revenue survives, or grows with, electrification; the EV transition is closer to a tailwind than a threat.
  • Diversification runway — four-wheelers are only ~9% of revenue, so PV/CV wins and higher content per vehicle can move the needle for years off a small base.
  • Deleveraged and self-funding — the IPO cut net debt-to-equity from ~1.0× to ~0.2×, freeing cash flow that once serviced interest.
  • Optionality in aerospace — the SDM and Chester Hall deals open a higher-margin, counter-cyclical business if integration succeeds.

The bear case

  • Two-wheeler concentration — ~81% of manufacturing revenue rides one volatile cycle and a handful of big OEMs, Bajaj foremost.
  • Thin margins, steel & OEM squeeze — ~12% EBITDA with little buffer against steel spikes and automaker price pressure; margin already compressed slightly in FY26.
  • Earnings growth is borrowed from interest, not operations — PAT +41% was largely deleveraging; that engine only fires once.
  • Leverage is not fully gone — net debt-to-EBITDA ~2.3×, and fresh acquisitions add debt and integration risk.
  • Aerospace and diversification are unproven — a French unit already booked an operational loss; competing away from home turf against JBM, Endurance and Motherson is hard.
  • Rich multiple on ordinary returns — ~42× earnings against RoE ~9% leaves the price leaning on a pivot that has barely begun.
The bottom line

Belrise is a thirty-year-old Aurangabad press shop that spent its first listed year doing exactly what it promised on paper — repaying debt, buying capability, and pointing every part of the business away from its two-wheeler dependence. The operating franchise is sound and the strategy is coherent. What the ~42× multiple demands is proof, and that proof has a specific address: the quarterly revenue mix. When four-wheeler, commercial-vehicle and aerospace revenue climb far enough that "two-wheeler parts maker" is no longer the first thing you say about Belrise — and the manufacturing margin holds near 14% while net debt-to-EBITDA slides back under 2× — the re-rating earns itself. Until the mix moves, the market is paying a premium price for a scooter-cycle business and a promise. The near-term test is concrete: whether the French SDM unit turns profitable in FY27 as guided, and whether four-wheeler revenue is visibly on its way to doubling. Those two milestones, more than any margin line, are where the transformation stops being a story.
