00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Fortis Healthcare is one of India's largest private hospital chains — but its story is unlike any other listed hospital in the country. It was built into a national brand, then very nearly destroyed by its own founders, the Singh brothers, who siphoned money out of it before it collapsed into losses in 2018. It was rescued that same year by Malaysia's IHH Healthcare, which took control, cleaned up the governance, installed professional management, and spent the next several years turning a scandal-ridden distressed asset into a well-run, growing chain. Today it is a two-engine company: a network of ~40 metro hospitals, plus Agilus Diagnostics (formerly SRL), one of India's largest pathology-lab chains.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The defining fact is the turnaround, not the origin. Fortis is the rare case where the controlling shareholder is the healing agent. In FY18 the company posted a loss of ~₹934 Cr on ~₹4,561 Cr of revenue. Under IHH's ownership and CEO Ashutosh Raghuvanshi, FY26 delivered ₹9,128 Cr of revenue (+17.3%), operating EBITDA of ₹2,085 Cr (+31.3%) at a 22.8% margin, and PAT of ₹1,064 Cr (+31.5%). That is a genuine, multi-year operating and governance rehabilitation.
  • It's a two-engine business. The hospitals did ~₹7,773 Cr of revenue (~85% of the total) at a ~22% margin; the diagnostics arm, Agilus, did ~₹1,527 Cr — a separate, high-ROCE pathology business Fortis now owns 100% of, after buying back its private-equity investors. The two engines are worth thinking about (and valuing) separately — a "sum-of-the-parts" story.
  • It carries real debt — and a rich price. Unlike debt-free Max, Fortis ended FY26 with net debt of ~₹2,334 Cr (~1.09× EBITDA) — the honest cost of the Agilus stake buyback and a run of acquisitions. And at a ~₹71,600 Cr market cap on ₹1,064 Cr of profit, the stock trades near ~70× earnings — pricing in years more of the turnaround compounding.
In plain terms

Think of Fortis as a rescued patient that is now back on its feet and running. Its founders looted it and it nearly died; a big, deep-pocketed Malaysian hospital operator (IHH) bought control, replaced the board and management, stopped the bleeding, and rebuilt it into a profitable, professionally-run chain. What you own today is two connected businesses under one roof: a set of premium city hospitals that do complex, high-value medicine (cancer, cardiac, transplants, robotic surgery), and a nationwide chain of blood-and-scan labs (Agilus) that Fortis fully owns. The business quality is real and improving fast; the two catches are that it took on some debt to buy back the labs and to buy hospitals, and that the market already prices in a lot of the good news.

01

The rescue: fraud, near-collapse, and the IHH turnaround

The one thing that truly defines Fortis — and separates it from every other listed hospital.

Most hospital investment cases are about beds, occupancy and margins. Fortis has all of those — but the force that actually defines it is a rescue story. To understand the company you have to understand that its founders nearly destroyed it, and that its salvation came from an unusual place: the outside company that bought control. This is the single feature no rival shares.

The Singh-brothers collapse

Fortis was founded by Malvinder and Shivinder Singh — the same brothers who inherited the drug-maker Ranbaxy and sold it to Japan's Daiichi Sankyo in 2008 for ~₹9,576 Cr. They ploughed part of that fortune into Fortis (hospitals) and Religare (financial services). But over the following decade, investigators allege the brothers systematically siphoned money out of the companies they controlled through a web of related-party loans and transfers. By early 2018 the situation was terminal: independent directors resigned, auditors raised red flags, and reports emerged that roughly US$78 million had been improperly moved out of Fortis. The Singhs quit the board in February 2018; the company closed FY18 deep in the red, with a loss of about ₹934 Cr. A trusted national hospital brand had been hollowed out from the inside.

Why this is the defining risk-and-reward: a hospital's most valuable asset is trust — patients, doctors and lenders must believe the institution is sound. The Singh-era fraud attacked exactly that. The whole subsequent story is about who could restore it, and whether the cleanup was real. It was.

The 2018 rescue by IHH Healthcare

With Fortis distressed and up for grabs, a bidding contest broke out (IHH versus a Manipal–TPG consortium and others). In November 2018, IHH Healthcare Berhad — a large, well-capitalised Malaysian hospital operator (itself controlled by Malaysia's sovereign-linked Khazanah and Mitsui of Japan) — won, injecting ~₹4,000 Cr of fresh capital via a preferential share allotment and taking a ~31.1% controlling stake. Crucially, this was primary capital: the money went into the company to stabilise it, not to the departing fraudsters.

The governance and operating cleanup

  • A new, independent-heavy board with separated Chairman and CEO roles — Non-Executive Chairman and a professional MD & CEO in distinct seats. This directly fixed the concentration of unchecked control that had let the Singhs divert funds.
  • Professional management installed: IHH brought in Dr. Ashutosh Raghuvanshi (an 18-year Narayana Health veteran) as MD & CEO to run the operational turnaround — a clinician-operator, not a promoter.
  • Operational focus restored: the new team drove occupancy, ARPOB (revenue per bed), specialty mix and cost discipline — the blocking-and-tackling that a distracted, fraud-riddled organisation had neglected.
  • The result in numbers: from a ~₹934 Cr loss in FY18, Fortis turned to profit within a few years and, by FY26, was earning a ~22.8% EBITDA margin and ₹1,064 Cr PAT — an unambiguous rehabilitation.
Why "the controller is the cure" is unusual

In most Indian governance scandals, the promoter is the problem and minority shareholders are left to hope for a clean-up that never fully comes. Fortis is the opposite: the fraudulent founders were replaced by a competent industrial owner with deep hospital expertise, capital and reputational skin in the game. IHH didn't just buy a stake — it re-engineered the company. That is why Fortis's premium valuation is partly a "governance re-rating": the market is paying for the fact that a once-toxic asset is now run to institutional standards by an operator that runs hospitals across Asia (Malaysia, Singapore, Turkey, India).

The Daiichi overhang — the loose thread that capped IHH's stake

There is one long-running complication. When the Singhs sold Ranbaxy to Daiichi, Daiichi later won a Singapore arbitration award (~₹3,500 Cr) against them for concealing regulatory problems. Chasing that money, Daiichi went after the Singhs' Fortis-linked shares — and in December 2018 India's Supreme Court imposed a "status quo" that froze IHH from increasing its Fortis stake through the mandatory open offer it was obliged to make. For seven years, IHH was stuck at ~31%, unable to consolidate control, while the courts untangled the mess.

02

Hospital economics 101: how a hospital earns

No medical degree required — just the four numbers that drive everything.

A hospital is, financially, a capacity business. It has a fixed number of beds and a largely fixed cost base — doctors, nurses, the building, the equipment — and it earns by filling those beds with patients, each of whom pays a certain amount per day. Four numbers tell you almost the whole story of Fortis's hospitals engine.

The only units you need — Fortis's FY26 numbers
TermWhat it means (and Fortis's FY26 figure)
Beds / capacityThe physical ceiling on in-patients it can treat. Fortis runs ~6,100 operational beds across ~36 facilities (including JVs and O&M-managed beds) in 12 states.
Occupancy (%)What share of beds are filled. Higher = more revenue on the same fixed cost. Fortis ran ~69% in FY26 — a healthy, mature level, with occupied beds up ~15% over the year.
ARPOB"Average Revenue Per Occupied Bed" — the price of a filled bed. Fortis's ran at ~₹2.51 Cr per bed per annum (rising ~6–10% YoY), driven by richer, more complex casework.
ALOS"Average Length Of Stay" — how many days a patient stays (~4.22 days). Shorter stays = faster bed turnover = more patients per bed per year.
The simple revenue equation

Roughly: Hospital revenue ≈ Beds × Occupancy × ARPOB × 365. So Fortis grows its hospitals three ways — add beds (capacity), fill them (occupancy), and raise the value of each filled bed (ARPOB, via more complex specialities). The magic is that beds and staff are mostly fixed costs: once a hospital crosses its break-even occupancy, almost every extra rupee of revenue drops to profit. That is "operating leverage", and it is exactly the lever the IHH turnaround has been pulling — pushing ARPOB up faster than costs so margins expand year after year.

Matured vs new hospitals — the two-speed portfolio

Not all of Fortis's beds earn the same. A hospital open for a decade — full roster of star doctors, steady referrals, word-of-mouth trust — runs at high occupancy and drops most revenue to profit. A freshly-acquired or freshly-expanded hospital runs half-empty while carrying its full cost base, so it barely breaks even at first. Fortis therefore has mature hospitals throwing off strong margins and newer/acquired beds dragging the average down until they fill. Every acquisition (People Tree in Bengaluru, Shrimann in Jalandhar) temporarily dilutes the blended margin — and every year of maturation quietly lifts it back up.

03

The company and who controls it

A national brand, a foreign industrial owner, and a professional management team.

Fortis today is a professionally-managed company controlled by a strategic industrial owner — the opposite of the promoter-family model that dominates Indian corporates (and the opposite of its own founding era). Its largest shareholder is a hospital operator that runs facilities across Asia; its board and management are independent professionals.

Ownership & management

  • Controlling shareholder — IHH Healthcare (~31.2%). Held via its subsidiary Northern TK Venture. IHH is one of Asia's largest private-healthcare groups (Gleneagles, Pantai, Parkway, Acibadem), majority-owned by Malaysia's sovereign fund Khazanah with Mitsui of Japan as a large holder. IHH has stated it wants to raise its Fortis stake toward 50%.
  • The rest is a broad institutional float. With the founders gone and no controlling family, Fortis has a large, influential register of domestic and foreign institutions — a governance positive, and part of why the open offer at ₹170 drew almost no takers (holders judged the shares worth more).
  • Management: MD & CEO Dr. Ashutosh Raghuvanshi (ex-Narayana Health) has led the operational turnaround since IHH took over; a Non-Executive Chairman sits separately, preserving the checks that were absent in the Singh era.
  • Balance sheet: Fortis carries net debt of ~₹2,334 Cr (~1.09× EBITDA) at FY26 — modest and well-covered, but a real number (see section 08). This is not a debt-free compounder like Max or Rainbow; it is a growing chain that has been buying assets and buying back its diagnostics arm.
Why the ownership structure matters to you

Two things follow from IHH's control. First, governance is institutional, not promoter-whimsy — the very failure that sank Fortis is the thing IHH exists to prevent, so the "governance discount" that once crushed the stock has flipped to a modest premium. Second, there is a permanent corporate-action overhang: a strategic parent that wants to go from ~31% to 50% means open offers, possible tender opportunities, and — in a tail scenario — a full buyout or delisting are always on the table. That is unusual for an Indian large-cap and it is a genuine, two-sided feature of owning Fortis.

The two-engine business model

Hospitals plus a fully-owned diagnostics chain — read this once and the company clicks.

Fortis's ~₹9,128 Cr of revenue comes from two businesses with different economics: the hospitals (the dominant engine) and Agilus Diagnostics (a separate pathology-lab chain). Understanding them separately is the key to valuing the company — because a lab business and a hospital business deserve different multiples.

Why "two engines" is the right lens

A hospital is a heavy, bed-and-real-estate business: high fixed costs, big capex, profit that comes from filling beds. A diagnostics lab chain is the opposite: light on capital, run on scale and a hub-and-spoke collection network, throwing off high returns on the money invested. Blend them and you understate how good the lab is and how capital-hungry the hospitals are. Fortis bought back 100% of Agilus in early 2025 precisely because it wanted full control of that high-quality second engine — and because a wholly-owned Agilus can later be monetised (a stake sale or IPO) to "unlock value", the way Apollo is doing with its pharmacy arm.

Engine 1 · The hospitals

The bulk of the business — and where the specialty mix is doing the heavy lifting.

The hospitals segment is the heart of Fortis. In FY26 it did ~₹7,773 Cr of revenue (+19.1%) — about 85% of the group — at an EBITDA margin around 22%, and it drove the group's profit surge. The network spans India's big metros: Delhi-NCR (Gurugram, Noida, Vasant Kunj, Shalimar Bagh), Mumbai (Mulund), Bengaluru, Kolkata, Jaipur, Chennai, Amritsar, Ludhiana, Mohali and more.

What "high-value care" actually means here

Fortis's edge, like every quality hospital chain, is doing the hard, expensive medicine most hospitals can't. A routine ward admission earns a modest ARPOB; an organ transplant, robotic cancer surgery, a complex cardiac or neuro case earns many times more — and needs scarce sub-specialists and advanced equipment. Under IHH, Fortis has deliberately tilted its mix toward these tertiary/quaternary specialities, which is why ARPOB keeps climbing even as occupancy is broadly steady.

The margin-improvement journey in one idea

The whole "IHH margin story" boils down to moving each bed up the value ladder. Instead of filling beds with low-complexity cases, Fortis fills them with oncology, robotics and complex surgery — the same bed, the same nurse, the same building, but far more revenue and profit per occupied day. That is why FY26 EBITDA margin rose to 22.8% from 20.4% even though bed count and occupancy moved only modestly: the mix improved. It is the cleanest, most repeatable lever Fortis has, and it is still running — Fortis's margins remain a few points below best-in-class Max, leaving room to close the gap.

06

The operating-leverage engine

ARPOB × occupancy × mix — how a steady bed count produces surging profit.

Here is the single most important mechanism in a hospital investment case, applied to Fortis. Because beds and staff are mostly fixed costs, profit is far more sensitive than revenue to occupancy and pricing. Fortis's FY26 numbers are a textbook example: revenue grew ~17%, but EBITDA grew ~31% and PAT grew ~32% — profit compounding roughly twice as fast as the top line.

When revenue rises through occupancy and ARPOB rather than new capacity, most of the extra rupee is profit — so EBITDA and PAT outrun revenue. This is the compounding the market pays a premium for.
The bull case in one mechanism

Stack three levers and you get the whole story. (1) Occupancy: at ~69%, Fortis has headroom before it hits the ceiling — filling existing beds is almost pure profit. (2) ARPOB / mix: shifting toward oncology, robotics and complex care lifts revenue per bed with no extra beds. (3) New-bed maturation: acquired and expanded beds (Bengaluru, Jalandhar, Greater Noida) start diluting margin, then add to it as they fill over 2–4 years. Run all three at once, on a still-improving cost base, and profit can keep compounding well above revenue for years. That is the operating-leverage engine — and it is precisely what a ~70× multiple is paying for.

The risk embedded in the same mechanism: operating leverage cuts both ways. If a wave of acquisitions fills slower than hoped, or if a competitive metro market caps ARPOB, the leverage that lifts profit in good years turns into margin drag — and a stock priced for continued acceleration de-rates hard on any stumble.

07

Engine 2 · Agilus Diagnostics — the second engine and the SOTP

A high-ROCE lab chain Fortis now fully owns — and can one day monetise.

Agilus Diagnostics — formerly SRL Diagnostics, one of India's largest pathology chains — is Fortis's quiet second engine. It runs a nationwide hub-and-spoke lab network (400+ labs and thousands of collection points) doing blood tests, imaging and specialised pathology. In FY26 it did ~₹1,527 Cr of revenue (+8.5%) — and, more importantly, its operating EBITDA jumped ~45% to ~₹360 Cr, lifting its margin to 23.6% from 17.7%. It is a smaller, capital-light, high-return business bolted onto the heavy hospital chain.

Why Fortis bought back 100% of it

  • The buyback (2024–Jan 2025): private-equity investors (IFC, Jacob Ballas, Avigo/Resurgence) exercised a put option to sell their ~31.5% Agilus stake; Fortis paid ~₹1,780 Cr, valuing Agilus at ~₹5,700 Cr, taking its stake to 100% by January 2025.
  • An IPO was shelved: Agilus had filed to list separately in 2023 but withdrew the papers in early 2024. Owning it fully gives Fortis flexibility to choose the moment and structure of any future monetisation.
  • The cost: that ~₹1,780 Cr buyback is a big chunk of why Fortis carries net debt today (section 08) — the company took on borrowing to own a high-quality asset outright.
Why the "SOTP" argument matters

"Sum-of-the-parts" means valuing the pieces separately and adding them. A diagnostics chain like Agilus is capital-light and high-ROCE, so listed diagnostics peers (Dr Lal PathLabs, Metropolis) trade at rich multiples of profit. A hospital is capital-heavy. If you value Fortis as one blended lump, you risk under-crediting the lab. Bulls argue that a wholly-owned, margin-improving Agilus is worth several thousand crore that the market half-ignores inside the hospital company — and that a future Agilus IPO or stake sale could crystallise that value (and pay down Fortis's debt). That optionality — a hidden, monetisable second engine — is a distinct part of the Fortis case that a pure hospital chain like Max simply doesn't have.

The honest caveat: Agilus grew revenue only ~8.5% in FY26 — organised diagnostics is fiercely competitive (aggressive online players, price pressure on routine tests). Its FY26 story was margin, not volume. The SOTP upside is real but not automatic; it depends on Agilus sustaining that margin and, eventually, a listing that a buoyant market is willing to reward.

Beds, debt and capital allocation

The growth engine — and the honest net-debt point that sets Fortis apart from Max.

Fortis is growing capacity through a mix of brownfield bed additions (expanding existing hospitals — the cheapest, fastest way to add beds), acquisitions, and lease / O&M (operate-and-manage) agreements. But unlike debt-free Max Healthcare, Fortis funds this with some borrowing — and it is important to be honest about that.

The honest comparison with Max

This is where Fortis genuinely differs from its premium peers. Max Healthcare and Rainbow are net-cash / debt-free and self-fund their expansion. Fortis is not — it ended FY26 with ~₹2,334 Cr of net debt, at ~1.09× EBITDA. That leverage is modest and easily serviced against ₹2,085 Cr of EBITDA and strong cash generation, so it isn't a warning sign in itself. But it means: (1) Fortis has less of a "fortress balance-sheet" premium than Max; (2) a chunk of its firepower went to buying back Agilus rather than only building beds; and (3) a future Agilus monetisation could wipe the debt out entirely. Net-net: it's a growing chain using a sensible amount of leverage — but you should not mistake it for the debt-free compounders it is often bracketed with.

09

Medical tourism and the metro network

A premium demand pool that fills high-value beds — and a structural industry tailwind.

Two forces feed Fortis's high-value beds: international patients (medical tourism) and the broader formalisation of Indian healthcare. Both push revenue toward the complex, well-paid end of the ledger that the whole margin story depends on.

The medical-tourism engine

  • ~₹639 Cr of international-patient revenue in FY26 — up ~18.5%, and ~7.8% of hospital revenue. Patients travel from South Asia, Africa, the Middle East and CIS countries for cardiac, oncology, transplant and orthopaedic care.
  • Why it's so valuable: international patients skew toward high-acuity, cash-paying procedures — exactly the ARPOB-lifting mix Fortis wants. India's cost advantage (world-class care at a fraction of Western prices) makes this a durable, structurally growing pool, and Fortis's metro flagships (Delhi-NCR, Mumbai) are the natural landing points.

The industry backdrop

India is massively under-bedded versus global norms, health insurance is spreading, incomes are rising, and lifestyle disease (cancer, cardiac, diabetes) is climbing — all of which lifts demand for exactly the tertiary care Fortis provides. At the same time, the market is formalising: organised chains (Apollo, Max, Fortis, Manipal, Narayana, Aster) take share from standalone nursing homes. Fortis is one of the handful of genuinely national brands positioned to ride that consolidation.

The regulatory backdrop: hospitals operate under the Clinical Establishments framework and state rules, and face periodic public/political pressure on pricing (notably around critical care, stents and high-value procedures). Fortis's premium, low-government-scheme mix insulates margins, but price-regulation risk is a permanent feature of Indian healthcare worth keeping on the radar.

How to think about the ~70× multiple

At ~₹71,600 Cr market cap on ₹1,064 Cr of FY26 profit, Fortis trades around ~70× trailing earnings (EV/EBITDA ~33×) — a full price even by Indian hospital standards, though a touch below ultra-premium Max (~74×). That price is underwritten by three things: (1) the operating-leverage runway (occupancy + ARPOB + new-bed maturation) still has years to run; (2) the margin gap to Max leaves room to keep improving; and (3) a wholly-owned Agilus adds "sum-of-the-parts" optionality plus a possible debt-clearing monetisation. Bulls add a corporate-action kicker — IHH's stated march to 50% keeps a takeover/tender possibility alive. Bears counter that ~70× on a debt-carrying chain leaves no room for error: any margin wobble, integration miss, price-regulation shock, or a disappointing Daiichi outcome, and a stock this richly priced can de-rate sharply.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine turnaround — priced for the good story to keep going.

Fortis is one of the more remarkable rehabilitations in Indian corporate history: from a fraud-hollowed loss-maker to a well-governed, ~23%-margin, profit-compounding chain with a fully-owned diagnostics jewel. The investment debate is now about price, debt and the pace of the remaining upside — plus a live corporate-action overhang — not about whether the business is sound.

The bull case

  • Proven, ongoing turnaround — IHH governance + professional management drove margin from 20.4% to 22.8%, with room to close the gap to Max's ~26%.
  • Operating leverage runway — ~69% occupancy plus a rising oncology/robotics mix means profit can keep compounding faster than revenue.
  • Two engines & SOTP — a wholly-owned, high-margin Agilus adds hidden value and a possible IPO/monetisation that could clear the debt.
  • Corporate-action optionality — IHH's stated intent to reach 50% keeps a tender/buyout possibility on the table.
  • Structural demand — under-bedded India, spreading insurance, rising complex-disease burden, and a growing medical-tourism pool.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~70× earnings leaves little margin for error; vulnerable to de-rating on any disappointment.
  • Carries real debt — ~₹2,334 Cr net debt (~1.09× EBITDA), unlike debt-free Max/Rainbow; acquisition-fuelled and Agilus-buyback-driven.
  • Legal / control overhang — the Daiichi dispute (Tokyo ruling due Sep 2026) and IHH's unfinished march to 50% keep uncertainty alive.
  • Diagnostics competition — Agilus grew only ~8.5%; organised pathology is a price war, so the SOTP upside isn't guaranteed.
  • Execution & regulation — integrating acquisitions and maturing new beds is hard; healthcare pricing is a perennial political target.
The bottom line

Fortis is a high-quality, professionally-run hospital chain with a genuine second engine — the product of one of India's cleanest corporate rescues. The business risk is far lower than it was a decade ago; what remains is valuation risk, a modest but real debt load, and a two-sided corporate-action overhang from IHH's ambitions and the Daiichi litigation. The story over the coming years will be written in a few numbers each quarter — hospital EBITDA margin (is the gap to Max closing?), ARPOB and occupancy (is the operating leverage still delivering?), Agilus margin and any listing move, and IHH's stake. Get those right and the compounding can justify the premium; stumble, and a ~70× multiple has a long way to fall.
