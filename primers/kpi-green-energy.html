00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

KPI Green Energy develops solar and wind-solar hybrid power plants in Gujarat under the brand "Solarism." The catch that makes it different from almost every other listed renewable name is that it does two things at once. In one business — IPP (Independent Power Producer) — it owns the plant, keeps it on its own balance sheet, and sells the electricity under 25-year contracts, collecting a steady annuity for decades. In the other — CPP (Captive Power Producer) — it builds a plant for a corporate customer (a factory, a textile mill, a chemicals unit) and effectively sells them the asset, booking the revenue quickly and moving on. One clock ticks for 25 years; the other resets every few months. Running both is the whole story.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It grows like a builder, not a utility. Pure power producers grow slowly — you can only add so many owned plants a year, and each one is a decade-long payback. KPI Green grows fast (FY26 total income up ~56% to ~₹2,742 Cr, PAT up ~57% to ~₹509 Cr) precisely because the CPP "develop-and-sell" leg lets it recycle capital and book value the moment a captive plant is handed over. Management still talks in terms of 40–50% annual growth — a builder's cadence, not a utility's.
  • The order book is enormous; installed capacity is a fraction of it. KPI Green's total renewable portfolio (built + under construction, across IPP and CPP) reached ~6.26 GW at FY26 exit — but only ~1.62 GW was actually installed and running. The other ~4.64 GW is contracted and under execution. As with any developer, the gap between "signed" and "switched on" is where the value — and the risk — lives.
  • The growth is increasingly debt-funded. To build IPP plants it must own, KPI Green borrows heavily. FY26 consolidated borrowings jumped nearly four-fold to ~₹4,532 Cr, lifting the debt-to-equity ratio to ~1.49 from ~0.46 a year earlier. A near-44.7% slice of the promoter's stake is pledged. The stock trades around ~12–13× earnings — cheap for the growth rate, which tells you the market is watching the balance sheet as closely as the order book.
In plain terms

KPI Green is really two companies stapled together. The first is a property-developer-of-solar: it builds power plants for corporate customers and sells them, taking cash out fast — that's the CPP leg, and it's what powers the eye-catching growth. The second is a landlord-of-electrons: it keeps some plants for itself and rents out the power for 25 years — that's the IPP leg, and it's what builds durable, annuity-like earnings but eats a lot of capital up front. The bet is that the fast, self-funding CPP business keeps throwing off the cash and credibility to finance the slow, debt-heavy IPP fleet — and that the debt taken on to own those plants stays comfortably serviced as it climbs.

01

Solar developers 101: where the money actually comes from

No engineering needed — just the handful of terms that drive a renewable-power business.

A solar plant is boringly simple physics and interesting finance. Panels turn sunlight into electricity; there is no fuel bill and almost no moving parts. So the entire economic question is: who paid to build it, and who collects the electricity revenue? Everything about KPI Green flows from the two different answers it gives to that question. To follow the business, you need six terms.

The one economic idea that splits this company in two: capital velocity

Imagine two ways to make money from the same solar plant. In the first, you keep it and collect ~₹3.5 per unit for 25 years — enormous total earnings, but you've sunk your capital into an asset that pays you back slowly, so you can only do a few a year. In the second, you build it for a customer, hand it over, and get most of your money back in months — far less total profit per plant, but you can recycle that capital into the next build and the next, compounding turnover. That trade-off — total lifetime earnings versus how fast your capital comes back to work — is capital velocity, and it is the single lever that separates KPI Green's two engines. The CPP leg is high-velocity; the IPP leg is low-velocity but high-annuity. The company deliberately runs both.

Who buys renewable power in India

  • State discoms & utilities (via PPAs) — bodies like GUVNL (Gujarat Urja Vikas Nigam) run auctions and sign 25-year PPAs. This is the classic IPP customer — huge volume, fixed tariff, slow-but-sure.
  • C&I (Commercial & Industrial) captive buyers — factories, textile units, chemical plants and mills that want their own dedicated solar/hybrid plant to slash power bills and green their operations. This is KPI Green's CPP customer base — and Gujarat's dense industrial belt is exactly where they cluster.
  • Group & open-access customers — via captive/group-captive structures that let a company draw power from a plant it partly owns, sidestepping expensive grid tariffs. The regulatory plumbing behind the CPP boom.
02

IPP vs CPP: the two clocks that run the company

Read this once and every number in the primer clicks into place.

KPI Green's defining feature is that it earns money on two completely different time horizons at once. The same team, the same land bank, the same "Solarism" brand — but one plant is kept and milked for 25 years, and the next identical plant is built and sold within the year. These are not two products; they are two business models with opposite cash rhythms, and the mix between them is the most important dial the company turns.

Most power companies you can value with a single mental model — a utility is an annuity, an EPC contractor is a project shop. KPI Green's whole identity is the deliberate refusal to be either.

03

The company, the KP Group, and who controls it

A Gujarat family group with three listed arms — and a supply chain that runs through itself.

KPI Green Energy — until 2022 named KPI Global Infrastructure — is the flagship listed company of the KP Group, a Gujarat industrial house founded and controlled by Faruk G. Patel. The group is unusual in that it has three separate listed companies that feed each other, which is central to understanding both KPI Green's advantages and its related-party risks.

Ownership & the group map

  • Promoter: Faruk G. Patel and KP Group entities hold the controlling stake. A notable feature investors must track: a large portion of the promoter holding — around 44.7% — has been pledged. Pledged promoter shares are a governance and refinancing flag worth monitoring in every shareholding filing, especially as group-wide debt rises.
  • The three listed arms (keep them straight): KPI Green Energy (KPIGREEN) is the solar/hybrid IPP + CPP developer — the company in this primer. KP Energy (KPEL) is the group's wind-power EPC and O&M specialist. KP Green Engineering (KPGREEN, formerly under the group's fabrication arm) makes the steel structures, galvanised towers and fabricated components that solar and wind plants need — it posted ~₹1,250 Cr revenue and ~₹136 Cr PAT in FY26 in its own right.
  • Why that map matters: when KPI Green builds a hybrid plant, it can source the wind EPC from KP Energy and the fabricated steel from KP Green Engineering — all inside one family group. That is a genuine execution edge (aligned incentives, secured supply, faster builds) and simultaneously a related-party dependency — a chunk of KPI Green's cost base and reliability rests on sister companies, and cross-group transactions need arm's-length scrutiny.
The group structure is a double-edged sword: a captive, in-house supply chain (steel from KP Green Engineering, wind EPC/O&M from KP Energy) lets KPI Green move faster and lock in capacity that outsiders queue for. But it also means the three companies rise and fall together, related-party transactions must be watched, and a stress at one group entity — or a pledged-share margin call at the promoter level — can ripple across all three. Treat KP Group as a system, not three unrelated tickers.
04

The CPP cash flywheel: why "build-and-sell" is the growth engine

The counter-intuitive heart of the story — and the reason KPI Green grows like no utility can.

Here is the fact that surprises most people meeting this company: for much of its history, KPI Green made more of its money selling plants than owning them. The captive (CPP) model — develop a solar or hybrid plant for a corporate buyer, hand it over, book the revenue, then service it under a long O&M contract — is what let a mid-sized Gujarat developer post 50%-plus growth year after year. It is closer to a high-turnover project developer than to a sleepy power utility, and that changes everything about how you read the numbers.

The mechanism is a flywheel. KPI Green uses its land bank, secured power-evacuation capacity and in-group EPC to build a captive plant relatively cheaply and quickly. It sells that plant to a C&I customer who wants cheaper, greener power without the hassle of developing it themselves. The cash comes back fast — often within the year — and gets recycled into the next build. Because the capital doesn't sit locked in a 25-year asset, the same equity can turn over multiple projects, compounding revenue at a rate a pure asset-owner simply cannot match.

Why the flywheel needs the land bank to keep spinning

The flywheel only turns as long as there are shovel-ready sites to build on. That is why KPI Green's 6,000-plus acre land bank and ~3.4 GW of secured power-evacuation capacity matter more than they first appear — they are the fuel tank. In Gujarat, the binding constraint on solar isn't sunlight or even demand; it is land with a grid connection. By warehousing both ahead of need, KPI Green can start a new captive plant the moment a customer signs, keeping the cash cycle short. A developer that has to go hunting for land and evacuation for every order loses months per project — and months are exactly what capital velocity cannot spare.

05

The IPP annuity: PPAs, tariffs and the 25-year promise

The slower, heavier, steadier engine — and where the durable earnings are built.

If CPP is the sprinter, IPP is the marathon runner. When KPI Green keeps a plant instead of selling it, it signs a long-term Power Purchase Agreement — usually 25 years — with a buyer like Gujarat's state discom GUVNL, and then collects a fixed tariff on every unit the plant generates for a quarter of a century. It is a much slower way to earn, but it builds something CPP never can: a contracted, inflation-resistant, decades-long income stream that keeps paying long after the build is forgotten.

The economics of an IPP plant are almost entirely front-loaded and then almost entirely predictable. KPI Green spends heavily to build and own the plant (funded mostly by debt — see the next section). Once it is running, the costs are tiny — sunshine is free, panels barely move — so most of that fixed-tariff revenue drops toward operating profit. A 25-year PPA at a known tariff turns a solar plant into something close to a bond: a long, visible stream of cash, as reliable as the buyer's creditworthiness and the sun's arrival.

Why KPI Green wants both engines, not just the fast one

A company that only built and sold plants would be at the mercy of every industrial cycle and every quarter's order flow — feast when factories invest, famine when they don't. The IPP annuity is the ballast against that. Each owned plant KPI Green commissions adds a slab of contracted, 25-year revenue that keeps paying regardless of whether new CPP orders show up next quarter. Over time, a growing owned fleet turns a lumpy project developer into a business with a thick, visible base of recurring cash — which is exactly why management keeps steering capacity into IPP even though it is the slower, more capital-hungry path. The annuity is what a builder buys with the cash the flywheel throws off.

The nuance to hold: the IPP annuity is genuinely attractive, but it is bought with debt and time. Every megawatt KPI Green keeps rather than sells is capital locked away for years and borrowings added today — so the very thing that makes the company more durable (a bigger owned fleet) is also the thing that makes its balance sheet heavier. The IPP and CPP legs are not just two revenue lines; they pull the capital structure in opposite directions.
06

Installed vs pipeline: the gap that is the whole valuation

6.26 GW sounds enormous — but only a quarter of it is actually generating.

When KPI Green talks about a ~6.26 GW portfolio, it is summing everything it has built and everything it has contracted and is building. Those are very different things. Capacity that is installed and running earns money today; capacity that is under execution is a promise that still has to survive land, permits, financing, module supply and grid connection before it earns a rupee. The distance between the two is the single most important operating gauge in the story.

Only ~1.62 GW of the ~6.26 GW portfolio was installed and generating at FY26 exit — roughly a quarter. The other ~4.64 GW is the coiled spring: contracted work that becomes revenue only as it is commissioned. The pace of that conversion is what turns a big-sounding pipeline into actual profit.

This funnel is why the company can grow so fast and why the risk is real. A ~4.64 GW execution book is years of visible work — for a company that installed ~0.85 GW in FY26, it is a multi-year runway that underwrites the 40–50% growth ambition. But every gigawatt in that book depends on things outside a spreadsheet: solar-module availability and price, grid-evacuation readiness, land and approvals, and financing on acceptable terms. When those line up, commissioning surges and revenue follows; when a module price spikes or an evacuation line slips, contracted megawatts sit idle as cost with no income — the developer's oldest trap.

The metric to actually track, quarter by quarter

Forget the headline 6.26 GW; it is a ceiling, not a scoreboard. The number that tells you whether the story is working is capacity commissioned per quarter — how fast the under-execution book converts into installed, revenue-generating megawatts — split by segment. A quarter where CPP commissioning runs hot means fast cash and booked revenue; a quarter where IPP commissioning runs hot means new annuity but fresh debt drawn. Read the two together and you can see, in real time, whether KPI Green is compounding on its own cash or on the bank's — which is the question the whole thesis turns on.

07

Funding the build: the debt that owning plants requires

The least glamorous number in the primer — and, right now, the most important.

Growth this fast is not free. To own IPP plants for their 25-year annuity, KPI Green has to pay for them up front, and it does that the way every asset-owner does — with borrowed money, typically around a 75:25 debt-to-equity structure at the project level. That is fine, even sensible, when the annuity comfortably covers the interest. But it means the balance sheet expands with every owned plant, and in FY26 that expansion turned sharp.

Consolidated borrowings jumped nearly four-fold in a single year, pushing debt-to-equity from ~0.46 to ~1.49. This is the price of leaning into the capital-heavy IPP leg — and the reason the market prices the stock cautiously despite the growth.

Two things make this manageable rather than alarming — for now. First, the CPP cash flywheel keeps generating self-funded revenue that isn't reliant on debt, giving the group internal cash to service and part-fund the build. Second, EBITDA is growing fast too — up ~73% to ~₹1,006 Cr in FY26 at a ~37% margin — so the earnings that cover the interest are rising alongside the borrowings. A rising debt load against a rising, high-margin EBITDA is a very different picture from debt against flat profits.

Why the debt trajectory is the real swing factor

The bull and bear cases on KPI Green both start from the same place — a fast-growing developer with a huge pipeline — and diverge entirely on one question: can it keep funding the IPP build without the balance sheet getting ahead of the cash flow? If commissioning stays brisk, EBITDA keeps compounding, and the CPP leg keeps recycling capital, then rising debt is just the normal cost of building an annuity, and interest coverage holds. If module prices spike, a big project slips, or discom payments lag, then debt at ~1.5× equity plus ~44.7% pledged promoter shares turns from a footnote into the headline. The growth is real; the financing of the growth is the thing to watch, every quarter, without fail.

Watch the pledge and the coverage: ~44.7% of promoter holding pledged, D/E at ~1.49, and a rapidly rising interest bill mean the balance sheet has less slack than the growth headlines suggest. Track net debt/EBITDA, interest coverage, and any change in the pledged-share percentage as closely as you track revenue — a growth developer's undoing is almost always its funding, not its order book.

Why India's solar cycle is running — and why Gujarat especially

The structural tailwind, and the reason this developer is standing in the right doorway.

KPI Green is not swimming against the current — it is riding one of the strongest structural trends in the Indian economy, and it happens to be headquartered in the state that best exploits it. Two forces stack up: a national renewable build-out of historic scale, and a Gujarat-specific set of advantages that make it the natural home for exactly KPI Green's kind of business.

The insight that separates this from a generic "green is growing" story

Plenty of companies claim to ride the renewable wave. What makes KPI Green's position specific is that Gujarat gives it all four ingredients of a solar developer in one geography: cheap sun and wind to generate power, secured land and grid evacuation to build on, a dense wall of industrial customers to sell captive plants to, and a state policy regime that has been friendly to renewables for years. Most developers have to assemble those pieces across states and stitch them together; KPI Green operates where they naturally overlap. That is why a mid-cap Gujarat company can run both an IPP and a CPP engine at once — the local density makes the two-clock model practical in a way it wouldn't be for a developer scattered across the country.

None of this removes the cyclicality: auctions still set tariffs, module prices still swing, and a policy shift can still cool the market. But the reason to keep building is structural — an economy that needs vastly more clean power — rather than a subsidy that could be switched off.

09

Not a pure EPC, not a pure IPP — and why that matters

The comparison that stops investors mis-pricing the company.

The quickest way to misunderstand KPI Green is to file it as one familiar thing. It is not a solar EPC contractor (which builds plants for a fee and owns nothing), and it is not a pure IPP utility (which owns plants and grows slowly). It is a hybrid that borrows the best — and the risks — of each. Seeing exactly where it sits between the two is what tells you how to value it.

Why the hybrid is genuinely hard to copy

The tempting critique is that KPI Green is "just" an EPC with an IPP bolted on — but the combination is more than the sum. The CPP business throws off cash and customer relationships; those fund and de-risk the IPP build; the growing IPP fleet provides the annuity ballast that makes the whole enterprise financeable and less cyclical; and a captive in-group supply chain (steel, wind EPC) keeps both legs' costs and timelines under control. A pure EPC can't build the annuity; a pure IPP can't grow at 50%; a newcomer lacks the land bank, evacuation and group supply chain to do either at KPI Green's speed. The moat isn't any single leg — it's the machine that runs both at once off a shared Gujarat base.

The flip side of that machine is that it must be judged on both yardsticks at once. Value it like a pure IPP and its growth looks impossible to justify; value it like a pure EPC and its balance sheet looks recklessly heavy. It is only coherent when you hold the CPP growth rate and the IPP debt load in the same frame — which is precisely why so many quick takes on the stock get it wrong in one direction or the other.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A fast, structurally-tailwinded developer — asked to prove its funding can keep pace with its ambition.

KPI Green enters FY27 with the best problem a developer can have: more demand than it has yet built, a structural tailwind behind it, and two complementary engines to grow from. The real debate is not whether it can win work — the ~4.64 GW execution book answers that — but whether it can commission that work fast enough, and fund the owned half of it prudently enough, to convert a big pipeline into durable, well-financed earnings.

The bull case

  • Dual-engine growth — CPP capital velocity powers 50%-plus revenue growth while IPP builds a 25-year annuity; few peers run both at scale.
  • Structural demand — India's ~500 GW target plus Gujarat's industrial captive market give multi-year visibility, not a subsidy-deadline sugar high.
  • Home-turf advantage — 6,000+ acre land bank, ~3.4 GW secured evacuation, and an in-group supply chain (KP Energy, KP Green Engineering) speed the build.
  • Margin & mix — EBITDA margin expanding toward ~37%; move into hybrid, BESS and floating solar lifts value per project.
  • Modest multiple — ~12–13× earnings for a 40–50% grower leaves room if the balance-sheet worry proves manageable.

The bear case

  • Debt trajectory — borrowings up ~4× to ~₹4,532 Cr, D/E ~1.49; if leverage outruns EBITDA, interest coverage and refinancing become the story.
  • Pledged promoter shares — ~44.7% of promoter holding pledged is a governance/margin-call flag, amplified by group-wide leverage.
  • Execution & module risk — a ~4.64 GW book hostage to module prices, evacuation readiness, land, approvals and financing terms.
  • Counterparty & cyclicality — discom payment delays on IPP PPAs; CPP orders tied to industrial capex appetite that can cool.
  • Related-party complexity — reliance on sister companies for supply and EPC needs arm's-length scrutiny; the three KP listcos rise and fall together.
The bottom line

KPI Green has done the hard part that eludes most renewable hopefuls: it has built a machine that grows like a developer and compounds an annuity like a utility, off a Gujarat base that supplies land, sun, customers and an in-house supply chain in one place. What is unproven is whether the funding can keep step with the ambition — whether the cash the CPP flywheel throws off, and the fast-rising EBITDA, can carry an IPP build that has already quadrupled the debt in a single year. The next few quarters answer it in two checkable prints: the pace at which the ~4.64 GW execution book converts into commissioned megawatts, and whether net debt/EBITDA holds as that build proceeds. Watch those two numbers together each quarter; they will tell you whether KPI Green is compounding on its own cash flow or on the bank's.
