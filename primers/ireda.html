IREDA, India's dedicated green-energy lender

A complete walk through the Indian Renewable Energy Development Agency — how a specialist infrastructure financier actually earns money (it takes no deposits; it borrows via bonds and on-lends to power projects), why a Navratna government NBFC is the purest listed way to lend against India's energy transition, the blockbuster 2023 IPO that turned it into a multibagger, and the three-way tension between super-cycle growth, rising bad loans, and a premium valuation.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

IREDA is a government-owned non-banking financial company (NBFC) that exists for one purpose: to lend money to India's renewable-energy projects. It is a Navratna Central Public Sector Enterprise under the Ministry of New & Renewable Energy (MNRE), ~72% owned by the Government of India. It does not take deposits like a bank; it raises money from bonds and loans and on-lends it to solar parks, wind farms, hydro plants, biomass units and, increasingly, green hydrogen, EV and transmission projects. In short: it is the specialist, policy-driven financier of India's energy transition — a pure-play way to lend against the country's clean-energy build-out.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A fast-growing green-lending machine. The loan book (loan assets) reached ₹92,980 Cr at March 2026, up 22% year-on-year; FY26 sanctions jumped 39% to ₹51,883 Cr and disbursements 16% to ₹34,946 Cr. Net profit hit a record ₹1,873 Cr (+10%) on revenue of ₹8,309 Cr (+23%). It is one of the fastest-growing lenders of its size in India.
  • A blockbuster IPO turned multibagger. IREDA listed in November 2023 at ₹32/share, opened at ₹50, and within ~10 months had risen ~550% to peak above ₹200 — one of the most celebrated PSU listings of the cycle. It was granted Navratna status in April 2024, the 17th company to earn it, giving it far greater autonomy to invest and grow.
  • But the easy part is over — and three tensions now define the stock. (1) Asset quality: gross NPAs rose sharply to 3.49% (from 2.45% a year earlier) as a few wind and legacy accounts soured; power projects can go bad. (2) The spread: net interest margin (~3.3%) is thin and volatile, and rising provisions dented Q4 profit. (3) Valuation: even after a sharp de-rating from its IPO frenzy, IREDA still trades at a premium to its larger cousins PFC and REC — the debate is whether "green growth" justifies paying up.
In plain terms

Think of IREDA as a government-backed "green bank without a bank licence." It borrows cheaply — because it is a sovereign-linked Navratna PSU — by selling bonds to investors, then lends that money to companies building solar farms and wind projects, keeping the small gap in between as its profit. Its entire reason to exist is India's promise to build a colossal amount of clean power (500 GW of non-fossil capacity by 2030), and someone has to finance it. That is the bull story in one line. The catch: power projects don't always work — a wind farm in a bad wind year can't pay its loan — so the risk is that its bad loans climb faster than its book grows. And after the stock quadrupled post-IPO, a lot of that growth is already in the price.

01

Infrastructure-lender economics 101

No finance degree required — just the five numbers that drive an NBFC like IREDA.

A bank and a lending NBFC do the same core thing — borrow money at one rate, lend it at a higher rate, and keep the difference — but IREDA is a special kind of lender. It has no branches, no savings accounts, no depositors. That single difference shapes everything about how it earns, and where its risks sit. Five numbers tell you almost the whole story.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and IREDA's number, FY26)
Loan book / AUMThe total money lent out and still owed to it. IREDA's stood at ~₹92,980 Cr — up 22% in a year. This is the engine; growing it is how the company grows.
Cost of fundsThe average rate IREDA pays to borrow. ~7.05% in FY26 (down from 7.61%). Because it has no cheap deposits, this is higher than a bank's — its whole edge is a sovereign-linked discount to what a private borrower would pay.
Yield on loansThe average rate it charges borrowers (~10%+). The gap between yield and cost of funds is the raw margin.
NIM / spread"Net interest margin" — net interest income as a share of assets, ~3.3%. This is the thin slice IREDA lives on. A tiny move in cost of funds or yield swings profits a lot.
GNPA / credit cost"Gross non-performing assets" — loans that have gone bad, 3.49% at Mar '26. Every soured loan eats into profit via provisions. For a project lender, this is the number that can wreck a good year.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ Loan book × (Yield − Cost of funds − Credit cost − Opex). So IREDA grows profit in three ways: grow the loan book (lend more), widen the spread (borrow cheaper or charge more), and keep bad loans low (so provisions don't devour the spread). The magic and the danger are the same: it borrows short-ish and lends long against 20-year power projects, so it earns a spread on a very large book — but that book carries concentrated project risk. When a big wind or solar borrower stops paying, there's no diversified retail cushion to absorb it. That is why, for IREDA, asset quality matters even more than for an ordinary bank.

Why "no deposits" is the defining feature

  • A bank funds itself with CASA (current & savings accounts) — cheap, sticky money from millions of savers. IREDA has none of that.
  • IREDA funds itself with borrowings — bonds (including green bonds and, historically, tax-free bonds), term loans from banks, and foreign-currency lines. Its cost of money is set by the market and its credit rating, not by a network of savers.
  • The upside: as a sovereign-linked Navratna PSU it borrows at rates a private NBFC could only dream of — that funding advantage is a core part of its edge. The downside: it can never enjoy a bank's near-zero-cost CASA float, so its spread is structurally thinner and more exposed to interest-rate cycles.
02

How the money flows: raise, spread, lend, build

Follow one rupee from a bond investor to a solar farm — and back.

IREDA sits in the middle of a chain that turns capital-market money into clean-power infrastructure. Understanding that chain explains both where the profit comes from and where the risk lives.

The cash then flows back: projects generate electricity revenue → repay IREDA → IREDA repays its bondholders and keeps the spread. Break the chain (a project fails to generate/repay) and the loan turns into an NPA.

The project-finance credit process — what IREDA actually does

Lending to a power project is not like handing out a personal loan. IREDA's core skill is appraising and structuring long-dated project loans — the discipline that separates a specialist from a generalist:

  • Appraisal. Before sanctioning, IREDA assesses the project's technical viability (will the solar/wind resource actually be there?), the developer's track record, the power-purchase agreement (PPA — who buys the electricity, and are they creditworthy?), and the cash-flow projections.
  • Sanction & disbursement. A "sanction" is a committed loan approval; "disbursement" is money actually handed over as the project is built. FY26 sanctions (₹51,883 Cr) ran well ahead of disbursements (₹34,946 Cr) — the gap is a pipeline of future loans.
  • Security & ALM. Loans are secured on project assets and cash flows. Because projects repay over 15–20 years but IREDA's borrowings are shorter, it must manage asset-liability matching (ALM) — making sure money coming in lines up with money going out, so it isn't forced to refinance at a bad time.
Why the appraisal skill is the real moat

Any lender can hand money to a solar developer. The hard part is knowing which projects will actually generate cash for 20 years — reading the wind data, judging whether a state electricity board will pay on time, structuring the loan so IREDA gets repaid even if things wobble. IREDA has done this since 1987; that domain expertise in renewable-project risk is genuinely differentiated, and it's why banks and developers value it as a specialist. But the same specialisation is its vulnerability: it is concentrated in one sector. When renewables hit a rough patch — a bad wind year, a policy change, a developer default — IREDA has no other business to lean on.

03

The company and who controls it

A 1987 government agency that became a listed Navratna.

IREDA is not a startup riding a theme — it is a nearly four-decade-old government institution that was set up specifically to develop and finance renewable energy in India, long before it was fashionable. It spent most of its life as an unlisted policy agency; the 2023 IPO put it on the stock market, and the Navratna upgrade in 2024 gave it the autonomy of a top-tier PSU.

Timeline

  • 1987
    IREDA is incorporated as a government financial institution under the MNRE to promote, develop and finance new & renewable-energy projects — decades ahead of the sector's mainstream boom.
  • 2010s
    Builds a specialist book across solar, wind, hydro and bioenergy; issues tax-free bonds and green bonds to fund lending; becomes the go-to policy lender for the sector.
  • Nov 2023
    IPO — priced at ₹32/share, lists on NSE & BSE on 29 Nov 2023, opening at ₹50 (a ~56% listing pop). Becomes a market sensation.
  • Apr 2024
    Granted Navratna status (17th company) by the Department of Public Enterprises — greater autonomy to invest up to ₹1,000 Cr per project without central approval.
  • 2024
    Incorporates IREDA Global Green Energy Finance IFSC Ltd in GIFT City to raise low-cost foreign capital and lend in foreign currency to developers.
  • Q1 FY26
    Raises ₹2,006 Cr via QIP (its first as a listed company) to fund growth; GoI stake eases from 75% to ~71.8% as a result.
  • FY26
    Record year on the top line — loan book ₹92,980 Cr, PAT ₹1,873 Cr — but GNPA rises to 3.49% on wind/legacy stress, and the stock de-rates from its post-IPO peak.

Ownership & management

  • Government of India ~72% (71.76% at Mar 2026) — IREDA is a promoter-controlled PSU; the President of India, through the MNRE, is the promoter. This sovereign parentage is central to its cheap funding and its policy mandate.
  • Public float ~28% after the FY26 QIP — retail public ~23–24%, with domestic institutions (~2.4%) and FIIs (~2.1%) holding the rest. The QIP diluted the government below its earlier 75% but kept it firmly in control.
  • Minimum public shareholding (MPS): as a listed PSU IREDA must eventually take GoI holding toward the 75% ceiling / lift public float toward 25%+; further stake sales or QIPs are likely, which is both a growth-funding lever and a supply overhang for the stock.
  • Governed as a CPSE — its board and leadership are government-appointed; strategy is tightly aligned with national renewable-energy policy, for better (mandate, low-cost funding) and worse (less commercial freedom than a private NBFC).
04

The IPO and the re-rating

How a ₹32 government stock became a household multibagger — and then cooled.

You cannot understand IREDA-the-stock without understanding the 2023 IPO frenzy. The listing turned a sleepy policy lender into one of the most talked-about names on the Indian market, and the round-trip in its valuation — euphoria, then a sharp reset — is central to the investment debate today.

Listing (29 Nov 2023)Opened at ₹50 — a ~56% premium to the ₹32 issue price. A strong debut.

Why the market fell in love — then reconsidered

The bull thesis at IPO was intoxicatingly simple: a government-backed pure-play lender to the single hottest structural theme in India — the energy transition, growing its book 25–30%+ a year, with a clean recent track record and Navratna prestige. Investors bid it to an extreme premium (well above 5× book at the peak). The reconsideration came from reality intruding: growth is still strong but lumpy; NPAs crept up on a few large accounts; margins are thin and rate-sensitive; and a lender is, in the end, only as good as its worst loans. The stock's journey from euphoria to a more sober multiple is the market learning to value IREDA as an NBFC, not a theme — a healthy, if painful, maturing.

The lesson for a reader: a great sector and a great stock price are different things. India's renewable build-out can be a decades-long boom and IREDA's shares can be expensive at the wrong moment. The company and the stock deserve separate scorecards — this primer keeps them apart.

The loan book, decoded

What IREDA actually lends against — and how the scope is widening.

IREDA's loan book is a map of India's clean-energy build-out. Historically dominated by solar and wind, it is deliberately broadening into the newer, faster-growing corners of the transition — green hydrogen, EVs, rooftop solar and transmission — which both diversifies risk and extends the runway.

The widening product scope — from solar loans to the whole transition

  • Green hydrogen. Under the ₹19,744 Cr National Green Hydrogen Mission (target: 5 million tonnes/year by 2030), IREDA is positioning to finance electrolyser and green-H2 production projects — a large, brand-new lending category.
  • EV & charging. IREDA has financed electric-fleet projects, extended lines of credit to NBFCs for EVs, and funded EV manufacturing, battery-swapping and charging infrastructure — lending to the demand side of the transition, not just generation.
  • Rooftop solar / PM Surya Ghar. The government's ₹75,000 Cr rooftop-solar scheme (targeting ~1 crore+ households) opens a route into retail-adjacent green lending — smaller, more granular loans that diversify away from big lumpy projects.
  • Transmission & evacuation. Renewable power is useless without wires to carry it; IREDA is lending into the grid infrastructure that connects new capacity — a structural bottleneck the whole sector must solve.
  • The retail push. IREDA has signalled plans to build out a retail green-lending business (rooftop solar, EVs, small green loans) — potentially a higher-yield, more granular book that could lift margins and cut single-project concentration over time.
Why the widening scope matters

Two reasons. First, runway: as solar and wind mature and margins on them compress, the newer segments (green-H2, transmission, EV, storage) are where the next decade of loan growth lives. Second, and just as important, diversification of risk: a book that is heavily one or two technologies is exposed if that technology stumbles (as the wind stress in FY26 showed). Spreading across more segments — and adding granular retail loans — makes the whole book steadier. The optionality on top: IREDA's Navratna status and GIFT-City arm hint at a broader ambition, with periodic speculation about an eventual expanded scope or even a banking-style licence. That remains optionality, not a plan — but it colours the bull case.

06

The 500 GW super-cycle — the defining tailwind

The structural force unique to a green financier — and IREDA sits right in its path.

Every lender cares about credit demand, but IREDA's demand input is unusually specific and unusually large: how much clean power India builds. And here the numbers are staggering. India has committed to ~500 GW of non-fossil generating capacity by 2030 — a build-out that, by IREDA's own estimate, needs roughly ₹30.5 lakh crore of investment between 2023 and 2030. Someone has to lend against all of it. That is the whole growth story.

India crossed 50% of installed power capacity from non-fossil sources in 2025, five years early. The plan is to add ~50 GW of renewable capacity a year to hit 500 GW by 2030 — an enormous, sustained financing requirement.

Put concretely: to add ~50 GW every year needs continuous project financing — debt for solar parks, wind farms, storage, transmission and green-hydrogen plants — at a scale that runs into lakhs of crores. IREDA is one of a small set of specialist lenders (alongside PFC, REC and banks) positioned to supply it, and the only listed one that does nothing else. That is why its loan book can plausibly compound at 20%+ for years even as it already sits near ₹93,000 Cr.

Why a dedicated green financier rides the transition directly

A diversified bank captures a sliver of the renewables boom — it's one line of business among dozens. IREDA captures the whole thing: 100% of its book is the energy transition, so every gigawatt India builds is potential loan growth for IREDA specifically. Layer on its low-cost, sovereign-backed funding (cheaper than any private renewable financier could match) and its decades of appraisal expertise, and you get a lender uniquely geared to a decade-plus structural theme. This is the crux of the bull case — and the reason the market was willing to pay such a premium. The honest counterpoint: being 100% geared to one theme is a double-edged sword. It captures all the upside and all the sector-specific downside, with no other business to cushion a bad patch.

The nuance bulls must hold in mind: a huge capacity target does not automatically mean huge profitable lending. As more banks and NBFCs chase the same fashionable renewable projects, competition can compress yields (spreads), and rushed lending into a hot sector is exactly how future NPAs are born. The size of the runway is real; the quality and profitability of the lending done on it is what actually determines returns.
07

Funding and the spread

Where IREDA's money comes from — and why cheap money is its lifeblood.

Because IREDA has no deposits, how cheaply it can borrow is arguably the single most important lever on its profitability. A specialist lender lives on a thin spread; shave a few basis points off the cost of funds and profits jump — widen it and they shrink. Its funding advantage is a sovereign-linked story.

Where the money comes from

  • Bonds & green bonds. IREDA's mainstay: it issues rupee bonds (taxable and, historically, tax-free bonds that let it borrow very cheaply) and dedicated green bonds earmarked for clean-energy lending. Its strong credit rating (sovereign-linked) keeps these cheap.
  • Bank term loans & refinance lines — borrowing from banks and multilateral/development institutions.
  • Foreign-currency borrowing. External commercial borrowings (e.g. a JPY 26 bn line from SBI Tokyo in FY26) and the new GIFT-City subsidiary, designed to tap global ESG-focused capital and lend in foreign currency — cutting hedging costs for developers.
  • Perpetual & hybrid instruments (e.g. ~₹1,247 Cr of perpetual bonds in FY26) that also count toward capital.
  • Equity (QIP). The ₹2,006 Cr FY26 QIP wasn't funding per se — it was capital, the cushion that lets IREDA lever up and lend more (see CRAR below).
Why the sovereign link is the whole edge

IREDA's borrowers — solar and wind developers — could not raise money as cheaply on their own. IREDA can, because it is a government-owned Navratna that lenders and bond investors treat as quasi-sovereign. It borrows at a fine rate and passes on a slightly higher one, keeping the spread. Take away the government backing and the model breaks: its cost of funds would jump, its spread would vanish, and it would have no advantage over a private NBFC. So the ~72% GoI stake isn't just an ownership footnote — it is the source of the competitive advantage. The risk that shadows this: if interest rates rise, or if the sector's risk perception worsens, IREDA's borrowing costs climb, and because its spread is so thin, profits are quickly squeezed. FY26 already showed how margin volatility can dent a quarter.

08

Asset quality and the NPA question

The number that can wreck a good year for a project lender — and it's rising.

For a fast-growing power-project lender, asset quality is the make-or-break risk. Renewable projects can and do go bad — a wind farm in a poor wind year, a solar project whose electricity buyer won't pay, a developer that turns out to be fraudulent. IREDA cleaned up a legacy pile of bad loans on its way to listing; in FY26, a fresh crop of stress pushed its bad-loan ratios back up. This section is the honest counterweight to the growth story.

GNPA rose from ~2.45% to a mid-year peak near ~4%, easing to 3.49% by year-end. Net NPA (after provisions) was 1.29% at Mar 2026. The direction — up — is what matters to the debate, even if the absolute level is still "manageable."

What went wrong in FY26

  • Wind-portfolio stress. A handful of large wind projects in Tamil Nadu and Rajasthan, financed in FY22–23, ran into poor wind years (generation below the "P90" downside estimate) and struggled to service their debt. This drove much of the GNPA rise — a vivid illustration of how renewable-specific risk (the wind literally not blowing enough) can hit a green lender.
  • A suspected fraud (Gensol group). Fraud was flagged in accounts of the Gensol/Hetero group; the loans were classified as NPA, provided for, and pushed into recovery (IBC/DRT proceedings). A reminder that borrower governance, not just technology, is a risk.
  • Sharply higher provisions. Impairment provisions surged ~228% to ₹777 Cr in FY26 (from ₹237 Cr), and a jump in Q4 provisioning is why Q4 profit actually slipped ~2% despite revenue growing. Provisions are how a bad loan bites the P&L.
How to read the NPA number without over- or under-reacting

Three balancing points. (1) Context: a 3.49% GNPA with 1.29% net NPA is elevated but not catastrophic for an infrastructure lender — and IREDA has provisioned against it. (2) The worry: the trend is up, it's concentrated (a few large accounts can move the ratio a lot, since there's no diversified retail buffer), and fast loan growth can mask problems — a rapidly growing book makes the bad-loan ratio look smaller than the underlying stress. (3) The structural point: this is exactly why concentration and appraisal discipline matter so much for IREDA specifically. A bank spreads risk across millions of small loans; IREDA's book is chunky, sector-specific and long-dated. Watch GNPA, net NPA and credit cost (provisions ÷ loan book) each quarter — if they keep rising while the book grows, the market will keep discounting the "quality" of the growth.

The honest risk: the same super-cycle that fuels IREDA's growth also tempts the whole industry to lend aggressively into fashionable projects. If a wave of renewable projects sours in a downturn — bad resource years, PPA disputes, developer defaults — a concentrated, fast-grown book like IREDA's is where that stress shows up first. Asset quality, not loan growth, is the number that ultimately decides whether the story works.
09

The rivals — PFC, REC and the banks

Bigger cousins, cheaper valuations — and IREDA's pure-play differentiation.

IREDA competes for the same renewable projects as much larger lenders. Its distinctiveness is not size — it is the smallest of the power financiers — but focus: it is the only listed lender that does nothing but clean energy. Whether that pure-play focus is worth a valuation premium over its diversified cousins is the central debate.

The pure-play premium — justified or not?

Here is the crux of the valuation debate. IREDA trades at a meaningfully richer price-to-book than PFC or REC — its cousins sit near ~1.2× book, while IREDA has commanded ~2.5× and, at its post-IPO peak, well over 5×. The bull argument for the premium: IREDA is a pure bet on the fastest-growing slice of the sector (renewables), growing its book faster than the diversified giants, with cleaner thematic appeal and Navratna optionality — a small company with a long runway deserves a growth multiple. The bear argument: IREDA is smaller, less diversified, and now showing higher NPAs than PFC/REC, which are bigger, better-capitalised, cheaper and pay fatter dividends — so paying 2× the book multiple for more risk and less scale is hard to justify. Many analysts lean toward PFC/REC as the safer value, treating IREDA as a higher-beta "renewable satellite." There is no free lunch: the premium is the market paying up for growth and theme, at the cost of value and diversification.

How to think about the valuation

At ~₹35,200 Cr market cap on ~₹1,873 Cr of FY26 profit and ~₹13,781 Cr of net worth, IREDA trades around ~19× earnings and ~2.5× book (per Screener, mid-2026). That is far cheaper than the frenzy peak (5×+ book) but still a clear premium to PFC/REC (~1.2× book). The multiple only makes sense if you believe IREDA compounds its book at 20%+ for years while keeping asset quality and spreads under control. Bulls argue a debt-free-of-worries, sovereign-backed, pure-play green lender with a decade-long runway deserves a growth premium and the P/B will be "grown into." Bears argue you are paying up for a smaller, more concentrated, higher-NPA lender than its cheaper cousins — and that any further rise in NPAs, squeeze in spread, or supply from PSU stake sales could de-rate it again. The two numbers that will settle it over time: book growth (is the super-cycle converting into loans?) and credit cost (is the growth clean?).

Dividend: IREDA pays a modest dividend (an interim ₹0.60/share plus a final dividend recommended for FY26), for a yield under ~0.5% — small, because it retains most earnings to fund growth. That contrasts sharply with PFC/REC, which are prized as high-yield dividend PSUs — another reason the two camps of investors want different things from these stocks.

IREDA's growth levers are unusually clear — a rare case where the demand side is almost guaranteed by national policy. The question is never "will there be projects to finance?" but "can IREDA finance them cheaply, profitably and safely?"

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A structurally advantaged lender — with real, lender-specific risks and a still-premium price.

IREDA is the cleanest listed way to bet on India's energy transition as a lender: a sovereign-backed, Navratna, pure-play green financier riding a decade-long, policy-guaranteed build-out. The investment debate is a genuine three-way tension — super-cycle growth versus asset-quality & spread risk versus a valuation that still isn't cheap.

The bull case

  • Pure-play on a mega-theme — 100% geared to the 500 GW / ₹30 lakh crore renewable build-out; the only listed lender that does nothing else.
  • Sovereign-backed cheap funding — Navratna PSU status gives it a real, hard-to-replicate cost-of-funds advantage over private financiers.
  • Fast book growth — loan book +22%, sanctions +39% in FY26; a visible pipeline and years of runway.
  • Widening scope — green-H2, EV, transmission, retail rooftop lending diversify the book and extend growth.
  • Capital & optionality — CRAR ~20.6%, QIP-boosted net worth, GIFT-City funding arm, and speculative upside on any scope expansion.

The bear case

  • Rising NPAs — GNPA up to 3.49% on wind & legacy stress; provisions surged and dented Q4 profit. Renewable projects can and do go bad.
  • Concentration risk — a chunky, single-sector, long-dated book with no diversified retail cushion; a few large defaults move the whole ratio.
  • Thin, volatile spread — ~3.3% NIM is rate-sensitive; competition from banks/PFC/REC can compress yields on prime projects.
  • Premium valuation — still ~2.5× book vs ~1.2× for bigger, safer, higher-dividend PFC/REC; leaves little room for error.
  • PSU & supply overhang — government control limits commercial freedom; future stake sales / QIPs to meet MPS add share supply. Modest dividend vs cousins.
The bottom line

IREDA is a genuinely well-positioned, policy-backed green lender with a runway few companies can match — the demand for its product is close to guaranteed by national policy for a decade. But it is a lender, and a lender is only as good as its worst loans, its cheapest funding, and the price you pay for it. The FY26 results captured all three tensions in one report: record profit and 22% book growth (the bull), rising NPAs and higher provisions (the bear), and a valuation that, while off its peak, still asks investors to pay a premium over larger, cheaper, safer cousins. Track three numbers each quarter — loan-book growth (is the super-cycle converting?), GNPA / credit cost (is the growth clean?), and net interest margin (is the spread holding?). Get all three right and IREDA compounds into its premium; let asset quality slip, and a 2.5×-book PSU has room to fall toward its cousins.
