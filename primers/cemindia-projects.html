00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Cemindia Projects is a heavy-civil engineering and construction (EPC) contractor — it wins large infrastructure contracts and physically builds them: ports and jetties, metro tunnels and stations, airport runways and terminals, highways and bridges, industrial plants, water systems and, most recently, data centres. For most of its life it was called ITD Cementation India, and it was — and remains — the country's foremost specialist in marine (port and harbour) works and deep foundations. In 2025 the Adani group took control and renamed it Cemindia Projects.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • A 94-year-old builder with a new owner. The company traces its Indian operations to 1931; the Thai construction giant Italian-Thai Development controlled it for two decades as "ITD Cementation India." In May 2025 an Adani entity (Renew Exim DMCC) bought that 46.64% block for ₹5,757 Cr and, after an open offer, reached 67.46% — then rebranded it to Cemindia Projects (effective 19 Aug 2025).
  • The numbers just inflected. FY26 (year to March 2026) revenue crossed ₹10,000 Cr (~₹10,061 Cr), net profit jumped ~60% to ₹598 Cr, EBITDA rose ~28% to ~₹1,199 Cr, and the order book hit a record ₹24,545 Cr — roughly 2.4 years of revenue already booked. Net debt is a modest ₹430 Cr (net debt/equity ~0.18×).
  • It is now, in part, building for its own parent. Around a quarter of the order book — and nearly half of new order inflow (L1 wins ~₹11,000 Cr) — is linked to Adani group projects: ports, the group's airports (Ahmedabad, Jaipur, Trivandrum), and a ~₹3,000 Cr data-centre pipeline. That captive demand is the whole re-rating story — and the whole governance question.
In plain terms

A construction contractor is a business that rents out its ability to build hard things on time. Cemindia's edge is that the hard things it builds are the hardest — structures in water, under rivers, and deep in the ground, where few rivals can compete. India's biggest private ports-and-airports operator, the Adani group, looked at that skill set and simply bought the builder. So the same company that spent 90 years constructing infrastructure for whoever paid now has a parent that is itself one of India's largest infrastructure owners — and a ready pipeline of work to hand it. The prize is years of visible revenue; the catch is that a big slice of that revenue is a related party, and the stock has already re-rated hard on the promise.

01

How a heavy-civil EPC contractor actually earns

No engineering degree required — just the chain from a signed contract to cash in the bank.

An EPC contractor doesn't sell a product off a shelf; it sells the execution of a project. "EPC" stands for Engineering, Procurement and Construction — the contractor designs the works, buys the materials, and builds the thing, usually for a fixed or negotiated price agreed up front. The economics of the whole sector run on four ideas.

The four concepts that explain an EPC contractor
IdeaWhat it means (and where Cemindia sits)
Order book (backlog)The total value of signed-but-not-yet-built work. It is the contractor's visibility into future revenue. Cemindia's is a record ₹24,545 Cr — about 2.4× a year's revenue.
Book-to-billNew orders won in a year ÷ revenue billed. Above 1.0 means the backlog is growing. FY26 inflow of ₹14,821 Cr on ~₹10,061 Cr revenue = ~1.5× — the book is still expanding.
Percentage-of-completionRevenue is booked as the job progresses, not when it's handed over. Pour 30% of the concrete, book ~30% of the contract. This is why a fat backlog converts to revenue steadily over 2–4 years.
Working capitalThe cash tied up in a job before the client pays — materials bought, wages paid, plus retention money the client holds back until completion. EPC is notoriously working-capital-hungry; managing it is half the game.
Where the money actually leaks — and lands

EPC margins are thin by nature. Cemindia earns an EBITDA margin of roughly 10–12% and a net margin of only about 6% — every rupee of revenue passes through subcontractors, steel, cement, diesel and labour before a sliver drops to profit. Two forces decide whether a contract is good or bad: input-cost inflation (a fixed-price job signed before steel prices jumped can bleed) and working-capital discipline (get paid slowly and you finance the client for free, wrecking returns). The prize for doing both well is operating leverage on overheads plus a lightly-levered balance sheet — which is exactly what FY26 delivered, with profit growing ~60% on ~9% revenue growth as margins widened and interest stayed contained.

Why "visibility" is the word analysts obsess over

Because revenue is booked as work progresses, a contractor with a deep, quality backlog has years of revenue it can already see. That is why the order book — its size, its margin, and how fast it converts — is the single most-watched number in the sector. A ₹24,545 Cr backlog does not guarantee profit, but it removes most of the guesswork about revenue: the debate shifts entirely to margin and cash.

02

What Cemindia builds — the segment portfolio

A genuine full-spectrum civil contractor, anchored by things that touch water.

Cemindia is not a one-trick firm. It executes across almost the entire heavy-civil spectrum, and the order book is spread across several distinct end-markets. The through-line is technical difficulty: marine structures, deep foundations, tunnels and underground stations — the work where getting it wrong is catastrophic and few contractors qualify to bid.

The build list, decoded

  • Maritime structures — jetties, container berths, quays, wharves, breakwaters, shiplifts, dredging and land reclamation. This is the crown jewel (see §03) and Cemindia's national leadership position.
  • Urban infrastructure & MRTS — metro rail viaducts, underground stations and tunnels (Delhi, Kolkata, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Chennai networks), plus airport runways and terminals.
  • Industrial structures & buildings — civil works for refineries, petrochemical, power, steel and fertiliser plants; commercial buildings; and a fast-growing data-centre construction line.
  • Highways, bridges & flyovers — road packages, elevated corridors and river bridges for NHAI and state agencies.
  • Hydro & underground — dams, hydropower tunnels, irrigation and other rock/soil-engineering works.
  • Water & environment — water and wastewater treatment plants, pumping stations and pipelines.
  • Specialist foundations — piling, diaphragm walls, geotechnical investigation, ground improvement and slope stabilisation — the deep-ground engineering that underpins everything else and is a business in its own right.

03

The marine franchise — the moat you can't see

The defining, non-copyable capability: building structures in and under water.

Most infrastructure contractors compete on roads and buildings, where hundreds of firms can bid and the winner is whoever quotes lowest. Cemindia's core is somewhere almost no one else can go: marine and deep-foundation engineering. Building a jetty that a fully-laden cargo ship can berth against, driving steel piles into the seabed, sinking a tunnel under a live river — this is a small club of qualified contractors, and Cemindia is at the front of it.

The résumé is unusually literal. The company has built jetties, berths, wharves and container terminals at a long list of India's ports — Chennai, Ennore, Haldia, Kakinada, Kandla, Pipavav, Dahej, Mormugao, Karwar, Nhava Sheva (JNPT), Paradip, Tuticorin and Visakhapatnam — handling everything from liquid and solid bulk cargo to containers. It constructed the second container terminal at Chennai and the shiplift at Karwar. And in Kolkata it delivered the piece of engineering that best captures its identity.

One project that says everything

Between the mid-2010s and 2020, Cemindia (then ITD Cementation) bored the twin tunnels for Kolkata's East–West Metro under the Hooghly river — a ~520-metre underwater stretch that made it India's first-ever transport tunnel beneath a river. When the tunnel-boring machine "Urvi" broke through, the country had crossed a civil-engineering threshold that no contractor had reached before. A road-builder cannot pivot into that overnight; it takes decades of accumulated marine and geotechnical know-how, specialist plant, and a track record regulators trust. That accumulated capability — not any single contract — is the real asset the Adani group acquired.

Why this niche resists competition

  • Qualification barriers. Port authorities and metro agencies restrict bidding to contractors with proven marine/tunnelling track records and the right equipment. A newcomer literally cannot qualify.
  • Specialist plant. Marine spreads, jack-up rigs, tunnel-boring machines, piling rigs and diaphragm-wall equipment are expensive, scarce, and useless without the crews who know how to run them.
  • Risk few will price. A tidal, seabed or under-river job carries geotechnical surprises that sink the unprepared. The firms that survive a few of these command a durable reputation premium.
  • Pricing power on the hard stuff. Where competition thins, margins hold better than on commodity road work — which is why Cemindia's blended margin sits above many pure road contractors.
The genuinely surprising part: the company that physically built jetties and berths at more than a dozen of India's ports is now owned by Adani Ports, the country's largest private ports operator — a business that competes with several of the very ports Cemindia constructed. The builder of the harbours has been absorbed by one of the harbour owners. That is the vertical integration at the heart of this story, and it has no parallel among Cemindia's listed peers.
04

The company's lineage and who controls it now

British roots, a Thai chapter, and — since 2025 — an Adani future.

Few listed Indian companies have changed hands as often as this one. Its DNA is British foundation-engineering, its adolescence was Thai, and its present belongs to India's most aggressive infrastructure group. Each owner left a mark on what it builds.

Why the Thai parent sold — the non-obvious backstory

The seller mattered as much as the buyer. Italian-Thai Development — the parent that controlled the company for two decades — spent 2023–24 under serious financial strain back in Thailand, wrestling with heavy debt. A distressed parent needing cash is a very different seller from a strategic one holding out for the top price. That backdrop helps explain why one of India's most specialised EPC franchises came up for sale at all, and why an acquirer with deep pockets and a captive pipeline — the Adani group — was so well placed to take it. The crown jewel changed hands partly because its former owner needed the money.

Ownership & control today

  • Promoter: Renew Exim DMCC (an Adani group entity), holding ~67.46% since May 2025. This is a controlling, not a passive, stake — the board and strategy now sit with Adani.
  • Public & institutions: the remaining ~32.5% is public float, including marquee domestic investors — reporting has flagged a build-up by well-known investor Madhu Kela alongside institutional holders drawn to the Adani-pipeline thesis.
  • Balance sheet: conservatively geared — net debt ~₹430 Cr, net debt/equity ~0.18× — unusual restraint for a fast-growing EPC contractor, and a deliberate management priority.
05

The Adani pivot — captive demand as a growth engine

The single force that re-rated the stock — and the one that most divides opinion.

Everything distinctive about Cemindia today flows from one structural change: it is no longer a merchant contractor bidding into an open market alone — it now has a parent that is one of India's largest builders of ports, airports, roads, transmission and data centres, and that needs an in-house EPC arm to execute all of it. That converts an uncertain, competitive order pipeline into something closer to a guaranteed one.

The pipeline spans the group's ambitions. On airports, Cemindia is executing specialised runway, terminal and allied works at Ahmedabad, Jaipur and Trivandrum, and is positioned for the enormous Navi Mumbai International Airport Phase 2. On ports, its marine expertise slots directly into Adani Ports' expansion. On data centres, a ~₹3,000 Cr book has appeared almost from nothing, riding the group's AdaniConneX build-out. This is captive demand of a scale no independent EPC contractor can replicate.

The mechanism, and why the market paid up for it

An EPC contractor's biggest recurring risk is order flow — the constant scramble to replace a completed contract with a new one, often by shaving margins to win a competitive tender. A captive parent short-circuits that. If roughly half of new wins are simply the parent commissioning the subsidiary, then order visibility stretches out for years and the marginal contract need not be won on price alone. Layer that onto a firm that already leads in high-barrier marine and tunnelling work, and you get the combination the market re-rated toward: rare technical capability + an assured demand tap. The stock's 52-week range — from about ₹503 to ₹1,597 — is the sound of investors repricing the company from "quality niche contractor" to "the Adani group's construction arm."

The other side of the same coin: captive demand is a related-party relationship. When the parent is also the biggest customer, the terms of those contracts — pricing, payment, margin — are set inside the family. That is fine when it is fair, and a value transfer risk when it is not. The same feature that guarantees the order book is the feature minority shareholders must watch most carefully (see §10).
06

The order book and how it converts

Growth is largely settled; the argument is entirely about margin.

With a backlog of ₹24,545 Cr against ~₹10,061 Cr of annual revenue, Cemindia has already booked roughly 2.4 years of work. Revenue growth over the near term is therefore reasonably visible — the backlog simply converts through percentage-of-completion. What is not settled is the profitability at which it converts, and that is where the entire debate now lives.

The one number that settles the thesis

For a contractor sitting on 2.4 years of backlog, order intake is no longer the swing factor — execution margin is. The number to fix on is the sustainable EBITDA margin. Management has guided it to roughly 10–10.5% "excluding one-time items," yet Q4 FY26 printed a flattering ~15% and FY26 blended near ~12%, boosted by favourable claim settlements and mix. The bull case needs the underlying margin to hold in double digits as the record backlog executes; the bear case is that reported margin drifts back toward the historic ~9–10% once the one-offs wash out, thinning the profit growth the 45× multiple is paying for. Watch that single line — sustainable EBITDA margin — quarter after quarter; it decides whether the re-rating was earned.

The supporting metrics are working-capital days (are receivables and retention under control as revenue scales?) and net debt (is the growth self-funded, or is it quietly levering up?). On both, FY26 was reassuring — net debt held at ₹430 Cr and gearing at ~0.18× even as the top line grew. The discipline is real; the question is whether it survives a doubling of scale.

07

The industry and the rivals

An infrastructure up-cycle, a crowded roads market, and a thin marine club.

India is in a sustained public-infrastructure build-out — ports, metros, airports, highways, water and, increasingly, data centres. That lifts every competent EPC contractor. But the competitive intensity varies enormously by segment: roads and buildings are crowded and price-driven; marine, tunnelling and deep-foundation work is a narrow field, and that is precisely where Cemindia is strongest.

The competitive picture in one line

On roads and ordinary buildings, Cemindia is one contractor among dozens and wins on price like everyone else. On marine works, under-river tunnels and deep foundations, the qualified field narrows to a handful — chiefly itself, Afcons and L&T — and pricing holds. The strategic significance of the Adani takeover is that it lets Cemindia lean harder into the high-barrier work it does best (ports, airport airside, data centres) via captive awards, while treating the crowded, low-margin road tenders as optional. The competitive question for the decade is whether Afcons's technical parity plus the majors' scale can keep Cemindia from converting the Adani pipeline into durable, above-sector margins.

A real earnings inflection — priced by a market that already believes the story.

FY26 was a step-change year. Revenue grew modestly, but profit surged as margins widened and the balance sheet stayed tight. The financial record is now the picture of a contractor executing well; the debate is whether the share price has run ahead of the fundamentals.

How to read the ~45× multiple

At a market capitalisation around ₹26,900 Cr on ₹598 Cr of FY26 profit, Cemindia trades near 45× trailing earnings — a rich rating for a business that historically earned single-digit net margins and traded at a low-to-mid-teens multiple as ITD Cementation. The re-rating rests on three claims: that the Adani pipeline delivers years of visible, less-competitive growth; that margins hold in double digits rather than sliding back; and that the group tag brings cheaper capital and bigger tickets. Each is plausible and partly evidenced by FY26. The risk is arithmetic: a contractor earning ~6% net margins has little cushion, so if the sustainable EBITDA margin settles nearer 10% and profit growth normalises, a 45× multiple becomes hard to defend. The number that adjudicates all of it is the same one flagged in §06 — the underlying EBITDA margin as the record backlog runs off.

Three demand streams stacked on top of an infrastructure super-cycle.

Cemindia's forward growth rests on three overlapping tailwinds — the national infrastructure build-out that lifts everyone, its own high-barrier specialisms, and the bespoke Adani captive pipeline that no rival enjoys.

The order book already covers the next two years of revenue; the runway is about what the parent hands over next — an airport phase here, a port berth there, a data-centre campus somewhere else.
10

The risks — and the governance question

The features that power the story are the same ones that carry its dangers.

Cemindia's risks are unusually intertwined with its strengths. The captive parent that guarantees demand also concentrates it; the technical work that commands margin also carries execution risk; and a stock that has tripled off a low base has re-rating risk built in.

  • Related-party concentration & governance. With ~25% of the book and ~50% of new wins from the promoter group, the fairness of intra-group contract pricing and payment terms is the central minority-shareholder concern. Adani-group companies have also periodically faced heightened investor scrutiny; disclosure quality and arm's-length terms need to stay clean for the premium to hold.
  • Margin normalisation. FY26's ~12% EBITDA margin flattered by one-offs; management's own ~10–10.5% guide implies the headline may soften. A slip toward the historic ~9–10% would undercut the profit-growth case.
  • Working capital. EPC is cash-hungry; scaling revenue without ballooning receivables and retention is the perennial test. FY26 was disciplined, but a doubling of scale strains any contractor's balance sheet.
  • Execution risk. Marine, under-river and deep-foundation jobs carry geotechnical surprises, weather delays and cost overruns; a single troubled marquee contract can dent a year.
  • Client & sector cyclicality. Beyond Adani, orders depend on government and PSU capex, which ebbs with budgets, elections and land/clearance delays.
  • Ownership-transition risk. A change of promoter, brand and (in time) senior management can disrupt an EPC firm whose real assets are its people and its qualifications; retaining the marine/tunnelling talent that Italian-Thai built is not automatic.
  • Valuation. At ~45× a thin-margin contractor's earnings, the price already embeds success; disappointment on margin or governance can de-rate the stock quickly.
The candid framing: most EPC contractors are cheap because their order flow is uncertain and their margins thin. Cemindia solved the order-flow problem by acquiring a captive parent — and in doing so replaced market risk with related-party risk. Whether that is a good swap depends entirely on the parent treating the subsidiary fairly over many years. That is a judgement about the Adani group as much as about the contractor.
11

The future, and the bull/bear

A rare builder with an assured pipeline — repriced for it to keep executing cleanly.

Cemindia is a genuinely capable heavy-civil contractor with a defensible marine niche, a record backlog, a tidy balance sheet and — since 2025 — a parent that can feed it work for years. The FY26 numbers show the machine working. The investment debate is about margin durability and governance, not whether the company can build.

The bull case

  • Rare capability — national leadership in marine and deep-foundation works, a niche few can enter.
  • Captive pipeline — ~half of new wins from the Adani group across ports, airports and data centres; order flow largely solved.
  • Record visibility — ₹24,545 Cr backlog, ~2.4 years of revenue, book-to-bill ~1.5×.
  • Real inflection — FY26 PAT up ~60% with margins widening and net debt held at ~0.18× gearing.
  • Group tailwinds — potential for cheaper capital, bigger tickets and marquee anchors like Navi Mumbai Airport Phase 2.

The bear case

  • Related-party dependence — order book and pricing increasingly set inside the promoter family; governance is the swing risk.
  • Margin may normalise — the ~12% FY26 EBITDA margin included one-offs; the guided ~10–10.5% implies softer headlines ahead.
  • Full valuation — ~45× earnings for a ~6%-net-margin contractor leaves thin cushion.
  • Working-capital & execution risk — scaling cash-hungry EPC without slippage is unproven at this size.
  • Transition risk — a wholesale change of owner, brand and eventually management around a people-and-qualifications business.
The bottom line

The story is no longer about winning work — the ₹24,545 Cr backlog has settled that for two-plus years. It is about converting that backlog at a double-digit EBITDA margin, on clean intra-group terms, without straining the balance sheet. The proof points are concrete and dated: whether the sustainable margin holds near the guided ~10.5% through FY27, whether receivables stay tame as revenue scales, and whether the Navi Mumbai International Airport Phase 2 award turns the parent-subsidiary promise into poured concrete. The firm that bored India's first tunnel under a river has never lacked the skill to build; the next chapter tests whether being the Adani group's in-house builder makes those skills more valuable — or simply harder to price.
