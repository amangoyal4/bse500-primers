WeWork India, long leases, short memberships

A complete walk through India's largest premium flexible-workspace operator — the company that leases whole buildings, fits them out, and re-sells them by the desk and the managed floor under a licence of the famous WeWork brand. How the flex-office model actually earns, why a mismatch between long building leases and short member commitments is the risk that defines it (and once bankrupted its American namesake), and why this Embassy-backed Indian company is a wholly separate business that never went bust.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

WeWork India Management Limited rents out ready-to-use office space by the desk, the room and the whole floor. It signs long leases on premium office buildings in big Indian cities, spends heavily to fit them out with furniture, meeting rooms, cafés and technology, and then re-lets that space flexibly — to a startup that wants ten desks, to a large enterprise that wants a bespoke "managed" floor, or to a member who just wants a hot-desk and a coffee. It does all this under an exclusive licence of the globally recognised "WeWork" brand, and it is majority-owned by the Embassy Group, one of India's largest office-property developers. As of mid-2025 it ran about 68 operational centres, ~7.7 million sq ft and ~1.14 lakh desks across eight top-tier cities — making it the largest premium flex operator in the country.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a landlord's tenant and a tenant's landlord at the same time. WeWork India takes on a building lease that can run nine to fifteen years, but sells desks and memberships that a customer can often leave in months. That gap — a long, fixed cost obligation funded by shorter, more cancellable revenue — is the maturity mismatch, and it is the single most important thing to understand about this industry. It is exactly what pushed the American WeWork into bankruptcy.
  • This is NOT the WeWork that went bankrupt. The US parent, WeWork Global, collapsed into Chapter 11 in 2023. WeWork India is a separate company, controlled by the Embassy Group, that merely licenses the brand — it kept operating throughout, went its own way, and listed on the NSE and BSE in October 2025. Same purple logo; entirely different owner, balance sheet and outcome.
  • It has just turned the corner on profit — carefully. FY26 revenue rose ~25% to ~₹2,432 Cr with a net profit of ~₹72 Cr and a record ~₹66 Cr fourth quarter, riding a wave of enterprise and Global Capability Centre (GCC) demand. But the stock trades on a very rich multiple (P/E north of 100×), so the market is paying today for years of profitable ramp still to come — which puts every point of occupancy and every rupee of desk realisation under a microscope.
In plain terms

WeWork India is in the business of turning bare floors into finished, flexible offices and pocketing the difference between what it pays the building owner and what it charges the people who sit there. The magic — and the danger — is timing. It commits to paying rent on a whole building for a decade or more, but its customers sign up for a fraction of that. When the centres are full, the maths is wonderful: fixed rent is spread over many paying desks and the operating margin blooms. When they empty out — a recession, a work-from-home wave, a rival opening next door — the rent bill does not shrink, and the losses can be brutal. The company's entire skill is keeping its buildings full enough, long enough, to stay on the right side of that equation. FY26 says it is winning that game right now; the price of the shares says a lot of future winning is already assumed.

01

Flex-office 101: what a managed-workspace operator does

No jargon needed — just the lease, the fit-out, and the markup.

A flexible-workspace company sits between the building owner and the office worker. It does not usually own the property; it leases it, invests capital to make it a fully serviced office, and then re-lets that finished space in smaller, more flexible slices at a higher effective rent than it pays. The value it adds is convenience: a company can walk into a WeWork floor and start work tomorrow, with no fit-out, no furniture order, no facilities team, and the freedom to grow or shrink without a fifteen-year property commitment of its own. To follow the business you need six terms.

The only flex-office terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
The head leaseThe long lease WeWork India signs with a building owner (often 9–15 years). This is the big fixed cost — rent is owed whether the desks are full or empty. Under accounting rules it also lands on the balance sheet as a large "lease liability" (section 08).
Fit-out (capex)The upfront money spent turning a bare floor into a finished office — interiors, furniture, meeting rooms, cabling, pantry. A centre must fill up and run for years to earn this back. New centres therefore lose money before they make it.
OccupancyThe share of available desks/seats that are actually let and paying. The master dial of the whole model: fixed rent divided across more paying desks is the difference between a fat margin and a loss.
Realisation per deskThe average revenue earned per desk (or per sq ft) let. Enterprise "managed office" deals and premium locations lift it; commodity hot-desks lower it. Occupancy × realisation is the revenue engine.
Managed officeA bespoke, private, branded office built and run for a single large client on a longer commitment — the enterprise end of flex. WeWork India's fastest-growing, stickiest line.
Mature vs new centreA "mature" centre has been open long enough to fill up and cover its costs; a "new" one is still ramping and dilutes reported occupancy and margin while it does. The mix of the two shapes the headline numbers.
The single mechanism that drives the whole sector

Everything in flex office reduces to one sentence: you take on a long, fixed rent, spend capital to make the space sellable, and then earn back both by keeping the space as full as possible for as long as possible at the best rent you can get. If occupancy and realisation clear the fixed rent plus the fit-out amortisation, a centre mints cash for years. If they don't, the fixed rent keeps arriving and the centre bleeds. That asymmetry — unlimited downside from an empty building, capped upside from a full one — is why the industry is simultaneously a wonderful cash machine at maturity and a graveyard for operators who over-expanded into a downturn. The discipline of when and where to sign the next head lease is the entire craft.

Who the customers are

  • Enterprises & GCCs — large corporates and multinational "Global Capability Centres" taking managed offices or big blocks of seats. The dominant, fastest-growing and stickiest demand, and the heart of WeWork India's strategy.
  • Startups & SMEs — smaller teams taking private offices or dedicated desks; flexible, but more cyclical and quicker to leave when funding tightens.
  • Individuals & freelancers — members buying hot-desks, day passes or "All Access" roaming plans, plus virtual-office and event customers. Lower per-head revenue, but useful footfall and ancillary spend.
02

The maturity mismatch: the fault-line the whole story runs along

The one structural fact that explains both the promise and the peril — and what actually broke WeWork US.

Here is the defining tension of this business, stated plainly. WeWork India's costs are long and fixed; its revenues are shorter and more cancellable. It promises a building owner rent for, say, a decade, and it hopes — but cannot guarantee — that a rotating cast of members and enterprises will keep those floors full for that entire decade. When the two sides of that promise line up, the model prints money. When they diverge, the rent bill does not care.

A single centre can be a cash machine or a cash pit depending only on whether it stays full — and the operator has signed away its exit for years either way. Multiply across dozens of centres and you have the flex-office balance sheet.

What actually happened to WeWork in America

The US WeWork did not fail because people stopped wanting flexible offices — they wanted them more than ever. It failed because it grew into the mismatch recklessly: it signed enormous volumes of long, fixed leases at aggressive rents, chasing global scale and a technology-company valuation, on the bet that occupancy would keep climbing forever. When demand wobbled — a failed 2019 listing, then the pandemic emptying offices — the memberships melted away on their short notice periods while the decade-long rent obligations stayed exactly where they were. The fixed leases became a crushing weight the shrinking revenue could no longer carry, and in 2023 the parent filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. The lesson is not that flex office is a bad business; it is that flex office run without discipline about how much fixed lease you take on, at what rent, in what location is a fragile one.

Why the same fault-line is managed differently here

WeWork India inherited the brand, but not the American growth-at-all-costs playbook. Two things soften the mismatch on this side. First, it is anchored to the Embassy Group, a large office developer — so a meaningful share of its buildings come from a landlord that is also its promoter, which can mean better-aligned, more flexible lease terms than an arm's-length third party would offer. Second, it has leaned hard into enterprise and managed-office demand, where clients commit for longer and in bigger blocks — shortening the gap between the length of its leases and the length of its revenues. The mismatch never disappears; it is structural. But a lease taken at a sensible rent, in a building your own promoter developed, pre-committed to a large enterprise, is a far tamer version of the risk that sank the parent than a speculative floor leased at the top of a boom on the hope that members show up.

03

What WeWork India actually sells

One building, several products — and a deliberate tilt toward the enterprise.

A WeWork India centre is not a single product; it is a menu. The same fitted-out floor can be sold in very different slices, each with different economics, stickiness and margin. The company's strategy is visible in which slice it is pushing — and the answer, emphatically, is the enterprise end.

The product ladder

  • Managed office (enterprise) — a private, custom-built, WeWork-run office for a single large client, on a longer commitment. This is the strategic growth engine: management has spoken of the managed-office line scaling past a ~₹500 Cr annualised run-rate as its fastest-growing unit. It shortens the maturity mismatch (longer client tenure), carries steadier revenue, and locks in blue-chip tenants.
  • Private offices & dedicated desks — enclosed offices and reserved desks for startups, SMEs and enterprise overflow, sold by the head on flexible terms. The classic co-working core: higher realisation than hot-desks, more cyclical than managed office.
  • All Access, On-Demand & Virtual Office — membership products: a roaming pass to work from any centre (All Access), pay-as-you-go bookings (On-Demand), and a registered-address/mail service (Virtual Office). Lower revenue per head, but high-margin, capital-light and a funnel into bigger products.
  • Value-added & ancillary services — space customisation, parking, technology, events, hospitality and the "Workplace" software layer. Small individually, but they lift revenue per desk and deepen the enterprise relationship.

Why the enterprise tilt is the smartest dial management owns

Most of what decides a flex operator's year — rents, the economy, how fast offices refill — is outside its control. Product mix is not. A hot-desk member is a coin toss who might leave next month; a multinational that has moved a 600-seat GCC into a custom WeWork-managed floor has signed for years, integrated its operations, and will not casually walk. Tilting the book from footloose memberships toward managed enterprise offices does three things at once: it lengthens the revenue to better match the long lease, it steadies occupancy through a downturn, and it raises realisation per desk. It won't rescue a badly chosen building, but it makes each occupied desk both more durable and worth more — which is precisely the quality of earnings a richly-valued stock needs to justify itself.

04

Embassy, the licence, and the tale of two WeWorks

An Embassy Group company that rents a famous American name — and outlived the American company.

The most misunderstood thing about this stock is its own name. "WeWork" is one of the most famous business failures of the last decade — a story of hubris, a scrapped IPO and an eventual bankruptcy. None of that is this company. WeWork India is an Indian business, built and controlled by a domestic property house, that pays for the right to use the WeWork brand. Understanding the ownership is essential to understanding the risk.

Timeline

  • 2016–17
    WeWork enters India through a franchise/licence partnership with the Embassy Group, the Bengaluru-based office developer. From the start the Indian operation is Embassy-controlled — a licensee of the brand, not a subsidiary of the US parent.
  • 2019–21
    The US WeWork implodes: its 2019 IPO is pulled amid governance and valuation concerns; founder Adam Neumann exits; SoftBank bails it out. WeWork India, separately owned, keeps operating and expanding through the pandemic.
  • 2023
    WeWork Global files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in the United States. The Indian company — a distinct legal entity majority-owned by Embassy — is unaffected operationally and continues to grow.
  • FY24
    Revenue ~₹1,662 Cr but still loss-making (net loss ~₹131 Cr) — a business scaling hard, with new centres and fit-out weighing on the P&L before they mature.
  • Oct 2025
    IPO on the NSE & BSE (BSE code 544570) — a ~₹3,000 Cr offer for sale at ₹648 top of band; the stock lists around the issue price. Notably, the issue was entirely OFS, so the money went to selling shareholders, not into the company.
  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter — the Embassy Group. Embassy, a major Indian office developer (and sponsor of the listed Embassy Office Parks REIT), is the controlling promoter. Around the IPO its holding stood near ~72%; post-listing the promoter block sits lower (roughly ~48% by mid-2026, per exchange filings) as float and institutions came in. Confirm the exact current promoter percentage in the latest shareholding pattern.
  • WeWork Global — brand licensor, minority holder. The US WeWork side (through an entity linked to WeWork Global) has historically held a minority economic stake (around ~22% pre-IPO) and is the licensor of the WeWork brand to the Indian company. This is the umbilical cord to the famous name — and the reason a bankruptcy an ocean away still shows up in the headlines here.
  • The brand licence itself. WeWork India operates under an exclusive licence to use the WeWork brand, systems and technology in India, for which it pays the global entity. It is a genuine asset (instant recognition with multinational tenants) and a genuine dependency (a royalty cost, plus reputational spillover from a name attached to a famous collapse).
The surprise worth sitting with: the celebrated "WeWork" that went bankrupt and the WeWork you can buy on the NSE are not the same company — one is an American business that failed, the other an Embassy-controlled Indian business that stayed profuse enough to list. The shared purple logo is a licence, not a lineage. That separation is the bull's best point (India got the brand without the balance sheet) and the bear's nagging worry (the name still carries baggage, and a slice of the economics still flows to, and depends on, the licensor).

How a single centre actually earns its keep

The most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a flex operator carrying long leases.

Forget the company-level P&L for a moment and look at one building. A WeWork India centre lives or dies on a single arithmetic: (occupancy × realisation per desk) − fixed lease rent − fit-out amortisation − running cost. Everything else — the brand, the app, the coffee — exists to move the first two numbers up and keep the third from crushing them. Watch how the sign of that sum flips as a centre fills.

Now put real numbers on the portfolio. WeWork India's blended occupancy at operational centres slipped from about 84% in FY23 to ~77% in FY25, and at mature centres from ~88% to ~81% — not because the business weakened, but because it was adding new, still-ramping centres faster than the old ones could hide them. The device that only fits a long-lease flex operator: occupancy × realisation − fixed rent

The number that runs WeWork India is not revenue and it is not the desk count. It is (occupancy × realisation per desk) measured against a fixed, contracted rent that does not move. Both halves matter, but asymmetrically: because the rent is fixed, a swing in occupancy falls almost entirely to the bottom line. A centre at 85% versus 70% occupancy pays the identical rent but earns a fifth more revenue — and nearly all of that fifth is profit. Run the same logic in reverse and a demand shock is devastating: revenue drops, rent doesn't, and the centre plunges from cash machine to cash pit overnight. This is the exact lever that swung WeWork US from darling to bankruptcy, and it is the exact lever WeWork India is now riding upward. Tonnes-and-prices runs a fertiliser company; occupancy-against-fixed-rent runs this one.

06

Going asset-light — and the price of the name

Two structural moves that reshape the risk: sharing rent with landlords, and paying to be "WeWork".

If the core danger is a long fixed rent funded by shorter revenue, the obvious defence is to make the rent less fixed. That is precisely the direction WeWork India has pushed: away from pure fixed-rent leases and toward revenue-share / management arrangements with building owners. It is the single most important evolution of the model, and it directly attacks the mismatch at its root.

Under a revenue-share (or management) deal, instead of promising the landlord a fixed monthly rent whatever happens, WeWork India shares a slice of the centre's actual revenue. If the centre fills up, both sides do well; if it struggles, the landlord absorbs part of the shortfall rather than WeWork India carrying all of it. The operator gives up some upside in the boom years in exchange for not being left alone with a decade of fixed rent in the bust years. It converts the scariest feature of the WeWork-US model — rigid, one-sided lease liability — into something that flexes with demand. For a company whose promoter, Embassy, is itself a large landlord, aligning interests this way is unusually natural.

Why asset-light is the antidote to the WeWork-US disease

The American parent's fatal flaw was rigidity: it owed enormous fixed rents that could not shrink when revenue did. A revenue-share book is the structural cure. In a downturn, a fixed-lease operator's rent stays at 100% while its revenue falls — the gap is the loss. A revenue-share operator's landlord cost falls with the revenue — the gap narrows automatically. It doesn't make empty centres good; nothing does. But it turns the model from brittle to bendable, so a bad year is a thin year rather than an existential one. The more of WeWork India's portfolio that sits on revenue-share or managed terms, the less it resembles the company whose name it carries — and that resemblance, or the lack of it, is much of the investment debate.

The other side of the name: the brand licence

Wearing the WeWork brand is not free. WeWork India pays the global licensor for the exclusive right to the name, systems and technology in India — a real, recurring cost, and a dependency on an entity that has been through bankruptcy abroad. The trade is deliberate: the "WeWork" name carries instant credibility with exactly the multinational and GCC tenants the company most wants, shortening sales cycles and lending a global sheen a home-grown brand would take years to build. The company judges that access worth the royalty and the reputational tail-risk. An investor has to judge the same trade — a premium brand that opens enterprise doors, attached to a name that a smart generalist still associates with failure.

The paradox of the ticker: the brand that once symbolised how not to run a flex-office company is, in India, the badge that helps win the most disciplined, longest-tenure customers.
07

The tailwind: enterprises, GCCs and "core plus flex"

Why big companies — not freelancers — are now the growth story of Indian flex office.

The popular image of a co-working space is a laptop-toting freelancer with a flat white. The real money, increasingly, is the opposite: large enterprises and Global Capability Centres taking whole managed floors. This shift — from individual members to corporate anchors — is the defining demand story of the Indian flex sector, and WeWork India is positioned squarely in it.

A Global Capability Centre is the in-house offshore office a multinational runs in India to handle its technology, analytics, finance and operations. India now hosts well over two thousand of them, and they have become the single biggest driver of office demand in the country — by some estimates accounting for close to 45% of office leasing in recent quarters, with the GCC base projected to keep growing for years. Crucially, many of these companies now run a "core plus flex" real-estate strategy: they keep a stable owned/long-leased core, and take a flexible buffer of managed workspace on top for teams they are scaling up, piloting or ramping quickly. That flexible buffer is exactly what WeWork India sells.

Why this tailwind flatters WeWork India specifically

An enterprise/GCC-led market is the best possible weather for a long-lease operator, because it attacks the maturity mismatch from the demand side. Enterprise clients sign for longer, take bigger blocks, pay more per seat for a managed, branded, serviced environment, and churn far less than individuals. Every managed-office deal WeWork India signs is a length of long revenue laid alongside a length of long lease — the two sides of the mismatch moving back into alignment. It is why the company has tilted so deliberately toward this segment, why its fastest-growing line is managed office, and why the "WeWork" brand — trusted by multinational real-estate teams — is a sharper weapon here than it would be selling hot-desks. The risk, of course, is concentration: an enterprise-heavy book is wonderful until a global cost-cutting cycle makes big firms shrink their GCC footprints at once.

The balance sheet the desks sit on

Where the long leases go once the accountants get hold of them — and the "first profit" that wasn't quite operational.

Open WeWork India's balance sheet and one number dwarfs the rest: a ~₹5,550 Cr pile of "borrowings" (FY26) against a company whose entire net worth is only a few hundred crore. That is not bank debt taken to build factories. Under modern accounting rules (Ind AS 116), every long lease is capitalised — the future rent is booked upfront as a liability, with a matching "right-of-use" asset. So that towering number is the maturity mismatch, made visible: it is the present value of all those 9–15-year rent promises, sitting on the books like debt, because economically that is what it is.

The lease liability is roughly 18× the company's own equity and more than double a full year of revenue. That leverage is the mathematical face of the mismatch: the business is a thin sliver of equity balancing a mountain of long-dated rent. Net worth approx. (book value × shares).

This is why the accounting quirk in the profit line matters so much. WeWork India loudly turned its first-ever net profit in FY25 — about ₹128 Cr. Look underneath, though, and the company was still loss-making before tax that year (a pre-tax loss of roughly ₹157 Cr); the reported profit came from a large, one-off deferred-tax credit of ~₹286 Cr. In other words, FY25's "profit" was an accounting event, not an operating one. The operating turn is the more recent story — FY26's ~₹72 Cr net profit and the record ~₹66 Cr fourth quarter, which look far more like the business genuinely earning above its fixed costs as centres matured.

The counter-intuitive fact to hold onto

A flex operator can report a "maiden profit" while its actual operations still lose money — because a one-time tax credit can flatter a single year, and because the capitalised-lease accounting means the P&L and the cash flows tell different stories. So the health of this business is never captured by the net-profit headline alone. Two questions do more work: is the company profitable before tax and one-offs (i.e. are the centres actually clearing their fixed rent), and is the lease liability growing faster or slower than mature, cash-generating occupancy? FY25's profit was a tax event on an operation still finding its feet; FY26's looks like the real thing beginning. Watching pre-tax profit and mature-centre occupancy together, rather than the tax-flattered bottom line, is how you tell which year you are actually in.

Why this reframes the whole company: WeWork India is best read as a thin layer of equity riding an enormous, long-dated rent obligation, whose fortunes swing on whether it can keep that rented space full and profitable through a whole lease cycle. The capitalised lease liability is not a scandal — it is the honest accounting of the model — but it is why occupancy discipline, revenue-share terms and enterprise tenure matter far more here than the raw profit number a casual glance would fixate on.

The industry and the rivals

A newly-crowded, newly-profitable listed sector — with WeWork India at the premium end.

Indian flex office has, in the space of two years, gone from a venture-funded curiosity to a cluster of listed, profitable companies. WeWork India is the premium, enterprise-focused name in a field that also includes value-focused networks and pure managed-office specialists. Each rival attacks the same maturity mismatch with a slightly different weapon.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, this is not primarily a price war — it is a discipline war. Every operator faces the same maturity mismatch, so the winner is whoever signs the right buildings at the right rent on the right (increasingly revenue-share) terms, and fills them with the stickiest tenants. WeWork India competes at the premium enterprise end, where realisation is highest and churn lowest, and leans on the global brand and Embassy's property engine to win there. Second, the whole listed sector just posted a strong, profitable FY26 on the GCC wave — which is a validation of the model and a warning: a rising tide has lifted everyone, and it is in the next downturn, not this boom, that the differences in lease discipline and revenue-share protection will separate the survivors from the strugglers.

10

Financials & valuation

A real operating turn — priced as though the ramp is guaranteed to continue.

FY26 is the year WeWork India's operations visibly turned: revenue up ~25% to ~₹2,432 Cr, a genuine (not merely tax-driven) net profit of ~₹72 Cr, and a record fourth quarter as enterprise demand surged and centres matured. The catch is the price. At a market cap near ~₹10,200 Cr the stock trades on a trailing P/E well above 100× and many multiples of book — a valuation that only makes sense if profits are set to compound for years as the new-centre tail matures and occupancy climbs. The debate here is not whether the business is improving; it is whether the improvement can be fast and durable enough to grow into that multiple.

The one number that settles the thesis: mature-centre occupancy, read against the fixed-rent base

At 100×-plus earnings, WeWork India does not need to be cheap on today's profit — it needs to keep ramping. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is occupancy at mature centres (the ~81% of FY25, and where it heads next), tracked against the fixed lease-rent base — because that is the ratio that converts the huge capitalised lease liability into either widening cash profit or a stranded cost. Read it with two companions: the pre-tax margin trend (is the business earning above its fixed costs without accounting help?) and the share of the book on revenue-share / managed-office terms (is the mismatch being structurally tamed?). If mature occupancy climbs back toward the high-80s while managed office and revenue-share keep growing, the profit compounds and the multiple can be defended. If occupancy stalls in the high-70s and the new-centre tail keeps diluting, FY26 will read as a good year in a still-fragile ramp, and a triple-digit multiple has a great deal of disappointment available to it.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A newly-profitable market leader riding a real tailwind — asked to justify a very full price.

WeWork India enters FY27 as the largest premium flex operator in a sector enjoying its best-ever conditions: a GCC-driven demand wave, a maturing centre base, a shift toward stickier enterprise revenue and safer revenue-share leases, and an Embassy property engine behind it. What no flex operator can promise is that the buildings stay full through the next cycle — and that is precisely what a triple-digit multiple assumes. That tension frames both cases.

The bull case

  • Market leader on a real tailwind — the largest premium flex operator, positioned at the enterprise/GCC end of a sector that just crossed 100 msf and keeps growing.
  • Genuine operating turn — FY26's ~₹72 Cr profit and record Q4 look operational, not accounting-driven, as centres mature and occupancy firms.
  • The mismatch being tamed — the tilt to managed office (longer tenure) and revenue-share leases (flexible cost) structurally softens the very risk that sank the US parent.
  • Embassy backing — a large office developer as promoter means aligned, potentially better lease terms and a pipeline of premium buildings few rivals can match.
  • Premium brand, sticky tenants — the WeWork name wins the longest-tenure multinational and GCC clients, lifting realisation and lowering churn.

The bear case

  • Priced for a long, smooth ramp — a 100×-plus P/E leaves the stock exposed if profit growth is slower or bumpier than the market assumes.
  • The mismatch is structural — a recession, a WFH resurgence or a GCC cost-cutting cycle empties desks while the capitalised ~₹5,550 Cr of long leases keeps demanding rent.
  • Occupancy has been drifting — blended occupancy fell into the high-70s through fast expansion; the case needs it to climb back, not stall.
  • Enterprise concentration & brand dependency — a book anchored to big GCC tenants is exposed if global firms shrink together, and the licensed name still carries reputational and royalty baggage.
  • An OFS IPO put no cash in — the listing was entirely an offer for sale, so growth capex still leans on the lease-heavy balance sheet, not fresh equity.
The bottom line

WeWork India is the disciplined Indian afterlife of a famous American failure — the same purple brand, an entirely different owner, and, so far, an entirely different outcome. It has taken the one business model that once looked ungovernable and is running it more conservatively: longer-tenure enterprise revenue laid against its long leases, revenue-share terms to bend the rent when demand softens, and an office-developer promoter to feed it buildings. FY26 shows that discipline turning into real, operating profit for the first time. The unresolved question is entirely about price: at north of 100× earnings, the market has already booked years of full buildings and rising occupancy. The next test is concrete and datable — whether mature-centre occupancy climbs back toward the high-80s and pre-tax margins keep widening through the FY27 leasing cycle as the new-centre tail seasons. Clear that bar and the model's cash-machine phase arrives to meet the valuation; miss it, and the shares are carrying the one risk this whole industry is built around, at a price that assumes it away.
