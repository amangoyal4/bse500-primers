Sheela Foam, the branded-sleep consolidator

A complete walk through the maker of Sleepwell — India's best-known mattress brand — that bought its biggest rival Kurlon to build a branded near-duopoly. How a mattress-and-foam business actually earns its money, why a "sleep" company's profit quietly rides the price of crude oil, and why the whole investment case turns on a thin, chemical-driven spread and how fast it can convert a still-largely-unbranded market.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Sheela Foam Limited makes the thing you sleep on. It is India's largest maker of branded mattresses and polyurethane (PU) foam, and its flagship Sleepwell is arguably the country's most recognised mattress name — the brand a whole generation grew up calling a mattress. In 2023–24 it did the single most important thing in its history: it acquired Kurlon (Kurl-on), its biggest branded rival, folding two of India's three best-known mattress brands under one roof and creating a branded near-duopoly. Around that core it runs a large foam and comfort business (pillows, furniture cushioning, technical and specialty foam), an online / D2C push, an international foam arm, and a minority bet on furniture-rental platform Furlenco.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A "sleep" company whose profit rides crude oil. A mattress is mostly foam, and foam is made from two crude-oil-derived chemicals — TDI (toluene di-isocyanate) and polyol. Together these move roughly with oil and account for well over half of what Sheela Foam spends on raw material. So the margin is a spread: the gap between what a mattress sells for and what its crude-linked foam costs. When those chemicals spike, margins compress; when they ease, margins bloom. FY26 was a year of easing costs — and it showed.
  • It bought its way to dominance. The Kurlon deal took Sheela Foam's share of the organised mattress market from roughly 20–25% to an estimated 35–40%, in a market where most mattresses are still made by unbranded local shops. The prize is consolidating a fragmented, largely-unorganised category behind two trusted brands. The risk is paying up for a rival and then having to integrate it well — which absorbed debt, interest and management attention for two years.
  • FY26 was a recovery year, and the stock is priced for more. Revenue rose ~11% to ~₹3,821 Cr, but the real story was margins and profit: core EBITDA up ~46% to ~₹414 Cr and net profit up ~79% to ~₹160 Cr as input costs eased and Kurlon turned. Yet the shares trade on a high earnings multiple — well into the 40s — because the market is valuing the recovery and the branded consolidation, not the depressed trailing profit. That gap between a modest present and a priced-in future is the crux of the whole debate.
The whole business in one breath

Sheela Foam takes two crude-derived chemicals, whips them into foam, cuts and covers that foam into Sleepwell and Kurlon mattresses, and sells them through thousands of dealers and a growing online channel. Its profit is not the price on the mattress tag — it is the sliver left after the chemical bill is paid, and that sliver swings with oil. The company's one big strategic move was to buy its largest branded competitor and try to own the branded end of a market that is still mostly unbranded. So the case rests on three things at once: how wide the foam spread is, how fast Indians trade up from a no-name mattress to a branded one, and whether the Kurlon bet pays off. FY26 lined the first up nicely. The other two are multi-year questions the price is already betting on.

01

Mattress & foam 101: what you're actually buying

No chemistry needed — just the foam, the products, and how a bed becomes a business.

Strip a modern mattress down and it is mostly polyurethane foam — sometimes with springs, latex, memory foam or a quilted top, but foam at its heart. Sheela Foam's entire business grows out of that one material: it makes the foam, and then it makes the things foam goes into. To follow the company you need only a handful of terms.

The only terms you need

The one dependency that shapes the sector: foam is a petrochemical

Here is the fact a first-timer never expects. A mattress feels like a soft-goods, consumer, lifestyle product — but underneath it is a petrochemical converter. The dominant cost is not fabric or labour; it is TDI and polyol, two chemicals whose price is set in global markets and moves with crude oil, the dollar and chemical-plant capacity in China and the Gulf. That single fact explains why a "sleep" company's margins whipsaw with oil, why the branded players fight so hard on brand and distribution (the one thing that is theirs, since the input cost is everyone's), and why buying and scaling — as Sheela Foam did with Kurlon — matters: more volume over the same overheads, and more buying power on the chemicals that decide the year.

Who buys, and how a mattress reaches the bedroom

  • The household — the end buyer, who replaces a mattress rarely (every 8–10 years) and increasingly wants a branded one for comfort, hygiene and health reasons. It is a discretionary, big-ticket, trust-driven purchase — exactly the kind a brand can command a premium on.
  • The dealer network — thousands of mattress and furniture retail points, plus exclusive-brand outlets. For an incumbent like Sleepwell, this dense physical distribution is a genuine moat that a pure-online rival has to spend years and heavy marketing to replicate.
  • The online / D2C channel — the fast-growing route, pioneered by insurgents like Wakefit and now contested by everyone. A "bed-in-a-box" ordered on a phone, shipped compressed, and returned free if it doesn't suit. This is where the competitive war is loudest (section 06).
  • Other manufacturers (B2B) — carmakers, furniture and footwear makers who buy Sheela Foam's technical foam as an input. A quieter, relationship-driven business that diversifies the mattress cyclicality.
02

The crude-linked spread: where the profit actually is

The single most important idea in the case — and it is specific to a foam maker.

Most people track a mattress company by the price of mattresses. That is the wrong number. Sheela Foam does not keep the price of a Sleepwell; it keeps the spread — what is left after the crude-linked chemical bill is paid out of what the mattress sells for. The whole company can be read through one relationship: selling price minus TDI-and-polyol cost, per unit of foam. Watch how much of the story lives in those two chemicals.

TDI and polyol together are roughly 55–60% of raw-material spend. A move of ₹30–40/kg on either — routine when oil and Chinese chemical capacity shift — flows almost straight to the margin line. Figures approx.; the two chemicals, not the mattress tag, run the P&L.

Now see why this dominates. A mattress maker cannot re-price its shelf products every week — brands hold price to protect their positioning, and dealers carry inventory. But the chemical cost does move week to week with global markets. So when TDI and polyol spike, Sheela Foam is briefly selling at yesterday's price while paying today's chemical bill, and margin compresses fast. When those chemicals ease — as they broadly did into FY26 — the same mechanism runs in reverse and margin expands even on flat volumes. That timing asymmetry is exactly why FY26's core EBITDA jumped ~46% on only ~11% more revenue.

The device that only fits a branded-foam maker: brand price − crude-linked chemical

Forget "mattress prices went up." The number that runs Sheela Foam is (what a Sleepwell/Kurlon mattress fetches) − (the TDI + polyol + other foam-chemical cost to build it), per unit. The two halves move on different clocks and for different reasons: the left half is set by brand strength, mix and how fast households trade up; the right half is set by crude oil, the rupee and chemical-plant capacity in China and the Gulf. The profit is the gap between a price the company partly controls and a cost it does not control at all. When the chemicals ease and the brand holds price, you get FY26. When a crude or TDI spike opens up before the brand can pass it on, the same mattress sells for the same money and the year quietly disappears into the input line. The mattress tag can look identical in both worlds; only the spread tells you which one Sheela Foam is living in.

03

Buying its biggest rival: the Kurlon consolidation

The defining strategic act — a branded near-duopoly, built by acquisition.

In a fragmented market, the fastest way to dominate the branded end is to buy the other big brand. That is exactly what Sheela Foam did. In FY24 it acquired a controlling stake — around 94.7% — in Kurlon Enterprise (the maker of Kurl-on mattresses) for roughly ₹2,000 Cr, and over FY25–FY26 folded it into the group, culminating in a legal merger effective April 2026. Overnight, two of India's three most recognised mattress brands sat under one owner.

The deal doubled down on the branded end of a market that is still mostly unbranded — the strategic logic — but it also loaded the balance sheet with debt and handed management a multi-year integration job. Both halves are real.

The logic is compelling. In a category where the biggest competitor is not another brand but the unbranded local mattress shop, scale is the weapon: two brands mean more shelf space, more dealer leverage, a wider price ladder (Kurlon skews a notch more mass-market than premium Sleepwell), and — crucially — more buying power on TDI and polyol, the chemicals that decide the margin. Shared plants and procurement mean the combined entity should convert foam more cheaply than either did alone. That is the synergy prize.

The genuine surprise: a "mattress" company's boldest move was an M&A play on its rival

It is easy to imagine Sheela Foam as a sleepy consumer brand compounding along on Sleepwell's name. The reality is more aggressive: its single most consequential decision of the decade was to spend ~₹2,000 Cr buying its largest branded competitor and taking on debt to do it — an M&A bet, not an advertising campaign. That reframes the company. It is not just a brand; it is a consolidator trying to roll up the organised end of Indian mattresses before the online insurgents and the still-huge unbranded market redraw the map. Consolidation plays live or die on integration, and the two years since the deal have been about exactly that — turning Kurlon's margins around, merging the entities, and proving the synergy was real rather than a slide.

Why the integration risk is the thing to watch: an acquisition adds the target's revenue on day one but only earns its keep if the buyer actually extracts the synergy — common sourcing, shared plants, a cleaned-up cost base, and a turned-around brand — without the two organisations grinding against each other. FY26's margin jump and Kurlon's improving contribution are the early evidence it is working; the legal merger in April 2026 removes the last structural friction. But integration is judged over years, not quarters, and a stumble here (culture, channel conflict, a botched cost-out) would undo the strategic logic. Read Kurlon's standalone margin trajectory as the scorecard on whether the ~₹2,000 Cr was well spent.

What Sheela Foam actually sells: the mix and the plants

Two brands, a big B2B foam business, and a growing online channel.

Sheela Foam is best understood as two businesses sharing one material. There is the branded mattress-and-comfort business — Sleepwell and Kurlon, plus pillows, cushions and sleep accessories, sold to households under trusted names. And there is the foam-as-input business — technical and specialty PU foam sold to other manufacturers. The same foam expertise feeds both; the economics differ.

The product lines

  • Kurlon mattresses — the acquired brand, skewing a notch more mass-market, extending the price ladder downward and widening reach. It grew faster in FY26 (~14% volume) as the turnaround took hold — the clearest sign the acquisition is earning its keep.

Why the brand is the only part of this the company truly owns

Every mattress maker in India buys the same TDI and polyol at roughly the same global price and whips it into roughly the same foam. What separates a ₹40,000 Sleepwell from a no-name mattress is not the chemistry — it is the brand, the retail presence, and the trust a household places in a name it has known for decades. That is why Sheela Foam guards distribution so fiercely and why the Kurlon deal was about brands and shelf space, not factories. In a business where the input is a commodity everyone shares, the durable edge is the label on the outside and the dealer who recommends it. The foam is a cost; the brand is the moat.

05

The company, the family, and who controls it

A founder-family business built over four decades on one brand.

Sheela Foam is a promoter-family company — built and controlled by the Gautam family, who turned a small foam operation into the owner of India's leading mattress brand. Understanding the founder-led, brand-obsessed culture explains both its distribution strength and the kind of bold, whole-company bets (Kurlon, Furlenco) it is willing to make.

Timeline

  • 2005
    First global step: acquires Joyce Foam, an Australian polyurethane-foam maker — the root of today's international foam operation.
  • 2016
    IPO on NSE & BSE (BSE code 540203); the company lists as India's dominant branded-mattress and PU-foam player.
  • 2022
    Acquires a ~35% stake in Furlenco (House of Kieraya), the furniture-rental platform, for ~₹300 Cr — a bet on new models of home comfort and "renting" furniture.
  • FY24
    The defining move: acquires ~94.7% of Kurlon Enterprise for ~₹2,000 Cr, taking on debt — consolidating branded mattresses into a Sleepwell + Kurlon near-duopoly.
  • FY25
    A tough transition year — Kurlon integration costs and elevated interest weigh on profit even as mattress volumes recover; management sets out margin-recovery and debt-reduction targets.
  • FY26
    Recovery: revenue ~₹3,821 Cr (+11%), core EBITDA ~₹414 Cr (+46%), PAT ~₹160 Cr (+79%); Kurlon legally merged (April 2026), net debt cut to ~₹543 Cr, and the company's first-ever dividend (₹1/share) declared.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter: the Gautam family holds the controlling stake; the balance is public float including domestic and foreign institutions. This is a founder-led company where the family's long association with the Sleepwell brand shapes strategy. Confirm the exact promoter percentage in the latest shareholding filing.
  • The adjacency bets: beyond the core, Sheela Foam holds a ~35% stake in Furlenco (furniture rental) and runs an international foam arm. These widen the "home comfort" footprint but also spread capital and management attention — minority investors should judge whether each adjacency earns its cost of capital or dilutes the core.
  • Scale-up by acquisition: the Kurlon deal (and its April-2026 legal merger) is the structural story — a debt-funded consolidation now being paid down, with net debt cut to ~₹543 Cr (~1.3× EBITDA) in FY26 and further deleveraging targeted.
The structure matters because it cuts both ways: a committed founder-family with a decades-deep brand is a genuine asset — it explains the distribution discipline and the willingness to make a bold, transformative acquisition few professional managers would risk. But a promoter-led company making large debt-funded acquisitions and minority bets on adjacencies (Furlenco, international foam) also puts capital allocation squarely in the spotlight: minority investors are trusting the family to buy well, integrate well, and not let the adjacencies become a drag on a core that is finally recovering.
06

The D2C insurgents: how an incumbent fights back

Wakefit and Duroflex changed how India buys a mattress — Sleepwell had to respond.

For decades, buying a mattress meant walking into a dealer and lying down on a Sleepwell. Then a wave of direct-to-consumer, online-first brands — led by Wakefit and a re-energised Duroflex — rewrote the script: order on your phone, a "bed-in-a-box" arrives compressed, sleep on it for 100 nights, return it free if you don't like it. This is the disruption Sheela Foam has spent the last few years responding to, and it is central to the case.

The most important thing to notice is the convergence. The online insurgents discovered that most mattress buyers still want to lie down before spending ₹30,000, so Wakefit and Duroflex are opening physical stores. Meanwhile Sheela Foam is building its own online and D2C presence. Everyone is ending up omnichannel — which quietly favours the incumbent, because building a nationwide physical network from scratch is far harder and slower than an established one adding a website.

Why the incumbent's response is more defensible than it first looks

The tempting narrative is that nimble online brands eat the old giant's lunch. The reality is more balanced. Distribution in mattresses is genuinely hard: a household wants to test a big-ticket, once-a-decade purchase in person, and thousands of dealer relationships take decades to build and cannot be conjured with an ad budget. Sleepwell's offline network is a real asset that the insurgents are now spending heavily to replicate. What Sheela Foam had to fix was the other side — a modern online presence, sharper younger-buyer branding, and a faster product cadence — and that is where its D2C push and the Kurlon brand's mass-market reach come in. The war is not incumbent-versus-online; it is who reaches omnichannel maturity first with the best cost position. On foam scale and offline reach, the incumbent starts ahead.

07

Unorganised to organised: the real growth engine

The biggest competitor isn't a brand — it's the local mattress shop.

Here is the structural fact that makes the whole category interesting: most Indian mattresses are still unbranded. A large share of the market is made by local shops stitching a mattress to order — no brand, no warranty, no factory. The multi-decade growth thesis for Sheela Foam is not stealing share from Wakefit; it is the slow, structural migration of that vast unorganised market toward branded, factory-made mattresses as incomes rise, hygiene awareness grows, and organised retail spreads.

This is why the branded-consolidation strategy makes sense. Two dynamics compound: (1) the organised market grows as unbranded buyers trade up, and (2) Sheela Foam holds a dominant, ~35–40% share of that growing organised pie. If both play out, the company rides a widening market and a strong share of it — a rare double. It is a classic "premiumisation-plus-formalisation" story, the same arc that reshaped paints, tiles, plywood and innerwear in India.

The patience the thesis demands

The unorganised-to-organised shift is real and powerful, but it is slow — measured in years and cycles, not quarters. Mattresses are replaced once a decade, so the conversion happens one household purchase at a time, gated by income growth, urbanisation and awareness. That is precisely why Sheela Foam trades on a rich earnings multiple: the market is paying today for a migration it expects to unfold over a long runway, with two strong brands positioned to capture it. The reward for being right is a long, structural tailwind behind a dominant player. The risk is that the runway is long enough for the online insurgents, or a still-cheap unorganised option, to keep the conversion slower and more contested than the multiple assumes.

08

The three levers — what actually writes the year

Input costs, branded volume, and integration: the trio behind every result.

Strip the business to essentials and a branded-foam maker's year turns on three forces. Sheela Foam manages around all three — through brand, mix and scale — but it controls only some of them. This is the honest core of the risk profile.

The uncomfortable summary a first-timer should internalise

Sheela Foam's job is to run a tight, scaled foam-and-mattress operation and then survive two weathers it does not control — chemical weather (crude-linked TDI and polyol) and consumer weather (discretionary, once-a-decade mattress demand) — while executing one thing it does control: integrating Kurlon and allocating capital well. It can improve its odds — push branded mix, use its scale to buy chemicals better, deleverage, sharpen the online channel. What it cannot do is guarantee a benign input cycle or a strong consumer year. FY26 handed it easing chemicals and a recovering consumer at once. A disciplined investor prices that knowing the model does not control whether the good cycle repeats.

Notice how the levers interact. Cheap chemicals (lever 1) are worth far more when branded volume is strong (lever 2) and the Kurlon cost base is optimised (lever 3) — the spread widens across a bigger, better-run book. The best years, like FY26, are when easing inputs, recovering demand and integration traction land together. The worst would be a crude spike hitting just as discretionary demand softens and integration still needs feeding — the FY25 texture, before the recovery.

The industry and the rivals

A branded near-duopoly, chased by online insurgents and a vast unbranded tail.

Indian branded mattresses have consolidated into a small set of serious names, because building a trusted brand, a foam supply chain and a nationwide distribution network takes decades. Sheela Foam, post-Kurlon, is the clear scale leader of the organised market — but it is pressed from two directions: nimble D2C insurgents above, and the still-huge unbranded market below.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, the branded players do not really compete on the input — everyone buys the same crude-linked TDI and polyol — so the fight is over brand, distribution, mix and cost position. The scaled, best-sourced, widest-distributed player earns the fattest spread on the same foam, which is exactly why Sheela Foam's size and the Kurlon buying-power matter. Second, the most important "competitor" is not a rival brand at all — it is the unbranded local shop. That reframes the whole industry as a formalisation opportunity more than a market-share knife-fight, and it favours the player with the trust, the shelves and the scale to convert a hesitant first-time branded buyer.

Financials & valuation

A recovery year on a rich multiple — the question is durability, not cheapness.

FY26 was a clear recovery: revenue up ~11%, but the real move was in profitability, with core EBITDA up ~46% and net profit up ~79% as chemical costs eased and Kurlon turned. Unlike a cheap cyclical, Sheela Foam carries a demanding valuation — a high-40s (or higher) trailing P/E — because the market is paying for the branded-consolidation runway and the margin recovery, not the still-modest trailing profit. The debate here is not whether the stock is cheap; it is whether the FY26 margin and volume trajectory is the start of a durable climb toward management's targets.

The one number that settles the thesis: EBITDA margin, read with branded volume and Kurlon synergy

At a high-40s multiple, the case does not need cheapness — it needs the margin to keep climbing toward the ~15% ambition on rising branded volume. So the metric that proves or breaks it is core EBITDA margin (the ~10.8% of FY26), tracked quarter by quarter against mattress volumes and read alongside two companions: Kurlon's standalone margin (is the acquisition actually earning its synergy?) and net debt (is the acquisition debt still falling?). If margins grind toward the mid-teens as inputs stay benign, Kurlon's cost base is optimised, and branded volume compounds, the rich multiple is defensible and earnings scale into it. If a TDI/polyol spike drags margin back toward single digits while volume softens and integration stalls, FY26 will read as a cyclical bounce the price got ahead of. Volume × spread, minus what the acquisition still costs to carry — that is the scoreboard.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A recovering, consolidating market leader — asked whether it can earn its multiple.

Sheela Foam enters FY27 in far better shape than a year earlier: a margin recovery underway, Kurlon legally merged and turning, net debt falling, a first dividend paid, and two dominant brands sitting on a long unorganised-to-organised runway. What no foam maker can promise is the one thing that most decides each year — that crude-linked TDI and polyol stay benign long enough for the spread to keep widening. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Branded near-duopoly — Sleepwell + Kurlon at ~35–40% of the organised market, positioned to ride the long unorganised-to-branded shift.
  • Margin runway — from ~10.8% toward a ~15% ambition via easing inputs, Kurlon synergy, richer mix and operating leverage on a bigger base.
  • Integration paying off — Kurlon volumes grew faster than Sleepwell in FY26, net debt is falling, and the legal merger removes the last friction.
  • Distribution moat — a decades-deep dealer network the online insurgents are spending heavily to replicate; the incumbent starts omnichannel from ahead.
  • Deleveraging & first dividend — a cleaner balance sheet and the first-ever payout signal confidence and cash generation returning.

The bear case

  • Crude-linked input whipsaw — a TDI/polyol spike ahead of price hikes can collapse the recovered margin fast; the spread is not the company's to control.
  • Rich valuation — a high-40s trailing P/E leaves the recovery and consolidation largely priced in; a margin wobble hits the multiple, not just the earnings.
  • Discretionary demand — a mattress is a big-ticket, once-a-decade buy; a consumer slowdown dents volume, and volume is what the spread multiplies against.
  • Online-D2C competition — Wakefit, Duroflex and others keep customer-acquisition costs and marketing intensity high across the industry.
  • Integration & capital allocation — synergy must keep flowing, acquisition debt keep falling, and the Furlenco/international adjacencies must not drag the recovering core.
The bottom line

Sheela Foam is a founder-built brand leader that made one bold bet — buying its biggest rival to consolidate branded sleep — and is now, in FY26, showing that bet beginning to pay: margins recovering, Kurlon turning, debt falling, a first dividend on the table. The strategic logic is sound and the runway is long, because most Indian mattresses are still unbranded and two trusted names are positioned to convert them. But the price already assumes a good deal of that future, and the profit underneath still rides a spread over two crude-linked chemicals the company cannot control. The next test is concrete and datable: whether core EBITDA margin keeps climbing from ~10.8% toward the mid-teens through FY27 as Kurlon's cost base is fully optimised and the input environment holds. Widen the spread and convert the unbranded, and the rich multiple looks like foresight; give the margin back to the next TDI spike, and FY26 will look like the recovery year the market simply paid for too early.
