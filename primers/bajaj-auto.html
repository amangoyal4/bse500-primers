00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Bajaj Auto is one of the world's largest makers of motorcycles and three-wheelers — and India's biggest exporter of both. It is the company behind the Pulsar (India's iconic sports commuter motorcycle) and the ubiquitous Bajaj autorickshaw, and it builds the KTM, Husqvarna and Triumph brands too. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) it sold a record 51.2 lakh (5.12 million) vehicles and earned ₹58,732 crore of revenue at a 20.5% operating margin — comfortably the fattest margin among India's big auto OEMs.

Four facts frame everything about this company:

  • It is an export machine, not just an Indian company. Roughly 44% of Bajaj's volumes — 22.5 lakh units in FY26 — went overseas, mostly to Africa, Latin America and South Asia. That makes it utterly different from domestic-focused peers: its earnings swing with emerging-market demand and currency crises in places like Nigeria, Egypt and Argentina, not just the Indian monsoon.
  • It owns the high ground on margin. A 20.5% EBITDA margin is exceptional for a mass-vehicle maker (Maruti runs ~12%, two-wheeler rivals low-to-mid teens). Bajaj earns it through premiumisation (Pulsar, KTM, Triumph), a rich three-wheeler/export mix, and famously tight cost control.
  • It rules the three-wheeler. Bajaj is the dominant force in autorickshaws — the little yellow-and-green taxis of the developing world — with a near-monopoly grip on the CNG three-wheeler segment in India.
  • It just bought KTM. After a dramatic insolvency in Austria, Bajaj took full control of KTM / Pierer Mobility in late 2025 — renaming it Bajaj Mobility AG — a bet that converts a long-standing minority stake into outright ownership of a global premium-motorcycle brand.
In plain terms

Think of Bajaj Auto as two very different businesses stapled together. One is a cash cow: the dominant maker of cheap, durable autorickshaws and commuter motorcycles for India and the emerging world, sold by the million at high margins. The other is an ambitious premium play: the Pulsar going up-market, plus the KTM/Husqvarna/Triumph brand stack, chasing the global enthusiast who pays a lot for a fast bike. The cash cow funds everything — the fat dividends, the buybacks, the KTM rescue, the Chetak EV. The whole investment debate is whether Bajaj can keep that cash engine humming through emerging-market turbulence while the premium and electric bets grow up into real profit centres — or whether KTM, currency shocks and EV competition end up eating the cash the cow produces.

01

2W/3W economics 101: how Bajaj earns

No finance degree required — just the handful of forces that drive a two- and three-wheeler maker's profit.

A motorcycle is a far simpler, cheaper machine than a car — fewer parts, less steel, no need for the elaborate safety and crash engineering of a four-wheeler. That changes the economics in two big ways: each unit is cheaper to make and sells for less, but the maker can earn a surprisingly high margin on it — because two-wheelers are a volume game where scale, brand and tight costs compound. Five levers explain almost all of Bajaj's profit.

The simple profit equation

Roughly: Revenue ≈ Volume × Realisation, and Profit ≈ Revenue − (cost of materials & making each unit) − fixed costs − tax. A two-wheeler maker grows profit three ways — sell more units (volume), sell dearer units (premium mix / more three-wheelers / exports), and hold the line on cost. Bajaj has spent two decades optimising all three, which is why it converts a smaller revenue base than Maruti into a much higher margin. The catch: two-wheeler demand is brutally cyclical and tied to the wallet of the price-sensitive mass buyer — in India that means the rural economy and the monsoon; abroad it means emerging-market growth and currencies. A bad year on either front leaves expensive capacity under-used.

Why two-wheelers are a "value buyer's" business

  • The customer is stretched. Most motorcycle buyers — at home and in Africa or LatAm — are first-time, low-income, finance-dependent buyers. When fuel, inflation or interest rates bite, they delay the purchase or buy used. That makes affordability and financing availability central to volumes.
  • Three-wheelers are a livelihood, not a luxury. An autorickshaw is a small business on wheels — bought to earn money. That makes 3W demand steadier and more credit-driven than 2W, and it carries a richer margin.
  • Brand and durability matter more than features. In emerging markets, "it never breaks down and parts are cheap everywhere" beats a fancy touchscreen. Bajaj's decades-old reputation for rugged reliability is a real moat in places where a broken bike means lost income.
02

The business and the value chain

From a Pune design studio to a delivery rider in Lagos and an autorickshaw driver in Lima.

Bajaj's edge is not one product — it is a full system: in-house engineering tuned for frugal, durable machines; one of the most cost-efficient manufacturing operations in the industry; a vast distribution and after-sales network spanning India and 70-plus export markets; and a portfolio that now ranges from a ₹60,000 commuter to a ₹20-lakh Triumph. Understanding where the value sits explains the margin.

What Bajaj actually sells

  • Motorcycles — the core. Entry/commuter (Platina, CT, Pulsar 125), the famous Pulsar sports-commuter range, the Dominar tourers, the world's first CNG bike (Freedom 125), plus the premium KTM, Husqvarna and Triumph brands made in India.
  • Three-wheelers (commercial vehicles) — passenger autorickshaws and goods carriers, dominant in India and across the developing world, increasingly CNG and electric.
  • Electric vehicles — the Chetak e-scooter (Bajaj's EV flagship) and a growing range of electric three-wheelers.
  • Quadricycle — the small Qute, a niche four-wheeled micro-vehicle for last-mile transport.

Bajaj's durable advantage sits at both ends: ultra-low-cost making (Stage 2), and an export-and-service reach (Stage 3–4) that rivals cannot quickly replicate in dozens of emerging markets. That combination — cheap to build, sold and serviced everywhere — is what underwrites the industry's best margins.
Why "India-as-an-export-base" is the structural advantage

Bajaj makes everything in India — one of the lowest-cost places on earth to build a motorcycle — and ships it to markets where local manufacturing barely exists or is far costlier. That gives it two things at once: a cost advantage (cheap, scaled Indian production) and a diversification advantage (when India is soft, Africa or LatAm may be strong, and vice-versa). The price of that model is exposure to things an Indian-only maker never worries about — destination-country currencies, import rules, dollar liquidity in poor countries, and geopolitics. The whole Bajaj story is the tension between that export advantage and that export risk, which the next two sections unpack in detail.

03

The company and the Bajaj family

A near-century-old industrial house, a famous family split, and a focused vehicle champion.

Bajaj is one of India's oldest and most respected business houses, founded in the 1920s. The Bajaj Auto of today is the two- and three-wheeler arm of that empire — deliberately carved out and sharpened over the past two decades into a focused, cash-rich global vehicle maker, while the family's giant financial-services businesses sit in separate listed companies.

Timeline

  • 1945–60s
    Bajaj begins importing and then assembling two- and three-wheelers; the Bajaj scooter becomes a symbol of post-independence middle-class India ("Hamara Bajaj").
  • 1980s–90s
    The Chetak scooter dominates, with famous multi-year waiting lists. Bajaj then pivots decisively from scooters into motorcycles as the market shifts.
  • 2001
    Launch of the Pulsar — the sports-commuter bike that redefined the segment and became Bajaj's defining brand and a premiumisation engine.
  • 2007–08
    Bajaj takes its first stake in Austria's KTM, beginning a long alliance that puts KTM and Husqvarna manufacturing in India for global export.
  • 2008
    The group restructures: Bajaj Auto Ltd (vehicles) is demerged from Bajaj Finserv (finance/insurance) and Bajaj Holdings — creating today's focused auto company.
  • 2017–20
    Triumph alliance signed (mid-size Triumphs built in India); Chetak reborn as an electric scooter — Bajaj's EV flagship.
  • 2024
    Launches the Freedom 125, the world's first CNG motorcycle — extending Bajaj's CNG playbook from three-wheelers to bikes.
  • 2025–26
    KTM/Pierer Mobility hits insolvency in Austria; Bajaj injects cash, then takes full control — renaming the group Bajaj Mobility AG (Jan 2026). Record FY26: revenue ₹58,732 Cr, PAT ₹9,825 Cr, volumes cross 51 lakh.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter: the Bajaj family holds ~55% (through Bajaj Holdings & Investment and family entities). Managing Director Rajiv Bajaj runs the company. The rest is held by domestic institutions (~14%), foreign investors (~9%) and retail/public (~22%).
  • A focused vehicle company. Unlike the diversified Mahindra, Bajaj Auto is a pure-play 2W/3W/EV maker. Its sister companies — Bajaj Finance, Bajaj Finserv — are separately listed financial giants; Bajaj Auto does not consolidate them. (This primer covers Bajaj Auto Ltd only.)
  • Cash-rich and capital-light. The auto business is essentially debt-free and throws off cash well in excess of what it needs to reinvest — which is why capital return (dividends + buybacks) and acquisitions like KTM are central to the story.
Don't confuse the Bajaj companies. "Bajaj" spans several large listed entities. Bajaj Auto (BAJAJ-AUTO) is the vehicle maker covered here. Bajaj Finance and Bajaj Finserv are lending/insurance businesses; Bajaj Holdings is the family holding company. They share a name and lineage but are different businesses with different drivers.

The export engine

The single biggest thing that sets Bajaj apart — and the lever that most swings its earnings.

Most Indian auto companies are bets on the Indian economy. Bajaj is only half that. In FY26 it shipped 22.5 lakh vehicles abroad — about 44% of all it made, up 21% on the year — making it by far India's largest exporter of both motorcycles and three-wheelers. This is the company's defining feature: it has built India into a global manufacturing base and sells across roughly 70 markets in Africa, Latin America, South Asia and the Middle East.

Where the vehicles go

  • Africa is the heartland. Africa accounts for roughly half of Bajaj's exports, and Nigeria alone is around half of Africa — so a single country's economy and currency can move the whole export line. Bajaj bikes are the workhorse of the African okada (motorcycle-taxi) economy.
  • Latin America is the growth engine. Markets like Mexico, Colombia, Peru, Guatemala and Central America drove the FY26 rebound — both motorcycles and three-wheelers, helped by refreshed products and sharper pricing.
  • South Asia & ASEAN. Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, the Philippines and neighbours — meaningful 2W and 3W volumes, though periodically hit by their own import curbs and FX crunches.
  • The Middle East and beyond. Plus a long tail of smaller markets that collectively add up.

Why exports are the whole game, not a footnote

For a high-volume manufacturer, a full factory is everything (Section 01). Exports give Bajaj a second, enormous demand pool to keep plants running flat-out even when India is soft — exactly what happened in FY26, when 21% export growth offset a near-flat domestic two-wheeler market. They also support margin (three-wheeler and premium exports skew richer) and diversify the company away from any single economy. But the same fact is the biggest risk: when an export market's currency collapses or its government rations dollars, Bajaj's vehicles suddenly become unaffordable there overnight — and a whole region's volume can evaporate for a year or two. That is the swing factor explored next.

05

Forex & emerging-market risk: the earnings swing

Why a currency crisis in Lagos or Buenos Aires shows up in a Pune company's quarterly profit.

Because nearly half its vehicles go to volatile developing economies, Bajaj's exports live and die by two things its domestic peers never face: local-currency stability and dollar availability in poor countries. When an export market's money weakens, a Bajaj bike priced in dollars becomes dramatically more expensive in local terms — and sales can crater. This is the most important non-Indian variable in the whole company.

The mechanism, step by step

  • 1. The buyer earns in naira (or peso, or taka). A motorcycle-taxi driver in Nigeria earns and saves in local currency.
  • 2. Bajaj exports get priced in hard currency (dollars). A devaluation means the bike now costs far more naira — even though its dollar price barely moved.
  • 3. Demand collapses — or the dollars simply aren't there. In a currency crisis, central banks often ration foreign exchange; importers and distributors literally cannot get the dollars to pay for shipments. Sales stop regardless of demand.
  • 4. Bajaj's volume and profit take the hit — for several quarters. A single large market freezing up (Nigeria has done this more than once) can knock meaningful volume out of the whole export line until the currency stabilises.
The Nigeria lesson — why it matters so much

Nigeria is roughly half of Africa, which is roughly half of Bajaj's exports — so it is, on its own, one of the most important markets the company has. In past naira devaluation and dollar-shortage episodes, Bajaj's Nigerian sales fell sharply and dragged the whole export number down for several quarters, only to recover strongly once the currency stabilised and pent-up demand released. That boom-bust rhythm is the texture of Bajaj's export business: long stretches of growth, punctuated by sudden country-specific shocks (Nigeria, Egypt, Sri Lanka, Argentina at various times), followed by sharp rebounds. FY26's 21% export growth was precisely such a rebound phase, led by Latin America and a recovering Africa. The investor's job is to judge how much of any given year's export number is structural growth versus a cyclical bounce off a depressed base.

How Bajaj manages the risk

  • Diversification across ~70 markets — so no single crisis (usually) sinks the whole export line; weakness in one region is often offset by strength in another.
  • Pricing & product agility — refreshing products and adjusting price points to restore affordability when currencies move.
  • Currency hedging — Bajaj hedges a portion of its dollar receivables forward, smoothing (but not eliminating) the FX hit on its reported numbers.
  • Local assembly in some markets — to soften import-duty and FX exposure where it makes sense.
The honest caveat: exports are simultaneously Bajaj's biggest growth lever and its biggest source of volatility. A great export year can flatter the trend; a currency shock in one or two big markets can mask genuine underlying strength. Treat any single year's export growth rate (here, +21%) as a point on a bumpy curve, not a straight line.

Premiumisation & the brand stack: the margin lever

How a maker of cheap commuter bikes earns the industry's fattest margin — by climbing up-market.

The most powerful idea in Bajaj's strategy is premiumisation: selling progressively dearer, higher-margin motorcycles to the same network, so revenue and profit grow even when unit volumes don't. Bajaj has assembled an unusual brand ladder — from a ₹60,000 commuter all the way to a ₹20-lakh Triumph — and each rung up lifts the average selling price and the margin. This is the single biggest reason Bajaj's ~20.5% EBITDA margin towers over peers.

Why mix lifts margin so much

A commuter bike might earn Bajaj a few thousand rupees of gross profit; a KTM, Triumph or top Pulsar earns multiples of that on far higher revenue per unit. So every percentage point of the mix that shifts from entry bikes to premium ones lifts both the average selling price and the margin. In FY26, the premium segment Bajaj sells through this network — KTM, Husqvarna and Triumph — crossed 30,000 units domestically (up ~30%), with billing value up roughly 70%, showing how much faster value grows than units when the mix richens.

Why owning the brands (not just licensing) matters

Anyone can sell a cheap commuter bike. Very few can offer a buyer a ladder — start on a Platina, graduate to a Pulsar, aspire to a KTM or Triumph — all serviced by the same dealer. Owning the premium brands (KTM now outright, Triumph in alliance, Husqvarna alongside) does three things: it captures the customer as they get richer instead of losing them to a rival; it lifts the blended margin as the mix climbs; and it gives Bajaj global technology and credibility in performance bikes that a pure value-maker can't build alone. The strategic logic of the whole KTM/Triumph project is exactly this margin ladder — which is also why the KTM rescue (next section) was about protecting an asset central to Bajaj's premium future, not just a financial bailout.

The GST twist (FY26): India's GST reform left motorcycles above 350cc taxed at a punishing 40%, while sub-350cc bikes sit at 18%. Bajaj responded by working with KTM and Triumph to build a new sub-350cc engine and reshape the premium line-up to land under the cheaper tax slab — a neat example of tax policy directly shaping product strategy.
07

The KTM / Pierer saga: from minority stake to full owner

How a long-standing alliance turned into a rescue, an insolvency, and outright ownership of a global brand.

For nearly two decades Bajaj held a growing minority stake in KTM, the famous Austrian maker of orange sportbikes, through its parent group Pierer Mobility — a tidy alliance under which KTM and Husqvarna's smaller bikes were built in India for the world. In 2024–25 that comfortable arrangement was upended: KTM ran out of money, fell into insolvency, and Bajaj stepped in to rescue it — emerging as the full owner of one of motorcycling's most storied brands.

What happened, in order

  • 2007–2023
    Bajaj builds a minority stake in KTM/Pierer Mobility over years; KTM expands aggressively into bigger bikes, the US, and adjacencies (bicycles, the X-Bow car).
  • Late 2024
    KTM hits a wall: too much inventory, a slump in premium-bike demand, and rising costs. It files for insolvency in Austria — a shock for a brand of its stature.
  • Feb–May 2025
    A court-approved restructuring requires KTM to pay creditors ~30% of debt (~€548m) by May 2025. Bajaj backs the rescue with a large cash injection (~€800m) to keep the company alive.
  • Jun–Nov 2025
    Bajaj exercises options to buy out the founding Pierer group's stake in stages, ending with 100% of the holding entity (Pierer Bajaj AG) and ~74.9% of Pierer Mobility.
  • Jan 2026
    Pierer Mobility AG is renamed Bajaj Mobility AG — the Pierer name retired, Bajaj now in full control. KTM secures a €550m bank refinancing in Feb 2026.

The cleanup & the recovery

  • Cost surgery. KTM cut its workforce (~500 roles), exited non-core ventures — the Felt bicycle brand sold, the X-Bow car project wound down, distribution partnerships pruned — and refocused on its core motorcycles.
  • A return to profit (on paper). For calendar 2025, Bajaj Mobility AG reported revenue of ~€1.0 billion on ~210,000 motorcycles, EBITDA of ~€874m and a net profit of ~€590m — but that profit was flattered by a ~€1.2 billion one-off restructuring gain (creditors taking a haircut), not by underlying trading. The operating turnaround is still in progress.
  • Refinanced. A €550m loan from an international banking consortium (Feb 2026) put the balance sheet on firmer footing.
Why Bajaj did it — and the risk it carries

The bull logic is strategic, not opportunistic: KTM is central to Bajaj's premiumisation ladder (Section 06) and its global ambitions. Letting it collapse would have damaged a brand Bajaj has spent ₹thousands of crore and two decades building into. By taking control at the bottom — after creditors absorbed a huge haircut — Bajaj got the whole brand, its technology and its global dealer network for a fraction of its peak value, and can now integrate KTM tightly with its Indian manufacturing cost base. The bear logic is just as real: premium-motorcycle demand globally is weak, KTM's troubles were partly structural (it grew too fast into a softening market), and Bajaj now owns a loss-prone, capital-hungry European operation with all the execution, currency and integration risk that brings. The €590m headline profit was a one-off; the test is whether KTM can earn its keep on an operating basis. This is the highest-variance bet in the entire Bajaj story.

Read the KTM profit carefully. Bajaj Mobility AG's CY2025 ~€590m net profit included a ~€1.2bn restructuring gain (debt forgiven by creditors). That is an accounting event, not cash earned from selling bikes. Judge the recovery on operating EBITDA and unit trends, not the headline.

Three-wheeler dominance & the CNG playbook

The quiet, high-margin cash engine most people forget Bajaj owns.

Behind the motorcycle headlines, Bajaj runs one of its most profitable franchises: the three-wheeler. The humble autorickshaw — a passenger or goods carrier bought to earn a living — is a higher-margin, steadier business than commuter bikes, and Bajaj dominates it both at home and across the developing world. In FY26 it sold 8.0 lakh three-wheelers, up 20%, with exports up a striking 49%.

The CNG playbook — a genuine moat

Where Bajaj's three-wheeler grip becomes a near-monopoly is in CNG (compressed natural gas) — a fuel far cheaper to run than petrol, ideal for a vehicle driven all day to earn money. Bajaj holds roughly 88% of the CNG three-wheeler market, and CNG variants are now about 70% of Bajaj's three-wheeler sales. That dominance is hard to dislodge: it rests on decades of optimised CNG engineering, the widest dealer-and-service network, and the trust of fleet buyers whose income depends on reliability.

  • Why drivers love CNG: running costs a fraction of petrol — decisive for someone driving 100+ km a day for a living.
  • Why it's sticky: CNG engineering and the refuelling/service ecosystem are specialised; Bajaj's scale and reliability reputation are very hard for a newcomer to match.
  • The CNG bike extension: Bajaj is now trying to replicate the playbook in two-wheelers with the Freedom 125, the world's first CNG motorcycle — a bet that cost-conscious commuters will value low running costs the way autorickshaw drivers do.
Why the three-wheeler matters more than its unit count suggests

Three-wheelers are barely a sixth of Bajaj's volume but punch far above their weight in profit, because they carry higher realisation and margin than cheap motorcycles and are bought on commercial logic (a tool to make money) rather than discretionary want. The CNG near-monopoly makes the franchise unusually defensible and gives Bajaj pricing power. The one cloud on the horizon is electrification: electric three-wheelers (and small e-rickshaws from a swarm of low-cost players) are growing fast, and the long-run question is whether Bajaj's electric 3Ws can defend this fortress as cleanly as its CNG ones did. So far Bajaj is among the leaders in electric three-wheelers too — but it is a more contested arena than CNG.

The EV transition: Chetak & electric three-wheelers

Bajaj's bet that it can win electric two- and three-wheelers — without wrecking its industry-best margins.

India's two-wheeler market is electrifying from the bottom up, and Bajaj's answer is the reborn Chetak — its EV flagship, named after the legendary scooter of the 1980s. From a slow start, the Chetak has scaled into one of India's best-selling electric scooters, and Bajaj is now a serious force in electric three-wheelers too. The strategic question is the same one facing every legacy maker: can it win the EV market without the EV market destroying the margins that make Bajaj special?

The two-sided EV bet

  • The opportunity: as India's scooter market electrifies, Bajaj's brand, dealer network and manufacturing scale let it convert legacy strength into EV leadership — and the Chetak has done exactly that, vaulting to #2 from a standing start. Electric three-wheelers extend the same logic into Bajaj's fortress segment.
  • The margin trap: EVs are fiercely price-competitive (Ola, Ather, Hero, TVS all fighting), batteries are costly, and the subsidy/regulatory backdrop keeps shifting. Selling EVs at scale today often means thinner margins than Bajaj earns on a premium petrol bike — so a faster EV mix could, paradoxically, dilute the company's headline 20.5% margin even as it wins share.
  • The competitive churn: the EV two-wheeler leaderboard reshuffles constantly (Ola's collapse from leader to also-ran in ~two years is a cautionary tale). Today's #2 is not guaranteed tomorrow.
How to think about Bajaj's EV position

Bajaj has done the hard part most legacy makers fail at: it built a genuinely competitive electric product (Chetak) and scaled it to a strong #2, while remaining disciplined about profitability rather than chasing share at any cost — a deliberate contrast with cash-burning EV-only startups. The bull reading: Bajaj gets to ride the EV wave with a real brand, a real network and a real balance sheet, and can afford to wait for the economics to improve. The bear reading: EV two-wheelers may structurally earn less than the premium petrol bikes that drive Bajaj's margin, so even a winning EV business could weigh on the blended margin that is central to the investment case. The honest answer is that EV is a must-win arena where Bajaj is competing well — but it is unlikely, for now, to be as profitable as the franchise it is gradually replacing.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A best-in-class cash machine with two big new bets — priced for them to pay off.

Bajaj Auto is one of India's highest-quality industrial franchises: the world's motorcycle exporter, the autorickshaw and CNG-3W champion, the maker of the Pulsar, the owner of KTM, and the holder of the industry's fattest margins and a fortress balance sheet. The investment debate isn't whether the core is excellent — it plainly is — but whether the two new chapters (premium/KTM and EV) grow into profit while the core rides out emerging-market turbulence.

The next chapter asks Bajaj to do three hard things at once — keep the export cash engine firing through currency shocks, make KTM earn its keep on an operating basis, and win the EV race without surrendering its industry-best margins.

The bull case

  • Best-in-class margins — ~20.5% EBITDA, structurally above peers, powered by premiumisation and a rich export/3W mix.
  • Unmatched export franchise — #1 Indian 2W/3W exporter, ~44% of volume, a second demand engine that cushions domestic softness.
  • Premiumisation ladder — Pulsar + KTM + Triumph + Husqvarna capture customers as they trade up; premium billing +70% in FY26.
  • 3W / CNG fortress — ~88% of CNG three-wheelers; a defensible, high-margin cash engine most investors under-appreciate.
  • Fortress balance sheet — debt-free, ~100% payout, the cash to fund KTM, EVs and buybacks at once.
  • EV credibility — Chetak is a genuine #2 in e-scooters, won without torching the balance sheet.

The bear case

  • Emerging-market / FX risk — ~44% export exposure means a Nigeria, Egypt or LatAm currency shock can knock out a whole region's volume for quarters.
  • KTM overhang — a newly-acquired, loss-prone European premium maker in a weak global bike market; the €590m "profit" was a one-off restructuring gain, not operating earnings.
  • EV margin dilution — winning EVs may mean structurally lower margins than the premium petrol bikes that define Bajaj's edge.
  • Domestic cyclicality — Indian 2W demand is tied to the stretched rural/first-time buyer; soft monsoons, inflation and finance availability all bite.
  • Rich multiple — ~26× on a cyclical maker leaves little margin for error if exports, KTM or EVs disappoint.
The bottom line

Bajaj Auto is a structurally superior, financially pristine 2W/3W maker — the rare auto company that earns ~20% margins, exports nearly half its output, dominates a high-margin niche (CNG three-wheelers), and returns almost all its profit to owners. The core business risk is modest; the open questions are about its new bets and its export cyclicality. The story over the next few years will be written in four numbers: export volumes (does the engine keep firing through FX shocks?), premium mix & margin (does premiumisation keep lifting profit?), KTM's operating EBITDA (does the rescue become a third engine or a drain?), and EV share-vs-margin (can Bajaj win EVs profitably?). Get those right and a best-in-class compounder justifies its premium; stumble on KTM or a major export market, and a cyclical maker priced for the bets to pay off has room to disappoint.
