Diamond Power Infrastructure, a grid-cables maker brought back from bankruptcy

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Diamond Power Infrastructure makes the wires that make up the electricity grid. Two product families dominate: conductors — the bare, un-insulated aluminium cables you see strung between transmission towers, carrying bulk power across long distances — and insulated power and control cables that run electricity through cities, factories, and distribution networks. It is a company based near Vadodara, Gujarat, with a large plant at Savli. And it carries one of the most unusual back-stories on the exchange: it went bankrupt, was taken through India's insolvency courts, and was bought and restarted by a new owner — then re-rated dramatically as it ramped production into a booming grid-cables market.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a turnaround built on someone else's ruins. The original Diamond Power defaulted on its debt and was admitted to India's Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process in 2018. A new promoter group — GSEC Ltd with the Rakesh Ramanlal Shah family — won the asset through the courts, cleaned up the old debt, and restarted operations at the end of 2022. Almost everything about the investment case dates from after that restart, not before.
  • It sells into a genuine grid capex boom. India is spending heavily on transmission lines, distribution upgrades, and — critically — new grid to evacuate power from solar and wind farms that are usually built far from where electricity is consumed. That demand lifts the entire cables-and-conductors sector: Polycab, KEI, RR Kabel, Apar, Havells all riding the same wave. Diamond Power is a small, fast-ramping newcomer inside that wave. FY26 revenue rose ~71% to ~₹1,910 Cr; the order book stands near ₹3,498 Cr.
  • The stock prices in success that hasn't happened yet. With a market value near ₹15,500 Cr against FY26 profit of only ~₹158 Cr, Diamond Power trades at roughly 100 times earnings (approx.) — a valuation that assumes the ramp keeps compounding for years. The whole debate is whether execution can catch up to the price.
In plain terms

Strip away the story and Diamond Power is a metal-and-plastic factory that turns copper and aluminium into the wires an electricity grid is physically built from. What makes it interesting is not the product — plenty of firms make cables — but the sequence: a bankrupt plant, bought cheaply out of the courts, dusted off and switched back on right as India started building grid at a furious pace. The bet is that a rebuilt, under-utilised asset can fill up with orders and climb into higher-value products faster than its share price has already assumed. The risk is that the price ran ahead of the proof.

01

The grid's wires 101: conductors versus cables

No engineering degree needed — just the difference between the two things this company makes.

Electricity has to travel from where it is generated — a coal plant, a solar park, a wind farm — to where it is used. That journey happens over two very different kinds of wire, and Diamond Power makes both. Get the distinction and the whole business becomes legible.

The two families of wire — and where each one lives
ProductWhat it is (and why it matters here)
ConductorA bare, un-insulated metal cable — usually aluminium, sometimes reinforced with a steel core (ACSR) or made of aluminium alloy (AAAC). These are the sagging lines you see strung between big lattice transmission towers. They carry bulk power over long distances in the open air, where the air itself is the insulation. Diamond Power makes conductors from aluminium wire rod up through finished ACSR/AAAC.
Power cableAn insulated cable — a metal core wrapped in layers of plastic/polymer insulation and armour — so it can run safely underground, through walls, and inside crowded cities. Rated by voltage: LT (low tension), HT (high tension), and EHV (extra-high-voltage, the premium end). Used for distribution, industry, and utility networks.
Voltage (kV)Kilovolts — the "pressure" the cable is rated to carry. Higher voltage = harder to make, more insulation engineering, fewer competitors, better margins. Diamond Power's cable capability now reaches up to 400 kV at the EHV end.
T&D"Transmission & Distribution" — the whole network of towers, lines, substations and cables between the power plant and the socket. The customer for everything Diamond Power sells. When India spends on T&D, this is where the money lands.
Wire rodThe raw drawn metal — aluminium or alloy rod — that both conductors and some cable cores are made from. Diamond Power makes its own rod, which lets it control cost and quality upstream rather than buying it in.
The one idea that organises the whole sector: value climbs with voltage

A tonne of aluminium drawn into a plain overhead conductor is a commodity — many firms can make it, and margins are thin. That same metal, wrapped in precisely engineered layers of insulation and rated to carry 220 or 400 kilovolts underground, becomes a specialised product that only a handful of factories in India can produce, and it commands a far richer margin. So the entire cables-and-conductors industry is a ladder: the money is made by climbing from bare conductors and LT cables up toward HT and EHV. Diamond Power's whole growth thesis is that a rebuilt plant can climb that ladder faster than the market expects.

Who buys this stuff

  • State transmission & distribution utilities — the government-owned bodies (state discoms, Power Grid, state transcos) that own the actual grid and buy conductors and cables to build and repair it. The bedrock customer.
  • Private grid & renewable developers — companies like Adani Energy Solutions building transmission lines and the connections that carry solar and wind power to the grid. A fast-growing buyer as private capital enters transmission.
  • EPC contractors & industry — the construction firms that build substations and lines, plus factories, metros, and infrastructure projects that need power cabling. Often the intermediary between Diamond Power and the end utility.
02

What Diamond Power actually makes

One factory complex, a spread of T&D products — read this once and the company clicks into place.

Diamond Power's plant at Savli is set up to be an integrated T&D manufacturing site — it takes metal in at one end and turns out grid hardware across several product lines. That integration, from wire rod through finished cable and conductor, is the operational spine of the business.

The product lines, decoded

  • Conductors — bare overhead lines: ACSR (aluminium conductor, steel-reinforced), AAAC (all-aluminium alloy conductor), and specialty conductors, drawn from in-house aluminium and alloy wire rod. This is the higher-volume, more commoditised end — but essential scale and cash flow, and the natural entry product for a plant restarting operations.
  • Power & control cables — the insulated range: low-tension and high-tension cables, and increasingly extra-high-voltage (EHV) cable up to 400 kV, plus control, instrumentation, fire-resistant, solar-DC and EV-charging cables. This is where Diamond Power is deliberately pushing, because voltage is where margin lives. The company markets its cable range under the "Dicabs" brand and exports it as well as selling at home.
  • Transmission towers & turnkey supply — steel lattice towers and the ability to package products together for T&D projects. This lets Diamond Power bid for larger, bundled scopes rather than selling a single component into someone else's project.
Why "integrated" is the operational bet, not just a brochure line

Making your own wire rod, then your own conductor, then your own cable on one site does two things. It lets you keep the margin at each step instead of paying it away to a supplier — important when metal is 60-70% of your cost. And it lets you offer a utility a wider slice of a project, which wins bids and builds stickiness. The flip side is that an integrated plant only pays off when it is running full: a half-utilised integrated line carries all the fixed cost of the whole chain while earning on only part of it. For a company restarting a plant from cold, filling that capacity is not a detail — it is the entire operating story of the next few years.

03

The resurrection: from default to a courtroom rebirth

The defining fact about this company — and the one most investors underestimate.

Diamond Power is not a young company. It was founded in 1970 and spent decades as an established cables-and-conductors maker. But the business you can buy today is effectively a second incarnation, separated from the first by a full corporate death. Understanding that break is the single most important thing about the stock.

The original Diamond Power over-borrowed and could not service its debt. In August 2018 it was admitted to the Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP) under India's Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code — the country's bankruptcy machinery, run through the National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT). In practice that meant the old owners lost control, an insolvency professional took charge, and the company's assets — chiefly that large Savli plant — were put up for a court-supervised rescue. Bidders competed to take over the company by proposing a "resolution plan": how much they would pay creditors, and how they would revive the business.

In June 2022, after years of process, the NCLT approved the winning plan submitted by GSEC Ltd in consortium with Rakesh Ramanlal Shah. The new owners settled the legacy creditors at a fraction of the old debt, took clean control of the plant, and restarted operations in December 2022 — running at first with only partial utilisation of the infrastructure they had inherited. The listing on NSE and BSE continued through it all, which is why an old scrip code (BSE 522163) now sits on what is, commercially, a fresh company.

A genuine surprise: the fast-rising "grid-cables super-cycle" stock that momentum investors have piled into is, quite literally, a company that went bankrupt and was reassembled inside a courtroom. Its clean balance sheet and low legacy cost base are not the product of good management alone — they are partly a gift of the insolvency process, which wiped away the old debt. That is a real structural advantage and a real reason to scrutinise the operating track record, which is only a few years long.
04

Who controls it now

A new promoter group with a very short public track record at this company.

Because the whole thesis rests on execution by the people who bought the company out of insolvency, the ownership question matters more here than at a typical established firm. The buyers become the whole story.

  • The promoters: the acquiring consortium — GSEC Ltd, the Rakesh Ramanlal Shah family and associated entities (including Monarch-linked investment vehicles) — hold the controlling stake. Promoter holding sits very high, around 84% (approx., mid-2026), which is common for a recently-resolved company but leaves a small public float that can amplify share-price moves in both directions.
  • What they inherited: a large, ~110-acre integrated cable-and-conductor plant at Savli, near Vadodara, Gujarat — built by the old company, valuable, and largely idle when they took over. The acquisition thesis was essentially to buy this asset cheaply through the courts and fill it with orders.
  • What to watch on governance: a freshly-resolved company scaling revenue at 70%+ a year is exactly the profile where investors should watch related-party dealings, receivables quality, promoter-pledge levels, and auditor commentary closely. The company has drawn routine exchange compliance queries (for example, an NSE cautionary note on an annual secretarial compliance report for FY25) — not unusual for a company rebuilding its processes, but a reminder that the governance apparatus is still maturing alongside the operations.
Why the owner's track record is the balance sheet

At an established company, you can lean on a long history of how management behaves in good times and bad. Here you cannot — the operating record under the new owners runs only from December 2022. So the promoter group's willingness to reinvest cash rather than extract it, to keep receivables clean as sales balloon, and to build the higher-voltage capability they have promised is not a side issue. It is the core variable. In a normal company you underwrite the numbers; in a post-insolvency ramp like this one, you are also underwriting the people, because there is not yet enough history to do otherwise.

05

The T&D super-cycle: why the whole sector is booming

The tailwind that lifts every cable and conductor maker — and why it looks durable.

Diamond Power's ramp is not happening in a vacuum. It coincides with the strongest wave of grid investment India has seen in a generation. Understanding why the grid needs so much new wire explains why demand for conductors and cables is running hot across the whole industry.

The insight that makes this cycle sturdier than a one-off spending spree

The reason the grid boom looks durable rather than fleeting is that it is chained to the renewables build-out, which has its own long runway of national targets. You cannot connect a solar park without the transmission line to carry its power — the wire is not optional, it is the physical precondition for the clean-energy capacity the country has already committed to adding. So every gigawatt of solar and wind that gets sanctioned drags a fixed quantum of conductor and cable demand behind it, years in advance. That linkage is what turns a cyclical construction spend into a multi-year structural pull, and it is why an entire cohort of listed cable makers has re-rated at once.

The catch, which Diamond Power's own valuation makes vivid, is that a boom visible to everyone invites everyone to expand. The tailwind is real; so is the risk that the whole industry over-builds capacity into it — a tension we return to in the rivals and risks sections.

06

Capacity, ramp and utilisation — the crux

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a resurrected plant.

For most cable makers you can start with the order book. For Diamond Power you must start one step earlier, with a question a normal company never has to ask: how full is the plant? The company inherited a large, capable factory that was sitting largely idle. The entire value proposition is the closing of the gap between the capacity that already exists and the capacity that is actually being used — and then the margin that comes as that used capacity shifts toward higher-voltage products.

Because the plant was acquired out of insolvency at a low asset base, every incremental tonne of output earns a return on capital that a greenfield rival — who paid full price to build — cannot match. Fill the plant (volume), climb to EHV (margin), and the return on the rebuilt asset can look exceptional. All three levers must keep working for the valuation to hold.

This is the whole company in one frame. A greenfield cable maker has to raise capital, build a plant, and then fill it — a slow, expensive path. Diamond Power skipped the build: it bought a finished, capable plant cheaply through the courts and only has to fill and upgrade it. That is why its revenue can grow 70% in a single year off a low base — it is monetising latent capacity that already existed, not laying new concrete. And because the asset base is low, the profit thrown off as the plant fills translates into an unusually high return on capital employed.

The frame that fits only a resurrected plant: existing capacity × utilisation × mix

Track this company with three numbers stacked in order. First, how much of the inherited capacity is running — a plant at 40% utilisation has enormous headroom to grow revenue with almost no new investment. Second, where in the voltage ladder that output sits — as the mix tilts from bare conductor toward 220 and 400 kV cable, the same tonnes earn a fatter margin. Third, the return the rebuilt asset generates — the payoff of having bought the factory for a fraction of its replacement cost. The bull sees all three compounding for years; the bear sees a plant that eventually fills up, a mix that plateaus, and a return that normalises once the cheap-asset advantage is fully harvested. The reported numbers each quarter tell you which path it is on.

The order book: proof the demand is real

Where the revenue of the next two years is already sitting.

A ramp story needs demand to feed it, and Diamond Power's order book is the evidence that the capacity it is filling has real buyers behind it. The backlog stood near ₹3,498 Cr — comfortably more than a full year's FY26 revenue — giving visibility into the growth the company is guiding toward.

The customer names matter as much as the number. Through FY26 the company reported a stream of fresh awards from marquee grid buyers — including multiple orders from Adani Energy Solutions, among them supply tied to the giant Khavda renewable-energy project in Gujarat (an order of roughly ₹276 Cr), plus other transmission-linked awards. Winning repeat business from a demanding, well-funded private transmission developer is a meaningful validation for a company only a few years into its restart: it says the product quality clears a high bar, not just that the price was low.

08

Climbing to EHV: the margin ladder

Why the push from bare conductor toward 400 kV cable is the margin story.

Filling the plant grows revenue. Climbing the voltage ladder grows margin. These are two different levers, and Diamond Power is pulling both — but the second is the one that separates a durable business from a commodity volume play.

Recall the organising idea from section 01: value rises with voltage. Bare conductors and LT cables are made by many firms and priced accordingly. Extra-high-voltage (EHV) cable — the 220 kV and 400 kV product that carries bulk power underground — is a different game. It demands precise insulation engineering, testing infrastructure, and a proven track record, which is why only a small set of Indian factories can make it. Diamond Power has invested to extend its capability up to 400 kV and added medium-voltage cable lines, deliberately shifting its mix upward. That is the mechanism behind FY26's margin expansion — EBITDA margin rose toward ~12%, up sharply from the low levels of the early restart, as premium products entered the mix.

Why voltage is a moat and volume is not

Any competitor with capital can build a conductor line and sell bare aluminium into the same tenders — that is why the low end stays competitive and margins there stay thin. The barrier at the EHV end is not money; it is qualification. A utility will not put an untested 400 kV cable in the ground, so a maker must build the capability, prove it, and win reference orders before the volumes flow — a process measured in years, not months. Once through that gate, though, the competition thins dramatically and the pricing improves. Diamond Power's climb toward 400 kV is therefore not just a product-line addition; it is an attempt to build the one part of a cables business that is genuinely hard to copy. Whether it fully clears that qualification gate at scale is one of the real open questions in the story.

The nuance to hold: reaching 400 kV capability is not the same as shipping 400 kV cable in volume and being paid a premium for it. The capability is the entry ticket; the payoff shows up only when EHV becomes a meaningful, growing share of the sales mix. Watch the product-mix disclosure, not just the marketing claim about maximum voltage.

09

Metals and working capital: the twin cost realities

The unglamorous mechanics that can make or break a fast-growing cable maker.

Two features of this industry sit beneath every headline number and deserve their own section, because for a company scaling as fast as Diamond Power they are where things quietly go right or wrong: the price of metal and the hunger for working capital.

Metal is most of the cost

Copper and aluminium make up the large majority of a cable or conductor's cost. That has two consequences. First, margins are sensitive to metal prices — when copper or aluminium spikes, a maker who has not priced or hedged carefully gets squeezed. Good operators pass metal costs through to customers (many contracts are structured to allow this) and make their real margin on conversion, engineering and mix, not on the metal itself. Second, it means a large part of "revenue growth" in a rising-metal year is simply higher input prices flowing through — which is why volume and mix matter more than the top-line rupee figure alone.

Growth eats cash

A cable maker growing revenue 70% a year has to buy far more metal, hold far more inventory, and extend far more credit to customers — all before it collects. That is the working-capital trap, and it is the same category of pressure (though not the same specific failure) that sank the original Diamond Power. The warning light to watch is receivable days: Diamond Power's debtor days lengthened over FY26 (from the high-60s toward the low-80s of days), a normal side-effect of rapid growth and selling to slow-paying utilities, but precisely the metric that tips from "manageable" to "dangerous" if it keeps climbing. This is also why the balance sheet carries some net debt (roughly ₹430-575 Cr through recent periods) despite strong profit growth — funding the ramp absorbs cash.

Why the cash-conversion cycle is the real health check

Reported profit can look wonderful while a fast-growing manufacturer is quietly starving for cash, because the profit is tied up in unsold inventory and uncollected bills rather than sitting in the bank. For a company whose predecessor died of exactly this ailment, the honest health check is not the profit line — it is the cash-conversion cycle: how many days of cash are locked up in inventory and receivables minus what suppliers fund. If Diamond Power can grow revenue while holding that cycle steady, the ramp is healthy and self-funding. If receivable days keep stretching and debt keeps climbing to plug the gap, the growth is being bought with borrowed working capital — the oldest and most avoidable way for a booming cable maker to come unstuck.

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, well-capitalised field — all expanding into the same boom.

Diamond Power is a small player in a sector full of large, established, well-run companies — and that context cuts both ways. The boom is big enough to lift a newcomer, but the incumbents are formidable and every one of them is adding capacity into the same tailwind.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two forces are in tension. On one side, the T&D boom is large enough that a small, cheaply-built, under-utilised plant can grow fast simply by filling up — it does not need to steal share from Polycab to double its revenue. On the other side, the very visibility of that boom has every one of these well-funded incumbents adding capacity at once, and a sector that all expands into the same demand at the same time eventually risks oversupply. If capacity across the industry outruns the grid's actual buying, pricing softens — and the smallest, newest, most richly-valued player has the least cushion to absorb it. Diamond Power's protection against that outcome is climbing fast enough up the voltage ladder that it competes on capability, not just on price.

The one metric that settles the thesis

At ~100× earnings, Diamond Power is not priced as a cheap turnaround; it is priced as a company that will keep compounding. So the figure that proves or breaks the case is the trajectory of EBITDA margin alongside capacity utilisation and the EHV mix — the three read together. Rising utilisation with a rising margin means the plant is filling and trading up the voltage ladder, which is the only combination that justifies the multiple; it shows the growth is quality, not just tonnes of commodity conductor bought with metal inflation. A margin that stalls or slips even as revenue climbs would signal the opposite — that the company is buying growth at the low-value, price-competitive end, filling the plant but not earning its premium. Utilisation tells you the plant is filling; the margin path tells you whether it is filling with the right products. That is the scoreboard.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A rebuilt asset in a real boom — asked to justify a price that already assumes it wins.

Diamond Power enters the years ahead with everything a bull could ask for: a capable plant bought cheaply, a full order book, a booming end-market, and a clear path up the voltage ladder. What it has not yet done is prove — over a long enough stretch and through a full cycle — that a few-year-old management team can execute a fast ramp cleanly while the whole industry expands around it. The valuation assumes it will.

The bull case

  • Cheaply-acquired, under-utilised asset — a capable plant bought out of insolvency at a low base; growth comes from filling it, not building it, so returns on capital can be exceptional.
  • Structural T&D super-cycle — renewables evacuation, distribution upgrades and rising demand pull conductor and cable volumes for years.
  • Margin ladder to climb — the push to 400 kV EHV cable and richer mix lifts margins above the commodity-conductor floor.
  • Demand-led backlog — ~₹3,498 Cr order book with marquee names like Adani Energy Solutions validates the ramp.
  • Clean legacy balance sheet — the old debt was wiped in the resolution, leaving a fresh capital structure to grow into.

The bear case

  • Execution risk is the whole game — a post-insolvency team with a short track record scaling revenue 70%+ a year; the defining risk, and it cannot be verified yet.
  • Extreme valuation — ~100× earnings prices in years of flawless compounding; any stumble in the ramp can trigger a severe de-rating.
  • Sector-wide capacity glut risk — Polycab, KEI, RR Kabel, Apar and Havells all expanding into the same boom; oversupply would squeeze the smallest, priciest player hardest.
  • Metals & working capital — copper/aluminium volatility plus stretching receivable days and rising net debt — the exact pressure that felled the old company.
  • Governance & concentration — high ~84% promoter holding, thin float, maturing processes, and customer concentration in a few large buyers.
The bottom line

Diamond Power is a rare thing: a company that literally died and was rebuilt inside a courtroom, then switched back on at the precise moment India began wiring itself for a renewables-powered grid. The asset is real, the boom is real, and the early ramp — revenue nearly doubling, margins climbing toward the low teens — is real. What is not yet proven is that a management team only a few years old can keep filling and trading up that plant, cleanly and self-funded, while five larger rivals expand into the same demand. The share price has already awarded the company the victory. The next several quarters will show whether it earned it, in one checkable pattern: capacity utilisation rising while EBITDA margin rises with it. If the plant fills with 400 kV cable rather than commodity conductor, the story compounds; if it fills with tonnes bought on stretching credit, the resurrection becomes a cautionary tale about paying tomorrow's price for yesterday's bankruptcy.
