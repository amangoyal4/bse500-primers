00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

The listed company is legally Schloss Bangalore Limited, but it trades and markets itself as The Leela Palaces, Hotels and Resorts — the operator of India's most expensive hotel rooms. It owns and runs a small, deliberately exclusive collection of five owned "palace" hotels (Bengaluru, Chennai, New Delhi, Udaipur, Goa) plus a wider set of managed and franchised Leela hotels — roughly 13–14 operating hotels and ~3,500 keys in India, and over 5,200 keys once the pipeline is counted. Its controlling shareholder is not a hotelier at all: it is Brookfield, one of the world's largest alternative-asset managers, which still owns about 76% after taking the company public in June 2025.

Three facts frame the whole company:

  • It sits at the very apex of the Indian market. The Leela's owned palaces command the highest room rates in the country — its blended average room rate ran near ₹19,000 (approx) in FY26, and the company markets a RevPAR premium of roughly 1.7–1.8× the broader Indian luxury segment. Revenue rose ~15% to ~₹1,527 Cr, EBITDA margin sat at an eye-catching ~49%, and reported profit leapt to ₹403 Cr from ₹48 Cr a year earlier.
  • The share of the story is the balance sheet, not just the hotels. This was a heavily indebted, asset-heavy portfolio. The 2025 IPO raised ~₹3,500 Cr, and roughly ₹2,500 Cr of fresh money went straight to repaying debt. Net debt fell from ₹2,568 Cr to ₹1,271 Cr in a single year, the interest bill shrank, and the profit line inflected. Deleveraging is not a footnote here — it is the plot.
  • It is asset-heavy by design, which is both the moat and the risk. Unlike asset-light peers who mostly manage other people's hotels for a fee, The Leela owns its crown jewels outright. You cannot build another Leela Palace Udaipur on Lake Pichola — that irreplaceability is the moat. But owned palaces carry heavy fixed costs and real capital intensity, and until 2025 they carried a punishing debt load too.
In plain terms

The Leela is the top shelf of Indian hospitality — a handful of palaces run for the guest who will pay ₹30,000, ₹50,000, sometimes far more, for a single night, and who books on reputation rather than price. Because those palaces are owned, The Leela keeps all of the room revenue and all of the wedding, banquet and restaurant spend that flows through them — but it also carries all of the fixed cost and, historically, a mountain of debt to have built them. Brookfield's plan is simple to state and hard to execute: keep the rooms full at record rates, use the fixed-cost base to convert rising rates into profit, pay down the remaining debt, and bolt on new Leela hotels that others build and Brookfield merely manages for a fee.

01

Palace-hotel economics 101: how the top shelf earns

No hospitality background needed — just the handful of numbers that run a luxury hotel.

A hotel is, in financial terms, a fixed box of rooms with a mostly fixed cost base. The building, the capital tied up in it, the chefs and butlers and gardeners, the electricity keeping a palace lit and cool — all of it runs whether the hotel is full or empty. The hotel earns by filling those rooms with guests who each pay a nightly rate, and by selling them food, weddings, spa treatments and services while they stay. Three numbers describe almost the whole business.

The three numbers that decide a hotel's fortunes
TermWhat it means (and The Leela's rough FY26 figure)
Occupancy (%)The share of available rooms actually sold on a given night. Higher occupancy spreads the fixed cost over more paying guests. The Leela ran roughly the low-to-mid 60s% (approx) — deliberately not chasing 90% by discounting, because holding the rate is the point at the luxury end.
ARR / ADR"Average Room Rate" (or "Average Daily Rate") — the average price of a sold room per night. This is where a palace lives. The Leela's blended ARR was around ₹19,000 (approx) in FY26; its trophy palaces charge well above that, and rates at a property like Udaipur can run into multiples of an ordinary five-star's.
RevPAR"Revenue Per Available Room" = occupancy × ARR. It fuses how full you are with how much you charge into one number — the single most-watched metric in the industry. The Leela's blended RevPAR was roughly ₹12,000+ (approx), and the company frames its palaces as earning a RevPAR premium of ~1.7–1.8× the broader luxury segment.
Why RevPAR is the metric that matters most

Two hotels can run the same occupancy and earn wildly different money, because one charges twice the rate. Two hotels can charge the same rate and earn differently, because one is half empty. RevPAR collapses both into a single figure — revenue per room you could have sold. Grow RevPAR and, because the cost base barely moves, profit grows faster than revenue. That is why every hotel earnings call opens with RevPAR, and why — later in this primer — the owned-palace RevPAR is the one number that will decide whether The Leela's valuation is earned.

Own the palace, or just manage it — two very different businesses

Behind every hotel brand sits a choice that shapes the entire economics — and The Leela's answer is unusual for a modern listing:

  • Owning the hotel — you put up the land and the building (or a long lease and a full fit-out), you carry the capital and the risk, and in return you keep all the room revenue, all the F&B, all the wedding money. This is asset-heavy: slow, capital-hungry, but you own the upside of an irreplaceable location outright. The Leela's five palaces are owned.
  • Managing someone else's hotel — an outside investor builds and owns the property and pays you a management fee (a slice of revenue plus an incentive on profit) to run it under your brand. This is asset-light: little capital, high-margin fee income, and you can grow the brand quickly without funding every building. It is how Marriott and Hyatt became giants.

Most companies coming to market today lean asset-light because investors like the capital efficiency. The Leela is the opposite — its value is concentrated in a few owned trophies. That is what gives it the highest rates in India and total control of the guest experience; it is also what loaded it with debt and fixed cost. The pipeline (Section 08) tilts toward asset-light management to grow the brand without the balance-sheet drag, but the heart of the company remains the owned palaces.

02

The Leela business model: where a rupee actually comes from

A palace is four businesses under one roof — and one of them is bigger here than almost anywhere.

People assume a hotel earns from rooms. Rooms are the engine, but at a palace they are often less than half the story. The Leela earns across four connected streams inside each hotel, and the mix skews unusually hard toward one of them that most first-time readers underestimate: the wedding.

Owned versus managed: where the money is concentrated

The Leela runs two kinds of hotel, and the revenue is lopsided between them:

  • Owned palaces (five of them) — these capture the full room, F&B and wedding revenue and generate the overwhelming majority of the company's revenue and nearly all of its EBITDA. When you read "The Leela earned ₹1,527 Cr," you are mostly reading these five buildings.
  • Managed & franchised hotels — Leela-branded hotels owned by third parties, which pay The Leela a management or franchise fee. This is asset-light fee income: small in absolute rupees today, but high-margin and the intended engine of future growth in the room count without new capital.
The surprise most people miss about a palace's revenue mix

At a conventional business hotel, rooms dominate and F&B is a convenience. At a Leela palace, the wedding and banquet business is disproportionately large — India's ultra-wealthy will book an entire palace for a multi-day celebration, filling every room, every restaurant and every lawn simultaneously at rates a normal hotel never sees. This is why a palace's revenue per available room can dwarf its room rate alone, and why the December-to-March wedding season swings the calendar. It also means demand here is tied not only to the travel cycle but to something quirkier: the size and confidence of India's marrying rich.

03

From distress to Brookfield: the strangest ownership story in Indian hotels

The most interesting thing about this company isn't a palace. It's how a Canadian asset manager came to own India's palaces.

Almost every famous hotel brand in India is controlled by an Indian business house — the Tatas own Taj, the Oberois own Oberoi, ITC owns ITC Hotels. The Leela is the exception. Its palaces were built by an Indian entrepreneur, drowned in debt, and then bought out of that distress by Brookfield, a global alternative-asset manager headquartered in Canada. Understanding that arc explains both the debt story and the deleveraging story.

The founder and the debt

The Leela was the life's work of Captain C.P. Krishnan Nair, a textile entrepreneur who entered hotels late in life and named the brand after his wife, Leela. He opened the first Leela in Mumbai in 1986 and then, through the 2000s, built a string of genuinely spectacular palace hotels — Bengaluru, Udaipur on Lake Pichola, the Chanakyapuri palace in New Delhi, Chennai on the Bay of Bengal. The properties were world-class. The financing was not. The listed vehicle of the era, Hotel Leelaventure, funded this palace-building spree with debt, and by the mid-2010s it was buckling under borrowings of several thousand crore that its cash flows could never service. Captain Nair died in 2014; the debt outlived him.

The Brookfield rescue

In 2019, after a drawn-out debt resolution, Brookfield acquired the core Leela hotels and the Leela brand — the Bengaluru, Chennai, New Delhi and Udaipur palaces, plus the intellectual property — for roughly ₹3,950 Cr in an asset sale. The deal was contested at the time (minority shareholders of Hotel Leelaventure, and rival ITC, objected to the terms), but it went through. Brookfield placed the assets in a new holding company, Schloss Bangalore Limited ("Schloss" is German for "palace" or "castle"), invested further, added the Goa palace, renovated, and set about signing management contracts to extend the brand.

Why this matters for an investor: the palaces you are buying were assembled out of someone else's insolvency by a financial owner whose business is buying, improving and eventually monetising assets. Brookfield is not a hotelier building a dynasty; it is a manager of capital that will, in time, want a return and an exit path. That shapes everything — the aggressive deleveraging, the discipline on capital, and the very fact of the IPO.

The IPO and the register today

In June 2025, Brookfield floated Schloss Bangalore on the NSE and BSE, raising about ₹3,500 Cr in an IPO that combined a fresh issue (new money for the company, used to repay debt) with an offer for sale (Brookfield selling a slice). Brookfield trimmed its holding by roughly a quarter, to about 76%, and the company later adopted the public-facing name The Leela Palaces, Hotels and Resorts. The float that trades in the market is therefore small — a quarter or so of the company — with Brookfield firmly in control and, eventually, an obvious future seller of more stock.

A device you won't find in any other hotel primer

Read The Leela not as a family hotel company but as a Brookfield portfolio asset that happens to be palaces. That single reframing explains the pattern: an aggressive, front-loaded debt paydown (Brookfield hates carrying expensive leverage on a mature asset), a public listing at a rich valuation into a hot hospitality up-cycle (Brookfield monetising), a controlling stake retained for now (Brookfield not yet done), and financial discipline over empire-building (Brookfield answers to its own fund investors). Every strategic move makes sense once you see the owner is a capital allocator, not a hotelier — and it means the biggest questions here (how fast Brookfield sells down, what returns it targets) are fund-manager questions, not hospitality ones.

The Leela runs a tight, ultra-premium portfolio rather than a sprawling one. Its power is concentration and scarcity — a handful of best-in-class palaces rather than hundreds of ordinary hotels. The core is the five owned palaces; a wider ring of managed and franchised Leela hotels extends the brand.

Why "small" is a feature, not a bug

The Leela is a fraction of the size of Taj/Indian Hotels by rooms and revenue, and that is deliberate. Luxury economics reward scarcity and reputation, not room count. A palace that ranks first in its city for guest experience can charge the highest rate in that market and keep charging it. The moat is not scale; it is the near-impossibility of building another lakeside palace at Udaipur, and the pricing power that irreplaceability confers. Five buildings, run flawlessly, can out-earn fifty ordinary ones — and they are far harder for a rival to copy.

05

The 2025 IPO and the interest cliff it removed

The most important event in the company's recent history is not a hotel opening. It is a debt repayment.

This is the section that separates The Leela from every other hotel primer, so it is worth slowing down. The palaces were profitable at the operating line even before the IPO — the problem was everything below that line. An asset-heavy portfolio built with borrowed money carries a large interest bill, and for years that interest swallowed most of what the hotels earned. The IPO's central purpose was to make that bill go away.

What the IPO actually did

  • It raised about ₹3,500 Cr in total, split between a fresh issue (new money into the company) and an offer for sale (Brookfield selling existing shares).
  • Roughly ₹2,500 Cr of the fresh money went straight to repaying and prepaying borrowings — of the company and its subsidiaries.
  • Net debt fell from ₹2,568 Cr at end-FY25 to ₹1,271 Cr at end-FY26 — cut essentially in half in one year — dropping net-debt-to-EBITDA to a comfortable ~1.6× (and, on a trailing basis at one point, far lower).
  • Less debt means a smaller interest bill, and a smaller interest bill flows straight to profit.

The mechanism no other primer can borrow

Run the arithmetic on The Leela's own numbers. Operating EBITDA rose a healthy but ordinary ~19%, to ₹743 Cr. Yet reported PAT went from ₹48 Cr to ₹403 Cr — roughly an eight-fold jump. Growth in the hotels explains only a sliver of that. The bulk is the interest bill collapsing after ~₹2,500 Cr of debt was repaid with IPO cash (approx). In plain English: five palaces earned a bit more money and kept vastly more of it, because they finally stopped paying most of it to lenders. This is why The Leela is, at heart, a deleveraging story wearing a luxury-hotel costume — and why the profit line will keep responding to the pace of further debt paydown as much as to room rates.

The subtlety worth holding onto: because so much of the FY26 profit surge came from lower interest rather than higher operations, the rate of improvement from here slows once the debt is mostly gone. The one-time gift of the IPO — turning interest into profit — happens once. After that, the profit line has to be driven the hard way, by RevPAR. Keep the two engines separate in your head: the balance-sheet engine (largely spent) and the operating engine (the one that must now do the work).
06

The India luxury up-cycle and the supply-starved top end

The demand force behind record rates — and why the very top of the market stays scarce.

The Leela's rising rates are not just good management; they ride a genuine, structural up-cycle in Indian premium travel. Its two halves — booming demand and constrained supply — are why room rates and occupancy have climbed together.

The demand half

  • A richer, more mobile India. A fast-growing affluent class travels more, for both leisure and business, and trades up to luxury. The top end of domestic travel has surged.
  • Weddings, MICE and marquee events. India's big-ticket weddings — a category almost tailor-made for a palace — plus the return of large conferences, corporate offsites and international summits fill room blocks and banquet lawns at premium rates.
  • Inbound luxury travel. Overseas visitors seeking a genuinely world-class Indian experience concentrate on a very small set of trophy properties, of which The Leela's palaces are prime examples.

The supply half — the part that makes it structural

A luxury hotel takes years and enormous capital to build, needs a rare site, and clears high approval hurdles — and a genuine palace effectively cannot be built at all; the heritage that defines it is one-of-one. So supply at the very top responds slowly and reluctantly. Across the luxury segment broadly, demand has been growing faster than new rooms; at the palace tier, new supply is close to fixed. When more affluent travellers chase a slowly growing pool of top rooms, hotels run fuller and raise rates at once — and both feed RevPAR.

Why the up-cycle shows up as fatter margins, not just more sales

This is the crux for a luxury owner. When RevPAR rises because of higher rates rather than just more guests, almost none of it costs extra to deliver — the room, the staff and the palace are already paid for. So a 10% lift in ARR flows to profit at a far higher rate than a 10% lift in an ordinary business's sales would. That is exactly how The Leela sustains a ~49% EBITDA margin — among the very highest in Indian hotels — and why the same five palaces can earn dramatically more in a strong year than a weak one. The up-cycle is real; the question the valuation asks (Section 10) is how much of it is already in the price.

07

Operating leverage: why owned palaces amplify every rate rise

The second engine — distinct from the interest cliff — and the one that must now carry the company.

"Operating leverage" sounds abstract until you attach it to a fixed cost base as heavy as a palace's. Because an owned luxury hotel's costs barely move with occupancy, changes in RevPAR don't move profit proportionally — they amplify it. The Leela's asset-heavy structure, so often described as a weakness, is precisely what makes this amplification so powerful in an up-cycle.

The growth runway: more palaces, mostly on other people's balance sheets

How an owner of five trophies plans to grow the room count without funding every new building.

The Leela's expansion marks a deliberate tilt. The historic model — own the great buildings — produced world-beating palaces but is slow, capital-hungry and (as history showed) dangerous when funded with debt. The plan now keeps the owned crown jewels while adding most new rooms through management and franchise contracts, where an outside investor funds the building and The Leela runs it for a fee.

New Indian destinationsSikkim, Mumbai, Jaisalmer, Ranthambore, Jaipur and moreExtends the estate into heritage, wildlife and metro markets — the wedding-and-experience heartland.

Some new palaces still involve The Leela's own capital — the company committed roughly ₹1,131 Cr to develop several properties — but the strategic centre of gravity is asset-light. Fee income carries very high margins and almost no balance-sheet risk, so a successful managed pipeline can lift returns on capital without adding debt. That matters especially here, where debt is the very thing the equity story is trying to escape.

The strategic trade-off, plainly put

Asset-light growth is how you compound a hotel brand without compounding the capital or the leverage. The cost is control and economics: on a managed hotel The Leela captures a fee, not the full room-and-wedding revenue, and it depends on an owner willing to invest to a palace standard. For a brand whose entire value is uncompromising luxury, that tension is real — Leela must scale its fee business without letting the guest experience that justifies the name slip. And there is a structural point: an asset-light hotel is worth a lower multiple of its fee than an owned trophy is of its EBITDA, so the mix shift changes what the company is, not just how fast it grows. The pipeline is the test of whether Leela can add rooms without diluting the mystique that lets it charge the most in India.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded field of listed hotel companies — with The Leela at the luxury apex but far from the top of the size table.

Indian hospitality spans every model, from asset-owners to pure brand-managers. The Leela competes for the single highest-spending guest in the country, but it does so against much larger domestic groups and the global asset-light giants.

The genuine surprise in this table

India's highest-rate hotel brand is one of the smallest listed luxury operators by revenue — a fraction of Taj/IHCL and roughly half of Oberoi/EIH. Rate and scale are different games entirely: The Leela wins on price per night from a handful of irreplaceable palaces, while IHCL wins on breadth and a vast managed pipeline. For an investor that frames the choice — The Leela is the concentrated, top-of-market, deleveraging bet; IHCL is the diversified, faster-growing-by-count bet; EIH is the debt-free family-owned luxury twin. And uniquely among them, The Leela's controlling shareholder is a financial owner with an eventual selling motive rather than a business house building for the grandchildren.

Regulatory & structural backdrop: hotels sit at the intersection of tourism policy, GST on rooms and F&B, and local approvals for new builds — the last of which is precisely what keeps luxury supply scarce (good for incumbents). At the palace tier, guests are largely price-insensitive, so tariff-related policy noise is light-touch; the bigger structural swing factors are the wedding calendar, inbound-tourism sentiment and the domestic affluent-consumption cycle.
10

Financials & valuation

A high-margin, freshly deleveraged, asset-heavy business — priced richly on a hospitality up-cycle.

The Leela's FY26 financials show two engines firing at once: an operating up-cycle (record rates, ~49% margins) and a one-time balance-sheet reset (debt halved, interest slashed). The valuation debate is whether today's economics — cyclical rates and a spent deleveraging boost — justify a premium multiple on a company that only just started reporting real profit.

How to read the multiple

At ~₹16,200 Cr for ~₹403 Cr of FY26 profit — roughly 40× earnings, ~23–24× EV/EBITDA — The Leela is priced as a scarce luxury franchise in a strong cycle. That price is defensible if two things hold: the up-cycle keeps rates and occupancy rising, and the deleveraging keeps compounding into lower interest and, eventually, free cash flow. The cautious reading is equally clear — much of the FY26 profit leap was a one-time interest saving that does not repeat, the ~49% margin and record ARR may sit near a cyclical high, and paying 40× on newly minted, part-one-off earnings leaves the price leaning hard on the cycle staying benign. Both readings turn on the operating line, because that is the engine that must now do the work the balance sheet did once.

The one metric that settles it: owned-palace RevPAR (occupancy × ARR), read alongside the pace of further net-debt reduction. If owned-palace RevPAR keeps climbing — holding the ~49% margin as new managed keys layer on fee income — while net debt grinds lower and interest keeps falling, then both engines are still running and the multiple is earned. If RevPAR stalls and palaces must discount to stay full, operating leverage reverses on that heavy fixed base and the deleveraging tailwind is already spent — leaving 40× earnings exposed. Watch owned-palace RevPAR and net debt together, quarter by quarter; the headline PAT growth will flatter the picture as the interest saving fades, but RevPAR and the debt line will tell the truth.

The risks, the future, and the bull/bear

An irreplaceable set of palaces, freshly deleveraged, at a genuinely full point in its cycle.

The Leela owns some of the best hotels in India, has just cut its debt in half, and earns industry-leading margins. The investment question is almost entirely about the cycle and the price — and about the residual risks that come with an asset-heavy portfolio and a financial owner.

  • Cyclicality of luxury travel. High-end discretionary spend — leisure, corporate, and above all weddings — is exposed to recessions, travel shocks and geopolitics. The same operating leverage that shines in an up-cycle is brutal in a downturn: a palace still costs a fortune to run when the rooms empty.
  • Asset-heavy balance sheet and residual leverage. Even after halving, ~₹1,271 Cr of net debt remains, and owned palaces are capital-intensive with high fixed costs. The company has escaped a debt trap, not the physics that created it.
  • Concentration. A handful of owned palaces generate the bulk of revenue and nearly all EBITDA — a problem at any one marquee property (a renovation, a local shock, a demand air-pocket) matters disproportionately.
  • Execution on the pipeline. Growth leans on managed and franchised keys, an asset-light model The Leela has not yet proven at scale at a palace-quality bar.
  • Competition. Taj/IHCL's scale, Oberoi/EIH's pedigree, a well-funded ITC Hotels, and global luxury operators all chase the same guest and the same rare sites.
  • Ownership overhang. Brookfield's ~76% leaves a small public float — and a large, motivated future seller. Sell-downs are likely over time; their timing and size will move the stock in ways no spreadsheet predicts.
The scar in the family history: these very palaces bankrupted their previous owner. Nothing was wrong with the buildings — Hotel Leelaventure built world-class hotels — but an asset-heavy portfolio funded with too much debt could not survive when the cycle turned. Brookfield has removed most of that debt, which is exactly why the story looks so different today. But the lesson is permanent: at the luxury end, the balance sheet decides whether a great collection of hotels is a fortune or a trap.

The bull case

  • Irreplaceable trophies — five owned palaces with the highest rate power in India; you cannot build another Leela Palace Udaipur.
  • Structural room shortage — luxury demand outrunning supply, sustaining occupancy and ARR together at the top of the market.
  • Operating leverage — a fixed palace cost base turns rate-led RevPAR gains into outsized profit and a ~49% margin.
  • Deleveraging still in motion — net debt halved to ₹1,271 Cr; every further rupee repaid keeps lifting the profit line toward free cash flow.
  • Asset-light optionality — a managed pipeline (Dubai, Sikkim, Jaisalmer) that can grow the brand and returns without adding debt.

The bear case

  • One-off profit boost — the FY26 earnings leap was largely a spent interest saving, not a repeatable operating gain.
  • Full valuation — ~40× earnings / ~23–24× EV-EBITDA on newly minted, part-one-off profit near a cyclical high.
  • Asset-heavy fragility — high fixed costs and residual debt make a downturn painful; the buildings can't flex with demand.
  • Concentration & execution — a few palaces carry the P&L; asset-light growth is unproven at Leela's quality bar.
  • Brookfield sell-down — a ~76% owner with an exit motive and a thin float; supply of stock is a standing overhang.
The bottom line

The Leela is a bet that India's wealthiest travellers keep wanting the best palaces in the country, that those palaces stay scarce, and that Brookfield keeps grinding the debt down while the rooms stay full at record rates. The balance-sheet gift is mostly given — net debt is already halved, and that engine will fade as it finishes its work. From here the case rests on the palaces themselves: whether owned-palace RevPAR can keep climbing, whether the ~49% margin holds as managed keys dilute the mix, and whether the first international opening on Dubai's Palm Jumeirah and the Sikkim and Jaisalmer additions prove the brand travels. The next chapters are concrete and datable — the Dubai debut, each quarter's owned-palace RevPAR print, and the next tranche of debt repaid. The palaces are extraordinary and now, at last, unencumbered; the price already assumes they stay both full and getting fuller.
