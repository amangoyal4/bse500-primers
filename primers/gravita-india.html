00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Gravita India is a recycler. Its flagship business is lead recycling: it collects millions of dead lead-acid batteries — the heavy black batteries in cars, trucks, inverters and telecom towers — plus assorted lead scrap, smashes and smelts them, and refines the metal back into clean recycled lead, lead alloys and lead oxides, which it sells to battery manufacturers (the Amara Rajas and Exides of the world) and other industrial buyers. It is India's largest organised lead-recycling company, and around that core it has built three more recycling verticals — aluminium, plastic and rubber — a turnkey business that sells recycling-plant technology to others, and a footprint that spans India, Africa and beyond.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Its profit is a conversion fee, not a metal bet. Gravita buys scrap, converts it, and sells refined metal at a price linked to the London Metal Exchange (LME). It hedges that price exposure, so what it actually keeps is the spread between the scrap it buys and the metal it sells — a roughly fixed rupees-per-kilo margin. That makes it a volume × spread business: tonnes processed and EBITDA-per-kg matter far more than whether lead is expensive or cheap this quarter.
  • It is formalising a backyard industry. Most of India's used batteries have historically been recycled by thousands of tiny, unregulated, often dangerously polluting informal smelters. New Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) rules legally require battery makers to channel end-of-life batteries to authorised recyclers. That regulation is pushing scrap out of the backyard and toward organised players like Gravita — a structural share-gain that has little to do with the economy's cycle.
  • It is a steady compounder, richly rated. FY26 revenue was ~₹4,265 Cr (+10%), with EBITDA ~₹452 Cr and PAT ~₹378 Cr (+21%). Volumes grew ~5%; the extra profit came from a richer value-added and non-lead mix. It runs low-debt with ROCE around 17%, and the stock trades near 35× earnings — a multiple that already prices in years of the circular-economy story playing out.
In plain terms

Gravita is a metals laundromat. You bring it something dirty and finished — a dead battery, a bale of aluminium scrap, a pile of used plastic — and it hands back the clean, reusable material a factory can buy off the shelf, keeping a small, steady fee for the transformation. Because India mines almost no lead of its own, that recycled metal is not a nice-to-have; it is where most of the country's lead actually comes from. The bet is that as regulation drags this enormous, messy, informal industry into the daylight, the biggest organised laundromat with the widest collection net and the most plants keeps taking share — and does it in four metals across three continents, not just one.

01

Recycling 101: why a battery is worth more dead than you'd think

No chemistry degree needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

A lead-acid battery — the one that starts your car and backs up your inverter — is, by weight, mostly lead. When it dies, that lead does not disappear; it just needs to be extracted, cleaned and re-cast. That is Gravita's entire reason to exist. To follow the business you need only a few terms and one very surprising fact.

The only vocabulary you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
Primary vs secondary leadPrimary lead is mined from ore. Secondary (recycled) lead is recovered from scrap — mostly old batteries. Gravita makes secondary lead. Recycling uses a fraction of the energy of mining and emits far less carbon.
Lead-acid batteryThe workhorse battery of cars, trucks, inverters and telecom towers. It is ~60%+ lead by weight and is almost infinitely recyclable — the same lead can be cycled again and again.

The surprise that reframes the whole company

The lead inside a brand-new car battery is, overwhelmingly, recycled lead from old batteries. India mines very little lead of its own, so the great majority of the country's lead demand is met by secondary (recycled) metal — recovered, refined and sold straight back to the battery makers. Gravita is one of the organised firms running that loop at scale: it is, quite literally, turning yesterday's dead batteries into tomorrow's new ones. Recycling here is not the eco-friendly afterthought it is in many industries; it is the main supply chain. That single fact is why a "recycler" can be a structurally advantaged, import-substituting business rather than a low-margin waste handler.

Why recycled beats mined — on cost and carbon

  • Energy & carbon: recovering lead from a battery uses a small fraction of the energy of digging and smelting fresh ore, so recycled lead carries a far lower carbon footprint — the ESG angle is real, not cosmetic.
  • Cost & supply: with almost no domestic lead mines, primary lead would have to be imported. Recycling the batteries already in the country is cheaper and import-substituting — it keeps both the metal and the value at home.
  • Endless loop: because lead can be recycled repeatedly with little loss, every battery Gravita processes feeds the next generation of batteries — a genuinely circular material, not a one-way resource.
02

What Gravita actually sells: one loop, four metals

The recycling value chain — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

Gravita's model is the same loop repeated across materials: collect scrap → smelt → refine into value-added product → sell to industry → the metal comes back as scrap years later. Lead is the flagship (the bulk of volume and revenue); aluminium, plastic and rubber are the growing satellites; and a turnkey arm sells the plant technology itself. Follow the lead loop first — the others are variations on it.

The metal never really leaves the loop — a battery sold today returns as scrap in a few years and re-enters at Stage 1. Gravita's profit is the fee it charges to run the metal around the circle once.

The four verticals, decoded

  • Lead recycling — the flagship, the large majority of volume and revenue. Batteries and lead scrap in; recycled lead, alloys and oxides out. This is the business Gravita has run and refined since 1992, and where its scale and collection network are deepest.
  • Aluminium recycling — the second metal. Aluminium scrap is melted into recycled aluminium alloys sold to autos, castings and other industries. A key plank of the "beyond lead" diversification, though volumes here are more sensitive to scrap and metal prices.
  • Plastic & rubber recycling — the battery casings and other plastic waste are recycled into plastic granules; rubber is a newer add. Smaller today but high-growth, and it makes the core battery-recycling process more complete (the whole battery gets used, not just the lead).
  • Turnkey projects & technology — Gravita designs and sells recycling plants to third parties, turning its own operational know-how into a product. It also seeds new geographies and newer streams (lithium-ion battery recycling, steel, paper) this way.
Why running the whole loop matters

The hard, defensible part of recycling is not the furnace — anyone can buy a furnace. It is getting enough scrap, reliably, at a sensible price, and then refining it into something a fussy customer will pay a premium for. Gravita owns both ends: a formal collection network that feeds the plants, and refining capability that turns plain metal into higher-value alloys and oxides. A backyard smelter can melt a battery; it cannot consistently source at scale, meet a battery-maker's alloy spec, satisfy pollution norms, or hand a customer the EPR paperwork they now legally need. Owning the full loop is what separates an organised recycler from a scrap dealer with a fire.

03

The family, the company, and the three-continent footprint

A Jaipur founder-run business that exported its own model abroad.

Gravita was founded in 1992 in Jaipur by the Saraf family, who built it from a single lead-recycling operation into a multi-metal, multi-country recycler. It remains a founder-led, promoter-heavy business, today run by Rajat Agrawal as Managing Director & CEO, with the promoter group holding roughly 55%+ (approx.). Its defining structural choice was to go where the scrap is — building plants near battery-scrap sources across several countries rather than importing everything to India.

Ownership & footprint

  • Geography: plants and operations across India, Africa and other regions, with a scrap-sourcing reach spanning 70+ countries (approx.). Overseas operations contribute a meaningful minority of revenue and profit (~a quarter, approx.), while a large share of total sales are exported — Gravita is as much a global recycler as an Indian one.
  • Balance sheet: run conservatively for a capital-hungry industrial — a low debt-to-equity (~0.3×, approx.), ROCE around 17% and ROE near 17%, giving it room to fund the aggressive capacity expansion largely from internal cash and modest borrowing.
Why "go to the scrap" was the pivotal decision: a recycler's single biggest constraint is scrap availability. By planting operations in Africa and other scrap-rich regions rather than dragging every battery back to Jaipur, Gravita secured feedstock near the source, cut logistics, and gained access to markets that import lead. The multi-country footprint is not a vanity map — it is the supply-security strategy, and it is unusually hard for a domestic-only rival to copy quickly.
04

The spread engine: why the lead price barely matters

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — and it is specific to how Gravita earns.

Newcomers assume a lead recycler is a leveraged bet on the lead price — buy when lead is cheap, sell when it is dear. It is almost the opposite. Gravita's selling price and its buying price both move with the LME, and it hedges the exposure, so the metal price largely washes out. What is left is the conversion spread: the gap between what it pays for scrap and what it sells refined metal for, expressed as a roughly fixed EBITDA in rupees per kilogram. Multiply that spread by tonnes, and you have the profit engine.

Because Gravita is paid a fee to convert, a company processing ~230,000+ tonnes a year is really selling ~230 million kilos of conversion — so every ₹1/kg change in the blended spread is worth on the order of ~₹23 Cr of EBITDA, regardless of where lead trades.
The device that only fits Gravita: volume × spread, not price × position

Here is the test that reframes everything. Take Gravita's ~230,000+ tonnes of annual volume — call it ~230 million kilos. At a blended spread of roughly ₹15–20 per kilo, the whole company's operating profit is essentially kilos processed × rupees kept per kilo. That means the two levers that actually move the business are (1) more tonnes — collecting and processing more scrap — and (2) a fatter spread per kilo, earned by shifting mix toward higher-value alloys, oxides and non-lead metals. The LME lead price, up or down, is a rounding error to a hedged converter: it inflates or deflates both revenue and cost together. So do not track "is lead up?" — track tonnes and EBITDA-per-kg. Every quarter's story is written in those two numbers.

This is also why the FY26 result looked the way it did: volumes grew ~5% but revenue grew ~10% and PAT ~21%. The extra profit did not come from lead rallying — it came from a richer value-added mix (a larger share of alloys and oxides rather than plain metal) widening the spread per kilo. A pure metal-price story could not produce that pattern; a spread-and-mix story does.

05

Formalising a backyard industry

The structural share-gain that has nothing to do with the economic cycle.

To understand Gravita's growth, you have to picture the industry it is taking share from. For decades, the great majority of India's used batteries have been recycled not by companies like Gravita but by a vast, fragmented network of informal, unregulated backyard smelters — men melting battery scrap in makeshift furnaces, often with no pollution control, no worker safety and no tax. That informal sector still handles a large slice of the country's battery scrap. The investment story is the transfer of that scrap from the backyard to the organised sector — and regulation is now forcing it.

Illustrative, directional — the point is the direction, not precise percentages. As EPR compliance tightens, scrap that once flowed to unregulated smelters is legally redirected to authorised recyclers, and the organised share climbs. Gravita grows even if the total pool of batteries stays flat, simply by taking share from the informal sector.

The mechanism is EPR — Extended Producer Responsibility. Under the Battery Waste Management framework, the maker of a battery is now legally on the hook for ensuring a matching quantity of end-of-life batteries is collected and recycled through authorised recyclers, evidenced by tradable certificates. A battery manufacturer can no longer look the other way while its dead products vanish into the backyard; it needs paperwork from a licensed recycler. That paperwork is something Gravita can supply and an informal smelter cannot.

Why this is a rare, cycle-proof growth driver

Most industrial growth stories depend on the economy — more cars, more construction, more demand. This one does not. Even if India bought exactly the same number of batteries next year as this year, Gravita could still grow briskly simply because regulation is reallocating existing scrap from unregulated smelters to authorised ones. It is a share-shift, not a demand-shift — and share-shifts driven by law tend to be durable and one-directional. Add the underlying growth in batteries (more vehicles, more inverters, more telecom and data-centre backup) on top, and you get two engines pulling the same way: a bigger scrap pool and a rising organised share of it.

The catch worth naming: the informal sector persists precisely because it is cheap — it dodges taxes, safety costs and pollution norms, so it can sometimes outbid organised players for scrap. Formalisation is real and regulator-backed, but it is a grind, not a switch. The pace at which EPR enforcement actually bites — and how much scrap-price inflation Gravita must swallow to win feedstock away from cheaper informal buyers — is one of the genuine variables in the story, not a settled win.
06

Beyond lead: the four-metal expansion

Why the company is deliberately diluting its own lead concentration.

Lead is the flagship — the large majority of volumes and revenue — but management is intentionally building the business away from single-metal dependence. The Vision 2028 plan explicitly targets a bigger non-lead share (aluminium, plastic, rubber and newer streams) and a bigger value-added share. FY26 pushed that agenda forward with new metals and plants.

Why deliberately reduce the lead share?

Two reasons. First, risk: a business that is ~85% one metal is hostage to that metal's scrap availability, spread and regulation. Spreading across aluminium, plastic, copper and lithium smooths the ride. Second, runway: the same collect-smelt-refine-sell playbook, and the same formalisation tailwind, apply to every recyclable material — so each new metal is a fresh, large, informal market to organise, using capabilities Gravita already has. The lithium-ion piece is the furthest out but the most tantalising: as India's first big wave of EV and storage batteries eventually dies, someone has to recycle them, and Gravita wants to be the organised recycler already in the room. The risk is the mirror image — aluminium, plastic, copper and lithium are different games, each with its own scrap dynamics and execution learning curve.

07

The scrap-collection moat

The genuinely defensible asset — and the reason a rival can't just build a furnace.

If the furnace is a commodity, the collection network is the moat. A recycler's hardest problem is securing enough scrap, reliably, at a workable price — and that is a logistics-and-relationships business built over years, across borders, in a market dominated by informal middlemen. Gravita's edge is the formalised, multi-country scrap-sourcing web it has assembled, and the scale that lets it keep the plants fed.

Consider what it takes to gather feedstock: you need channels to buy dead batteries from thousands of dispersed points — garages, dealers, fleet operators, aggregators — often competing against cash-paying informal buyers who owe no tax and follow no rules. You need it in enough volume to run smelters efficiently. And increasingly you need it documented, so the resulting metal carries valid EPR credentials. Gravita has spent three decades building exactly that network, and reinforced it by putting operations near scrap sources abroad (Africa and elsewhere) rather than relying on a single country's supply.

Anyone can buy a furnace; almost no one can quickly replicate a 30-year, multi-country, formalised scrap-collection network feeding it — and then refine the output to a battery-maker's exact alloy spec with valid compliance. The moat lives at the two ends of the loop, not in the middle.
The moat and the risk are the same sentence: Gravita's advantage is scrap access, which means Gravita's biggest vulnerability is also scrap access. If informal buyers, other organised recyclers, or a scrap shortage bid up feedstock prices, the conversion spread compresses from the input side — the company can be doing everything right operationally and still watch its per-kilo margin thin because it had to pay up for batteries. Volume and spread are the whole game (see §04), and the scrap market is where the spread is won or lost.
08

The circular-economy tailwind

Three structural forces pushing the same direction at once.

Gravita sits at the intersection of three durable, multi-year themes — and unusually, all three push the same way. This is the backdrop that lets a recycler be valued as a growth compounder rather than a cyclical scrap handler.

What's driving organised recycling

The insight that makes recycling different from mining

A miner's growth depends on finding and digging more ore — a slow, capital-heavy, geology-bound process. A recycler's feedstock, by contrast, is created automatically by the economy's own consumption: every battery sold is a future battery to recycle, every car on the road is scrap-in-waiting. The material already exists, above ground, inside the country. So while recycled and mined metal compete on price, the recycler has a structurally easier supply story and a lower carbon footprint and, in India's case, a regulator actively herding scrap toward it. Few industrial businesses get a supply tailwind, an ESG tailwind and a regulatory tailwind at once; organised recycling is one of them.

The industry and the rivals

Who Gravita actually competes with — and it isn't only other recyclers.

Gravita's competitive set is unusual because it spans three very different kinds of rival: the vast informal sector it is taking share from, a handful of other organised recyclers, and the primary-metal producers whose mined output recycled metal displaces. Each competes on a different axis.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two features stand out. First, Gravita's fiercest competitor is not a listed peer — it is the informal sector, and the contest is fought in the scrap yard, on price, every day. That is why formalisation is existential and not merely nice: it is the referee tilting a fair fight in the organised player's favour. Second, some of Gravita's biggest customers — the battery makers — are also its potential competitors if they recycle captively. That dual relationship keeps a natural ceiling on pricing power and is a reason the moat has to live upstream in sourcing and scale rather than in any single customer contract.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A structurally advantaged recycler asked to prove the spread and the diversification hold.

Gravita enters FY27 with the wind at its back: a formalising industry, a low-carbon import-substituting product, a 30-year scrap network, low debt, and a plan to push into copper, lithium and more. The fair debate is not whether recycling is a good business — it plainly is — but whether Gravita can keep widening the spread and executing the multi-metal, multi-country expansion at a pace that justifies a premium multiple, against a stubbornly cheap informal sector.

The bull case

  • Formalisation share-gain — EPR is legally redirecting scrap from backyard smelters to authorised recyclers; a durable, cycle-proof engine.
  • Spread, not price — a hedged, per-kg conversion model means margins ride volume and mix, not volatile metal prices.
  • Widening value-added mix — FY26's profit outran volume ~4× on richer alloys/oxides; Vision 2028 targets more of the same.
  • Scrap-network moat — a 30-year, multi-country collection web that a furnace-buyer cannot quickly replicate.
  • Multi-metal runway — the same playbook and tailwind extend to aluminium, plastic, copper and eventually lithium-ion — several fresh informal markets to organise.

The bear case

  • Scrap-supply & input-cost risk — a cheaper, tax-dodging informal sector and rival recyclers can bid up feedstock and compress the spread from the input side.
  • Metal / FX / inventory whipsaw — hedged and spread-driven, but LME swings, scrap-price moves, FX on overseas operations and inventory timing can still jolt a quarter's margin.
  • Diversification execution — aluminium, plastic, copper and lithium are different games with their own capital and learning-curve risk; new geographies add more.
  • Regulatory / environmental — smelting faces pollution norms and enforcement; EPR itself can be re-shaped, and compliance costs cut both ways.
  • Rich valuation — ~35× leaves little cushion if volume growth or spread disappoints, and battery makers recycling captively cap pricing power.
The bottom line

Gravita has spent three decades quietly building the unglamorous thing that turns out to matter most in recycling — a formalised way to get enough scrap, everywhere, and refine it to spec — and it is now doing that across four metals and three continents just as regulation drags the whole industry toward the organised players it favours. The business is genuinely advantaged; the question is one of degree and price. The next concrete tests are checkable and near: the Mandvi copper plant commissioning and the Phagi lead expansion landing on schedule, the non-lead and value-added share ticking up quarter after quarter, and — above all — EBITDA-per-kg holding firm while volumes compound even as it fights the informal sector for feedstock. Watch those prints; they will tell you whether the circular-economy compounder is widening its spread or merely running faster to stand still.
