00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Hexaware Technologies is an Indian IT-services company — it runs, builds and modernises the technology that large Western enterprises depend on: their software applications, cloud systems, data platforms, cybersecurity and back-office operations. Like its peers, it does most of the work from lower-cost delivery centres in India while staying close to clients in the United States and Europe, and it bills largely in US dollars. In CY2025 it earned $1,537 million of revenue (about ₹13,430 Cr) with a ~17.1% EBITDA margin and ~33,800 employees.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The listing history is the story. Hexaware was publicly listed for years, then delisted and taken private in 2020 by its then-owner Baring Private Equity Asia, sold to US buyout giant Carlyle in 2021 for roughly $3 billion, and re-listed via a ~$1 billion IPO in February 2025 — India's largest IT-services IPO ever and the biggest technology-services listing globally in over a decade. Uniquely, the whole IPO was an offer for sale by Carlyle: not a rupee went to the company.
  • It grows faster than the giants, but earns a bit less per rupee. Hexaware has consistently out-grown large-caps like TCS and Infosys (CY25 revenue up ~7.6% in USD and ~12% in rupees, versus low-single-digit growth at the majors), but its ~17% EBITDA / low-teens EBIT margin sits below the ~24–29% margins of the top tier. It is a growth-and-execution story, not a margin-leadership one.
  • A great business landing in a bad cycle. The stock listed at ₹745 and touched a $5bn+ valuation on day one — then fell to around ₹539 by mid-2026 (down ~29%) as the entire IT-services sector hit discretionary-spend cuts, macro softness and a genuine fear that generative AI could compress the industry's pricing. On top of that sits a Carlyle sell-down overhang: a private-equity owner still holds ~74% and will, eventually, exit.
In plain terms

Think of Hexaware as an outsourced engineering-and-operations department for big foreign companies. A US bank or an American insurer doesn't want to hire thousands of its own programmers to keep its systems modern, migrate to the cloud, wrangle its data, and run routine back-office processing — so it pays Hexaware to do it, cheaper and faster, mostly using skilled Indian engineers. Hexaware's edge is being nimbler and faster-growing than the lumbering giants, with a modern "AI-and-automation-led" pitch. The catch: it's a mid-sized fish in an ocean of much larger sharks, it just re-floated onto the market right as that ocean got choppy, and its private-equity owner is standing at the exit with three-quarters of the shares.

01

IT services 101: how a company like this earns

No tech background required — just the core idea of "labour arbitrage plus expertise."

The Indian IT-services industry — TCS, Infosys, Wipro, HCLTech and dozens of mid-caps like Hexaware — is built on a simple, powerful idea: a global enterprise's technology work can be done remotely, to a high standard, by skilled engineers who cost far less in India than in New York or London. Add domain expertise, process discipline and now automation, and you have a multi-hundred-billion-dollar export industry.

What "IT services" actually covers

  • Application development & maintenance (ADM) — writing new software for a client, and keeping their existing systems running, patched and improved. The bread-and-butter, and still the largest slice of the industry.
  • Cloud & infrastructure — moving a client's systems off their own servers onto Amazon/Microsoft/Google cloud, and running that environment. A big multi-year growth wave.
  • Data & AI — organising a client's data, building analytics and machine-learning, and (increasingly) generative-AI tools. Where the industry's future is being fought.
  • Digital & customer experience — building the apps, websites and digital products a company's customers actually touch.
  • Business Process Services (BPS / BPO) — running operations, not just technology: claims processing, finance-and-accounting, customer support. Hexaware is unusually strong here for its size.
  • Cybersecurity — protecting clients' systems; a fast-growing, higher-value niche Hexaware has been acquiring into.
The money model in one paragraph

An IT-services firm sells people's time and expertise, packaged as outcomes. It hires an engineer in India, bills that engineer's work to a client in dollars at a marked-up rate, and pockets the spread. Revenue therefore comes down to three levers: how many billable people you have, how fully they're utilised (idle staff are pure cost), and the rate you can charge. Because most costs are salaries, and Indian salaries are far below Western ones, the "offshore" model produces healthy margins. Two currency facts matter: you earn in USD but pay most costs in INR, so a weaker rupee helps profits; and clients are mostly American, so US corporate confidence drives demand.

The two things every IT investor watches

  • Growth — is revenue expanding faster or slower than peers? Discretionary tech spending is cyclical; when clients tighten, growth stalls across the whole sector at once.
  • Margin — the gap between the billing rate and the delivery cost, protected by keeping work offshore (cheap India) rather than onsite (expensive client location), keeping utilisation high, controlling attrition and wage inflation, and — the new frontier — using automation and AI to do more with fewer people.
02

The business model: offshore delivery, verticals & platforms

Three design choices explain how Hexaware makes money — read this once and the strategy clicks.

Hexaware's model rests on the classic Indian IT machine — a global delivery model that keeps most work in low-cost India — layered with two things it leans on harder than most: a focused set of industry verticals and a set of home-grown automation/AI platforms it uses to win deals and protect margins.

The global delivery model — where the margin comes from

Every dollar a client pays flows through a delivery pyramid. A small, expensive team sits onsite near the client to manage the relationship; the bulk of the actual building and running happens offshore in India at a fraction of the cost. The higher the offshore mix, the fatter the margin.

The further right you push work — more offshore, more automated — the higher the profit on the same contract. That leftward drag (onsite, manual) is exactly what erodes margins.

The "AI-led / automation-led" pitch and the platforms

Hexaware markets itself as an "AI-first" / automation-led services company, and backs that with three named platforms it puts in front of clients:

  • Tensai® — its automation-and-AI platform: bots and now generative-AI agents that take over repetitive engineering, testing and IT-operations tasks, so a project needs fewer human hours.
  • RapidX™ — a digital-and-cloud transformation framework/studio to speed up modernisation projects.
  • Amaze® — an agentic-AI-powered platform for modernising and migrating applications, data and mainframes to the cloud faster and cheaper. This is the flagship of its GenAI story and a repeated theme in recent results.
Why the platforms matter to the investment case

For a mid-sized firm, home-grown platforms do two strategic jobs. First, differentiation: against giants with far bigger sales armies, Hexaware needs a reason for a client to pick it — "we do this faster and more automated" is that reason. Second, and more subtly, platforms are Hexaware's answer to the GenAI deflation fear (see §07). If AI is going to let the same work be done with fewer people, a services firm's revenue could shrink — unless it captures that productivity itself, sells the platform, and reinvests the savings into winning more work. Hexaware's bet is that being early and loud on "automation-led" turns the AI threat into a share-gain opportunity. Whether that holds is the single biggest open question over the business's story.

The margin trade-off: Hexaware runs a strong Business Process Services arm (BPO-style operations work) and a high share of newer clients — both of which tend to carry lower margins than the pure high-end consulting the top tier does. That's a big reason its EBITDA margin (~17%) sits below TCS/Infosys (~24–29%). The upside: BPS is stickier and grows well; the strategy is to lift margins gradually via automation, better utilisation and higher offshore mix, not to match the majors overnight.

The company and who controls it

A 30-year-old firm on its second trip through the public markets — with a private-equity owner at the wheel.

Hexaware is one of the older names in Indian IT, but it has an unusual ownership journey: founder-built, then owned by two global buyout firms in succession, delisted, and re-listed. Today it is controlled by The Carlyle Group, one of the world's largest private-equity firms, which holds roughly 74% of the shares as the "promoter."

Ownership & management

  • Promoter — Carlyle (through its holding vehicle, historically "CA Magnum Holdings"): ~74% of equity as of early 2026, down from ~95% pre-IPO. This is a financial owner, not a family — its job is ultimately to sell and return money to its own investors.
  • Public float is still thin: after the IPO, institutions (FIIs ~4%, domestic ~15%) and retail hold the remaining ~26%. A small free float can amplify price moves in both directions.
  • Management: led by CEO R. Srikrishna ("Srikrishna Ramakarthikeyan"), a long-tenured leader through the Baring and Carlyle eras, giving operational continuity across the ownership changes.
  • Balance sheet: effectively net-cash at the operating level (~$237 Mn cash at CY25), with strong ~76% cash conversion — a healthy, asset-light services balance sheet.
04

The re-listing and the private-equity overhang

The single most distinctive thing about Hexaware as a stock — and the one an analyst leads with.

Most companies on the market got there once, the ordinary way. Hexaware's defining feature is that it left the market and came back — and that it is still ~74% owned by the private-equity firm that will, one day, sell that stake down. Understanding this "round trip" and the exit overhang it creates is essential to reading the stock.

What actually happened, and why it's unusual

  • Delisting (2020) is the rare part. Big, profitable, listed IT firms almost never go private. Baring did it to run Hexaware without quarterly public scrutiny and reshape it — then handed it to Carlyle in 2021.
  • The 2025 IPO was 100% "offer for sale." In a normal IPO, a company issues new shares and keeps the cash to invest. Here, every rupee went to Carlyle, the seller — Hexaware raised no fresh capital. That's typical of a PE-sponsor exit: the IPO is a way for the owner to start cashing out, not to fund the company.
  • It was record-breaking in size. At ~$1 billion / ~₹8,750 Cr, it was the largest IT-services IPO in Indian history and the biggest technology-services listing worldwide in over a decade — a milestone that generated scarcity buzz on debut.
The overhang, explained: A private-equity fund is not a permanent shareholder — it raises money from its own investors, buys a company, improves it, and must return the cash within a fund's life. Carlyle sold ~21% at IPO and still holds ~74%. Sooner or later it will sell more, in blocks. Each sale adds a large chunk of shares to the market, which tends to cap the price (buyers know supply is coming) and can trigger sharp dips on the days blocks are placed. This "PE selldown overhang" is a structural weight on the stock that ordinary founder-run peers don't carry.
Two ways to read the same fact

The optimistic read: Carlyle spent years professionalising Hexaware — sharpening its verticals, investing in platforms, doubling revenue — and a staged, orderly selldown simply increases the free float over time, bringing in index inclusion and more institutional coverage as the PE stake shrinks. Good companies survive their sponsors leaving. The cautious read: a 100%-OFS listing means the smart, informed owner chose to sell at the IPO price; the remaining ~74% is a multi-year supply of stock hanging over the market; and a fresh-listing scarcity premium can evaporate fast (as it did — the stock fell ~29% from listing to mid-2026). Both are true. The overhang is real, but it is a known, finite pressure that fades as Carlyle exits — not a flaw in the underlying business.

The demand engine: deals, bookings and above-industry growth

How an IT-services firm keeps the pipeline full — and why Hexaware's growth outpaces the giants.

A services firm's future revenue is visible in the deals it signs today. The industry tracks Total Contract Value (TCV) — the full multi-year value of new bookings — and large deals (typically $30 Mn+ contracts) as the leading indicators of growth. Hexaware's calling card is that it has consistently grown faster than most large-cap peers, driven by strong bookings and market-share gains.

Why the mid-caps out-grow the majors

  • Smaller base. Adding $100 Mn of revenue moves a $1.5 bn firm far more than a $30 bn one — the law of large numbers works against the giants.
  • Focus & agility. A tighter vertical set and faster decision-making let mid-caps chase specific deals and pivot to new capabilities (cloud, then data, then GenAI) quicker.
  • Share-gain, not just market growth. Even in a soft market, a nimble mid-cap can win logos and expand accounts at incumbents' expense — Hexaware's healthcare-and-BPS depth is a good example.
  • Acquisitions. Hexaware actively bolts on capability (e.g. cybersecurity via CyberSolve), buying growth and new skills — though acquisitions can dent near-term margins and profit, as they did in Q4 CY25.
Reading the leading indicators

Because contracts are signed now and delivered over years, the numbers that predict Hexaware's revenue are bookings/TCV, large-deal count, and net new-client additions — watch these more than the current-quarter revenue print. A quarter of soft revenue with strong bookings is a coiled spring; strong revenue with weak bookings is a warning. Two supporting gauges: utilisation (Hexaware ran ~81% in Q4 CY25 — high utilisation means demand is being met efficiently) and attrition (~11%, comfortably in the healthy range — low attrition protects delivery and margins). Together these tell you whether the growth engine is genuinely revving or just coasting.

07

The GenAI question: rocket fuel or wrecking ball?

The force reshaping every IT-services firm — and the one Hexaware has bet its positioning on.

Generative AI is the defining strategic question for the entire Indian IT industry, and it cuts both ways at once. It can be a huge new demand wave (every enterprise now wants to "do AI"), and it can be an existential threat (if AI writes and maintains code, a business that sells human coding hours could shrink). How a firm navigates this determines whether it thrives or fades over the next decade — and Hexaware has deliberately planted its flag on the "AI-led" side.

The opportunity

  • A new spending cycle. Enterprises need help building GenAI into their operations — data readiness, model integration, guardrails, custom copilots. That's a fresh, large services opportunity, and modernisation projects (the kind Amaze targets) often precede AI adoption.
  • Doing more with the same people. If Hexaware's own engineers, armed with AI, deliver projects faster, it can take on more work without proportionally more headcount — expanding margins if it keeps the productivity gain.
  • Platform monetisation. Selling Tensai/Amaze-driven outcomes lets Hexaware charge for results rather than pure headcount — a route to breaking the link between revenue and bodies.

The threat

  • Pricing deflation. If a task that took 100 engineer-hours now takes 40, clients will eventually demand to pay for 40 — and competition ensures the productivity gain gets passed on. The industry's fear is a structural deflation of billing.
  • The "linear headcount" model breaks. For decades, growth meant more people. If AI decouples output from headcount, the whole revenue-per-employee model has to be rebuilt — painful for firms slow to adapt.
  • Client insourcing. Better AI tools could let some enterprises do more themselves, shrinking the outsourced pie at the low-complexity end.
Where Hexaware sits in this — honestly

Hexaware's strategy is to be loudly on the front foot: brand itself "AI-first," push Amaze/Tensai hard, and try to capture the productivity wave rather than be flattened by it. For a mid-cap, this is arguably the right posture — being early and differentiated on AI is how a smaller player takes share from slower giants. But two honest caveats. First, everyone is saying this — every IT firm now claims to be AI-led, so the pitch alone isn't a moat; execution and proof are. Second, the deflation risk is real and unresolved industry-wide — nobody yet knows whether GenAI is net-additive or net-deflationary to IT-services revenue over five years. Hexaware's above-industry growth and platform focus put it among the better-positioned mid-caps, but this is the sector's great unanswered question, and Hexaware is fully exposed to whichever way it breaks.

The useful mental model is a three-tier market. The giants (TCS, Infosys) win on scale, brand and the biggest transformation deals, and earn the fattest margins. 11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A quality mid-cap growth story — carrying a private-equity owner and a sector in transition.

Hexaware is a genuinely good IT-services business — above-industry growth, improving margins, strong cash generation, a differentiated AI-led pitch and deep healthcare/BPS niches. The open questions are almost entirely about the cycle, the AI transition, and the Carlyle overhang, not about whether the company can execute.

The bull case

  • Above-industry growth — consistently out-grows TCS/Infosys; a nimble share-gainer with a smaller base and room to run.
  • Margin upside — ~17% EBITDA with clear levers (automation, offshore mix, utilisation) to close the gap with the top tier over time.
  • AI-led differentiation — early, loud positioning with Tensai/Amaze; well-placed to turn GenAI into share gains if it executes.
  • Sticky niches — healthcare/insurance depth and strong BPS give resilient, recurring revenue.
  • Cleaner price now — the ~29% de-rating has removed the fresh-listing froth; ~24× for a grower is defensible.

The bear case

  • PE selldown overhang — Carlyle still owns ~74% and will keep selling; a multi-year cap on the stock and a source of block-deal dips.
  • Sector in a cold cycle — discretionary cuts, macro/BFSI softness and a heavy US/financials skew hit demand directly.
  • GenAI deflation risk — the unresolved threat that AI compresses billing; Hexaware is fully exposed to which way it breaks.
  • Lower margins than the giants — a structurally thinner margin (BPS mix, newer clients) limits profit quality vs top-tier peers.
  • Priced for growth — a premium multiple that still needs above-industry growth to persist; vulnerable if the cycle stays weak.
The bottom line

Hexaware is one of the better mid-cap Indian IT franchises — faster-growing than the giants, sharper on AI positioning, and strong in resilient niches — that had the bad luck (or, for a buyer, good luck) to re-list right as the sector turned. The business risk is modest; the risks that matter are external and structural: the IT spending cycle, the GenAI question the whole industry is grappling with, and the Carlyle stake still hanging over the market. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers — bookings/TCV (is growth still outpacing peers?), margin (is the automation payoff showing up?), and Carlyle's holding (is the overhang clearing?). Get those right and the growth premium is earned; a cold cycle or an AI stumble, and a still-premium multiple has room to fall.
