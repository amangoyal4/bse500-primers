LIC Housing Finance, the big, cheap, LIC-backed mortgage lender

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

LIC Housing Finance (LICHFL) is a mortgage lender — it gives people money to buy or build homes and earns interest on those loans. It is not a bank: it can't take your salary account or your savings deposit. It is a Housing Finance Company (HFC), promoted by and carrying the name of Life Insurance Corporation of India (LIC), the country's giant state-owned insurer. With a loan book of ~₹3.21 lakh crore, it is one of India's largest dedicated home-loan companies.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a spread machine. LICHFL borrows big pots of money (bonds, bank loans, deposits, refinance from the National Housing Bank) at one rate, and lends it out as 15–25-year home loans at a higher rate. The gap — the net interest margin (NIM), ~2.68% in FY26 — is essentially its raw material. Everything about the company comes down to keeping that gap wide and the borrowers paying.
  • Big, steady, and slow. FY26 net profit was ₹5,604 Cr (up ~3%) on a loan book that grew ~4%. This is a mature incumbent, not a fast grower — its core is the plain-vanilla individual home loan (~84% of the book), mostly salaried borrowers, with a small, riskier project/developer book on the side.
  • Famously cheap — for reasons. The stock trades at roughly 0.7× its book value and ~5× earnings, unusually low for a profitable lender. That discount reflects a real debate: sluggish loan growth, a spread squeezed by banks that fund themselves more cheaply, a low return on assets (~1.8%), and a long memory of developer-loan bad debts. The bull says "cheap, huge, LIC-backed, improving asset quality"; the bear says "cheap for good structural reasons."
In plain terms

Think of LICHFL as a wholesale money re-seller. It doesn't have a network of branches collecting cheap savings deposits like SBI or HDFC Bank; instead it raises money in bulk from bond markets and banks, then retails that money to home-buyers at a markup. Its edge is the trust of the LIC name and a strong credit rating that let it borrow relatively cheaply, plus the army of LIC agents who bring in customers. Its problem is that banks can borrow even cheaper — because they hold your current and savings account money almost for free — and are increasingly poaching the best home-loan customers on price. So LICHFL is a solid, safe, boring lender being slowly out-muscled on its home turf, which is exactly why it's so cheap.

01

Housing finance 101: how an HFC actually earns

No finance degree required — just the handful of numbers that drive everything.

A home-loan company is, at its core, a margin business: it makes money on the difference between what it pays to borrow and what it charges to lend. Get five ideas straight and the whole company clicks into place.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and LICHFL's number)
Loan book / AUMThe total money it has lent out and is still owed — its main asset. LICHFL's stood at ~₹3.21 lakh crore at March 2026.
Cost of fundsThe interest rate it pays to raise money (on bonds, bank loans, deposits, refinance). Roughly ~7.5–7.8% for LICHFL. Lower is better.
Lending yieldThe interest rate it charges home-buyers — roughly ~9.5–10%. Higher is better (but competition caps it).
Spread / NIMThe gap between the two, as a % of assets. LICHFL's NIM was 2.68% in FY26. This is the engine of profit — widen it and profits jump; squeeze it and they stall.
Asset quality (Stage 3 / GNPA)The share of loans where borrowers have stopped paying. LICHFL's Stage-3 was 2.16% at March 2026. Bad loans eat straight into profit via provisions.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (Lending yield − Cost of funds) × Loan book − Bad-loan losses − Running costs. So an HFC grows profit in three ways: lend more (grow the book), keep the spread wide (borrow cheap, lend dear), and lose less to defaults (good underwriting). The catch: home loans are the most competitive, lowest-margin lending product in India because everyone wants them — they're big, long, and backed by property collateral, so they rarely go bad. That safety is why the spread is thin. An HFC lives or dies on funding cost and credit discipline, not on flashy growth.

Why an HFC is not a bank — the one distinction that explains the whole stock

  • No CASA. Banks hold your Current and SAvings Account balances and pay you little or nothing on them — near-free money. An HFC has no such deposits; it must buy all its money at market rates. That structural funding disadvantage is the single biggest reason banks can out-price HFCs on home loans.
  • Wholesale-funded. LICHFL raises money in bulk — issuing bonds (NCDs), taking bank term loans, accepting public deposits, and drawing refinance from the National Housing Bank (NHB). It's a re-seller of borrowed money.
  • Regulated differently. HFCs are overseen by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) (with the NHB as the supervisor for housing-specific rules) as a special class of Non-Banking Financial Company. Rules on capital, provisioning and NPA recognition have been progressively aligned with banks.
  • Focused. A bank does dozens of things (payments, cards, corporate loans, deposits). An HFC does essentially one thing — mortgages — so it is a cleaner, more understandable business, but also a more exposed one.
02

The business model: borrow wholesale, lend long, pocket the spread

Follow one rupee from the bond market to a home-buyer and back — the model is that simple.

LICHFL's entire machine can be drawn on one line: it raises money in bulk, converts it into long-dated home loans, and lives on the margin in between. The two things that make its version of this model work are a low funding cost (from a strong credit rating and the LIC halo) and a huge, trusted distribution reach (LIC's agents).

The two edges the LIC name buys

(1) Cheap-ish funding. LICHFL carries a top-tier AAA credit rating — partly on its own book, partly on the comfort that it is the housing arm of LIC. A AAA rating means bond investors lend to it at a low spread over government yields, shaving its cost of funds. In a spread business, a few basis points of funding advantage is worth a lot. (2) Distribution. LIC has one of the largest agent forces on earth. Those relationships, plus LICHFL's own marketing arm of agents and Direct Sales Agents, funnel home-loan customers to the company cheaply — trust that a new fintech lender has to buy with expensive advertising. The flip side: the LIC connection also means the promoter is a government-linked insurer with its own priorities, and LICHFL can never fund as cheaply as a bank that holds free CASA deposits.

03

The company and who controls it

Born from India's biggest insurer, listed for three decades, still ~45% LIC-owned.

LICHFL was set up in 1989 by Life Insurance Corporation of India to channel the insurer's long-term savings into housing — a natural fit, since a mortgage is a long-dated asset and an insurer sits on long-dated money. It listed in the 1990s and grew into one of the country's largest housing lenders. LIC still owns roughly 45%, making the company both blessed and constrained by its parentage.

  • 2000s

  • FY24

  • FY26

04

The loan book, broken into parts

A plain-vanilla home-loan giant with two smaller, spicier side businesses.

Nearly the entire company is one product: the individual home loan. Two adjacent books — individual non-housing loans (mainly loan-against-property, or LAP) and project/developer finance — are small in size but outsized in their effect on risk and yield. Understanding this split is understanding LICHFL's risk profile.

The three books, decoded

  • Individual home loans (~84%) — the bread and butter: 15–25-year loans to families buying or building a home, secured by the property itself. Split between salaried borrowers (the safest, most sought-after segment, where banks compete hardest on rate) and self-employed borrowers (slightly higher risk, slightly higher yield). Low default rates, thin margins, intense competition.
  • Non-housing individual / LAP (~13%) — loan-against-property: an existing homeowner pledges their property to borrow for business or personal needs. Higher yield than a home loan (better spread), but higher risk — the money isn't buying the collateral, so borrowers are more stretched. A key lever LICHFL is using to defend its margin.
  • Project / developer loans (under 3%) — lending to builders to construct housing projects. The highest-yield, highest-risk book: developers are cyclical and cash-flow-fragile, and this is where LICHFL's historical NPA spikes came from. Management has deliberately kept it small (barely growing) while it rebuilds trust in its underwriting.
The strategic tension in one picture

LICHFL faces a genuine dilemma. Its core home-loan book is safe but low-margin, and banks are stealing the best customers on price. To defend its NIM, it is nudging toward the higher-yield books — more LAP, more self-employed and affordable-housing borrowers, and eventually a bigger project book — where the spread is fatter. But those are exactly the books that bit it before. So the whole game is a balance: grow the profitable, riskier segments enough to protect margin, but not so fast or so carelessly that asset quality blows up again. Every quarter, watch the mix shift and the Stage-3 number together — that's the story.

05

The spread squeeze: banks vs HFCs

The single most important force acting on this company — read this once and the low valuation makes sense.

India's home-loan market has become a brutal price war, and HFCs are on the losing side of the funding math. The reason is simple and structural: banks fund themselves more cheaply than any HFC can, so they can offer lower home-loan rates and still make money. Since a home loan is a near-commodity — the same product wherever you take it — the borrower shops on rate, and the cheapest funder wins. That's LICHFL's core problem.

NIM slipped from ~3.1% to ~2.7% in two years. On a ₹3.2-lakh-crore book, every 0.1% of margin is worth roughly ₹320 Cr of pre-tax income — so small spread moves are large profit moves.

The bank advantage, spelled out

  • CASA is near-free money. A big bank funds a large chunk of its lending with current- and savings-account balances that cost it ~2–3% or less. LICHFL must pay ~7.5%+ for every rupee. That's a permanent, structural 1–3 percentage-point funding handicap before either even makes a loan.
  • Home loans are the banks' favourite prize. Mortgages are large, long, low-default, property-backed loans — the safest retail asset a bank can hold. So banks want them and will discount aggressively to win them, especially from prime salaried borrowers. LICHFL's best customers are precisely the ones banks target.
  • Balance-transfer leakage. Existing LICHFL borrowers get cold-called by banks offering to "take over" their loan at a lower rate. This churn — customers refinancing away — quietly shrinks the book and forces LICHFL to cut rates to retain them, squeezing spread from the other side.
  • The HDFC precedent looms. When HDFC Ltd — the gold-standard HFC — merged into HDFC Bank in 2023, it was a public admission that even the best mortgage lender concluded it needed a bank's cheap deposits to compete. That's the strategic cloud over every standalone HFC.
How LICHFL fights back

It can't win a pure rate war against banks, so it plays a different game on three fronts. (1) Push into higher-yield segments — affordable housing, self-employed borrowers, and LAP — where banks are less aggressive and the spread is wider. (2) Lean on the LIC brand and reach — trust and agent distribution in smaller towns, where a fintech has no presence and a big bank has thin coverage, and where customers value a known name over a few basis points. (3) Grind down its own funding cost — as older, high-cost borrowings mature and are refinanced at today's lower rates, its cost of funds eases, which is why NIM has been stabilising rather than collapsing (the FY26 dip was just 5 bps). Management is guiding to hold NIM around 2.5–2.7% and grow the book ~10–12% — a defence of the moat, not an expansion of it.

The honest read: LICHFL is the big, lower-growth incumbent in a market where the economics favour banks. It is not going out of business — it is a profitable, well-capitalised lender with a real brand. But it is ceding share and margin to cheaper-funded rivals, and its strategy is fundamentally defensive. That structural squeeze, more than anything else, is why the market values it below the book value of its own loans.
06

Asset quality and the developer-book scars

The second thing every LICHFL investor argues about — and it's genuinely improving.

For a lender, the money it's owed is only worth something if borrowers actually repay. LICHFL's history here is a tale of two books: a rock-solid retail home-loan book and a troublesome project/developer book that, despite being tiny, has repeatedly driven ugly NPA spikes and big provisioning hits. The good news in FY26 is that asset quality is clearly on the mend.

What the terms mean

  • Stage 3 / GNPA — loans where the borrower is 90+ days overdue; effectively the "bad loan" bucket. At 2.16% (₹ terms, of the total book), most of this sits in the small project and older LAP books, not the retail home loans.
  • Net NPA (~1.08%) — bad loans after subtracting the money already set aside against them. The gap between gross (2.16%) and net (1.08%) shows LICHFL has provided for roughly half of its problem loans.
  • Credit cost / provisions — the amount charged to the profit statement each year to cover expected losses. When the developer book was blowing up, provisions surged and gutted profit; as it normalises, provisions fall and profit recovers — which is exactly what powered the FY24 earnings jump.
Why the developer book matters so much despite being tiny

Project/developer loans are under 3% of the book but have caused a majority of the pain. A single large builder defaulting on a few-hundred-crore loan can wipe out a quarter's profit, because there's no diversification — it's a handful of big, chunky, cyclical exposures rather than lakhs of small home loans. That's the opposite of the retail book, where risk is spread across hundreds of thousands of families each owing a manageable amount against a home they live in. LICHFL's post-2019 response was the right one: freeze the project book's growth, recover and provision aggressively, and let the safe retail book carry the company. FY26's falling Stage-3 says that clean-up is working — but the memory of it is one reason the market still discounts the stock, and any renewed push into higher-yield project lending will be watched nervously.

07

The industry: a long mortgage runway

The structural tailwind that keeps even a slow incumbent's story alive.

The bull case for any Indian housing lender starts with one striking fact: India is dramatically under-mortgaged. Home loans as a share of the economy are a fraction of what they are in developed countries — and even well below other emerging markets. As incomes rise, cities swell, families shrink into nuclear units, and government policy pushes "housing for all", that gap is expected to close over decades. It's a long, structural growth runway that lifts the whole sector.

The structural drivers

  • Rising incomes & affordability. As household incomes grow faster than home prices in many cities, the EMI a family can service rises — pulling more first-time buyers into the formal mortgage market.
  • Urbanisation & nuclear families. More people moving to cities, and joint families splitting into separate households, both multiply the number of homes needed.
  • Government push. Schemes like Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (PMAY) and interest subsidies for affordable housing, plus tax breaks on home-loan interest and principal, deliberately grease the wheels of home ownership.
  • Formalisation. As more of the informal, self-employed economy gets documented (GST, digital payments, credit bureaus), lenders can underwrite borrowers they previously couldn't — expanding the addressable market, especially in affordable housing.
The catch for LICHFL specifically: a rising tide lifts all boats, but it doesn't decide who grows fastest. The mortgage market is expanding ~12–15% a year, yet LICHFL grows its book only ~4–7% — meaning it is growing slower than the market and losing share. So the industry tailwind is real and supportive, but it is being captured disproportionately by cheaper-funded banks and nimbler affordable-housing specialists. For LICHFL, the runway exists; the question is how much of it the company can actually run on.
08

The competitive set

Squeezed from above by banks and from below by nimble specialists.

LICHFL competes in an unusually crowded field. Above it sit the giant banks with unbeatable funding costs; below it, a pack of focused, faster-growing HFCs attacking specific niches — affordable housing, self-employed borrowers, particular geographies — often at higher yields and much higher growth rates. LICHFL is the big, cheap, slow-growing middle.

The valuation tell in the peer set

Look at how the market prices these companies and the whole story is laid bare. The fast-growing, high-RoA specialists (Bajaj Housing, Aavas, Home First, Aptus) trade at rich premiums to book value — 2× to 5× or more — because investors pay up for growth and returns. LICHFL, despite being far larger and profitable, trades below book (~0.7×). The market is voting clearly: it prizes growth and return-on-capital over sheer size. LICHFL has the size and the safety but lacks the growth and the RoA — so it sits in the value bucket, not the compounder bucket. The bull's whole thesis is that this gap is too wide.

How lenders are valued — and why "below book" is striking

Banks and HFCs are valued mainly on price-to-book (P/B) — the market price versus the accounting value of the company's own equity (its "book"). The link is return on equity (RoE): a lender earning a high, sustainable RoE deserves a P/B well above 1×; one earning a mediocre RoE gets a P/B near or below 1×. A P/B below 1.0 means the market is effectively saying the company is worth less than the equity sitting inside it — a signal of either scepticism about future returns, or a genuine bargain.

The low-P/B debate, both sides

At ~0.72× book and ~5× earnings, LICHFL is one of the cheapest large lenders in India. The bull: it's a ₹3.2-lakh-crore, LIC-backed, well-capitalised (CRAR ~24%) lender with improving asset quality, a ~1.8% RoA and ~14% RoE — buying that below book value is a margin-of-safety bargain, and even a modest re-rating toward 1× book is a big upside. The bear: it's cheap for reasons that aren't going away — structurally squeezed spreads, sub-2% RoA, mid-single-digit growth, a government-linked promoter, and a business model losing share to banks. A low-RoA, low-growth lender should trade below book, and it can stay cheap for years. Both are internally consistent; the tie-breaker is whether you believe LICHFL can lift RoA and growth, or whether the bank squeeze caps them permanently.

Where a slow incumbent tries to find a second gear.

LICHFL's management knows the plain-vanilla home-loan book alone won't out-grow the market. Its plan to lift both growth and return-on-assets rests on mix shift, distribution, and cost — pushing into fatter-margin lending while defending the funding advantage that scale and the LIC name provide.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A safe, cheap, structurally-challenged lender — the debate is whether cheap is enough.

LICHFL is a study in tension: an enormous, well-capitalised, LIC-backed lender with a fortress balance sheet and improving asset quality, but stuck in a business where the economics quietly favour its bank competitors. It is unlikely to disappoint on safety; the whole question is whether it can ever grow and earn enough to escape its below-book valuation.

The bull case

  • Deep value — a profitable ₹3.2-lakh-crore lender at ~0.7× book and ~5× earnings; even a partial re-rating toward book value is meaningful upside.
  • LIC backing — brand trust, AAA-rated funding access, and a vast agent distribution reach few rivals can match.
  • Improving asset quality — Stage-3 down to 2.16%, net NPA ~1.08%; the developer-book pain is receding.
  • Fortress capital — CRAR ~24% means years of growth without diluting shareholders.
  • Structural runway — India's low mortgage penetration supports the whole sector for decades.

The bear case

  • The bank squeeze — cheaper CASA-funded banks out-price it on prime home loans and poach its book via balance transfers.
  • Thin, falling spreads — NIM has slid from ~3.1% to ~2.7%; the margin defence may not fully hold.
  • Low RoA & slow growth — ~1.8% RoA and mid-single-digit book growth justify a below-book multiple; it can stay cheap for years.
  • Developer-book memory — any renewed reach for yield in project/LAP lending risks another asset-quality cycle.
  • Promoter overhang — a government-linked parent brings governance and management-continuity questions, and a strategic pace slower than nimble private rivals.
The bottom line

LICHFL is a safe, cheap, structurally-challenged lender. The business risk is low — it is well-capitalised, its retail book is sound, and its asset quality is improving. The opportunity risk is the issue: it is losing ground to better-funded, faster-growing competitors, and its returns sit at the modest end of the sector. The stock's below-book price is the market pricing exactly that. The whole investment debate reduces to two questions to track each quarter: can NIM hold near 2.7% while the book grows double-digits (the margin-defence working), and can RoA edge toward 1.8%+ without asset quality slipping (the mix shift working). If both drift up, a below-book value stock has room to re-rate; if the bank squeeze wins, it stays cheap — and cheap-and-stuck is a perfectly plausible outcome.
