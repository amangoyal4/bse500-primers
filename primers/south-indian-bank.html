00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

South Indian Bank (SIB) is one of India's oldest private-sector banks — set up in Thrissur, Kerala in 1929, decades before most of today's private and public banks existed. It takes deposits from savers and lends to households, small businesses and companies. It is not a giant: total deposits are ~₹1.23 lakh crore and the whole bank is worth roughly ₹9,500 crore on the market — small enough that the entire company is valued at less than what India's largest bank earns in a couple of months. What makes SIB interesting today is not its size but its direction.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A rare deposit franchise, historically wasted on bad lending. For decades SIB had a genuinely good deposit base — sticky Kerala households, a large non-resident Indian (NRI) remittance book, and a secured gold-loan tradition — but it repeatedly lent that money to lumpy corporate borrowers who defaulted. The result was years of thin, volatile profit despite a solid funding side.
  • A profitability turnaround is visibly working. Under a CEO brought in during 2023, SIB has been de-risking: shrinking corporate exposure, growing retail, gold and SME loans, and clearing legacy bad debt. FY26 net profit hit a record ₹1,455 Cr (+11.7%); gross bad loans (GNPA) fell to 1.43% from 3.20% a year earlier, and net NPAs to just 0.29%. Return on assets crossed 1.03% — a threshold the bank spent years below.
  • The debate is durability, not the story. The turn is real; the question is whether SIB can keep earning a ~1% RoA once the easy wins (falling provisions, one-off recoveries) fade — and do it while margins are being squeezed and it competes for deposits against far bigger banks. That is the entire investment case, and it will be settled by a handful of numbers each quarter.
In plain terms

Picture a 97-year-old Kerala deposit machine that never quite learned to lend well. The savings side always worked — loyal local depositors, Gulf money flowing home, gold pledged for small loans. The lending side kept letting it down, because a few big corporate loans would sour and wipe out the profit. The last three years are the story of new management finally re-underwriting the loan book: fewer big corporate bets, many more small secured loans, and a determined clean-out of the old bad debt. When a bank does that successfully, the same deposits suddenly start producing steady profit instead of nasty surprises — which is exactly what SIB's numbers have started to show.

01

What an "old private-sector bank" actually is

Three species of Indian bank — and why SIB's is the trickiest.

Indian banks come in three broad families, and knowing which one you're looking at explains most of SIB's character. It belongs to the smallest and least glamorous of the three — the old private-sector banks — and its whole modern history is an attempt to shed that group's traditional weaknesses.

The three families of Indian bank

Mostly southern, regional roots.Deep local deposit franchises and long histories, but often sub-scale, tech-lagging, and prone to legacy corporate bad loans. This is SIB's box.

The old private banks are a peculiar middle child. They have something the new-gen banks had to spend billions building — a century-old base of loyal, low-cost local depositors. But they historically lacked the new-gen banks' underwriting discipline and scale, so their loan books carried more legacy corporate stress and their costs ran higher relative to their size. For years the market treated them as perennial laggards, trading below or around book value while the new-gen banks compounded at premium multiples.

Why the category matters for the thesis

The bull case on any old private bank is the same shape: the deposit franchise is worth far more than the market credits, if only the lending gets fixed. Fresh, disciplined management inherits a ready-made funding advantage, cleans up the loan book, tilts toward safe secured retail lending, and closes the return gap with the new-gen banks. When it works — Federal Bank and City Union are the cleaner regional examples — the stock re-rates from "distressed laggard" to "steady compounder." SIB is running exactly this playbook, roughly a lap behind the leaders. The question is whether it finishes the lap.

02

How a bank actually earns — the five numbers

No finance degree needed. Learn these five and the rest of the primer reads easily.

A bank is a spread business: it gathers money cheaply (mostly from depositors) and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap between the two, scaled to its assets, is the engine. Five terms carry almost the whole story — and each one is a live pressure point in SIB's turnaround.

The only banking vocabulary you need — with SIB's FY26 numbers
TermWhat it means (and SIB's figure)
NIINet Interest Income — interest earned on loans minus interest paid on deposits and borrowings. The core revenue line. SIB: ~₹3,437 Cr in FY26 (roughly flat, as funding costs rose).
NIMNet Interest Margin — NII as a % of interest-earning assets; the "spread", standardised. SIB: ~3.0% (Q4 FY26 ~2.95%), squeezed as deposits repriced higher.
CASACurrent + Savings Accounts — everyday money that pays little or no interest. Cheaper funding = fatter margin. SIB: ~32% of deposits are CASA.
GNPA / NNPAGross / Net Non-Performing Assets — the % of loans gone bad. The single best gauge of lending discipline. SIB: 1.43% / 0.29% at Mar 2026 — the headline of the turnaround.
RoAReturn on Assets — annual profit divided by total assets; the cleanest measure of how well a bank turns its balance sheet into profit. SIB: 1.03% in FY26 — the number the whole story hinges on.

A useful rule of thumb for Indian banks: a return on assets around 1% is the line between an ordinary bank and a decent one, and ~1.5%+ marks the elite (HDFC and ICICI live there). SIB spent years well below 1% because bad-loan provisions kept eating its profit. Crossing 1% in FY26 is precisely why the stock and the story woke up.

How the money flows — deposits in, loans out, spread harvested

The mechanism is a conveyor belt. Cheap deposits come in one end; loans go out the other; the spread between them is collected as net interest income. Two dials govern how much profit drips out: how cheap the deposits are (the CASA and NRI mix) and how few of the loans go bad (the credit cost). SIB has always been reasonable on the first dial and historically terrible on the second — which is the whole reason the turnaround focuses on lending, not funding.

The simple profit equation, and where SIB kept failing

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee income. Notice the fourth term, loan losses. A bank can earn a perfectly good spread and still make almost no profit if that one line balloons. That was SIB for years: a fine spread destroyed by provisions on soured corporate loans. The turnaround is not really about earning more spread — margins are actually under pressure — it is about making the spread stick by lending to borrowers who repay.

03

The company and who runs it

Nearly a century old, Kerala-rooted, no promoter — and a CEO hired to fix the lending.

South Indian Bank was incorporated in Thrissur on 29 January 1929 — the first bank in Kerala to receive an RBI licence under the Banking Regulation Act, and among the oldest surviving private banks in India. It was founded by a group of local citizens to give Kerala's traders and savers an alternative to informal money-lenders; that community-bank DNA still shapes its deposit franchise today.

Timeline

  • 1929
    Incorporated in Thrissur, Kerala, founded by local citizens; later the first private bank in the state to secure an RBI licence.
  • 1946–2000s
    Expands across Kerala and South India, builds a strong NRI-remittance and gold-loan franchise off the Kerala–Gulf corridor, and becomes an early adopter of core banking technology among old private banks.
  • 2010s
    Grows the balance sheet aggressively, including into large corporate loans — the seeds of the bad-debt problem that would later cap profitability.
  • 2020–22
    The low point: corporate defaults and COVID-era stress drag net profit down to just ~₹62 Cr (FY21) and ~₹45 Cr (FY22) — a rounding error for a bank this size. Asset quality and market confidence both bottom out.
  • Oct 2023
    P. R. Seshadri appointed MD & CEO (ex-Karur Vysya Bank, ex-Citi). A career retail banker, he sets an explicit strategy: de-risk the corporate book, grow secured retail/gold/SME, and lift return on assets.
  • FY23–FY25
    Profit rebuilds fast — ₹775 Cr → ₹1,070 Cr → ₹1,303 Cr — as provisioning normalises and the book re-mixes toward retail.
  • FY26
    Record net profit ₹1,455 Cr (+11.7%); GNPA down to 1.43%, NNPA 0.29%, provision coverage ~94.5%; RoA 1.03%; a 45% dividend declared — the clearest proof yet that the turn has legs.

Ownership & management

  • No promoter — widely held from birth. Like many old private banks, SIB has 0% promoter holding; it has been a professionally-managed, dispersed-ownership institution since 1929. RBI caps any single shareholder's stake, so no individual or family controls it.
  • A retail-heavy shareholder base. The register is dominated by retail and public shareholders alongside domestic institutions and a modest foreign holding — unusual for a listed bank, and a reason the stock can be volatile and sentiment-driven.
  • Management is the whole bet. With no promoter and a franchise that lives or dies on underwriting discipline, the continuity and credibility of P. R. Seshadri's team is the single most important qualitative variable. The strategy is sound and evidenced in the numbers; the risk is execution and management stability over a multi-year haul.
  • Footprint. Around 955 branches and ~1,290 ATMs/cash-recyclers across 26 states and 4 union territories — still Kerala-heavy, but increasingly present in the metros and the wider south.
04

The Kerala & NRI franchise — the asset that was always there

Why a small bank's funding side is worth more than its size suggests.

Everything good about SIB starts with its deposit franchise. A bank's real moat is not its loans — anyone can lend — but its ability to gather cheap, sticky deposits year after year. SIB's near-century in Kerala gives it three funding advantages that a newer bank would spend a fortune and a decade trying to build, and which no turnaround has ever put at risk.

Three sources of durable, low-cost funding

  • Loyal Kerala household deposits. Generations of families in Thrissur and across Kerala have banked with SIB. This is patient, relationship-based savings — the stickiest kind of deposit, far less flighty than rate-shopping money.
  • The NRI remittance corridor. Kerala sends more migrant workers to the Gulf than any other Indian state, and the money they remit home flows disproportionately through Kerala-rooted banks. SIB has a long-standing, oversized non-resident deposit book — a structural funding advantage tied to a demographic reality that will not reverse quickly.
  • Gold as a way in. In Kerala, pledging household gold for a small secured loan is completely normal, and gold-loan customers often bring their deposits too. Gold lending is also SIB's safest loan product (fully secured, quick to recover), so it strengthens both sides of the balance sheet at once.

Why this is the part the market undervalues

For years SIB looked like a sub-scale bank with a bad loan book, and the market priced it as such — often below the accounting value of its own equity. But hidden inside that unloved shell was a funding machine most banks would envy: a ~32% CASA base, a large NRI deposit book, and a deep gold-loan culture, all gathered at low cost from a loyal southern base. The turnaround thesis is really a bet that this franchise was never the problem — the lending was — and that fixing the lending finally lets the funding franchise show up in the profit line. The FY26 numbers are the first clear evidence of that.

A genuine surprise: SIB is older than almost every bank it competes with — founded in 1929, it predates HDFC Bank by 65 years and most of India's public-sector banks in their current form. Yet its entire market value (~₹9,500 Cr) is smaller than HDFC Bank's profit in a single quarter. Age and pedigree, it turns out, count for very little in banking; underwriting discipline is everything — which is exactly why a 97-year-old bank's investment case rests entirely on a change made in the last three years.
05

The turnaround, step by step

The single most important thing happening at SIB — and how to tell if it's working.

The whole investment case is one idea: a good deposit franchise is being re-wired to a better loan book. Under P. R. Seshadri, SIB has run a deliberate, mechanical de-risking programme since 2023. It has four moving parts, and each one is measurable, so you can check the story against reality every quarter.

The four levers of the turn

  • 1. Shrink lumpy corporate exposure. The old bad loans came from a handful of large corporate borrowers. SIB has been deliberately reducing the corporate share of its book — from north of 40% toward the high-30s — and, crucially, only writing new corporate loans to higher-rated names. Fewer big bets means fewer big blow-ups.
  • 2. Grow secured, granular retail. In place of corporate risk, SIB is adding personal loans, housing, vehicle, gold and SME lending — thousands of small loans instead of a few enormous ones. A granular book diversifies risk: one default is a rounding error, not a profit-wrecker.
  • 3. Clean out the legacy bad debt. Recognise, provide for and recover or write off the old NPAs, so the balance sheet is genuinely clean rather than hiding stress. This is what drove GNPA from mid-single-digits down to 1.43%.
  • 4. Lift the return on assets. The scorecard for all of the above: as bad-loan costs fall and the mix improves, RoA climbs. Crossing ~1% in FY26 is the proof the machine is working — and holding it there is the next test.

The non-transferable fact at the heart of this

Look at what changed and what didn't. SIB's balance sheet grew modestly over four years — deposits up roughly a third. But net profit went from ₹45 Cr in FY22 to ₹1,455 Cr in FY26 — about a 32-fold jump. The bank did not discover a new business, enter a new market, or triple its size. Almost the entire difference is credit cost: the money set aside for bad loans collapsed as legacy NPAs were cleaned out and new lending stayed secured. Same 955 branches, same Kerala deposit base, same gold-loan counters — profit moved ~32× because the loan book stopped defaulting. That is the whole turnaround in one comparison, and it is why the bad-loan and credit-cost lines matter more here than anywhere else in the accounts.

How to tell if it's really working: ignore the headline profit for a moment and watch two things together — the retail/secured share of new lending (is the mix genuinely shifting?) and the credit cost (are provisions staying low without hiding fresh stress?). If the mix keeps tilting to secured retail and credit costs stay contained through a full cycle, the turn is structural. If provisions jump the moment corporate loans season or the economy wobbles, it was mostly a clean-up, not a cure.

Cleaning up the bad loans

The most dramatic chart in the story — and the one to read most sceptically.

Asset quality is where SIB's transformation shows up most starkly. In a single year, gross bad loans nearly halved and net bad loans fell to a level that would flatter almost any bank in India. This is genuinely impressive — and precisely because it is so dramatic, it deserves a careful, sceptical read.

What "provisioning" is, and why coverage matters here

When a loan turns bad, the bank sets aside money to cover the expected loss — a provision, charged against profit. The provision coverage ratio tells you what fraction of bad loans is already covered by such reserves; SIB's ~94.5% (including write-offs) is high, meaning the recognised bad loans are almost fully absorbed. That is why net NPA is just 0.29%: the gross bad loans exist, but the bank has already taken the pain on nearly all of them. The flip side is that this heavy provisioning and clean-up is why profit was suppressed in earlier years — and why, as provisioning normalises to lower levels, profit springs back. Much of SIB's FY26 profit jump came from provisions falling sharply year on year.

The sceptic's checklist

  • Was the drop real or cosmetic? A GNPA fall can come from genuine recovery, from write-offs (moving bad loans off the books), or from fast new lending diluting the ratio. SIB's improvement reflects a mix of recoveries, upgrades and write-offs on old corporate accounts — real balance-sheet repair, but partly a one-time clean-up that cannot repeat every year.
  • The new book is young. Loans rarely go bad in their first year or two. The retail and SME loans SIB is adding now have not yet been through a full stress cycle. Low NPAs on a fast-growing, young book are expected — the true test comes when that book seasons.
  • Unsecured creep. Personal loans are higher-yield but unsecured; if the retail push leans too hard on unsecured lending, a future consumer-credit downturn could bite. The gold and housing tilt is the reassuring counterweight — most of the new lending is secured.
The honest caveat: the FY26 asset-quality numbers are excellent, but a single clean year after a clean-up is not the same as a proven through-the-cycle record. The new-generation banks earn their premium on decades of ~1% bad loans across multiple crises. SIB has one strong year and a credible plan — impressive, but the durability still has to be earned, not assumed.
07

The industry and the rivals

A favourable market — but SIB competes from the back of the pack.

The backdrop for Indian banking is structurally kind: credit penetration is still low versus developed economies, nominal GDP compounds at ~10%+, and formal credit keeps deepening. But SIB competes in a crowded field, and it is neither the biggest nor the fastest of its peers. Its most useful comparisons are the other old private banks — the same species, running the same fix, at different stages.

The competitive reality to keep in mind

SIB does not set prices in Indian banking — it takes them. When the giants raise deposit rates to fund their own growth, SIB has to follow or lose funding; when they compete hard for the best retail borrowers, SIB is often left with either lower yields or slightly weaker credits. This is the quiet tax of being sub-scale: your margin is squeezed from both ends by players a hundred times your size. It is also why SIB's edge has to come from its own franchise — the sticky Kerala and NRI deposits, the gold-loan culture, the local relationships — rather than from any pricing power. The turnaround works only if that franchise advantage is real and defended.

The regulatory backdrop: SIB operates under the Reserve Bank of India's tight prudential regime — Basel III capital rules, cash-reserve (CRR) and statutory-liquidity (SLR) requirements, priority-sector lending quotas, and rules on NPA recognition and provisioning. For an old private bank, RBI oversight also touches governance and management appointments (the CEO is RBI-approved). A stable, RBI-blessed leadership and a fortress capital ratio (SIB's capital adequacy runs comfortably above the regulatory minimum) are themselves part of the franchise's credibility.
08

Finally earning its cost of capital

The one metric that settles the thesis — and the margin headwind fighting against it.

Strip away the narrative and SIB's investment case reduces to a single question: can it sustainably earn a return on assets around 1% — and a return on equity comfortably above its cost of capital — rather than the sub-1% it managed for most of the last decade? FY26's 1.03% RoA and ~12.8% RoE say it just did. Whether it stays there is the entire debate.

Crossing ~1% RoA is the threshold that separates an also-ran from a genuinely profitable bank. FY26 is the first year SIB cleared it. The next few years are about whether ~1%+ is a new floor or a one-year peak.

Here is the tension that makes it a real question rather than a foregone conclusion. The RoA improvement so far has been powered largely by falling provisions — the credit-cost line normalising as the clean-up completed. That tailwind is finite: once provisions are already low, they cannot keep falling. From here, holding a ~1% RoA has to come from the harder, more durable sources — margin, fee income and cost efficiency — and one of those is currently working against SIB.

The margin headwind fighting the story

SIB's net interest margin has been squeezed, slipping from ~3.2% to around 2.95% in Q4 FY26. The cause is ordinary but stubborn: deposits reprice higher as older, cheaper fixed deposits mature and are replaced at today's rates, while loan yields lag. A tilt toward lower-yield secured lending (housing, gold) also trims the headline margin even as it lowers risk. So SIB is trying to hold its return up on credit-cost normalisation while margin pulls it down. The bull case needs the mix shift, fee income and operating leverage to more than offset the margin drag; the bear case is that a sub-scale bank simply cannot out-earn that squeeze for long. This is the genuine crux — not the past clean-up, but the future arithmetic of margin versus mix.

Financials & valuation

Record profit, a clean balance sheet — priced at a turnaround discount.

SIB's financials now read like a bank mid-recovery: record profit, sharply improved asset quality, a comfortable capital position — and a valuation that still reflects years of scepticism rather than the improved reality. The debate is no longer "is it a bad bank" (it clearly earns money now) but "how much of the improvement is durable, and how much is already in the price."

How to think about the valuation

For a bank, the multiple that matters is price-to-book value against return on equity, not price-to-earnings. A bank earning a low RoE deserves to trade near or below its book value; one earning a high RoE earns a premium to book. SIB has spent years trading at a discount to book — the market's way of saying "we don't believe the profit is durable." At ~₹9,500 Cr against its equity, it still trades cheaply, roughly around book value, well below the ~1.2–1.5× that better-regarded old private banks like Federal Bank, CSB and Karur Vysya command. The bull argument is simple arithmetic: if SIB proves a sustainable ~1%+ RoA and mid-teens RoE, a bank of this quality should trade above book, and the re-rating from a discount does the heavy lifting. The bear argument is that the discount is deserved — a sub-scale bank facing margin pressure, with one good year after a clean-up, is not the same as a proven compounder, and the cheapness may simply persist.

What has to go right from here

The clean-up is done; the compounding has to be earned.

The easy phase of the turnaround — cleaning out old bad loans and letting profit spring back as provisions normalised — is largely complete. The next phase is harder and less spectacular: turning a clean, mid-sized bank into one that compounds steadily without slipping back. Several things have to line up.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A real turnaround at a cheap price — with durability still to prove.

South Indian Bank is a genuine, evidenced turnaround: a century-old deposit franchise that has finally cleaned up its lending and started earning a respectable return. The investment debate is almost entirely about durability and pace — whether a sub-scale bank can hold a ~1% RoA against margin pressure, and whether the market re-rates it from a turnaround discount to a franchise premium.

The bull case

  • Cheap on an improving reality — trades around book value versus 1.2–1.5× for cleaner old-private peers; a sustained ~1%+ RoA argues for a re-rating.
  • Undervalued funding franchise — sticky Kerala deposits, a large NRI remittance book and a deep gold-loan culture, all low-cost.
  • Asset quality transformed — GNPA 1.43%, NNPA 0.29%, ~94.5% coverage; very little hidden stress on the recognised book.
  • Mix shift is de-risking the book — corporate down, secured retail/gold/SME up; a granular book is far harder to blow up.
  • Record profit and a credible, RBI-approved CEO — the strategy is working and the numbers are showing it.

The bear case

  • Margin squeeze — NIM down to ~2.95% on deposit repricing and a lower-yield mix; the return could stall as the provision tailwind fades.
  • One clean year isn't a cycle — the improved asset quality hasn't been tested through a downturn; the new retail book is young.
  • Sub-scale disadvantage — no pricing power; squeezed from both ends by banks a hundred times larger.
  • Provision-driven profit — much of the FY26 jump came from falling provisions, which can't keep falling forever.
  • Execution & management risk — no promoter, so the whole thesis rests on team continuity and disciplined underwriting over years.
The bottom line

South Indian Bank is a franchise that was always worth more than its lending let it earn — and, after a determined three-year clean-up, it has started to prove it. The clean balance sheet, the record profit and the crossing of the ~1% RoA line are real, not cosmetic. But the interesting money is not on the clean-up, which is largely done — it is on the next number: whether SIB can hold RoA at ~1%+ once provisions can no longer keep falling and while its margin is being squeezed. Watch the credit cost and the retail-mix shift together, quarter by quarter, against the RoA. If RoA stays above 1% as the provision tailwind fades, a 97-year-old bank finally earns its keep and the discount to book has no reason to persist. If RoA slips back toward the high-0.80s as margin bites, the market's long scepticism will have been right, and the turn will read as a good clean-up rather than a lasting cure.
