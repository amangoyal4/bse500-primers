00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

NMDC Limited is India's largest iron-ore producer — a Navratna public-sector company owned ~60.8% by the Government of India, under the Ministry of Steel. It mines high-grade iron ore from two mining complexes — Bailadila in Chhattisgarh and Donimalai in Karnataka — and sells almost all of it to India's steel industry. In FY26 (year ended March 2026) it became the first company in India's mining history to cross 50 million tonnes of annual production, hitting a record 53.16 MnT.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A cluster of low-cost mines is the whole story. NMDC is one of the lowest-cost iron-ore producers in the world, sitting on some of India's richest, shallowest, highest-grade hematite deposits. Digging a tonne of ore costs it a modest amount; it sells that tonne at several thousand rupees. That gap makes it a high-margin cash machine: FY26 turnover was ₹31,554 Cr (+33%), EBITDA ~₹10,737 Cr (~34% margin), and net profit ₹7,421 Cr (+11%) — with almost no net debt.
  • It is now a pure-play miner again. NMDC spent years building a 3-MTPA steel plant at Nagarnar, then in 2023 demerged and separately listed it as NMDC Steel Ltd. That returned NMDC to what it does best — mining ore — and left it a cleaner, cash-rich, high-dividend commodity company whose fortunes track iron-ore prices and India's steel output.
  • It is a cheap, cyclical, high-dividend PSU on a growth march. The shares trade around 10× earnings and ~6.5× EV/EBITDA with a ~3.9% dividend yield — a classic cheap cyclical. But NMDC is also chasing a big volume ramp: from 53 MnT in FY26 toward a 60-MnT FY27 target and 100 MnT by 2030, funded from its own cash while it eases evacuation bottlenecks with new rail lines and a slurry pipeline.
In plain terms

Think of NMDC as the owner of a set of enormous, cheap-to-run iron-ore mines that almost nobody else in India can replicate — the raw-material tap that a huge chunk of the country's steel industry drinks from. Iron ore is the basic rock steel is made from, and NMDC sits on some of the best, cheapest ore in the world. It pulls that ore out of the ground for a low cost and sells it at a market-linked price it largely sets itself, so it earns a fat, dependable margin and piles up cash — much of which it hands back as dividends. For a while it tried to become a steel-maker too, building a plant at Nagarnar; then it hived that plant off into a separate listed company and went back to being a pure miner. The prize is a low-cost, cash-rich mining monopoly-of-sorts that grows volumes toward 100 million tonnes a year; the risk is that iron-ore prices, steel demand and government policy — three things it doesn't fully control — swing its profits around the cycle.

01

Iron-ore mining economics 101: why a low-cost mine is a money machine

No commodities background required — just the handful of ideas that drive everything.

To understand NMDC you first have to understand why owning a large, low-cost iron-ore mine is so unusually profitable. Iron ore is the raw rock from which almost all steel is made. A mine is, financially, the extreme opposite of a factory: nearly all the value is in the right to extract a resource that sits in the ground for free, and the cost of getting it out is small relative to what it sells for. When your ore is also high-grade and cheap to mine, that gap widens — and the whole business becomes a cash machine.

The only concepts you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for NMDC)
Iron oreThe rock (mostly iron oxide) that steel is made from. Higher "grade" (iron content, e.g. 62% Fe) sells for more and needs less processing. NMDC's Bailadila ore is premium hematite — lumps at ~65.5% Fe, fines at ~64% Fe — among the highest-grade in the world.
Grade & costThe two levers of a mine's margin. NMDC's deposits are rich, shallow and easy to mine, so its cash cost per tonne is low and its grade is high — the classic recipe for a bottom-of-the-cost-curve producer that stays profitable even when prices fall.
Resource rentThe gap between the low cost to extract a tonne and the market price it sells for. This "rent" — not manufacturing profit — is why a good mine's margins dwarf a factory's. NMDC's EBITDA margin runs ~30–40%; a typical steel mill's is far lower.
Lump vs finesOre comes as lumps (larger pieces, fed directly to furnaces, priced higher) and fines (powder, usually pelletised first). NMDC sells both, at separately administered prices.
MTPA"Million tonnes per annum" — the mine's permitted/production capacity. More tonnes at a fixed low cost = more profit. NMDC produced 53 MnT in FY26 and is aiming for 100 MTPA by 2030.
Reserves & life-of-mineHow much ore is in the ground and how long it will last. NMDC's Bailadila belt holds vast, long-life reserves — a multi-decade runway that underpins the whole equity story.
Why the mining leases are the moat

In India, you cannot simply decide to open a giant iron-ore mine. Mining leases are granted by the state, tied to specific parcels of ground, valid for decades, and — since a 2015 law change — mostly auctioned at fierce competition or held by legacy owners. Large new iron-ore leases almost never come to market. So the leases themselves are the scarce, protected asset: multi-decade, government-granted rights to extract a commodity the whole steel industry needs, from specific patches of the richest ore in the country that no competitor can conjure. NMDC — set up by the government in 1958 — holds and has developed the best of these over half a century. That legacy portfolio of low-cost, high-grade leases is the entire foundation of the equity story: not a brand, not a factory, but the ore beneath the ground and the right to sell it.

The two ways a miner grows

  • Dig more (volume) — mine and sell more tonnes from existing and new deposits. NMDC has gone from ~44 MnT (FY24) to 53 MnT (FY26) and targets 60 MnT (FY27) and 100 MnT (2030). At a low fixed cost per tonne, each extra tonne is highly profitable — provided it can be evacuated (moved out by rail/pipeline, see Section 07).
  • Sell it for more (price) — realise a higher price per tonne, either because market ore prices rise or because NMDC lifts its own administered prices (Section 04). Because costs are largely fixed, most of any price rise drops straight to profit.
02

Bailadila & Donimalai: the crown-jewel mines

The handful of deposits that explain the entire company — and most of the profit.

Almost everything about NMDC radiates from two mining regions: the Bailadila range in Chhattisgarh and Donimalai in Karnataka. Between them they produce the bulk of the company's ore. Understanding these mines is understanding the stock.

The Bailadila belt (Chhattisgarh) — the core engine

  • Kirandul & Bacheli complexes. The Bailadila range, in the Dantewada/Bastar region of southern Chhattisgarh, is NMDC's heartland — a belt of rich, shallow, high-grade hematite in "one of India's most important high-grade iron-ore belts." The two main producing complexes, Kirandul and Bacheli, together deliver most of NMDC's output and both posted best-ever results in FY26.
  • Premium grade. Bailadila lumps run ~65.5% Fe and fines ~64% Fe — well above the ~62% global benchmark. Higher grade means the ore commands a price premium and needs less beneficiation before it can be used, reinforcing the low-cost advantage.
  • New capacity: Deposit 4. NMDC commissioned Deposit 4 at Bailadila, adding fresh, high-grade capacity — part of the ramp toward 100 MTPA and, in policy terms, earmarked to help feed the Nagarnar steel plant (now NMDC Steel).

Donimalai (Karnataka) — the second pillar

  • The Karnataka operation. Donimalai is NMDC's flagship mine in Karnataka, complete with its own pellet plant, and delivered best-ever results in FY26 alongside the Chhattisgarh mines. Karnataka ore has historically been subject to state-specific rules (production caps, e-auction requirements) that make it a distinct operating environment from Chhattisgarh.
  • Value-add via pellets. The Donimalai pellet plant lets NMDC turn low-value fines into higher-value, furnace-ready pellets — a modest step up the value chain that improves realisation on fine ore.
Why "low-cost + high-grade" is the whole moat

In any commodity, the durable winner is the lowest-cost producer — the one who still earns money when prices are on the floor and higher-cost rivals are losing. NMDC sits near the bottom of the global iron-ore cost curve because its deposits are unusually rich (so a tonne of rock yields a lot of sellable iron), shallow (cheap open-cast mining, no deep shafts), and already developed (decades of sunk infrastructure). That combination — scarce, government-granted leases over some of the world's best ore, mined at low cost — is a moat competitors simply cannot replicate: you can't manufacture a new Bailadila. It is why NMDC has been consistently, structurally profitable for decades, and why even a soft patch in ore prices dents rather than destroys its earnings.

The location nuance: Bailadila sits in Bastar/Dantewada, historically a Naxal (Maoist-insurgency) affected region of Chhattisgarh — a genuine security and social-licence backdrop, with periodic tensions over land, forests and tribal rights. As with other central-Indian mining, this is both an operating risk and, paradoxically, part of why these world-class deposits stayed in relatively few hands. Security has broadly improved over the years, but land, forest-clearance and community issues remain live constraints on expansion.
03

The steel-demand linkage: NMDC is a bet on Indian steel

Iron ore has exactly one big use — so NMDC's demand is India's steel output.

Here is the single most important thing to internalise about NMDC: iron ore is the essential raw material for steel, and almost nothing else. Roughly 98% of the world's mined iron ore goes into steel-making. So NMDC does not really sell to a diversified customer base — it sells, overwhelmingly, to India's steel industry. Its volumes and its pricing power therefore track one master variable: how much steel India is making.

NMDC captures the first, highest-margin step. Its customers are the steel mills; its ultimate demand driver is India's construction, infrastructure and auto boom feeding through the chain.

Why this linkage matters so much

  • Volume demand = steel output. Every tonne of steel needs roughly 1.5–1.6 tonnes of iron ore. As India's steel production grows — driven by infrastructure, housing, capital goods and autos — the country needs more ore, and NMDC, as the dominant domestic supplier, is a natural beneficiary. India is one of the world's fastest-growing steel markets, targeting ~300 MnT of steel capacity by 2030; that structural demand is NMDC's long-run tailwind.
  • Pricing power = steel-mill health. When steel demand and prices are strong, mills happily absorb NMDC's ore-price hikes; when steel is weak (imports flooding in, a construction slowdown), mills push back and NMDC's ability to raise prices — or even hold volumes — is squeezed. So NMDC's realisation is really a derivative of steel-mill profitability.
  • Domestic tilt. NMDC sells the vast majority of its ore domestically to Indian mills, so it is more a play on India's steel cycle than on the volatile global seaborne iron-ore market that dominates the likes of Vale, Rio Tinto and BHP — though global prices and Chinese demand still set the backdrop against which India's prices move.
The mental model

The cleanest way to think about NMDC is as a leveraged, low-cost play on Indian steel demand. When India is building — roads, railways, housing, factories, cars — steel mills run hot, they need every tonne of ore they can get, and NMDC sells more ore at firmer prices, so its cash gushes. When the economy cools or cheap imported steel undercuts domestic mills, ore demand softens, price hikes get harder to push through, and NMDC's earnings dip. Because its costs barely move while its volumes and prices swing with the cycle, NMDC is a geared way to own the Indian steel story — with the bottom-of-the-cost-curve safety net that it keeps making money even when the cycle turns down.

04

Administered pricing: how NMDC sets its own ore price

The unusual mechanism at the heart of NMDC's realisation — and its earnings swings.

Unlike most commodities, where the producer is a pure price-taker, NMDC administers its own iron-ore prices. Periodically — sometimes several times a year — it announces revised base prices for its Bailadila lump and fines, and Indian mills largely pay those posted prices. This gives NMDC a degree of pricing agency, but it is not a free hand: the administered price has to track the market, or NMDC either loses volume (if too high) or leaves money on the table (if too low).

How the mechanism works

  • Posted base prices. NMDC publishes a base price per tonne for each product, revised periodically to reflect market conditions, input costs and steel-mill demand. Buyers pay this base plus statutory levies.
  • Lump commands a premium. Lump ore (used directly in furnaces) is priced above fines (which need pelletising), so NMDC's mix of lump vs fines affects blended realisation.
  • It moves both ways. When steel is strong, NMDC hikes prices (as it did ~10–11% in April 2026); when steel weakens or global prices fall, it cuts them. This is exactly why quarterly earnings can wobble: FY26 saw record volumes but softer margins because realisations fell in parts of the year even as tonnage rose.
Why realisation, not just volume, drives profit

Because NMDC's mining cost per tonne barely changes, its profit in any given period is essentially (price it realises per tonne − cost per tonne) × tonnes sold. Volumes have been marching steadily up (a good, controllable trend). But the price it realises is the swing factor — and that price moves with domestic steel demand, global ore prices and NMDC's own administered decisions. So you can get a quarter, like several in FY26, where NMDC sells more ore than ever yet earns a thinner margin because the per-tonne price softened. The lesson for reading NMDC: always separate the two. Rising volumes are the structural growth story; the administered/realised price is the cyclical overlay that decides whether a record-volume year is also a record-profit year.

How to read a soft quarter: if NMDC reports higher production and sales but flat or lower profit, the culprit is almost always realisation — a lower administered/market ore price — not a broken business. Check whether tonnes kept climbing (they have) and whether prices have since been hiked back up (as in April 2026). Volume growth with a soft price is a cyclical dip; volume stalling would be the real worry.
05

The commodity & steel cycle: what really moves earnings

A miner lives and dies by prices set by markets and policy it does not fully control.

However dominant its position, NMDC is at heart a cyclical commodity company. Its earnings are geared to the iron-ore price and, through its customers, to the steel cycle — and while its administered-pricing mechanism gives it some agency, it cannot escape the cycle. Understanding this is essential to reading the volatile, cheaply-rated share price.

The forces that swing NMDC's realisation

  • Indian steel demand. The master variable (Section 03). Strong construction/infra/auto demand → mills run hot → NMDC sells more ore at firmer prices. A slowdown does the reverse.
  • Global iron-ore prices & China. Even though NMDC sells mostly domestically, the global seaborne price — driven overwhelmingly by Chinese steel demand — sets the backdrop. When global prices fall (e.g. a Chinese property slump), imported ore and domestic prices soften, and NMDC's administered prices follow.
  • Steel imports & policy. Cheap imported steel (often Chinese) squeezes Indian mills' margins, weakening their appetite to pay up for ore. Government safeguard duties / minimum import prices that protect domestic steel indirectly support NMDC's demand.
  • Export duty on ore. India levies export duties on iron ore to keep it at home for domestic steel. This caps NMDC's ability to sell abroad when domestic prices are weak, tethering its realisation to the Indian market.
The low-cost-position cushion

Here is the reassuring flip side of being a cyclical. In any commodity, the winner is the lowest-cost producer — the one who still makes money when prices are on the floor and everyone else is bleeding. Because NMDC mines its own high-grade ore at a fraction of the market price, it sits near the very bottom of the cost curve. So even in a downturn, when high-cost miners and mills lose money, NMDC stays profitable and keeps generating cash and paying dividends. That low-cost position doesn't remove the cycle — a sharp fall in ore prices still hits earnings hard and fast — but it means NMDC is the last one standing, and can even gain share as weaker players retreat. That resilience is exactly why a cyclical miner can also be a dependable dividend payer.

06

The NMDC Steel demerger: back to being a pure miner

Why NMDC built a steel plant, then spun it off — and what that means for the equity.

One of the most important structural events in NMDC's recent history is the demerger of its Nagarnar steel plant into a separately-listed company, NMDC Steel Ltd. This move reshaped what an investor in "NMDC" actually owns today: a clean, pure-play iron-ore miner rather than a hybrid miner-cum-steelmaker.

The story of the demerger

  • The steel ambition. Years ago, NMDC decided to move up the value chain by building a greenfield 3.0 MTPA integrated steel plant at Nagarnar, Chhattisgarh — turning some of its own ore into finished steel (HR coils). The logic was the same value-addition idea that tempts every miner: steel sells for far more per tonne than raw ore.
  • The demerger & listing. But building and running a steel plant is a very different business from mining — more capital-intensive, more cyclical on the output side, and a drag on the clean mining P&L. So under a government-approved scheme, the under-construction plant was hived off into a new entity, NMDC Steel Limited (NSL), which was separately listed on the BSE on 20 February 2023. Existing NMDC shareholders received NMDC Steel shares.
  • The plant went live. The Nagarnar plant began commercial production of HR coils on 31 August 2023. NMDC Steel is now its own listed Government-of-India PSE under the Ministry of Steel (Govt holding ~60.8%), on a path from loss-making ramp-up toward profitability.
  • The result: a pure-play miner. After the demerger, NMDC Limited is once again a focused iron-ore miner — no steel plant on its books, no steel-cycle margin drag on the output side, just the high-margin, cash-generative mining business. This is the NMDC you buy today.
Why the demerger is good for the mining story

Miners and steelmakers are fundamentally different animals. A mine earns a fat, stable resource rent on a low-cost input; a steel plant earns a thin, volatile spread and swallows enormous capital. Bolting them together muddies both — the market never quite knows how to value a hybrid, and the steel plant's heavy capex and losses can mask the mine's brilliance. By demerging Nagarnar, NMDC let each business stand on its own: NMDC Limited became a clean, cash-rich, high-dividend mining pure-play that the market can value simply on ore volumes, prices and cash returns; NMDC Steel became a separate bet on the steel cycle for those who want it. For an NMDC shareholder, the payoff is clarity — you own the mine, not the mill.

Contrast worth noting: NMDC's path is the opposite of a company like Lloyds Metals, which is a merchant miner deliberately integrating forward into steel. NMDC tried integration, decided the mining pure-play was cleaner, and demerged out of steel. Two rational miners, two opposite structural choices — reflecting NMDC's scale, PSU capital discipline and desire to stay the low-cost ore supplier to the whole industry rather than compete with its own steel-mill customers.
07

The march to 100 MTPA — and the evacuation bottleneck

The volume-growth ambition, and the rail-and-pipeline plumbing that makes it possible.

NMDC's headline growth story is simple: mine more tonnes. It has set a target to nearly double capacity to 100 million tonnes a year by 2030, with a stepping-stone of 60 MnT in FY27. But there is a catch that dominates the plan — you can only sell ore you can physically move. In remote, hilly Bastar, the real constraint is not digging the ore but evacuating it, which is why the growth capex is heavily about rail and a slurry pipeline.

Easing the bottleneck: rail + slurry pipeline

  • Rail doubling. Doubling the Kirandul–Jagdalpur railway line is set to lift rail evacuation from NMDC's Bailadila sector from ~28 MTPA to 40 MTPA — a step-change in how much ore can leave the mine gate. Without it, extra production simply piles up at the pithead.
  • The slurry pipeline. NMDC is building a Bacheli/Bailadila-to-Nagarnar slurry pipeline (~130–140 km, designed for up to ~15 MTPA), which pumps ore mixed with water through a pipe instead of trucking or railing it. It is cheaper, lower-carbon and removes a physical transport ceiling — a structural logistics advantage at scale.
  • Processing & pellets. New screening plants (at Kirandul and Donimalai), a pellet plant at Nagarnar (2 MTPA, expandable to 6 MTPA) and the Donimalai pellet plant add processing and value-addition to match the higher ore throughput.
  • Funded from cash. FY26 capex was ~₹3,690 Cr; the multi-year programme (slurry pipeline, rail, processing plants, new deposits) is comfortably funded from NMDC's own operating cash flow — no strained balance sheet required.

    • Why evacuation is the real growth lever

    It is tempting to think a miner's growth is about how much ore is in the ground — but NMDC has ore for decades. The binding constraint is getting it out and to customers. Bailadila is deep in central India, far from ports and mills, on congested single-track rail through difficult terrain. So NMDC's growth to 100 MTPA is, in practice, an infrastructure project: double the railway line, build the slurry pipeline, add screening plants — and the tonnes follow. This is why the capex story matters and why delays in rail/pipeline projects (which depend partly on the Railways and on land/forest clearances outside NMDC's control) are the thing that could slow the ramp. The reserves are certain; the plumbing is the wager.

    08

    Royalty, duties & policy: the regulatory overhang

    A state-owned miner of a strategic mineral lives inside a thicket of government levies and rules.

    Because iron ore is a natural resource extracted from state land, NMDC operates inside a web of royalties, cesses and duties — and because it is itself a government company selling a strategic input to a strategic industry, it is exposed to policy risk in a way private manufacturers are not. These levies and rules are added on top of its base ore price and can meaningfully affect both its costs and its market.

    The levies and policies that shape NMDC's economics

    RoyaltyA percentage of ore value paid to the state government for the right to extract. A direct cost/levy that rises with ore prices; states periodically press for higher royalties.
    DMF (District Mineral Foundation)A levy (a share of royalty) funding development in mining-affected districts — added on top of the base price.

    Environmental / forest clearancesNew mines and expansions need environmental and forest-clearance approvals — often the binding constraint on when NMDC can grow, given Bailadila's forested, tribal setting.
    PSU / policy dimensionAs a Govt-of-India company, NMDC can face policy nudges — on pricing, dividends, capital allocation, or supplying strategic buyers — that a purely commercial miner would not.
    The double-edged policy sword

    Government ownership and heavy regulation cut both ways for NMDC. On the plus side, being a state-owned Navratna gives it privileged access to leases, political heft in securing clearances, and a stable, strategic role as the country's ore supplier — a big part of why its moat is so durable. On the minus side, the same status means it is a policy-taker: royalties and cesses eat into economics, export duties fence it into the domestic market, forest and environmental clearances can delay expansion for years, and the government (as majority owner) can steer pricing, dividends or capital decisions toward national rather than purely shareholder goals. For an investor, this is the essential trade: NMDC's regulatory embeddedness is both the source of its protected position and the origin of its biggest non-market risks.

    09

    Owners: a Government-of-India Navratna PSU

    Who controls NMDC, what "Navratna" means, and why ownership shapes the thesis.

    NMDC is a Central Public Sector Enterprise — the Government of India owns ~60.8%, under the Ministry of Steel — and carries Navratna status, a designation given to strong, financially sound PSUs that grants greater operational and investment autonomy. This ownership is fundamental to both the appeal and the caveats of the stock.

    What ownership means in practice

    • Government majority (~60.8%). The state is the controlling shareholder; the remaining ~39% is public float (institutions and retail). Board and top-management appointments run through the government.
    • Navratna autonomy. Navratna status lets NMDC make sizeable investments and form ventures with less bureaucratic sign-off than an ordinary PSU — useful for its expansion and diversification plans.
    • Dividend engine for the exchequer. As a cash-rich PSU, NMDC is a reliable dividend payer, partly because the government (its majority owner) values the dividend stream. This aligns nicely with minority shareholders who also want cash returns (Section 10).
    • Strategic mandate. Beyond profit, NMDC carries a national mandate — developing mineral resources, ensuring ore supply to Indian steel, exploring for critical minerals, and operating in regions the government wants developed. Most of the time this coincides with shareholder interest; occasionally (e.g. supplying strategic buyers, absorbing policy priorities) it may diverge.
    The diversification arm: NMDC is also pushing beyond iron ore — into critical minerals (lithium, cobalt, nickel) via a subsidiary, Legacy Iron Ore Ltd, including lithium exploration in Australia; it opened an international office in Dubai; and it operationalised its first coal mine in Jharkhand. These are early-stage, small relative to the core, and partly a national mandate to secure strategic minerals — optionality to watch, not yet a needle-mover.

    Financials, cash & dividends: the high-yield cash machine

    Record FY26 volumes, fat margins, a fortress balance sheet, and a generous payout.

    NMDC's financials are the picture of a low-capital-intensity, cash-generative miner: record turnover, strong (if cyclically softer) margins, almost no net debt, and a chunky dividend. The debate is not whether the business is good — it plainly is — but how much a cheap, cyclical PSU should be worth (Section 11).

    Why the balance sheet is a fortress

    NMDC generates far more cash than it needs to run and grow the mines, so it has piled up a large cash-and-investments hoard while carrying only modest debt — it holds more cash than borrowings, i.e. it is net-cash. That matters for three reasons. First, safety: a net-cash miner cannot be sunk by a downturn — it simply earns less for a while. Second, self-funded growth: the entire 100-MTPA capex programme (rail, slurry pipeline, plants) is paid for from internal cash, with no dilution or strained leverage. Third, dividends: with capex modest relative to cash flow, NMDC returns a big slice of profit to shareholders — a ~3.9% dividend yield on top of the cheap valuation. This combination — low-cost mine + net cash + high payout — is what makes NMDC a favourite among income-oriented and value investors.

    The valuation debate: cheap for a reason, or too cheap?

    A dominant, cash-rich miner — priced like a commodity PSU. Bargain or value trap?

    NMDC trades at a strikingly low multiple: around 10× trailing earnings, ~6.5× EV/EBITDA, roughly ~2× book, with a ~3.9% dividend yield, at a market cap near ₹75,000 Cr. For a business this dominant and profitable, that looks cheap — but there are real reasons the market applies a discount, and the debate between "bargain" and "value trap" is the crux of the equity case.

    Why it's cheap (the value case)

    • Low-cost dominance — India's largest, lowest-cost, highest-grade iron-ore miner; a moat you cannot replicate.
    • Net cash + high yield — fortress balance sheet, self-funded growth, ~3.9% dividend — you're paid to wait.
    • Volume growth — a rare cheap cyclical that's also growing: 53 → 60 → 100 MnT, with steel demand as a structural tailwind.
    • ~10× P/E, ~6.5× EV/EBITDA — undemanding even for a cyclical; little optimism priced in.

    Why the discount (the trap case)

    • Cyclical earnings — a fall in ore prices / steel demand cuts profit fast; "cheap" P/Es on peak earnings can be a mirage.
    • PSU discount — government control means policy/pricing/dividend decisions may not maximise minority value; PSUs trade at structural discounts.
    • Policy & royalty risk — royalty hikes, export duties, clearance delays and administered-price politics all cap the upside.
    • Evacuation-dependent growth — the 100-MTPA ramp hinges on rail/pipeline projects partly outside NMDC's control.
    How to think about the valuation

    The tension is this. On paper, a dominant, low-cost, net-cash miner growing volumes and yielding ~4% at 10× earnings looks like a clear bargain — the kind of quality-plus-value combination that's rare. But commodity companies are hard to value precisely because the "E" in the P/E swings with the cycle: buy a cyclical at a low P/E on peak earnings and you can still lose money as earnings normalise down. Layer on the PSU discount — the market's standing scepticism that a government-controlled company will always act in minority shareholders' interest on pricing, dividends and capital allocation — and you get a stock that is persistently cheap rather than temporarily cheap. The bull says: the discount is too harsh for an asset this good with growth and cash returns; the bear says: cheap PSUs stay cheap, and cyclical earnings plus policy risk justify it. The honest read is that NMDC is genuinely a high-quality mining asset at a low price — but it's a PSU cyclical, so the re-rating that value investors hope for depends on the cycle staying firm, the volume ramp delivering, and the government's stewardship staying shareholder-friendly.

    12

    The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

    A rare, low-cost mining asset — wrapped in commodity cyclicality and PSU realities.

    NMDC is one of the most distinctive names in Indian materials: the dominant, lowest-cost supplier of the raw material that builds the country, throwing off cash, paying dividends, and marching its volumes toward 100 MTPA. The upside is a cheap, growing, net-cash miner with a genuinely durable moat; the risk is that a cyclical, policy-bound PSU stays cheap while its earnings ride the iron-ore and steel cycle.

    The bull case

    • A rare, scarce asset — India's largest, lowest-cost, highest-grade iron-ore mines; ~17–20% of national output; near-impossible to replicate.
    • Record, growing volumes — 53 MnT in FY26 (first Indian miner past 50), on the way to 60 (FY27) and 100 (2030), backed by rail + slurry pipeline.
    • Fortress balance sheet — net cash, self-funded capex, and a ~3.9% dividend — you're paid to wait.
    • Structural steel tailwind — India's steel capacity heading toward ~300 MnT by 2030 pulls ore demand along with it.
    • Cheap valuation — ~10× P/E, ~6.5× EV/EBITDA for a dominant, high-ROCE miner.

    The bear case

    • Commodity cyclicality — a fall in ore prices / steel demand slashes earnings fast; FY26 already showed margins compress on lower realisation.
    • PSU discount & governance — government control can steer pricing/dividends/capital toward national rather than minority-shareholder goals.
    • Policy & royalty risk — royalty hikes, export duties, clearance delays and administered-price politics cap upside.
    • Evacuation dependency — the 100-MTPA ramp hinges on rail-doubling and the slurry pipeline, partly outside NMDC's control.
    • Location & social licence — Bailadila's forested, tribal, historically Naxal-affected setting brings land/forest/community constraints on growth.
    The bottom line

    NMDC owns something genuinely rare in Indian industry — a cluster of the country's best, cheapest iron-ore mines, the raw-material tap for a huge share of India's steel. That asset makes it a structurally low-cost, high-margin, cash-gushing miner that keeps paying dividends even when the cycle turns. Having demerged its steel plant, it is now a clean pure-play on that mining strength, growing volumes toward 100 million tonnes a year while easing the rail-and-pipeline bottleneck that gates the ramp. The catch is that it remains a cyclical, policy-bound PSU: its per-tonne realisation swings with iron-ore prices, steel demand and its own administered decisions; royalties, duties and clearances shape its economics; and government control keeps a discount on the shares. The two numbers to watch each quarter are tonnes (is the ramp toward 60-then-100 MnT on track?) and realisation per tonne (is the administered/market price holding up?). The mine is the certainty; the cycle and the PSU discount are the wager.
