Accent Microcell, the invisible half of every pill

A complete walk through a small Gujarat maker of pharmaceutical excipients — the inactive ingredients that make a tablet physically possible. Accent makes microcrystalline cellulose and its cousins, the bland white powder that gives a pill its body and lets it break apart in your stomach; almost every tablet on Earth contains some. Here is what an excipient actually does, why a certified pharma-grade version is a fortress-walled niche rather than a commodity, how Accent turns imported wood pulp into it, and why the whole investment case rides on volume, grade-mix and a wood-pulp bill.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Accent Microcell Limited is a small, Ahmedabad-based specialty manufacturer of pharmaceutical excipients — the inactive ingredients inside a tablet or capsule. Its flagship product is microcrystalline cellulose (MCC), the single most widely used tablet excipient in the world, which it makes in 22 different grades from purified wood pulp. Around this it has built a small family of related products: croscarmellose sodium (CCS) and other disintegrants that make a pill break apart, magnesium stearate, and co-processed grades such as silicified MCC. It sells to pharmaceutical, nutraceutical, food and cosmetics customers — more of it abroad than at home — and it is a very recent listing: a December 2023 IPO on the NSE Emerge SME platform (not the mainboard, and not on the BSE at all).

Three facts frame everything:

  • It sells the part of the pill nobody thinks about — and that is the point. When you swallow a paracetamol tablet, the active drug is often a small fraction of the weight; the rest is excipient — binder, filler, disintegrant — and MCC is the workhorse of that "rest." Accent doesn't make medicines; it makes the matrix that carries them. It is a picks-and-shovels supplier to the entire pharmaceutical industry, one step removed from any single drug.
  • The moat is a certificate, not a molecule. MCC is chemically simple and, as a bulk industrial powder, close to a commodity. What is not a commodity is pharma-grade, regulator-approved, batch-consistent MCC. A drugmaker cannot casually switch excipient supplier — the new source has to be validated, documented (a US Drug Master File), audited and written into the drug's regulatory filing. That approval wall is Accent's business. It is why the company chases certifications (US-DMF, EXCiPACT, GMP, ISO, Kosher, Halal) the way a drugmaker chases patents.
  • Small, profitable, growing — and priced like it. FY25 revenue was ~₹265 Cr with net profit ~₹33 Cr and an EBITDA margin near 17%; the balance sheet is almost debt-free and return on capital sits in the mid-20s percent. Sales have compounded ~21% and profit ~54% over five years. The market caps it around ₹1,210 Cr — roughly 27× earnings — a rating that already assumes the capacity expansion lands and the premium-grade mix keeps climbing.
In plain terms

Every time you take a tablet, you are swallowing a tiny amount of active drug packed into a much larger amount of inactive powder that holds the pill together and then lets it crumble apart on cue. Accent Microcell makes that powder. It is not a drug company and it is not a bulk chemicals company — it sits in a strange, comfortable middle: a specialty converter that turns imported wood pulp into a pharma-certified ingredient the drug industry cannot manufacture without, and cannot swap out casually once it has been approved. The business grows when it sells more tonnes, when a bigger slice of those tonnes are the higher-value certified and specialty grades, and when the price it charges stays comfortably above the wood-pulp bill. FY25 delivered on all three, quietly. The open question is whether a company this small can keep scaling a niche that rewards the patient and the certified.

01

What an excipient actually is

The unglamorous majority of the pill — and why it is a real product with real engineering.

Start with the pill in your hand. A pharmaceutical tablet has two kinds of ingredient. There is the Active Pharmaceutical Ingredient (API) — the molecule that actually treats you — and then there is everything else: the excipients. The word means "inactive" in the sense that it does not treat the disease, but that is deeply misleading, because without excipients there is no tablet at all. They are the difference between a heap of drug powder and a manufactured, swallowable, shelf-stable dose that delivers the right amount at the right speed.

For many common drugs the API is a surprisingly small share of the tablet's weight — sometimes a few milligrams in a 500 mg pill. The remaining bulk, often the large majority, is excipient. Someone has to make that bulk to pharmaceutical standards, batch after identical batch. That someone is a company like Accent.

The jobs excipients do inside a tablet
Excipient roleWhat it does (why the pill needs it)
Filler / diluentBulks a tiny amount of active drug up to a handleable, pressable tablet size. MCC is the classic filler — it gives the pill its body.
BinderHolds the powder together so the compressed tablet doesn't crumble in the bottle. MCC is prized because it also binds — one ingredient, two jobs.
DisintegrantMakes the tablet break apart fast once it hits the stomach, so the drug is released. This is what croscarmellose sodium (CCS) does — a "super-disintegrant."
Glidant / lubricantHelps powder flow through high-speed tablet presses and stops it sticking to the machine. Magnesium stearate is the standard lubricant.
Flow / compaction aidCo-processed excipients (e.g. silicified MCC) are engineered blends that flow and compress better than the plain ingredient — a premium, higher-value grade.

Accent plays in every one of those boxes except the API itself. Its core is the filler/binder (MCC); it has added the disintegrant (CCS), the lubricant (magnesium stearate) and the engineered co-processed grades (silicified MCC, MCC spheres). Notice the shape of that list: it is a deliberate march from the biggest-volume, most commodity job (filler) toward the smaller-volume, higher-value, more technical jobs (super-disintegrants and co-processed blends). Hold that direction of travel — it is the whole margin story, and we return to it in section 08.

The surprise hiding in plain sight

Here is something most people never register: the ingredient that makes up the bulk of an enormous share of the world's tablets is manufactured by a handful of specialist firms almost nobody has heard of — and one of them is a company in Ahmedabad that only listed on a small-company exchange in December 2023. You have almost certainly swallowed Accent's category of product hundreds of times without knowing the category exists. The tablet industry is visible and branded; the powder that physically constitutes most of the tablet is invisible and unbranded. That invisibility is exactly why it is a quietly attractive place to sit: unglamorous, essential, and hard to displace once you are approved into a drug's recipe.

02

Microcrystalline cellulose: a pharmaceutical made from wood

What MCC is, where it comes from, and why the raw material sits in a forest.

Microcrystalline cellulose sounds exotic; its origin is not. It is a purified, refined form of cellulose — the same structural fibre that makes up the walls of every plant cell. Accent's feedstock is high-grade wood pulp: purified sheets of cellulose sourced from managed forests. The MCC-maker's craft is to take that pulp, chemically break the long cellulose fibres down to their crystalline core, wash and purify it to pharmaceutical cleanliness, and then dry it into a fine, odourless, off-white powder with tightly controlled particle size.

Accent occupies Stage 2 only — the conversion step. Its cost is set upstream by wood-pulp prices (Stage 1); its price is set by what certified formulators will pay (Stage 3). The gap between the two is the business.

Why is MCC so ubiquitous? Because it is unusually good at several jobs at once and is remarkably inert and safe. It compresses beautifully into hard, stable tablets; it binds; it can help a tablet disintegrate; it is chemically unreactive, so it won't degrade the drug it carries; and it works in wet-granulation, dry-granulation and direct-compression manufacturing alike. That versatility is why formulators reach for it by default, and why a good MCC comes in many grades — different particle sizes and moisture levels tuned for different tablet recipes and machines. Accent's 22 grades exist precisely because a single "MCC" would not fit the thousands of different formulations its customers run.

The particle-size point that makes "22 grades" meaningful

To a casual eye, MCC is just white powder. To a formulator, the grade is everything: a coarse, free-flowing grade suits a high-speed direct-compression line where powder must pour like sand, while a fine grade suits a different tablet texture and dissolution profile. Get the grade wrong and tablets cap, crack, flow poorly or release the drug at the wrong rate. So an MCC supplier is not selling one product — it is selling a library of tightly specified grades, each validated for particular customers and formulations. Breadth of grades is a competitive weapon: it lets Accent be the single approved supplier across many of a customer's products, which multiplies the switching cost of ever replacing it.

03

Why pharma-grade excipients are a fortress, not a commodity

The single most important idea in the investment case — the approval wall.

If MCC were just a bulk powder, anyone with a chemical plant could flood the market and margins would collapse to nothing. They have not, and the reason is regulatory. A pharmaceutical excipient is not judged only on what it is, but on how consistently and traceably it is made, and whether the buyer's regulator has blessed that source. That transforms a simple chemical into a certified, defended niche.

Walk through what a drugmaker actually needs from an excipient supplier, and the wall builds itself:

  • Regulatory documentation (the US-DMF). To sell an excipient into US-regulated drug manufacturing, a supplier files a Drug Master File with the US FDA — a confidential dossier describing exactly how the material is made and controlled. A drugmaker's own filing then references that DMF. Accent holding a US-DMF is not a marketing badge; it is the ticket that lets its material be written into a regulated drug's recipe.
  • Good Manufacturing Practice & independent certification. GMP, plus schemes such as EXCiPACT (an excipient-specific GMP/GDP certification) and ISO, tell a customer's auditors that every batch is made under controlled, documented conditions. Kosher and Halal certificates open food, nutraceutical and regional pharma markets.
  • Batch-to-batch consistency. A formulation validated on one supplier's MCC assumes that supplier's MCC will behave identically forever. Drift in particle size or purity can force costly re-validation. Reliability, not novelty, is the product.
  • Validation & audit time. Qualifying a new excipient source into an existing drug can take many months to years of testing, documentation and regulatory change control. That time is a cost the customer pays — and therefore a reason not to switch away from an incumbent that works.
Why the approval wall cuts in Accent's favour once it is inside

Put the buyer's incentives together and a striking asymmetry appears. Before approval, Accent must invest years and money to get validated into a customer's product — a real barrier to entry. But after approval, that same barrier protects it: the customer now faces the identical years-and-money cost to replace Accent, for a low-value ingredient that is working fine. So a drugmaker will haggle on price at the margin but rarely rip out a qualified excipient supplier over a few percent. The result is unusually sticky, recurring, low-drama revenue — the commercial texture of a component supplier whose component is cheap, essential and painful to change. The whole strategic game is to get inside as many customer formulations as possible and then simply not give them a reason to leave.

The nuance that keeps it honest: this moat is real but local, not absolute. It protects Accent product-by-product and customer-by-customer, where it is already the approved source. It does not stop a large, well-certified rival from winning the next new formulation, and it does not let Accent charge monopoly prices — the buyer always has other qualified suppliers to benchmark against. The moat lowers churn and steadies margins; it does not remove competition. That distinction matters for how you value the business.

Accent's grade ladder: from bulk filler to specialty disintegrant

The product family, arranged by value — and the deliberate climb up it.

Accent's portfolio is best read not as a list but as a ladder, from the highest-volume, most commodity-like product at the bottom to the smaller-volume, higher-value, more technical products at the top. The company's stated strategy — and the crux of its margin trajectory — is to keep shifting its mix up that ladder.

Two things are worth pausing on. First, the one-stop-shop logic: by offering filler, disintegrant, lubricant and co-processed grades together, Accent can become a customer's single approved excipient partner rather than one of several — deepening the switching-cost moat from section 03. Second, the deliberate premiumisation: the biggest new capacity Accent is building is for croscarmellose sodium and other disintegrants, not more plain MCC. That is a conscious tilt toward the top of the ladder, where per-kilogram realisation and margin are structurally higher.

Why the tiny disintegrant matters more than the bulky filler

The counter-intuitive economics of an excipient maker: the product you use the most of is the one you make the least margin on, and the product you use a pinch of is where the money is. Standard MCC is the bulk of the tonnage but the thinnest spread — it is the closest thing here to a commodity. Croscarmellose sodium is a small dose in each tablet, but at a reported ~3.5× the price per kilogram it drops far more margin per tonne produced. So Accent's growth is not just "sell more powder"; it is "sell a richer basket of powder." A tonne of capacity pointed at CCS is worth several tonnes pointed at plain MCC. That single mix lever, more than headline volume, is what can bend the margin line upward as the new plant fills.

05

The company, the plants, and the SME listing

A twelve-year-old Gujarat specialty firm that reached the public market via NSE Emerge.

Accent Microcell is a relatively young, promoter-led company. Founded in 2012 and headquartered in Ahmedabad, Gujarat, it built itself into a multi-grade MCC exporter before taking the unusual-for-its-size step of a public listing on the NSE Emerge SME platform in December 2023. Understanding that listing status is important: this is a small-company exchange, with different liquidity, disclosure cadence and investor base from the NSE/BSE mainboard.

The manufacturing footprint

  • Unit-1 — Pirana Road, Ahmedabad. The founding site; core MCC manufacturing.
  • Unit-2 — Dahej SEZ, Bharuch. A Special Economic Zone location, useful for an export-oriented maker (SEZ status carries logistics and duty advantages for goods shipped abroad).
  • Unit-3 — Navagam / Nayka Kheda (new). The growth engine: purpose-built for premium disintegrants (croscarmellose sodium), sodium starch glycolate and CMC, plus a large standard-MCC block. This is where the mix-shift and the volume leap both come from.

Ownership

  • Promoter-controlled. The founding promoter group holds roughly 53% (as of mid-2026), with the balance in public hands. A concentrated promoter stake is typical for a company of this vintage and size; investors should confirm the exact figure and any pledge in the latest shareholding filing.
  • SME-board context. As an NSE Emerge company it trades with thinner liquidity and a smaller institutional following than a mainboard peer. That can mean sharper price moves in both directions, and it makes the eventual question of a migration to the mainboard (a path SME companies can take once they meet size/track-record criteria) a real future catalyst to watch.
Why the listing venue is part of the thesis: the SME tag is double-edged. On one side, it flatters some ratios — the stock's ~27× multiple and ~65% one-year price run reflect an enthusiastic, retail-heavy SME market that can re-rate small profitable names hard. On the other, it means less liquidity, less analyst scrutiny and more valuation volatility than the fundamentals alone would justify. A mainboard migration, if and when it comes, would widen the investor base — but it would also expose the stock to a more demanding crowd. Size the position for the venue, not just the business.

The export engine and the import-substitution story

More than half its revenue is earned abroad — a strength and a specific exposure.

For a company most Indians have never heard of, Accent is strikingly global. A clear majority of its revenue — around two-thirds in recent years — is exported, reaching 75+ countries and 200+ customers. That export tilt is central to both the opportunity and the risk profile, and it is the clearest sign that Accent is competing on quality and certification, not just on being cheap and local.

Two forces sit behind that export orientation. The first is a genuine global market for certified excipients: pharma and nutraceutical manufacturers worldwide need reliable, US-DMF-backed MCC and disintegrants, and a well-certified Indian maker can win that business on quality and price against the global majors. The second is import-substitution running in reverse and forward at once — India historically imported a good deal of its pharma excipients, so a domestic maker like Accent both displaces imports at home and exports to markets that value a diversified, cost-competitive supply source outside the incumbent Western producers.

The double edge of earning abroad

An export-heavy model is, for a small company, mostly a compliment: it means Accent has cleared the certification bars that let it sell into demanding regulated markets, and it earns hard currency that flatters margins when the rupee is soft. But the same fact concentrates specific risks. Revenue now rides on foreign-exchange swings, on trade policy and tariffs in destination markets, and on the health of overseas customers Accent cannot easily visit or replace. And because the raw material (wood pulp) is also largely imported, the company is exposed to global freight and currency on both the buy and the sell side. The export engine is what makes Accent more than a local commodity player — and also what ties its fortunes to conditions well beyond Gujarat.

Domestically, there is a concentration wrinkle worth naming: Accent's Indian sales have historically clustered in a handful of states (Gujarat, Maharashtra, Telangana, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand — the pharma-manufacturing belts). That is logical — it sells where the drug factories are — but it means the domestic third of revenue leans on a few regional customer clusters rather than a broad national spread.

07

The wood-pulp spine: the cost the company doesn't control

Where the money goes out — and why one imported raw material sets the floor on margin.

If the approval wall is what protects Accent's price, wood pulp is what governs its cost. MCC is made overwhelmingly from purified cellulose pulp, and Accent sources premium grades of it — the kind consistent and clean enough for pharmaceutical use — largely from outside India, from suppliers in the USA, South Africa and Sweden. That single input is the spine of the cost base, and it is not one Accent authors.

This creates a familiar specialty-chemicals shape: a defended, sticky, value-added output sitting on top of a globally-priced, imported, cyclical input. When pulp prices are benign and the rupee steady, the spread between Accent's certified selling price and its pulp bill is comfortable — which is much of the story behind the margin step-up to ~17% EBITDA. When pulp prices spike, or freight and currency move against it, that spread compresses until Accent can pass the cost through — and passing cost through to price-sensitive formulators takes time and negotiating power.

The dependency a first-timer should sit with

India grows relatively little of the high-purity wood pulp that pharmaceutical MCC demands, so Accent must import its most important raw material — and the sources are concentrated in a few countries. Read Accent's gross margin each year as a live readout of that spread — it is the most revealing single gauge of whether the business is being paid well for its conversion craft, or quietly giving it back to the pulp market.

08

The value equation: volume × grade-mix × the pulp spread

The single most important idea in the case — and it fits only an excipient maker like Accent.

Strip Accent to its economic core and its worth reduces to one compact expression. The value the company creates in a year is essentially: the tonnes of excipient it sells, multiplied by how rich the grade-mix is (how much premium CCS and co-processed material rather than plain MCC), multiplied by the spread it keeps over its imported wood-pulp cost. Every part of this primer feeds one of those three terms. Get all three moving up together — which FY24 and FY25 did — and a small company compounds fast. Let any one stall and the growth flatters to deceive.

Now see why this framing is not transferable to a generic "capacity ramp" story. A plain-vanilla manufacturer grows by adding tonnes at roughly constant margin. Accent has a second, more powerful lever built into its product ladder: it can grow value faster than volume by pointing new capacity at the top rungs. The Unit-3 plant is designed to do exactly that — its headline new products are the premium disintegrants, not more commodity filler. So when the plant fills, revenue should rise faster than tonnage, and margin faster than revenue, if the mix-shift lands as designed. That "if" is the whole debate.

The device that only fits Accent: value = tonnes × grade-mix × pulp spread

Forget "sales grew X%." The number that runs Accent Microcell is (tonnes sold) × (how premium the grade-mix is) × (selling price − imported pulp cost). Each term has its own driver on its own clock. Tonnes are set by capacity and utilisation — the Unit-3 ramp. Grade-mix is set by how fast CCS and co-processed grades grow as a share of the whole — the premiumisation strategy. The pulp spread is set by global cellulose prices and the rupee — the one term Accent cannot author. A year where all three rise is FY25. A year where the plant ramps (tonnes up) but pulp spikes (spread down) and the mix stays commodity (grade-mix flat) could show more revenue and less profit. The top line will not tell you which world you are in; the product of the three terms will.

09

The capacity leap: the small-cap's big bet

Roughly doubling-and-more the plant — the event that will make or unmake the next few years.

Accent has been running its existing plants close to full utilisation — a good problem, but a hard ceiling on growth. You cannot sell tonnes you cannot make. The company's answer is the Unit-3 expansion at Navagam / Nayka Kheda, a phased build designed to lift total capacity from roughly 9,200 MTPA toward ~24,000 MTPA — a step-change for a company this size.

The sequencing here is the tell. A less thoughtful expansion would simply bolt on more of the commodity product. Accent's plan leads with the high-value disintegrants — croscarmellose sodium, sodium starch glycolate, CMC — the very grades that sell at a multiple of plain MCC. That means the early revenue from Unit-3 should carry a richer margin than the company average, before the bulk standard-MCC block adds the volume ballast. If it works, the expansion lifts both the "tonnes" and the "grade-mix" terms of the value equation at once.

Why an expansion this size is the real risk and the real reward

For a company of Accent's scale, more than doubling capacity is a genuine bet-the-few-years move, and it deserves respect on both sides. The reward: if certified demand absorbs the new tonnes — especially the premium disintegrants — Accent breaks through its utilisation ceiling and compounds from a bigger, richer base, funded largely without heavy debt. The risk: new pharma capacity does not fill itself. Every new grade from a new line must be re-qualified by customers' regulators before it can sell into regulated drugs — the same approval wall that protects Accent slows down how fast it can monetise brand-new capacity. So the danger is a timing gap: fixed costs and depreciation arriving with the plant, while validated demand catches up quarter by quarter. Utilisation of Unit-3, at the premium grades, is the number to watch.

The industry and the rivals

A concentrated global excipient market — a domestic peer and a set of Western majors.

The MCC and excipient world is a consolidated one, precisely because the certification and consistency barriers keep casual entrants out. Accent competes on two fronts: against a close domestic peer for the "Indian MCC champion" story, and against long-established global majors for the world's regulated-market business.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

The excipient market rewards two things above all: certification breadth (how many regulated markets and grades you are approved into) and cost position (how cheaply you convert pulp to certified powder). Indian makers like Accent and Sigachi win on the second while steadily building the first, taking share from higher-cost Western incumbents in export markets that value a diversified, competitively-priced supply source. Accent's specific angle is its grade breadth plus the deliberate premium tilt toward disintegrants. It will not out-scale DFE or Sigachi soon; its game is to be the nimble, well-certified specialist that a formulator is happy to qualify in and reluctant to swap out — winning the niche one approved product at a time rather than by brute volume.

A high-quality small-cap on a rich multiple — the question is whether the ramp earns it.

At around 27× earnings the market is not paying for what Accent is today; it is paying for the capacity expansion and premium mix to compound the business meaningfully from here.

The quality markers are genuine. Return on capital in the mid-20s percent says Accent earns good money on the assets it deploys — a sign the certified-niche model works. Near-zero debt means the big expansion is not a leveraged gamble that a bad year could threaten. The one number that settles the thesis: Unit-3 utilisation at premium grades

At ~27× earnings, the case does not rest on Accent being cheap; it rests on the expansion converting into sold, premium tonnes. So the metric that proves or breaks it is the utilisation of the new Unit-3 capacity, weighted toward the high-value disintegrant grades — read quarter by quarter through two companions: blended EBITDA margin (is the grade-mix actually richening, or is the plant just adding commodity volume?) and gross margin (is the pulp spread holding?). If Unit-3 fills with CCS and co-processed grades and margin drifts up from ~17%, the rich multiple is defensible and earnings can grow into a much larger base. If the new lines qualify slowly and sell mostly plain MCC while pulp costs bite, the market will re-rate a single-digit-growth small-cap hard and fast. Tonnes made premium, and paid for above the pulp bill — that is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A quality niche compounder, richly priced, betting on a big expansion it must fill.

Accent Microcell enters its next phase with a clean balance sheet, a defensible certified niche, a proven margin trajectory and a large new plant coming online tilted toward its highest-value products. What it must now prove is that a small SME-listed specialist can absorb a more-than-doubling of capacity — and do it at the premium end — without the ramp outrunning validated demand or the wood-pulp market clawing back the margin. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Defended, sticky niche — certified pharma excipients are painful to switch out once approved; recurring, low-drama, picks-and-shovels revenue across the whole drug industry.
  • Premiumisation lever — CCS at ~3.5× plain MCC and co-processed grades let Accent grow value faster than volume; Unit-3 leads with these grades.
  • Clean, capital-efficient balance sheet — ~25% RoCE and near-zero debt; the expansion is not a leveraged gamble.
  • Global demand & import-substitution — ~65% export to 75+ countries; a cost-competitive certified Indian maker taking share from Western incumbents.
  • Optionality from the venue — a future mainboard migration could widen the investor base and lift liquidity for a proven small-cap.

The bear case

  • Expensive on cooled growth — ~27× earnings after FY25 organic growth slowed to single digits; the price needs Unit-3 to deliver on schedule.
  • Imported wood-pulp exposure — the key raw material is a concentrated, cyclical import; a price spike or supply shock compresses the spread the margin rests on.
  • Ramp / re-qualification risk — new lines must be re-approved by customers' regulators before they can sell; fixed costs can arrive before validated demand.
  • FX & trade-policy exposure — an export-heavy small-cap is at the mercy of currency swings and tariffs in destination markets.
  • SME-board and concentration risks — thin liquidity, retail-driven volatility, a ~53% promoter stake, and domestic sales clustered in a few states.
The bottom line

Accent Microcell is a rare thing on the SME board — a genuinely high-quality small business, making an essential, invisible ingredient the drug industry cannot do without, at strong returns on capital and almost no debt. The niche is real and the premiumisation strategy is smart: point new capacity at the disintegrants that earn multiples of the commodity powder. What tempers the enthusiasm is arithmetic — a ~27× multiple already assumes the big Unit-3 expansion lands at the premium end and the pulp spread behaves. The next test is concrete and datable: whether Unit-3's premium croscarmellose-sodium and co-processed lines qualify with customers and fill through FY26–FY27, lifting blended margin above FY25's ~17% as the standard-MCC block adds volume behind them. Fill that plant with certified, high-value tonnes and a small compounder grows into a much larger one; leave it ramping slowly while pulp costs bite, and the market will remember that a single-digit-growth small-cap does not stay at 27× for long. The powder is invisible; the plant utilisation will not be.
