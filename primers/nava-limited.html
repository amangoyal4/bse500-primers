00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Nava Limited (formerly Nava Bharat Ventures) is a Hyderabad-based diversified group controlled by the Devineni family. On paper it is one of India's older ferro-alloy makers. In practice, most of the profit comes from somewhere unexpected: a 300 MW coal-fired power station in Zambia, southern Africa, that Nava built next to its own coal mine and that sells electricity to the Zambian state grid under a long-term contract. Around that African core sit Indian ferro alloys, some Indian power, coal mining, and newer bets in agriculture and healthcare.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The engine is in Africa, not India. Through a 65%-owned subsidiary, Nava owns Maamba — Zambia's largest coal mine and a mine-mouth power plant that supplies roughly a tenth of the country's installed electricity. That plant runs at high utilisation, earns in US dollars, and throws off cash far out of proportion to Nava's Indian factories. It is the reason a "ferro-alloy company" reports a ~44% EBITDA margin — a number no ferro-alloy maker on earth actually earns from ferro alloys.
  • The catch is a single customer in a stressed country. The plant's entire revenue flows through one buyer — Zambia's state utility ZESCO — inside a country that defaulted on its sovereign debt in 2020. For roughly three years ZESCO struggled to pay, arrears swelled past half a billion dollars, and Nava had to win a London arbitration to force settlement. That fight — now largely resolved — is why the market has long applied a discount.
  • FY26 was a solid operating year with a noisy bottom line. Consolidated revenue rose ~8% to ~₹4,291 Cr and EBITDA stayed high at ~₹1,905 Cr, but reported net profit fell to ~₹1,039 Cr (from ₹1,434 Cr) largely on a higher tax charge, not weaker operations. The company paid a record ₹8.50 dividend, cut debt, and pushed ahead with a second 300 MW unit at Maamba, targeted for around January 2027.
In plain terms

Nava is an Indian-listed wrapper around an African infrastructure annuity. The ferro-alloy plants near Hyderabad are the historical identity and still earn real money in good years, but they are cyclical and thin-margin. The thing that makes Nava unusual — and unusually profitable — is that it dug a coal mine in Zambia, built a power station on top of it, signed a 20-year contract to sell the electricity, and now collects a hard-currency cheque from an essential piece of a power-short country's grid. The whole investment case turns on one question that has nothing to do with ferro alloys: does the plant keep running, and does Zambia keep paying?

01

The whole group on one map

Read this once and the odd shape of Nava clicks — it's a portfolio, weighted heavily to one asset.

Nava is a conglomerate, but a lopsided one. It runs five kinds of business across two continents, yet the earnings are dominated by a single subsidiary. The cleanest way to hold it in your head is: a modest Indian industrial base (ferro alloys + power + mining) that spins off cash, and a large African power asset (Maamba) that spins off far more cash — plus a handful of small, early-stage ventures the group is seeding with that cash.

Why the shape matters

Most Indian conglomerates are diversified within India — a bank here, a cement plant there, all riding the same domestic economy. Nava's diversification runs the other way: its single most important asset is 6,000 km away, sells into a different currency, and answers to a different government. That gives shareholders something rare — a dollar-denominated, essential-infrastructure earnings stream uncorrelated with Indian steel demand — and something uncomfortable: a concentration of profit in one plant, one customer, and one African country's ability to pay its bills. The rest of this primer is really about weighing those two things against each other.

02

A mine-mouth power plant, explained from scratch

No energy background needed — just why this particular kind of plant is a cash machine.

To understand Nava you first have to understand what an independent power producer (IPP) with a mine-mouth plant and a power purchase agreement (PPA) actually earns from. Three ideas do almost all the work.

1. The PPA — a 20-year contract, not a spot market

Maamba doesn't sell electricity into a fluctuating market the way an Indian merchant plant does. It sells under a long-term power purchase agreement with ZESCO, the Zambian state utility. A PPA fixes the buyer, the tariff formula and the tenor (here, 20 years). Crucially, most of the tariff is a capacity charge — the buyer pays for the plant being available, whether or not it draws the full power — plus an energy charge for fuel actually burned. That structure is what turns a power plant into an annuity: as long as the plant is available and the counterparty pays, the cash arrives on a schedule.

2. Mine-mouth — the fuel never rides a train

A coal-fired plant's most expensive, most fragile input is usually getting the coal to the boiler: rail freight, handling and evacuation logistics that can rival the fuel's own cost. Maamba dodges nearly all of it because the power plant sits on top of its own coal mine — the fuel travels a conveyor, not a country. "Mine-mouth" means the mine and the plant are one integrated site, so Nava controls both the fuel price and the fuel supply. In a landlocked country with thin rail infrastructure, that integration is a genuine structural edge.

3. Availability (PLF) — the number the whole thing hinges on

The single operating metric that matters is how much of the time the plant can run — its plant load factor / availability. Maamba has sustained a PLF around 90%, which is high for a coal plant and means it is reliably delivering the capacity ZESCO contracted for. High availability + a capacity-charge PPA + captive fuel = a stream of dollar cash flows that looks far more like a toll road than like a commodity factory.

The one relationship that governs Maamba's P&L

Cash from the plant ≈ (capacity available × contracted tariff) − (own coal cost + O&M) − (whatever ZESCO fails to pay). Notice where the risk lives. The first bracket is engineering — keep the plant running, and Maamba controls that well (~90% PLF). The second is cheap, because the coal is captive. It is the third bracket — collection — that has decided Nava's fortunes. A plant can run flawlessly and still starve if the state utility on the other end of the wire can't pay its bill. For most of the last decade, that third bracket, not the boiler, was the story.

Why does Zambia need this plant so badly? Because Zambia's grid is overwhelmingly hydroelectric — dependent on the Kariba dam and the rains. In drought years the water drops, hydro output collapses, and the country suffers crippling blackouts. A large, dispatchable thermal plant like Maamba is exactly the drought insurance a hydro-heavy grid lacks. That is why Maamba is treated as strategic national infrastructure — and why, even through the payment crisis, shutting it off was never really an option for Zambia.

03

The company and who controls it

A half-century Hyderabad industrial house that made an outsized bet on Africa.

Nava began in 1972 as a single ferro-silicon smelter in what is now Telangana, promoted by a group led by Dr D. Subba Rao, a metallurgist. Over five decades it added silico-manganese, sugar and captive power — a fairly ordinary Indian industrial story. The extraordinary chapter began in 2010, when the group took over Maamba Collieries in Zambia and, over the following years, sank close to US$1 billion into building a coal mine and power plant there. Today the company is led by Chairman D. Ashok and his son, Group CEO Ashwin Devineni, who has driven the global expansion.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters (Devineni family): ~50% as of mid-2026 — a controlling but not overwhelming stake, notably lower than the 70%+ typical of Indian promoter groups. Foreign institutions hold ~10%, and the public float is a substantial ~39% — an unusually large free float for a family-run mid-cap.
  • The Zambia holding chain: Maamba is held through Nava Bharat (Singapore), which owns 65% of Maamba Energy Limited (MEL, formerly Maamba Collieries). The other 35% belongs to ZCCM-IH — the Zambian government's mining-investment arm. So the Zambian state is simultaneously Nava's partner in the plant and, via ZESCO, its customer.
  • Balance sheet: the group has de-levered sharply — consolidated borrowings fell to ~₹2,226 Cr in FY26 from ~₹3,074 Cr a year earlier, helped by ZESCO collections. It is cash-generative, pays dividends, and has funded a Nava Global buyback (US$50 Mn upstreamed to the parent in FY26). Book value is ~₹309/share; the stock trades under 2× book.
The quirk worth pausing on: Nava's 35% partner in Maamba, ZCCM-IH, is controlled by the same Zambian state that owns ZESCO, the utility that spent three years failing to pay Maamba. The Zambian government sits on both sides of Nava's most important asset — co-owner and delinquent buyer at once. That dual role cuts both ways: it is a governance entanglement, but it also gave Zambia every incentive to settle rather than let its own flagship IPP fail.
04

Maamba — the crown jewel

The first bespoke crux: what exactly Nava owns on Lake Kariba, and why it's worth so much.

Everything distinctive about Nava lives in one place: Maamba, in Zambia's Southern Province, near Lake Kariba on the Zimbabwe border. Here Nava owns the country's largest coal mine and, sitting on top of it, a 300 MW coal-fired power station (two 150 MW units) that supplies roughly 10% of Zambia's installed generating capacity. It is the largest thermal IPP in the country, and — for a nation whose grid is otherwise almost entirely hydro — a piece of strategic drought insurance.

The device: a ~44% margin that isn't a metals margin

Here is the single figure that gives the game away. Nava reports a group EBITDA margin around 44%. No ferro-alloy producer earns that — silico-manganese is a cut-throat, price-taking commodity where 15–20% is a good year. So where does a 44% margin come from? Almost entirely from Maamba's power business, which carries the fat, capacity-charge economics of a contracted IPP. Run the logic backwards and it tells you the truth about Nava: strip out the plant on Lake Kariba and you're left with an average Indian ferro-alloy company on a mid-teens margin. The 44% is Zambia. The margin line, more than any org chart, is where you can see that this is an African power company wearing an Indian metals company's name.

Why a mid-cap Indian firm owning this is genuinely unusual

Owning essential power infrastructure in a foreign country is normally the preserve of sovereign wealth funds, development banks and global IPP majors — not a Hyderabad ferro-alloy house. Nava got there by being early (2010) and patient (a decade of build-out and a payment crisis it had to litigate through). What it now holds is scarce: a dollar-earning, essential-infrastructure asset in a power-short country, protected by a long PPA and a captive fuel source. Assets like that, when the counterparty pays, are valued as annuities. The entire debate over Nava's share price is whether the market should value Maamba like the annuity it resembles — or keep discounting it for the country it sits in.

05

The ZESCO payment saga — the discount, explained

The second bespoke crux, and where both the surprise and the core risk live.

A perfect plant is worthless if the customer won't pay. For roughly three years, that was Nava's reality — and it is the reason the stock has spent much of its life cheap. Maamba sells all its power to one buyer, ZESCO, a state utility inside a country that defaulted on its sovereign debt in November 2020. As Zambia's finances unravelled, ZESCO stopped paying its bills in full, and the money it owed Maamba piled up.

How it was resolved

Rather than wait, Maamba took ZESCO to international arbitration in London in 2020. In 2022 the tribunal issued a consent award under which ZESCO agreed to discharge outstanding arrears of roughly US$518 million. Because ZESCO's obligations are effectively backed by the Zambian state, and because Zambia has been renegotiating its wider debt with international creditors, the settlement has been honoured in steady instalments. By FY26, Nava reported that more than 90% of the arrears had been recovered, with the outstanding balance down to around US$30 million — and it was those dollar collections that let the group cut debt, raise the dividend and keep funding expansion.

The surprise a generalist should carry away

The instinct is to see "coal plant in Zambia" and assume the risk is the coal, or the plant, or African operating conditions. It isn't — the plant runs at ~90% and the coal is captive. The real exposure is a US$518 million IOU from a defaulted sovereign's utility — a sum equal to roughly a third of Nava's entire market capitalisation. For a stretch, this company's single largest asset was, in effect, an overdue receivable from the government of Zambia, enforceable only through a London tribunal. That is the counter-intuitive heart of Nava: the danger was never operational; it was a collection problem the size of the company itself. As that IOU has been paid down, the discount has begun to lift — and if it stays paid, the annuity is what remains.

The risk hasn't vanished — it has narrowed. Zambia remains a low-income, drought-exposed economy still working through its sovereign-debt restructuring. A future fiscal shock, a currency collapse in the kwacha, or a change in government energy policy could all reopen the payment question. The arrears are largely cleared, but the structure — one plant, one state off-taker, one fragile sovereign — is permanent. That is the non-diversifiable risk you accept when you own Nava, and no amount of Indian ferro-alloy diversification cancels it.
06

Phase II — doubling the plant

The single clearest growth lever, and a datable milestone to judge management by.

Nava's biggest near-term catalyst is not in India. Maamba is building a second 300 MW unit (Phase II) alongside the first, which would roughly double the plant to ~600 MW and correspondingly enlarge the dollar cash flow. Nava committed fresh equity to it — including a US$65 million contribution toward Phase II's equity — and, as of the FY26 results, the mechanical and electrical erection was progressing toward a targeted commissioning of around January 2027.

Why this matters so much: Phase II adds capacity to an asset whose economics are already proven and whose fuel is already captive. The mine can feed a second boiler; the grid connection exists; the off-take framework is established. If the new unit signs a PPA on terms similar to the first and Zambia keeps paying, Phase II is close to a repeat of the most profitable thing Nava owns — but built at brownfield cost and speed, next to infrastructure that's already there.

The catch on Phase II

Doubling down on Maamba also doubles down on the concentration. Every rupee Nava invests in a second Zambian unit deepens its dependence on the same country, the same grid and the same off-taker that caused the arrears crisis. The bull reading is that Zambia demonstrably needs more dispatchable power and has every reason to contract for it; the bear reading is that Nava is compounding a single-country, single-customer exposure rather than diversifying away from it. Phase II is simultaneously the best growth Nava has and the sharpest expression of its central risk. The January-2027 commissioning date is the first hard test of which reading is right.

07

Ferro alloys — the Indian core

The legacy business that gave Nava its name — cyclical, exported, and no longer the profit driver.

Nava's Indian identity is ferro alloys — chiefly silico-manganese and ferro-manganese, the manganese-based additives that steelmakers blend into molten steel to give it strength and remove impurities. There is no steel without ferro alloys, but they are a small, price-taking slice of the steel chain. Nava is one of India's established producers, running furnaces in Telangana and Odisha and exporting a meaningful share of output.

How a ferro-alloy maker earns — and why it's volatile

The product is a global commodity sold by the tonne, so the only lever a producer fully controls is cost — above all electricity, which can be 40–50% of the cost of a tonne of silico-manganese, since smelting is brutally power-hungry. Nava's edge here is the same instinct that drove the Zambia bet: it pairs its alloy furnaces with captive power, so it can keep smelting profitably when merchant rivals paying grid tariffs are squeezed. Even so, the segment is genuinely cyclical: manganese-ore prices, Chinese steel demand, alloy realisations and the rupee all swing independently, and the spread can compress from either end. Ferro-alloy income rose ~29% in FY26 on higher volumes — a good year — but "good" here still means mid-teens margins, a world apart from Maamba.

Where ferro alloys fit in the story

Read the alloy business as the cash-generative base that funded the African adventure, not as the reason to own the stock today. It is a competent, integrated, export-capable metals operation — but it sits in a commodity where nobody earns annuity margins, and its swings are dwarfed by what happens at Maamba. When a Nava quarter surprises, it is almost never the ferro alloys that did it. The segment matters for continuity and for the Indian balance sheet; the valuation debate is settled elsewhere.

The rest of the group — power, mining, and the new bets

The smaller limbs: Indian power & mining, plus early-stage agri and healthcare.

Beyond Maamba and ferro alloys, Nava runs a cluster of smaller businesses — some mature and supportive, some brand-new and speculative. None moves the profit needle much yet, but together they show how the group is redeploying its African cash.

It is early, and the amounts are small relative to Maamba. The capital-allocation question: with Maamba throwing off cash and the arrears cleared, the pivotal choice is what Nava does with it — return it (dividends/buybacks, as in FY26), reinvest in proven power (Phase II), or fund unproven new verticals (agri, healthcare). So far the mix has leaned toward the first two, which is reassuring. The risk in any cash-rich conglomerate is that surplus funds chase diversification for its own sake; Nava's newer bets are where that discipline will be tested.
09

The industry and the rivals

Nava sits between two worlds — Indian ferro alloys and African power — so it has no single clean peer.

Because Nava is a metals company by heritage and a power company by economics, it doesn't map onto any one comparable set. On alloys it competes with Indian ferro-alloy houses; on power its economics resemble emerging-market IPPs; and its African footprint puts it in a category almost no other Indian listed company occupies.

The comparison that actually clarifies it

Set Nava beside Sarda Energy — the nearest Indian relative — and the difference is geography, not structure. Both bolt a power business onto a metals business; both earn most of their profit from the electrons, not the alloys. But Sarda's power sits inside India's grid and rupee, subject to Indian regulation and Indian demand. Nava's sits in Zambia, earns dollars, and depends on a foreign sovereign's solvency. That single swap — Indian power for African power — is the entire investment difference: it is what gives Nava a higher, hard-currency margin and a country risk that Sarda simply doesn't carry. You are not choosing between two ferro-alloy stocks; you are choosing whether you want the Zambia exposure at all.

Financials & valuation

A cash-rich, high-margin business trading at a discount — and the debate over whether that discount is deserved.

How to weigh the valuation

At a ~₹16,700 Cr market cap, Nava trades under 2× book value and — on normalised earnings — at a high-single to low-teens multiple, well below what a contracted power annuity would fetch on its own. The market is applying a triple discount: a country discount (Zambia's sovereign fragility and the memory of the arrears), a coal/ESG discount (a coal-fired plant in an energy-transition world), and a conglomerate discount (a metals-plus-power-plus-agri jumble that no single analyst covers cleanly). The bull argument is that each of those discounts is now easing — arrears cleared, debt down, Phase II adding proven capacity — so a business earning 44% margins and paying record dividends is mispriced. The bear argument is that the discounts are rational insurance against a genuinely non-diversifiable Zambia risk that one bad year could reprice violently.

The metric that settles it: dollars actually banked from ZESCO each quarter, Maamba's plant availability (PLF), and the Phase-II commissioning date. Not revenue, not the ferro-alloy spread — those are sideshows. If cash collections from ZESCO stay current, the plant holds ~90% availability, and the second 300 MW unit synchronises to the grid on schedule, Nava is a cash-compounding African power annuity that the market is discounting as a metals conglomerate. If collections stall or Phase II slips badly, the discount is the market being right about the country risk. Watch the cash-received line and the commissioning calendar; everything else is noise around them.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, cash-rich annuity — shadowed by a country it cannot diversify away from.

Nava's next chapter is unusually legible for a conglomerate: commission Maamba Phase II (~600 MW), keep collecting from ZESCO, return cash, and prove out the agri/healthcare bets. The upside case is a re-rating as the annuity is recognised; the downside case is a reminder that everything depends on one plant, one buyer and one fragile sovereign. Both live in the same set of numbers.

The bull case

  • A dollar-earning power annuity — a 300 MW mine-mouth plant on a 20-year PPA, ~90% PLF, ~44% group margins, in a power-short country that needs it.
  • The overhang is lifting — over 90% of ZESCO arrears recovered; debt cut sharply; the London award enforced in instalments.
  • Cheap and shareholder-friendly — under 2× book, record ₹8.50 dividend, buybacks upstreamed — with a modest promoter stake and large public float.
  • Proven, brownfield growth — Phase II roughly doubles Maamba at low incremental risk, on infrastructure already built.
  • Optionality on top — Zambian agriculture and SE-Asian healthcare give free call-options funded by African cash.

The bear case

  • Concentration + sovereign risk — one plant, one off-taker (ZESCO), one fragile, drought-exposed, recently-defaulted country. Non-diversifiable, and Phase II deepens it.
  • Forex exposure — kwacha, US dollar and rupee all sit between the plant and the reported profit; a currency shock can distort results.
  • Coal / ESG transition — the crown jewel is a coal-fired station, facing long-run financing and reputational headwinds.
  • Cyclical metals leg — the Indian ferro-alloy base swings with China and manganese prices, adding noise.
  • Conglomerate opacity — a metals + power + agri + healthcare mix across two continents is hard to model, and the FY26 tax-driven profit drop shows how noisy the bottom line can be.
The bottom line

Nava is one of the more unusual things on the Indian market: a ferro-alloy company by name whose fate is decided by a coal plant on Lake Kariba and by the solvency of the Zambian state. The operating story is quietly excellent — a high-availability plant, captive fuel, a 20-year contract, and an arrears crisis that has been ground down from over half a billion dollars to a rounding error. The risk that keeps it cheap is equally real and cannot be engineered away: strip out Zambia and there is no annuity; keep Zambia and you own its sovereign fragility. What makes the case datable rather than abstract is a single event on the calendar — the second 300 MW unit at Maamba synchronising to Zambia's grid around January 2027. Commission it on time, with ZESCO's cheques still clearing, and the company Nava has quietly become — an African power producer wearing a metals company's name — becomes impossible for the market to keep pricing as anything else.
