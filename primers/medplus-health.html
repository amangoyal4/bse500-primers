00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

MedPlus Health Services runs the green-signboard pharmacy shops you see clustered across South Indian towns — and, increasingly, beyond. It is India's second-largest pharmacy retail chain by store count (behind Apollo's pharmacy network), operating roughly 5,330 stores at the end of FY26 across 13 states and one union territory, all knitted to an online ordering and home-delivery layer that makes it an "omnichannel" (click-and-mortar) retailer rather than a plain chain of chemist shops. It sells prescription medicines, over-the-counter drugs, wellness and general merchandise, runs a growing diagnostics arm, and pushes its own higher-margin private-label products. It was founded in Hyderabad in 2006 by Gangadi Madhukar Reddy and listed in December 2021.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Selling medicines is a thin-margin business, so the model is volume × density. A pharmacy buys a strip of tablets and sells it for a regulated, modest markup; the gross margin on branded pharma is slim. MedPlus's FY26 gross margin was ~26.2% and its operating EBITDA margin just ~5.3%. You do not get rich on one bag of medicine — you get there by pushing enormous volume through a dense network at low cost per order, and by selling higher-margin things (private label, general merchandise, diagnostics) alongside the pills.
  • The reported margin deliberately understates the mature business. Here is the counter-intuitive heart of it. A brand-new MedPlus store loses money for months before it matures. In FY26 the company opened 618 net new stores, so a big slice of the network was young and loss-making — dragging the blended margin down. Strip to the mature stores (open 12+ months) and the store-level EBITDA margin was ~11.9%, more than double the blended 5.3%. The company is, in effect, taxing today's reported profit to plant tomorrow's.
  • FY26 was a strong year, and the stock is priced for a long runway. Revenue rose ~12%, gross margin expanded 180 bps as private label grew, and net profit jumped ~46% to ~₹220 Cr. But at a market cap near ₹9,600 Cr the stock trades on a rich multiple (a P/E in the high-30s) — the market is paying not for today's slim profit but for the maturing of hundreds of young stores and the climb of private label. That gap between today's optics and tomorrow's promise is the whole debate.
In plain terms

A MedPlus store is a low-margin machine that only works at scale. On any single box of medicine the shop keeps very little, so the business is built on three multipliers stacked on top of each other: how many stores it can pack into a region, how mature those stores are (a two-year-old store earns roughly double a new one), and how much of each basket is higher-margin private label rather than another company's branded pill. FY26's numbers look modest at the surface — 5% operating margin, 46% profit growth off a small base — precisely because the company keeps opening loss-making young stores to feed the first multiplier. The investment case is a bet that those three multipliers keep compounding faster than online discounters can chip away at the fourth thing that matters: price.

01

Pharmacy retail 101: how a chemist shop actually earns

No jargon needed — just the markup, the mix, and why one store is a hard business.

To understand MedPlus you have to first understand why running a single pharmacy is a surprisingly tough way to make money. A chemist buys medicines from distributors and sells them to walk-in customers at prices that are, for many drugs, effectively capped by regulation. The markup is real but modest, the rent and staff are fixed, and a lot of the shelf is slow-moving. The economics only turn attractive when you do three things at once: drive high volume, keep cost-to-serve low, and enrich the basket with better-margin products. MedPlus is a machine engineered around exactly those three.

The dependency that shapes the whole sector: medicines are a low-trust-to-substitute, high-frequency purchase

Two features of the product make pharmacy retail what it is. First, medicine is bought on trust and urgency — a patient wants the right drug, in stock, near home, right now, and will not gamble on a shop that might not have it. That rewards density and reliability over the lowest price, which is exactly the ground a physical chain can defend against a warehouse in another city. Second, it is high-frequency and often chronic — the diabetic, the hypertensive, the elderly parent come back every month — so the prize is a repeat relationship, not a one-off sale. A pharmacy that is always nearby, always stocked, and cheaper on the refill wins the household. MedPlus's entire design — dense clusters, an app that remembers your prescription, private-label refills at a discount — is aimed at owning that recurring monthly basket.

Who the customer is, and how they buy

  • The chronic-care household — the most valuable customer, buying the same maintenance medicines month after month. Predictable, loyal, and the natural target for private-label substitution and home delivery.
  • The acute walk-in — someone who needs an antibiotic or a paracetamol strip today. Values proximity and stock availability above all, which a dense store cluster supplies better than a distant online warehouse.
  • The wellness & OTC shopper — buying vitamins, supplements, personal care and general merchandise. This is the higher-margin, less-regulated part of the basket that lifts blended profitability.
  • The digital orderer — placing a refill on the app for same-day delivery from the neighbourhood store. Same customer, different channel; MedPlus wants to serve them through its own stores, not lose them to a pure-online rival.
02

The cluster moat: why packing stores in is the whole strategy

The single most important structural idea — grasp it and the rest of MedPlus follows.

Most people picture a retail chain expanding the way a flag is planted: one store here, one store there, spreading thin across the map. MedPlus does the opposite, and it is deliberate. It saturates one local geography with stores before moving to the next — a "cluster" strategy. The reason is not brand vanity; it is arithmetic. A dense cluster of shops sharing one warehouse, one delivery fleet and one advertising spend has a structurally lower cost per order than scattered outlets — and it is the only way to make fast home delivery pay.

Each advantage lowers cost or lifts sales for every store in the cluster — so density is self-reinforcing. This is why MedPlus fills a region before entering a new one, rather than spreading thin.

The delivery point deserves emphasis, because it is where the cluster moat and the online threat collide. A pure-online pharmacy fulfils your order from a large central warehouse, which is cheap to stock but slow and expensive to deliver the last few kilometres. MedPlus flips that: because a MedPlus store is likely within a couple of kilometres of the customer, the store itself becomes the fulfilment centre for online orders. The same physical footprint that captures walk-ins also enables same-day, low-cost delivery — an advantage that only exists because the network is dense. Thin the network and you lose both.

Why the map matters: the South-India concentration is a feature, not a limit

MedPlus grew up in and around Hyderabad and remains heavily concentrated in South India — Telangana, Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu form the dense core, with expansion pushing outward and increasingly into smaller (non-tier-1) towns. To a casual eye that looks like a limitation: a regional chain, not a national one. Read through the cluster lens, it is the source of the advantage. Deep saturation of a home region is what delivers the low logistics cost, the fast delivery and the brand density that make the unit economics work. A company that had instead sprinkled 5,000 stores evenly across all of India would have a weaker business, not a stronger one. The debate is whether MedPlus can replicate that home-region density in new clusters as fast and as profitably — because a half-built cluster carries the costs of density without yet earning its rewards.

03

The store J-curve: why a new shop is a loss before it's a profit

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a store-rolling retailer.

Here is the mechanism that explains why MedPlus's headline margin looks thin while insiders talk about the business being healthy. Every new store follows a J-curve: it opens with the full weight of rent, staff and fit-out cost but only a trickle of customers, so it loses money. As the neighbourhood learns it is there and the chronic-care households sign up, sales climb, the store crosses breakeven, and — once past its first year or so — it settles into a healthy store-level margin. A company opening hundreds of stores a year is therefore always carrying a cohort of young, loss-making shops that drag the blended number down.

The mature-store margin (~11.9%) is more than double the blended margin (~5.3%). The gap is the drag from young, sub-scale stores — a cost the company chooses to bear to keep growing the network. As young stores mature, the blended number should climb toward the mature one.

Now look at how much of the network sits in that young cohort. At the end of FY26, roughly 24% of all stores were less than two years old — nearly a quarter of the base still climbing its J-curve, still diluting the average. That is not a weakness to be fixed; it is the visible cost of an aggressive rollout. The company's own historic disclosures show its stores mature quickly by retail standards — a large majority reach breakeven within the first several months — so the young cohort is not a graveyard of bad sites; it is a pipeline of tomorrow's mature-margin stores that simply have not aged in yet.

The device that only fits a store-rolling pharmacy: the same shop, one year apart

Take one MedPlus store and photograph its P&L twice. On opening day it carries full rent and a full staff roster against a thin stream of first customers — it loses money. Twelve to eighteen months later, in the identical premises with the identical shelves, the neighbourhood's chronic-care households have made it their monthly stop, footfall has multiplied, and that same store now earns a store-level EBITDA margin near 12% on an operating return on capital the company puts around 80%. Nothing about the store changed except its age. That single before-and-after is the entire company in miniature: MedPlus's reported ~5.3% margin is not the economics of a MedPlus store — it is the economics of a MedPlus store averaged with a quarter of the network that hasn't finished being born yet. Value the mature machine, then decide what the young cohort is worth as it matures.

This J-curve is also why judging MedPlus on a single year's margin is a trap. Slow the store openings and the blended margin would rise almost immediately, because the young drag shrinks — but you would be trading tomorrow's growth for today's optics. Accelerate openings and the blended margin dips even as the company gets more valuable, because the young cohort swells. The honest way to read the business is to hold two numbers side by side every year: the mature-store margin (is the machine still good?) and the pace of maturing (is the young cohort turning into mature stores on schedule?). Miss that, and you will misread a healthy, investing company as a low-margin one.

04

Private label: the quiet margin engine inside the pills

The lever MedPlus fully controls — and the one that turns a thin-margin retailer into a profitable one.

If density and store maturity are two of the multipliers, private label is the third — and the one management most directly controls. A private-label product is MedPlus's own brand: a generic medicine, a wellness supplement, a consumable, sold under the MedPlus name instead of a third party's. Because the company captures the manufacturer's margin as well as the retailer's, each private-label rupee of sales carries far more profit than a branded-pharma rupee. Growing its share is the single most powerful thing MedPlus can do to lift gross margin — and it has been doing exactly that.

Private label has grown from roughly a tenth of sales at listing to over a fifth — and its rise is the main reason FY26 gross margin expanded ~180 bps to 26.2%. MedPlus now carries more than 1,550 private-label SKUs across pharma and non-pharma categories.

The mechanism is straightforward but powerful. When a customer refilling a chronic prescription accepts the MedPlus-brand generic instead of the branded original — often at a lower shelf price and a higher margin to MedPlus — everybody's incentive lines up: the customer saves, MedPlus earns more, and the relationship deepens because the private-label product is only available at MedPlus. Over a monthly-refill relationship, that substitution compounds. This is why the private-label share and the gross margin move together, and why an extra couple of points of private-label mix matters more to profit than a similar move in headline revenue.

The surprise a generalist misses: MedPlus is quietly becoming a brand owner, not just a shopkeeper

The instinctive read of a pharmacy chain is "it's a distributor — it resells other people's medicines at a thin markup." That is decreasingly true of MedPlus. With over 1,550 of its own SKUs and private label already north of a fifth of sales, a growing chunk of the business is manufacturing-margin economics wearing a retailer's clothes. The dense store network is the distribution engine; private label is the profit engine riding on top of it, and it only works because the stores exist to place the product in a trusting customer's hands. That combination — own the shelf, own the product on it, own the monthly relationship — is what lets a business with a 26% gross margin and a 5% blended operating margin still earn an ~80% return on capital in its mature stores. The pills get the footfall in the door; the private label is where a surprising amount of the money is actually made.

Why this reframes the multiple: a pure reseller of branded medicine deserves a thin retail multiple. A retailer that is steadily converting a fifth-and-rising of its basket into own-brand, manufacturing-margin product — with the physical moat to keep placing it — is a different, better animal. Much of MedPlus's premium valuation is a bet that the private-label share keeps climbing without provoking a customer or regulatory backlash on generic substitution.

The company, the founder, and who owns it

A twenty-year Hyderabad build, backed by marquee private-equity money.

MedPlus is not an overnight roll-up; it is a two-decade operating build by a founder who is still running it. Understanding that lineage — and the private-equity capital that fuelled the store expansion — explains both the discipline of the cluster strategy and the ownership structure a public investor inherits.

Ownership & structure

  • Founder-led: Gangadi Madhukar Reddy remains the promoter and driving force — a rare case of a large Indian retail platform still run by the person who opened its first store. That continuity underpins the consistency of the cluster-and-private-label playbook.
  • Private-equity heritage: the company was built with backing from Warburg Pincus (via the Lavender Rose vehicle) and affiliates of Premji Invest. Post-IPO, institutional and PE holders remain a meaningful part of the register alongside the promoter and public float — investors should confirm the exact promoter and PE percentages in the latest shareholding filing, as PE positions can be sold down over time.
  • Subsidiary structure: the omnichannel and adjacent businesses run through group entities (the MedPlusMart online platform, diagnostics/lab operations, optical and related lines), all hanging off the same physical store network and brand.
Why the ownership shape matters: a still-engaged founder plus disciplined PE capital produced the patient, cluster-first expansion that is MedPlus's edge — this was never a land-grab chasing store-count headlines. The flip side for a public shareholder is the usual one for a recently-listed, PE-backed growth company: watch for the pace of any residual private-equity sell-down, and judge capital allocation on whether each new cluster earns the mature-store returns the model promises, rather than on store-count growth alone.
06

Click-and-mortar: the store network is the delivery network

MedPlus's answer to pure-online pharmacy — and why owning shops turned out to be an advantage, not a legacy cost.

For a while the market narrative was that online pharmacies would hollow out the physical chemist the way e-commerce hit some other retail. MedPlus's bet was the opposite: that in medicine, a dense physical network plus a digital layer beats a pure-online model — because the store you already own is the cheapest, fastest way to fulfil an online order. That is the "click-and-mortar" or omnichannel thesis, and FY26's results suggest it is holding up.

The physical store does double duty: shopfront for walk-ins and micro-warehouse for online orders. The denser the network, the shorter and cheaper the last mile — an advantage a warehouse-only rival structurally cannot match.

The strategic point is that the two channels are not rivals inside MedPlus; they are the same customer served two ways off one asset base. A chronic-care household might walk in one month and order on the app the next — MedPlus keeps the relationship either way, and the marginal cost of serving the online order is low because it ships from a store that already exists and is already stocked. A pure-online competitor, by contrast, must build and subsidise its own last-mile logistics from scratch, which is expensive and slow in exactly the neighbourhoods MedPlus has saturated. Convenience the customer wanted from online, delivered off infrastructure MedPlus already paid for.

Why "omnichannel" is a defence, not a buzzword, for this company

The word gets thrown around loosely, but for MedPlus it names a concrete defensive advantage. The online pharmacies compete hardest on price and convenience. MedPlus can match a lot of the convenience — app ordering, remembered prescriptions, same-day delivery — without the online player's structural cost of building last-mile logistics, because its stores already sit next to the customer. And on price, its rising private-label mix lets it discount the refill while still earning more than it would reselling a branded pill. The physical network that looked like a heavy legacy cost in the online-disruption story turned out to be the cheapest delivery infrastructure in the market. That reframing — stores as an asset for the digital age, not a liability from the analog one — is central to why MedPlus has kept growing profitably while several pure-online pharmacies have struggled to make the unit economics work.

07

Diagnostics & adjacencies: widening the basket

The layer that turns a pharmacy relationship into a broader health relationship.

Once a company owns a dense network of neighbourhood shops and a trusted health brand plus a recurring monthly relationship with chronic-care households, it has a natural platform to sell more health services through the same doors and the same app. That is the logic behind MedPlus's diagnostics business — pathology and lab testing — and its other adjacencies (optical, wellness). Each rides on the existing store network and customer relationship, so the incremental cost is low and the margin can be attractive.

Diagnostics is the most developed of these. Sample collection can happen at or near the store, results flow through the same app, and a customer already buying monthly medicines is a warm lead for a blood-sugar or lipid panel. The line is still small relative to the pharmacy business — diagnostics revenue was in the order of a few tens of crore per quarter in FY26, growing at a healthy double-digit rate — but it is strategically important out of proportion to its size: it deepens the customer relationship, adds a higher-value-per-visit service, and moves MedPlus from "the place I buy medicine" toward "the place I manage my health."

How the layers reinforce each other

The three layers are not separate businesses bolted together — they compound. The pharmacy footfall makes the private label possible (you can only substitute a generic if the customer is standing in your store or on your app). The private label makes the low-price refill possible, which strengthens the relationship. And the deep, trusting relationship makes the diagnostics cross-sell possible, which brings the customer in more often, which feeds more pharmacy and private-label sales. Density is the foundation; each layer stacked on top raises the lifetime value of the same household without a proportional rise in cost. That flywheel — not any single product line — is what a MedPlus bull is really buying.

08

The rivals: Apollo above, the online players alongside

A three-front contest — the scaled incumbent, the digital discounters, and a million local chemists.

MedPlus competes on three fronts at once, and each demands a different weapon. Above it in scale sits Apollo's pharmacy network, the market leader. Alongside it are the venture-funded online pharmacies competing on price and convenience. And beneath both is the vast, fragmented sea of independent neighbourhood chemists that still dominate Indian pharmacy retail. Organised chains like MedPlus are, collectively, still taking share from that fragmented base — which is the real growth pool.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

MedPlus is not really trying to out-scale Apollo or out-spend a Tata-funded app — that is not a winnable war on those terms. Its position is more specific: be the lowest-cost, densest, most reliable network in the regions it has saturated, use private label to be price-competitive on the refill, and use the stores to deliver online orders cheaper than a warehouse-only rival can. Against the fragmented independent chemist, it wins on range, reliability and price; against the online players, it wins on the last mile and trust; against Apollo, it competes cluster by cluster on local density rather than nationwide. The risk is that the online players, backed by patient capital, keep discounting to buy market share and compress everyone's margins for years — a war of attrition MedPlus must survive on genuinely superior unit economics rather than a subsidised balance sheet.

09

The risk stack: what can go wrong

A thin-margin, expansion-hungry, digitally-contested, regulated business — the risks are real and specific.

MedPlus's model is elegant, but it runs on thin margins and constant capital deployment, and it operates in a regulated, fiercely contested market. The risks are not exotic — they are the structural pressures inherent in the very design that makes it work. A first-time investor should hold all five clearly.

The risk that ties the others together

The single thread running through the stack is that MedPlus has thin margins and no financial slack, and it is spending constantly to grow into a market where a rival can choose to lose money to take share. That combination means the company has to be right on execution more or less every year: open stores that mature on schedule, keep lifting private label without a backlash, and defend price against discounters without torching its own slim margin. It has done this well so far, and the mature-store economics prove the model is sound. But there is no comfortable cushion — a couple of years of slower maturity, a regulatory tightening on generic substitution, or a prolonged online price war would show up quickly in a business that keeps only about five paise of operating profit on each rupee of sales.

10

Financials & valuation

A thin-margin retailer on a growth multiple — the whole case rides on convergence.

FY26 was a genuinely good year for MedPlus: revenue up ~12%, gross margin expanding on the private-label tilt, and profit inflecting up ~46% as an earlier store cohort matured. But the stock does not trade like a thin-margin retailer — at a market cap near ₹9,600 Cr and a P/E in the high-30s, it trades like a growth compounder. The question the numbers pose is therefore not whether MedPlus is cheap on today's earnings (it plainly is not) but whether the mature-store economics, private-label climb and store maturation justify paying up for what the reported margin still hides.

The one number that settles the thesis: blended margin converging toward the mature-store margin

At a high-30s P/E, MedPlus does not need a re-rating — it needs the gap between its two margins to close. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is the blended EBITDA margin (~5.3%) climbing toward the mature-store margin (~11.9%), tracked year over year, and read alongside two companions: the private-label share (still rising past ~22%, without a backlash?) and the pace of store maturation (is the ~24% young cohort turning mature on schedule, and are new clusters hitting mature-store returns?). If those converge — young stores age in, private label keeps lifting gross margin, and new clusters earn the ~80% store-level returns — then today's thin blended margin is a temporary artefact of fast growth and earnings compound hard. If maturation stalls, a price war caps private-label pricing, or regulation clips generic substitution, the convergence is deferred and a high-30s multiple on a 5%-margin retailer looks expensive. Store count times mature-store margin, less the young drag — that is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A proven model still early in its national build — asked to keep converging faster than rivals can discount.

MedPlus enters FY27 with the pieces in place: a dense, profitable mature-store base, a fast-growing private-label engine, a click-and-mortar network that beats pure-online on the last mile, and a founder still steering the cluster playbook. What it must now prove is that it can keep opening stores that mature into ~12% store-level margins, keep lifting private label, and keep defending price against deep-pocketed digital rivals — all while carrying almost no margin cushion. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Hidden earnings power — the blended 5.3% margin badly understates the ~11.9% mature-store economics; as ~24% of the network ages in, profit converges upward without any re-rating needed.
  • Private-label flywheel — own-brand at ~22% and rising is lifting gross margin structurally; more than 1,550 SKUs and a captive shelf to place them on.
  • Cluster + click-and-mortar moat — the densest network delivers online orders cheaper than any warehouse-only rival, turning stores into a digital-age asset.
  • Long runway — organised chains are still a minority of Indian pharmacy; MedPlus keeps taking share from fragmented independents, cluster by cluster.
  • Founder-led discipline — patient, density-first expansion with ~80% store-level returns on capital, not a store-count land-grab.

The bear case

  • Priced for the convergence — a high-30s P/E on a 5%-margin retailer bakes in years of flawless maturation; any slip in store ramp deflates it fast.
  • Online price war — Tata 1mg, Reliance's Netmeds and others can lose money for years to buy share, capping MedPlus's pricing and lifting its delivery costs.
  • Thin margin, no cushion — with ~5 paise of operating profit per rupee, a wage/rent shock or a weak private-label year hits the bottom line hard.
  • Regulatory reach — drug-price controls, e-pharmacy rules and generic-substitution policy can all clip the two levers MedPlus most depends on.
  • Regional concentration — heavy South-India (Telangana-centred) exposure means a regional shock hits an outsized slice of the network; national clusters are unproven at scale.
The bottom line

MedPlus is a well-run, founder-led pharmacy retailer that has quietly solved the two hardest problems in its trade: it makes a thin-margin product pay through density and private label, and it turned a physical store network into the cheapest delivery infrastructure in an online-threatened market. The mature-store economics — roughly a 12% store-level margin at an ~80% return on capital — prove the machine works; the reported 5% blended margin is mostly the shadow of a quarter of the network that hasn't finished growing up. The price already assumes that shadow lifts. The next test is concrete and datable: whether the FY27 store cohort matures on schedule and the blended EBITDA margin visibly closes the gap toward the mature-store number, while private-label share keeps climbing and the online players fail to force a lasting price war. Watch a store open, and watch the same store a year later — do that across the network, quarter by quarter, and you will know before the multiple does whether MedPlus is growing into its value or merely growing.
