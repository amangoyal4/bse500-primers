00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Balu Forge Industries makes crankshafts and forged, precision-machined metal components. A crankshaft is the long, heavy steel spine inside an engine that turns the up-and-down thump of the pistons into the round spin that drives the wheels — a part that has to be forged strong and then machined to microns. Balu is one of the larger independent crankshaft specialists in the world, selling into more than 80 countries, and it has spread out from there into a wide catalogue of forged parts — shafts, gears, flanges, rings, chassis and transmission components — for trucks, industrial machinery, railways, oil & gas, marine and, most recently, defence and aerospace. It is run out of Mumbai and manufactures principally from Bhachau in the Kutch region of Gujarat, with a large new plant coming up at Belgaum (Belagavi) in Karnataka.

Three facts frame everything about this company:

  • It earns forging-industry margins that shouldn't be possible. A commodity forge — a firm that hammers hot steel into rough shapes and sells them by the tonne — typically runs on a low-to-mid-teens EBITDA margin. Balu Forge posts an EBITDA margin around 27%, richer than even the domestic giants. That single number is the whole story: Balu is not really selling forgings by weight, it is selling finished, machined, engineered parts, mostly for export and replacement markets, where the value sits in the machining, not the metal.
  • Growth has been vertical, and profit has grown faster than sales. Revenue roughly doubled from about ₹560 Cr in FY24 to ₹1,107 Cr in FY26, while net profit nearly tripled from ~₹93 Cr to ~₹259 Cr over the same span. That is operating leverage: as higher-value machined and defence work fills the plant, more of each extra rupee of sales drops to the bottom line.
  • It re-pointed its order book at defence almost overnight. As recently as a couple of years ago Balu was, to the market, an auto-and-industrial forger. By FY26 it disclosed that defence, aerospace and railways make up roughly half its order book, having commercialised an automated empty-shell line for artillery ammunition and signed a five-year supply agreement tied to a NATO-affiliated buyer. That pivot is the source of both the excitement and the scepticism around the stock, which trades near 29× earnings.
In plain terms

Most people picture a forge as a place that starts with a glowing steel bar and ends with a rough lump of the right shape — heavy, cheap, sold by weight. Balu Forge built its business at the opposite end of that line. Its edge has always been the slow, precise machining and finishing that turns a rough forging into a crankshaft an engine-maker can bolt straight in — and, for years, a big slice of its output was premium replacement crankshafts shipped to workshops across dozens of countries, where buyers pay for exactness and availability. That finishing-end tilt is why its margins look nothing like a tonnage forger's. The current bet is that the same precision-machining muscle, aimed at artillery shells and aerospace parts, can keep those margins high while the top line races — and that a company scaling this fast can add its own heavy forging capacity without wrecking the returns that made it special.

01

Forging 101: how a lump of steel becomes a paid-for part

No metallurgy degree needed — just the handful of ideas that decide who makes money.

Forging is one of the oldest ways to shape metal: you heat a steel billet until it is soft and glowing, then squeeze or hammer it under enormous pressure into a die so the metal flows into the shape you want. Because the grain of the steel is pressed into shape rather than cut or cast, a forged part is unusually strong for its weight — which is exactly why the most safety-critical parts of an engine, a truck axle, an aircraft or a gun barrel are forged, not cast. But a forging straight off the press is only a rough blank. The money in this industry is made in the steps that come after the press. To follow Balu Forge, you need five ideas.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
ForgingShaping hot metal under pressure in a die. Produces a strong but rough blank that is oversized and unfinished. This is the tonnage, commodity end of the trade.
MachiningCutting, grinding and drilling the rough forging on precision (often CNC) machines to the exact final dimensions. Slow, skilled, capital-heavy — and where the margin is added. Balu's core strength.
CrankshaftThe engine's central rotating shaft. A long, complex forging that must be machined to microns and balanced perfectly. Balu's founding product and still its calling card.
OEM vs aftermarketSelling a part to the maker of a new engine/vehicle (OEM) versus selling a replacement part to repair an old one (aftermarket). Aftermarket parts, especially exported, carry higher margins and stickier demand.
MTPA"Metric tonnes per annum" — the unit for forging and machining capacity. Balu is expanding capacity toward six figures of MTPA; how full that capacity runs decides the returns on it.
The one economic idea that runs through the whole company

The same kilogram of steel is worth wildly different amounts depending on how far down the line you take it. As a raw forging it might sell for a modest premium over the steel and the energy that went into it. Machine that forging to a finished crankshaft and its value can multiply several times over, because now you are being paid for precision, tooling, inspection and reliability — not metal. Turn it instead into a finished defence or aerospace component, certified and traceable, and the value climbs again. Balu Forge's entire strategy is to keep pushing its product mix up that ladder — away from selling weight and toward selling finished, engineered, hard-to-qualify parts. Every other section in this primer is really a variation on that one move.

Why the steel price matters — and why it matters less than you'd think

Special and alloy steel bar is the main raw material, and it is a large share of a forger's cost. A pure tonnage forger is therefore a spread business — its profit is the thin gap between the steel it buys and the rough parts it sells, and that gap gets squeezed whenever steel prices jump. A finishing-and-machining house like Balu is less exposed: because so much of its selling price is the machining value it adds, a swing in the underlying steel price is a smaller fraction of the invoice, and much of it can be passed through to customers over time. That relative insulation from the steel cycle is one quiet reason Balu's margins are steadier than a commodity forger's.

02

What Balu Forge actually makes — and sells three ways

One core skill, applied across products, customers and geographies.

Strip Balu Forge back to its essence and it is a precision-machining and finishing house built around forgings, with the crankshaft as its flagship. From that base it has fanned out into a broad catalogue of forged and machined components, and it reaches its customers through three different doors — OEMs, the aftermarket, and, newly, government/defence buyers — each with its own economics.

The product range, decoded

  • Crankshafts (finished & semi-finished) — the founding product and still the anchor. Balu machines crankshafts for a wide span of engines — commercial vehicles, tractors, industrial, marine, oil & gas gensets — in both fully finished form (ready to fit) and semi-finished form (for customers who do their own final machining). Being credible in something as unforgiving as a crankshaft is the qualification badge that opens every other door.
  • Forged & machined components — a widening catalogue beyond crankshafts: shafts, gears, flanges, rings, chassis and undercarriage parts, transmission and clutch components, brake parts. This is how Balu turns one customer relationship into many parts per customer.
  • Defence & aerospace parts — the newest and highest-barrier line: empty artillery-shell casings (152 mm and 155 mm classes), and early aerospace and engine components. Long to qualify, but sticky and high-value once you are in.
Why "the aftermarket" is a hidden engine, not a footnote

Selling a crankshaft to a truck-maker for a new engine is good business, but it is competitive and price-checked. Selling a replacement crankshaft to a repair workshop in Turkey, Oman or Nigeria for a truck that broke down is a different game: the buyer needs the exact part, needs it now, and there is no assembly-line purchasing team squeezing the price. Balu built a genuinely global replacement-parts presence across 80-plus countries over many years, and that aftermarket book is a big reason its margins run so far above a typical OEM supplier's. It is unglamorous — spare crankshafts for old engines — but it is precisely the kind of fragmented, service-led demand that a commodity competitor finds hard to attack.

03

The 27% margin puzzle

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — and it is specific to this company.

Here is the thing an analyst leads with. Forging, as a business, is not supposed to be this profitable. Hammering steel into rough shapes is capital-heavy, cyclical, and priced by the kilo; margins are structurally thin. Yet Balu Forge earns an EBITDA margin around 27% — comfortably above the listed forging pack and, remarkably for a company a fraction of their size, at or above the giants. Understanding why is understanding the entire company, because the whole bull case is that this margin is real and durable, and the whole bear case is that it is too good to hold as Balu scales.

Illustrative, not Balu's disclosed pricing — the point is directional. Each rung up the ladder adds value that is engineering, not steel, which is why a mix tilted toward machined and certified parts carries a far higher margin than a tonnage forger's.

Balu's margin is not one trick but a stack of them, all pulling the same way. Machining mix: a large share of what it ships is finished or semi-finished — sold for its precision, not its weight. Aftermarket and export tilt: replacement parts across 80-plus countries dodge the price pressure of domestic OEM contracts. Product selection: Balu chases complex, hard-to-make parts (crankshafts, now shells) rather than easy high-volume commodity forgings. And historically asset-light sourcing: for years Balu leaned on bought-in forgings and concentrated its own capital and skill on the machining end — which flattered its return on capital because it wasn't tying up money in heavy forging presses.

The device that fits only Balu Forge: it built the business back-to-front

Read most forging companies front-to-back: they start with the press — the heavy, expensive forging capacity — and add machining downstream. Balu Forge grew up the other way. Its edge began at the finishing end — machining and supplying premium crankshafts, much of it sourced-and-finished for the global replacement market — and only now, at scale, is it building large in-house forging capacity upstream at Belgaum. So the very expansion that makes it a "real" integrated forger is also the moment it takes on the heavy, capital-hungry, lower-margin part of the chain it had cleverly avoided. That is the tension the 27% margin hides: the mix that produced it was partly a function of not owning the forging step Balu is now spending hundreds of crores to own.

A genuine surprise: the instinct is that a company earning 27% margins in a low-margin industry must be doing something the competition can't. Part of the answer is less flattering and more interesting — for much of its history Balu was as much a precision-machining and trading house for crankshafts as it was a forger, which is exactly why its capital intensity was low and its margins high. The re-rating story and the capital-efficiency worry are two sides of the same coin: as Balu becomes a heavier, more integrated forger, it is deliberately trading some of that asset-light magic for scale and control.
04

The company, the promoters, and the watch-points

A Chandock-family business, freshly re-rated — with a few things worth stating plainly.

Balu Forge traces its crankshaft lineage back to the late 1980s and the Balu Industries name, built over decades into a global replacement-crankshaft supplier before the listed Balu Forge Industries vehicle became a market favourite in the current up-cycle. It is controlled by the Chandock family (promoter Jaspalsingh Prehladsingh Chandock and associated entities), operates from a Mumbai head office, and manufactures principally from Bhachau, Kutch (Gujarat), with the new Belgaum campus in Karnataka the centrepiece of its expansion.

Ownership & the caveats worth tracking

  • Promoter holding: the Chandock promoter group holds roughly 53–54%, having topped up via preferential allotments used to fund the expansion. A controlling stake, but one built partly through fresh issuance — worth tracking for dilution.
  • Promoter pledge: in early 2026 a portion of promoter shares (reported around 8.75 lakh shares, ~₹40 Cr) was pledged to a lender. Pledging is not inherently alarming, but for a richly valued stock it is exactly the kind of item minority investors should monitor in each shareholding filing.
  • The IT search: the January 2026 Income-Tax search rattled the stock before the company reported a clean conclusion. It is disclosed here not to sensationalise it but because a fast-rising small-cap with a recent regulatory brush deserves to be represented as it is, not airbrushed.
  • Capital efficiency under scrutiny: some analysts have flagged Balu's working-capital intensity — inventory and receivables that swell as it grows and exports — and questioned how much of the growth is funded by capital raised versus cash generated. This is the central fundamental debate and returns in the financials section.
Why the governance items matter more here than usual: a stock trading near 29× earnings is priced for clean, compounding execution. Preferential allotments, a promoter pledge and a recent tax search do not, individually, break a thesis — but each one nibbles at the "trust the ramp" premium the valuation depends on. The right posture is neither to dismiss them nor to catastrophise them, but to keep them on a checklist and watch whether they grow or fade.

The end-markets — and the defence pivot that re-rated the story

A diversified customer base, tilted hard and fast toward defence, aerospace and railways.

One of Balu Forge's real strengths is that it is not a one-end-market forger. The same machining skill serves trucks, tractors, industrial engines, oil-and-gas equipment, marine, railways and now defence and aerospace — so a slump in any one sector needn't sink the company. But the striking feature of FY26 is how quickly the mix has swung toward the highest-value work.

What the defence line actually is

The headline product is the empty artillery-shell casing — the forged-and-machined steel body of a large-calibre round (152 mm and 155 mm classes, including extended-range ERFB types), supplied "empty" (without explosive fill) to ammunition integrators. Balu commercialised a fully automated, indigenously built production line at Belgaum with capacity for 360,000 shells a year, secured a multi-month domestic order, and signed a five-year MoU to supply empty large-calibre shells into a NATO-affiliated supply chain. Alongside ammunition, it has begun taking artillery, armoured-vehicle and engine components, and booked a maiden aerospace order from a US customer (Alpha Aircraft Systems).

Why a shell casing is really the same business as a crankshaft

It is tempting to treat "Balu makes artillery shells now" as a jarring left-turn. It isn't. A large-calibre shell casing is a forged steel body machined to tight tolerances, pressure- and quality-critical, fully traceable — which is a near-perfect description of the crankshaft discipline Balu already had. The company is not learning a new trade; it is aiming an existing precision-machining capability at a buyer (governments and their integrators) that pays more, contracts for longer, and switches suppliers less. That is why the pivot was fast: the hard part — the machining competence and the qualification credibility — was already in the building. The genuinely new risk is the customer type: defence demand is lumpy, export-controlled, and hostage to geopolitics and government budget cycles.

The nuance to hold: a defence/aerospace order book is worth more per rupee than an auto order book — longer, stickier, higher-barrier — which is precisely why the market re-rated the stock as that share climbed to ~50%. But "order book" is a promise, not revenue. Empty-shell lines have to run at high utilisation, export approvals have to clear, and geopolitical shifts can slow a NATO-linked pipeline. The re-rating rewards the mix; only delivered, invoiced defence revenue vindicates it.
06

The capacity build-out at Belgaum

The bet that decides whether the next few years look like the last two.

Growth this fast eventually runs into a wall: you cannot ship parts you have no capacity to make. Balu's answer is a large greenfield campus at Belgaum (Belagavi), Karnataka — a roughly 46-acre site meant to lift forging capacity toward 150,000 MTPA and machining capacity beyond 80,000 MTPA, and the home of the automated empty-shell line. This is the most important operational story of the next few years, and it cuts both ways.

Both figures are tonnes per annum. Separately, the automated defence line adds capacity for 360,000 empty shells a year. The plan meaningfully deepens Balu's own forging — the heavy, capital-intensive step it historically leaned on others for.

Backward-integrating into heavy forging is strategically sound: it gives Balu control over quality, cost and delivery of its own blanks instead of depending on outside forgers, and it is a prerequisite for winning larger defence and aerospace work that demands traceable, in-house material. But it also changes the company's financial character. Heavy forging presses are expensive, they must be kept busy to earn their keep, and the working capital to feed a much bigger plant — steel inventory, work-in-progress, export receivables — climbs with the volume. A greenfield ramp is rarely smooth: commissioning slips, early utilisation is low, and returns dip before they recover.

Why the phrase "operating leverage" cuts both ways here

When a plant fills up, operating leverage is a gift: fixed costs are already paid, so each extra part is highly profitable, and margins expand — which is exactly what drove FY25–FY26. Run the same logic in reverse and it is a warning. A big new campus arrives with a big fixed cost — depreciation, interest, overheads — from day one, but the revenue to cover it builds only as the plant fills. If Belgaum ramps on schedule and the defence and export orders convert, the operating leverage that lifted margins on the way up keeps lifting them. If the ramp lags the spending, the same fixed costs weigh on returns until volumes catch up. The capacity chart above is therefore not just a growth chart; it is the single biggest swing factor in the next two years of margins and return on capital.

07

Exports, forex and the concentration question

A global footprint is a strength — and a set of dependencies to price in.

Balu Forge earns a large share of its revenue outside India, spanning replacement crankshafts and components across dozens of markets and, increasingly, defence and aerospace exports. That international tilt is central to the high-margin story — but it comes bundled with exposures a purely domestic forger would not carry.

  • Currency: exports are typically billed in hard currency (largely US dollars), so a stronger rupee trims realisations and a weaker rupee flatters them. Forex is a genuine swing factor on margins quarter to quarter, and a reason reported profitability can wobble even when volumes are steady.
  • Geopolitics and logistics: management explicitly attributed a softer sequential quarter in FY26 to Middle East geopolitical disruptions. When your customers and shipping lanes are spread across Turkey, the Gulf, Africa, Europe and North America, a regional flare-up or a freight shock can dent a quarter — a fragility that comes with the export premium.
  • Customer and order concentration: as the defence book grows, a handful of large, lumpy contracts (a multi-year shell MoU, a big domestic ammunition order) can dominate the pipeline. That is high-quality demand, but it concentrates the company's fortunes on a few relationships delivering on schedule — the opposite of the diffuse, thousands-of-workshops aftermarket that built the base.
  • Regulatory and export-control overlay: defence and dual-use exports live under licensing and end-use rules. Balu's NATO-linked pipeline is an opportunity, but defence trade can be switched on or slowed by policy on either side of a border in ways an auto-parts exporter never faces.
The trade-off in one line

Balu's export-and-aftermarket model is the reason it earns more than a domestic tonnage forger — and the reason its earnings are more exposed to the rupee, to freight, and to events far from Kutch. You cannot separate the premium from the exposure; they are the same business seen from two angles. An investor's job is to decide whether the durability of the demand (spare parts the world always needs, defence budgets that only seem to grow) outweighs the volatility of getting it to market. The honest read is that the demand is real and the volatility is unavoidable — so the quarter-to-quarter line will be bumpier than the multi-year trend.

08

The industry and the rivals

A scaled, competitive forging landscape — with one true giant and several mid-cap peers.

Forging in India is a serious, capital-heavy industry with a clear pecking order. At the top sits a global-scale major; below it, a cluster of sizeable listed forgers compete on autos, exports and, increasingly, defence. Balu Forge is not the biggest, but it is distinctive — smaller, higher-margin, and more tilted to machining, exports and aftermarket than most.

The competitive shape to keep in mind

Balu is not trying to out-tonnage Bharat Forge — that race is unwinnable and, for a mid-cap, beside the point. Its game is to stay at the high-value, high-margin end: complex machined parts, exported replacement crankshafts, and certified defence and aerospace components that are slow to qualify and sticky once won. The competitive risk is less that a rival undercuts it on a rough forging and more that the bigger players — with deeper defence relationships and balance sheets — crowd into the same high-value defence and aerospace niches Balu is racing toward. Balu's defence is speed, focus and a margin structure that lets it invest; its vulnerability is that everyone now wants the defence-and-aerospace rung of the ladder it is climbing.

The balance sheet is a relative comfort: reported net debt is modest (of the order of ~₹150 Cr against a company this profitable), and management has framed the expansion as funded largely through internal accruals and equity rather than heavy borrowing. Return ratios are healthy — ROE around the high-teens, ROCE in the low-20s. The question the bears press is not whether Balu is profitable today — plainly it is — but whether it can stay this capital-efficient while it pours money into forging capacity and carries the working capital a bigger export-and-defence book demands.

The one number that settles the thesis: return on capital through the Belgaum ramp

At ~29× earnings, Balu Forge is priced as a company that will keep growing and keep earning outstanding returns on the capital it deploys. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is return on capital employed (ROCE) as the Belgaum forging capex and the associated working capital hit the balance sheet — read alongside the cash-conversion of profit (does the growing PAT turn into operating cash, or get trapped in inventory and receivables?) and the defence order-to-revenue conversion (does that ~50% order book actually invoice?). If ROCE holds in the low-20s while revenue climbs — proof the new heavy forging earns its keep and the working capital is controlled — the premium multiple is defensible. If ROCE sags as capital balloons faster than profit, the market will conclude the high-margin magic was a phase of the asset-light model, not a permanent feature, and re-rate accordingly. Margins get the headlines; the capital behind them settles the argument.

10

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality compounder in the making — or a re-rated mid-cap meeting the hard part of scaling.

Balu Forge enters its next phase with real momentum: a doubling of revenue behind it, sector-leading margins, a fast-growing defence and aerospace book, and a large new plant coming online. What it has to prove is the least glamorous thing of all — that it can get much bigger, and much more capital-intensive, without giving back the returns that made it a star.

The bull case

  • Structurally high margins — a machining, aftermarket and export tilt that earns ~27% EBITDA, well above tonnage forgers, and looks defensible if the mix holds.
  • Defence/aerospace re-rating — ~50% of the order book in high-value, sticky, high-barrier work, with a five-year NATO-linked shell MoU and a maiden US aerospace order.
  • Proven growth engine — revenue doubled and profit nearly tripled in two years, with operating leverage still to come as the mix improves.
  • Capacity runway — the Belgaum build-out to ~150,000 MTPA forging removes the volume ceiling and deepens control of the value chain.
  • Global aftermarket moat — a decades-built replacement-crankshaft presence across 80-plus countries that a commodity rival cannot quickly copy.

The bear case

  • Capital-efficiency risk — heavy forging capex plus rising working capital could pull ROCE down; the asset-light margin story may not survive integration.
  • Rich valuation — ~29× earnings prices in flawless execution; any margin or ramp disappointment can de-rate sharply.
  • Export & geopolitical exposure — forex swings and events (a Middle East flare-up already dented a quarter) make earnings bumpy; defence trade is policy-sensitive.
  • Governance watch-items — a promoter pledge, preferential-allotment-funded stake, and a recent (concluded) IT search all sit against a premium multiple.
  • Bigger rivals crowding in — Bharat Forge and peers want the same defence/aerospace niches, with deeper relationships and balance sheets.
The bottom line

Balu Forge is a rare thing in a heavy, cyclical industry: a forger that made itself look like a precision-engineering company, and got paid a precision-engineering margin for it. The next chapter tests whether it can bolt a genuinely heavy forging business onto that light, high-margin core — and aim the whole thing at defence and aerospace — without the returns reverting to what forging usually earns. The scoreboard is not the order book, which already looks the part, and not the margin, which is already high; it is the return on capital reported in each of the next several quarters as Belgaum's forging lines and the defence working capital land on the balance sheet. Watch that figure. If it holds while the plant fills and the shells ship, Balu will have proven the margin was a moat, not a moment. If it slips, the market will have its answer about which kind of forger this really is.
