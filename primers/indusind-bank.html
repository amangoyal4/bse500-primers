IndusInd Bank, a high-flyer grounded by its own books

The most damaging corporate-governance shock in Indian private banking since Yes Bank — but a completely different kind of failure. IndusInd didn't over-lend to reckless borrowers; it mis-accounted its own hedging trades, quietly overstating profits and net worth by ~₹2,000 crore over years until an internal review blew it open in March 2025. The CEO and his deputy resigned, the stock crashed 27% in a day, and depositors got nervous. This is a full, honest walk through what actually went wrong, the distinctive vehicle-finance and microfinance franchise underneath the scandal, and the slow, still-unfinished rebuild — set against a stock that now trades below its own book value.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

IndusInd Bank is a large-mid Indian private-sector bank — the country's fifth-largest private lender, promoted by the Hinduja Group — with a distinctive twist: it is India's leading vehicle financier (commercial vehicles, two-wheelers, tractors) and its second-largest microfinance lender through its subsidiary Bharat Financial Inclusion (BFIL). For two decades it was a market darling — high-margin, fast-growing, run like a nimble challenger. Then, in March 2025, it detonated a crisis entirely of its own making: an internal review found that the bank had been mis-accounting its own currency-hedging derivative trades for years, quietly inflating its reported income and net worth. The correction was a one-time hit of roughly ₹1,960 crore, more discrepancies surfaced in the microfinance and fee books, the CEO and deputy CEO resigned, and the share price fell 27% in a single day.

Three facts frame everything:

  • This was an accounting/governance failure, not a bad-loan blow-up. Unlike Yes Bank (which nearly died in 2020 from reckless corporate lending) or the classic banking collapse, IndusInd's core problem was internal controls: for 5–7 years it valued its internal hedging trades on a method that suppressed real losses and overstated profit. The loans themselves were largely fine; the books were not. That distinction matters — it is arguably more fixable, but it destroyed something harder to rebuild: trust in the numbers.
  • The bank survived, but its earnings were gutted. FY26 (year to March 2026) consolidated net profit was just ₹889 crore, down 65% from ₹2,576 crore, dragged by the derivatives clean-up, a microfinance stress cycle, and a shrinking loan book as the bank went defensive. The last quarter (Q4FY26) swung back to a ₹594 crore profit after a brutal ₹2,329 crore loss a year earlier — a first sign the worst is behind it, but only a first sign.
  • It trades below book — a deep-value recovery bet. At a share price around ₹974 and a book value near ₹842, IndusInd trades at roughly 1.15× book — cheap for a private bank, expensive-looking on a temporarily crushed P/E. The whole investment question is whether new CEO Rajiv Anand (ex-Axis Bank) can restore the numbers and the credibility — and whether the high-NIM vehicle-and-MFI franchise underneath is still intact.
In plain terms

Think of IndusInd as a strong, fast car that was found to have a rigged odometer. The engine — its vehicle-finance and microfinance lending business — is genuinely good and high-earning. But for years the dashboard was showing better numbers than the car was really producing: the bank was recording its own internal hedging trades in a way that hid losses and flattered profits. When that was discovered in March 2025, the bank had to knock ~₹2,000 crore off its accounts in one go, admit the readings had been wrong, and watch its boss and his deputy quit. Depositors got jittery and pulled some money; the stock cratered. Since then a new driver (CEO Rajiv Anand from Axis Bank) has taken the wheel, the books have been corrected and re-audited, and the bank is slowly steadying. The bet from here is simple to state and hard to judge: is this a good bank that had an accounting scandal and will recover — or a bank whose culture and controls were so weak that the scandal is a symptom of deeper rot? The honest answer in mid-2026 is: we don't fully know yet, and that uncertainty is exactly why the stock is cheap.

01

Bank economics 101: how a bank earns — and where the numbers can lie

No finance degree required — the six numbers that drive a bank, plus the one thing IndusInd's story turns on: the integrity of those numbers.

A bank is, at heart, a spread business: it borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors) and lends it out at a higher rate; the gap, scaled to its assets, is the engine. IndusInd has always run a fatter spread than most banks because it lends to higher-yield borrowers — truckers, two-wheeler buyers, microfinance women's groups. But IndusInd is the rare bank where you also need a second idea: a bank's reported profit depends on accounting choices, and if those choices are wrong, the "spread" you're admiring may be partly fictional. That is precisely what happened here — so read these units with one eye on what they mean and one eye on how they can be misstated.

The simple profit equation — and why "how you account for it" is a real risk

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) − operating costs − loan losses + fee/trading income. A bank gets richer four ways — lend more, fund cheaper (more CASA), lose less (low NPAs), and earn more fees. Most bank blow-ups come from the loan-losses term: too many loans go bad. IndusInd's crisis was different and, in a way, more insidious — it lived in the trading/other-income and net-worth lines. By recording its internal hedging derivatives on a favourable valuation method, the bank booked income that wasn't really there and carried a higher net worth than reality. Nobody defaulted; the money simply hadn't been earned. That's why "internal controls" and "audit quality" — usually invisible, boring words — are the whole story for this bank. A bank can be lending perfectly well and still have unreliable accounts. Understanding that is the key to everything that follows.

Why capital and confidence are what actually keep a bank alive

  • Capital is the shock absorber. A bank funds its loans mostly with depositors' money and a thin sliver of its own (measured as the CET1 ratio and total capital adequacy, CRAR). That sliver absorbs losses before depositors are touched. IndusInd's crucial saving grace in 2025 was that its capital was strong — CRAR ~17.5% — so the ~₹2,000 Cr hit dented net worth (~2.3%) but never threatened solvency. This is why IndusInd, unlike Yes Bank in 2020, needed no rescue.
  • Confidence is the fragile part. Because a bank has lent out most of the money it holds, it runs on trust. IndusInd's danger in 2025 was never that it would run out of capital — it was that depositors, spooked by "the bank's own numbers were wrong," might pull money out faster than a bank can comfortably handle. Managing that confidence — with liquidity buffers, RBI reassurance and a credible new CEO — was the real crisis-management job.
  • Why a promoter and a regulator matter here. A committed promoter (the Hinduja Group, §09) willing to inject capital, plus visible RBI support, are what tell depositors "this bank is money-good even if its accounting was not." Both were present — and that is a big reason the crisis was contained rather than fatal.
02

The 2025 accounting & governance crisis — the defining event

The single most important thing that has happened to IndusInd, and the reason everything now is a "rebuild". Read this closely.

On 10 March 2025, IndusInd Bank disclosed that an internal review had found discrepancies in the accounting of its derivatives portfolio that would knock roughly 2.35% off its net worth — an estimated post-tax hit of about ₹1,580 crore (a pre-tax impact later assessed by Grant Thornton at ₹1,959.98 crore). The next trading day, 11 March 2025, the stock crashed 27% — its biggest single-day fall since listing. What made this a full-blown governance crisis rather than a one-off correction was what came next: more discrepancies surfaced, the CEO and deputy CEO resigned, investigators moved in, and the market realised the problem wasn't a single trade — it was the bank's internal controls and the reliability of its accounts.

The crisis, in the order it unfolded

  • The derivatives discrepancy (~₹1,960 Cr). The core issue: for 5–7 years, the bank had mis-accounted internal currency-hedging derivative trades in a way that suppressed real losses and overstated income and net worth. (The mechanics get their own section — §03.) A ₹1,960 Cr correction on a bank this size is survivable, but the discovery shattered confidence in every other number.
  • Then the microfinance book (~₹674 Cr). A subsequent review found roughly ₹674 crore of interest incorrectly booked in the microfinance (BFIL) portfolio across three quarters — income recorded that had to be reversed. Separately, a ~₹173 crore fraud was flagged in microfinance during an internal audit.
  • And the fee & "other assets" lines. The forensic work also flagged ~₹172.6 crore wrongly booked as fee income in FY25 and ~₹595 crore of unsubstantiated balances sitting in "other assets" accounts. Individually small; together, a pattern of an accounting function that was not to be trusted.
  • Leadership collapse. MD & CEO Sumant Kathpalia and Deputy CEO Arun Khurana resigned in April 2025, taking responsibility. The CFO had exited earlier. The bank was suddenly running without a permanent top team in the middle of a crisis.
  • Investigators and forensics. The board commissioned Grant Thornton (root-cause) and independent forensic work (including PwC); the reports found incorrect accounting of internal derivative trades, especially on early termination, and named around 25 employees including the former CEO and deputy CEO. India's Serious Fraud Investigation Office (SFIO) summoned the ex-CEO, ex-deputy CEO and ex-CFO over the ~₹1,979 crore of accounting irregularities.
Why this happened — the lesson in one paragraph

IndusInd's failure was not reckless lending; it was a breakdown of internal controls and governance. Somewhere in the treasury, an accounting shortcut that flattered the numbers was allowed to run for years — and the checks that are supposed to catch that (internal audit, risk, finance, the board, the external auditors) did not, until a rule change forced a clean-up that exposed it. That is a cultural failure as much as a technical one: a bank where numbers could be quietly massaged to look better is a bank where the incentive to report well beat the discipline to report truly. Every part of the modern rebuild — a new CEO from outside, a new chairman, re-stated books, tighter controls, RBI oversight — is a deliberate answer to that specific failure. When you read that IndusInd is "strengthening internal controls," this scandal is what it is running away from.

A bank can survive a ₹2,000 crore accounting hit. What it cannot easily survive is the question that hit plants in every depositor's mind: "if this number was wrong, which others are?" Rebuilding IndusInd is, above all, rebuilding the credibility of its own accounts.
The regulator's stance mattered. Crucially, the RBI did not take control of the bank (as it did with Yes Bank in 2020). In June 2025, RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra publicly said the bank had "taken enough steps to improve its accounting and other practices" and was "on the whole… doing well." That vote of confidence — combined with IndusInd's strong capital — is a large part of why a scandal that looked existential in March 2025 became a manageable (if painful) clean-up rather than a collapse.
03

The derivatives trick, explained in plain English

The single most confusing part of the story — made simple. This is the "what actually went wrong" section.

The heart of the scandal sounds impossibly technical — "mismatched accounting of internal forex derivative trades" — but the idea underneath is simple, and worth understanding because it's the whole reason the crisis exists. IndusInd was hedging currency risk for its borrowers, and in doing so it created two sets of trades — an internal one and an external one — that were valued by two different accounting methods. The gap between those methods quietly leaked fake profit into the accounts for years.

Step by step — how the gap opened up

  • Why the trades existed. When IndusInd took on foreign-currency exposure (say a customer's dollar borrowing), it wanted to protect itself from exchange-rate swings. So its lending/deposit side entered an internal derivative contract with the bank's own treasury desk. The treasury desk then went out and hedged that exposure externally in the real market with another counterparty. Normal, routine risk management.
  • The two-methods problem. Here's the flaw. The external trade was marked to market — revalued daily with market prices, so its gains and losses flowed through the profit-and-loss account. But the internal trade was accounted for on a different basis (swap-cost / swap-valuation), which did not track market movements the same way and largely sat in the asset book rather than P&L.
  • How that created fake profit. Because the two legs were valued differently, they didn't cancel out the way a proper hedge should. The mismatch boosted reported net interest income and treasury gains while suppressing the matching losses. Year after year the bank looked slightly more profitable than it really was — and its net worth was carried a little too high.
  • Why it surfaced when it did. The RBI banned banks from these internal derivative trades from 1 April 2024. Unwinding and squaring off the old positions (and an internal review) is what finally forced the hidden mismatch into the open — surfacing as a one-time ~₹1,960 crore correction, the accumulated gap of 5–7 years crystallising at once.

A properly-matched hedge nets to roughly zero — that's the whole point of hedging. The one-sentence version

IndusInd was booking the gains on one side of its own internal hedges but not fully booking the matching losses on the other side — because the two sides were valued by different accounting rules — so for years its profit and net worth looked bigger than they truly were, until the whole gap had to be corrected at once. It's not that money was stolen; it's that money was counted before it was earned, and some of it never was. The failure was in the plumbing of the accounting, and in the controls that should have spotted it.

Why this section is the crux: almost every judgement about IndusInd from here depends on how you read this. A charitable view: it was a genuinely technical accounting-consistency error, now corrected, with the loss recognised and the mechanism (internal trades) banned — a bad chapter that's closed. A harsh view: an error this large, running this long, undetected by every internal and external check, reveals a governance and control culture so weak that you cannot trust the accounts even now. Both readings are defensible in mid-2026. The stock's discount to book is the market pricing the uncertainty between them.
04

The vehicle-finance engine — IndusInd's crown jewel

The distinctive, high-margin franchise underneath the scandal — the thing that makes IndusInd IndusInd.

Strip away the crisis and you find a genuinely differentiated bank. IndusInd's heritage strength — inherited from its roots in the Ashok Leyland / Hinduja vehicle-financing world and consolidated through the erstwhile IndusInd Enterprises & Finance — is vehicle finance: lending to buy commercial vehicles (trucks, buses), two-wheelers, three-wheelers, cars, tractors and construction equipment. It is one of India's largest financiers of commercial vehicles, with deep relationships across dealers, fleet operators and small transporters. This is a high-yield, hard-to-replicate business, and it is the single biggest reason IndusInd historically ran a NIM near the top of the industry.

Why vehicle finance is such a good franchise

  • High yields, secured by an asset. A trucker or two-wheeler buyer pays a much higher interest rate than a blue-chip corporate — yet the loan is secured against the vehicle itself, which can be repossessed. High spread and collateral: the rare combination that lets a bank earn a fat margin relatively safely.
  • A moat built on distribution and data. Financing a used-truck buyer in a small town requires an on-the-ground network — hundreds of specialist vehicle branches, dealer tie-ups, and decades of repayment data on how transporters behave through cycles. IndusInd has spent years building this; a new entrant cannot buy it overnight. The bank is expanding this, aiming to grow its dedicated vehicle branches from ~300 toward ~600, co-locating them with regular branches to cross-sell deposits and other products.
  • A window on the real economy. Commercial-vehicle demand tracks freight, construction and GDP; two-wheeler and tractor demand tracks rural incomes and the monsoon. IndusInd's book is a leveraged bet on India's goods-movement and rural cycles — which cuts both ways (great in an upcycle, painful in a slowdown).
  • A deposit-gathering hook. Vehicle customers and dealers are also a source of deposits — the bank noted deposits sourced from vehicle customers grew ~35% in the year. The franchise isn't just a lending machine; it feeds the funding side too.

Why the franchise is intact even though the accounts weren't

It's important to separate two things the crisis blurred. The accounting scandal was in the treasury and controls — it did not mean IndusInd's truckers stopped repaying or its vehicle-lending edge evaporated. The vehicle-finance engine kept running through the whole episode, and in FY26 the bank explicitly leaned back into it — a "selective growth" strategy focused on well-secured vehicle finance and SME lending while trimming riskier wholesale exposure. That's the recovery logic in a nutshell: the bank's best, most defensible business is exactly the one it's choosing to grow. If IndusInd re-rates, it will be because this engine — high-yield, secured, moated — proves it can still power the profitability the accounting scandal temporarily hid.

05

Microfinance (BFIL) — the second engine, and a second wound

The other high-yield franchise — and the stress cycle that compounded the crisis at exactly the wrong time.

IndusInd's other distinctive business is microfinance: tiny, unsecured loans to low-income borrowers (mostly rural women, in joint-liability groups) run through its wholly-owned subsidiary Bharat Financial Inclusion Limited (BFIL). This made IndusInd India's second-largest microfinance lender, serving over 13 million customers. Like vehicle finance, MFI is very high-yield — but unlike vehicle finance it is unsecured, which makes it the riskier of the two engines. And in FY25–FY26, at the worst possible moment, the entire Indian microfinance industry hit a stress cycle that hammered IndusInd's book.

Why microfinance both helps and hurts

  • The upside: yields and reach. Micro-loans carry high interest rates and serve borrowers no other formal lender reaches — a socially useful, profitable niche when it works, and a big contributor to IndusInd's above-average NIM.
  • The downside: unsecured and cyclical. These loans have no collateral, so when borrowers can't pay — after a bad monsoon, a local shock, over-indebtedness, or (as recently) sector-wide over-lending followed by a tightening — losses come fast and hard. There's nothing to repossess.
  • The FY25–26 stress cycle. India's microfinance sector went through a painful downturn: too much lending in prior years, borrower over-leverage, and then rising defaults. IndusInd's MFI book was badly hit — the microfinance loan book shrank sharply (down ~46% YoY at one point) as the bank pulled back, and it took heavy provisions and accelerated write-offs. In one quarter, provisions jumped ~49% sequentially to ~₹2,631 crore on MFI write-offs, tipping the bank to a loss.
  • Where it compounded the accounting crisis. Cruelly, this MFI stress hit at the same time as the derivatives scandal — and one of the accounting discrepancies (~₹674 Cr of wrongly-booked interest, plus a ~₹173 Cr fraud) was in the microfinance book. So MFI was simultaneously a source of real credit losses and a source of accounting problems. Two wounds, one franchise.

In plain terms — the two engines, side by side

IndusInd runs on two high-yield engines. Vehicle finance is the reliable one — high rates, but secured against trucks and bikes, and moated by distribution. Microfinance is the volatile one — even higher rates, serving people no one else lends to, but unsecured and prone to violent cycles. In good times both fatten IndusInd's margin; in FY25–26 the MFI engine backfired at the same instant the accounting scandal broke. The encouraging recent sign is that by Q4FY26 microfinance slippages were falling and management framed the improvement as durable discipline rather than luck — and it plans to grow both vehicle finance and (carefully) microfinance as part of the recovery. Watching whether the MFI book stabilises without re-inflating risk is one of the two or three things that actually decide the recovery.

06

How the money flows: high-yield loans out, deposits in — and the confidence in between

Read this once and both the strength and the fragility of the model click into place.

A bank is a conveyor belt for money: deposits come in one end, loans go out the other, and the spread is harvested as net interest income. IndusInd's belt is distinctive at both ends. On the lending side it runs unusually high-yield assets (vehicle finance, microfinance) that give it a fat margin. On the funding side it has historically been more reliant on costly wholesale/bulk deposits and less on cheap sticky CASA than the very best banks — a structural weakness the crisis exposed, because bulk money is the first to flee when confidence cracks.

The whole model runs on confidence (the dotted line under it all): high-yield lending is great only as long as depositors keep funding it cheaply. The 2025 crisis attacked exactly that — spooked depositors, forcing the bank to hold extra liquidity and pay up for deposits, which raised funding costs and squeezed the margin. Rebuilding trust is rebuilding the profit engine.

The two levers that set the spread

A bank's margin is just (what it earns on loans) minus (what it pays for funding). IndusInd's position on each:

  • Earns a lot on loans (strength). Its vehicle-finance and microfinance assets yield far more than plain-vanilla corporate or mortgage lending — the reason its NIM sits near the top of the industry. This blade of the scissors is genuinely sharp.
  • Pays too much for funding (weakness). A thinner CASA base and reliance on bulk deposits and CDs — made worse by the post-crisis need to hold extra liquidity — mean IndusInd's cost of funds is higher than the best banks'. Growing cheap, sticky retail deposits is the single most important structural repair (§07).
Why IndusInd's margin is high but its funding is its Achilles' heel

Most banks with IndusInd's kind of fat margin got there by lending riskier; IndusInd did too, but smartly — secured vehicle loans and reach-driven microfinance. The catch is the other side of the balance sheet. A bank that funds itself heavily with big, rate-sensitive wholesale deposits is running a hidden fragility: that money is "hot" — it chases the best rate and flees at the first sign of trouble. IndusInd's high margin has always been partly lent against that funding fragility. The 2025 crisis was the stress-test that exposed it: when trust cracked, the hot money got nervous, the bank had to hold costly liquidity buffers, and the celebrated margin compressed. The durable fix isn't just fixing the accounts — it's building a deposit franchise sticky enough that IndusInd's great asset engine is funded like a great bank, not a finance company.

07

The deposit shock and the rebuild

The recovery arc — how a confidence crisis hits the funding base, and the quiet grind of winning it back.

When a bank's own numbers are shown to be wrong, the most immediate real-world danger is not the accounting hit — it's depositors deciding to move their money somewhere safer. IndusInd never suffered a full-blown run (its capital was strong, the RBI was reassuring, and the Hinduja promoters were visibly committed), but it did feel real deposit pressure: it had to hold elevated liquidity buffers and issue high-cost certificates of deposit to be safe, which pushed up funding costs. By March 2026, deposits had drifted down to ~₹4.00 lakh crore (about 3% lower year-on-year) and CASA had slipped to 31.3% from 32.8% — the fingerprints of a franchise that took a confidence knock.

Both sides of the balance sheet shrank in FY26 — deposits under confidence pressure, advances because the bank deliberately went defensive (cutting microfinance and trimming wholesale). A shrinking bank is a bank in recovery mode, not growth mode. The task now is to grow deposits — especially cheap CASA — back.

Why the deposit rebuild is the foundation of everything

  • Stickiness = survival. The whole vulnerability the crisis exposed was a funding base that was too "hot". Rebuilding a granular, retail, CASA-heavy deposit base is literally rebuilding the bank's resilience — the antidote to the fragility of 2025.
  • Cheaper funding = restored margin. Every point of CASA-ratio recovery, and every high-cost CD that can be let go as trust returns, feeds directly back into NIM. The margin the crisis compressed is rebuilt mostly on the funding side.
  • The vehicle-branch cross-sell. IndusInd's plan to co-locate vehicle-finance branches with regular banking and grow them toward ~600 is partly a deposit strategy — turning its lending relationships (dealers, fleet operators, customers) into a source of sticky deposits. Deposits from vehicle customers grew ~35% in the year.
  • It de-risks the loan-to-deposit ratio. As advances shrank faster than deposits, IndusInd's loan-to-deposit ratio eased — meaning it is funding its lending more comfortably from stable deposits. A safer, more sustainable configuration than the stretched pre-crisis setup.
Why "boring deposits" are the hardest and most valuable thing IndusInd must do

Across India, savers are shifting money out of bank deposits into mutual funds and stocks — so every bank is fighting over a shrinking pool of deposits. For a bank recovering from a trust shock, gathering cheap, sticky retail money is doubly hard: you're competing for scarce deposits and living down a scandal. This is precisely where a credible new CEO (§08), visible RBI comfort, and a committed promoter (§09) earn their keep — each makes a nervous depositor a little more willing to stay. The deposit franchise isn't glamorous and it won't rebuild in a quarter, but it is the quiet floor under the entire recovery. Watch deposit growth and CASA every quarter: rising = trust returning; flat-to-falling = the shadow of 2025 still hanging over the funding base.

08

The leadership reset

A crisis of governance demands a change of the people in charge — here's who left, who arrived, and why it matters.

A scandal rooted in internal controls and accounting integrity is, at its core, a leadership failure — so the most important part of the fix is changing the people at the top and importing credibility from outside. That is exactly what IndusInd did. Within weeks of the disclosures, its MD & CEO Sumant Kathpalia and Deputy CEO Arun Khurana resigned; the CFO had already exited; and the chairman's term ended. The bank then reached outside for a new leader with a clean, respected pedigree.

Why the new leadership matters — and what it must prove

  • An outsider with credibility. Rajiv Anand came from Axis Bank, where as Deputy MD he ran the wholesale bank and drove its digital agenda — a respected, clean-hands banker with no ties to the accounting mess. That "outside credibility" is itself a tool: it reassures depositors, staff and the regulator that the people who broke the bank's trust are gone.
  • The job is controls first, growth second. The mandate is unusually clear: fix the plumbing — internal controls, risk management, accounting rigour, board oversight — before chasing growth. A bank rebuilds credibility not with a big quarter but with several boring, clean, believable ones.
  • The proof is in the quarters. The return to profit in Q4FY26 (and a restored dividend) is an encouraging first data point, but analysts are explicit that it takes several stable, clean quarters — and probably two-plus years — to fully restore confidence. The leadership reset is necessary; it is not, by itself, sufficient.
The honest read on the reset: changing the CEO, deputy CEO, CFO and chairman is exactly the right response, and Rajiv Anand is a credible choice. But a fresh top team inherits a bank whose culture allowed the problem to fester for years — and culture is slow to change. The bull sees a clean-hands leader with a clear mandate and RBI backing; the bear notes that the same institution, structures and much of the staff remain, and that trust, once broken, is rebuilt slowly and can be broken again. Both are watching the same thing: consistency, quarter after unremarkable quarter.
09

The Hinduja promoter question

The committed owner behind the bank — and the stake-increase intentions that could shape the recovery.

Unlike Yes Bank (which has no promoter), IndusInd has a clear, committed promoter: the Hinduja Group, one of India's largest and oldest diversified business families, holding its stake mainly through IndusInd International Holdings Ltd (IIHL). Promoter entities held roughly 15.8% as of March 2026 — and, critically, the Hindujas have signalled a desire to put more money in, not take it out. That committed-owner backing was a major reason the 2025 crisis was contained rather than fatal.

Why the promoter question matters

  • A capital backstop. The RBI gave IIHL in-principle approval back in 2023 to lift its stake toward 26%, and the Hindujas have discussed investing $1 billion-plus — raising fresh capital (potentially with sovereign-wealth and pension partners) and injecting it into the bank. A promoter willing to add capital during a crisis is the strongest possible signal that the owner believes in the franchise and stands behind the bank.
  • Confidence for depositors and the regulator. A committed, deep-pocketed promoter reassures depositors (their money is behind a family that won't walk away) and gives the RBI a responsible counterparty. This is a structural advantage IndusInd has that a no-promoter bank does not.
  • But promoter groups carry their own governance scrutiny. The Hinduja Group is large and complex, and any promoter stake-raise in a bank is heavily scrutinised by the RBI (which caps promoter voting and holding). Promoter share pledging for refinancing has also featured — a normal but watched practice. A strong promoter is a plus; concentrated promoter control is also a governance dimension the market keeps an eye on.
In plain terms

The Hinduja backing is one of IndusInd's quiet strengths through this crisis. Where Yes Bank had to be rescued by outsiders in 2020, IndusInd had an owner who not only stood firm but has been trying to put more money in — approved by the RBI to go toward 26% and talking about a billion-dollar-plus injection. For a bank rebuilding trust, "the promoter is doubling down" is about the most reassuring sentence a depositor can hear. The nuance: exactly when and how that stake-raise happens has moved slowly, and a bigger promoter stake brings its own regulatory and governance considerations. But directionally, a committed owner leaning in during trouble is a genuine differentiator versus the ownerless turnarounds.

10

Financials & valuation

The crushed FY26 numbers, and the below-book, deep-value lens that governs a recovery bank.

FY26 was a wreck by design — the year the crisis fully hit the accounts. Consolidated net profit fell 65% to ₹889 crore (from ₹2,576 Cr), gutted by the derivatives clean-up, microfinance provisions and a shrinking book. But the shape of the year matters: the pain was front-loaded (a huge Q4FY25 loss, weak early FY26), and the bank returned to profit by Q4FY26 (₹594 Cr) and even restored a small dividend. For a recovery bank like this, the market's key valuation lens is price-to-book (P/B) — and IndusInd is one of the few private banks trading below its own book value.

Why P/B is the right lens — and why "below book" is a debate, not a verdict

For a bank, price-to-book is the master gauge: book value is the bank's net worth, and the fair multiple on it is driven by the return the bank earns on that net worth (RoE). A high-RoE bank like HDFC commands ~2.5–3× book; a bank earning almost nothing right now "deserves" much less. IndusInd at ~1.15× book — actually close to, and at times below, 1× — is the market saying: on today's crushed returns this is roughly worth its net worth, no more. The optically absurd P/E (80×+) is meaningless — it just reflects near-zero current earnings, not expense. So the real question is entirely forward-looking: if IndusInd restores its pre-crisis ~1.8% RoA and mid-teens RoE, a bank earning that should trade at ~2× book or more — implying big upside from a below-book starting point. That's the deep-value bull case. The bear case: the returns may not come back (or come back slowly), the accounts may hold more surprises, and paying ~1× book for a bank whose numbers you can't fully trust is not the bargain it looks. This gap — between a proven-recovery valuation and today's price — is the entire debate.

One-offs to flag: FY26 profit is depressed by non-recurring pain — the derivatives correction, elevated microfinance provisions and write-offs, and higher funding costs from crisis-era liquidity buffers and high-cost CDs. Strip those out and the underlying earning power (a high-NIM vehicle-and-MFI franchise) is far better than the reported ₹889 Cr suggests — which is the crux of the recovery thesis. But the mirror-image caution also holds: because the accounts were found to be unreliable, an investor must treat even the restated numbers with more-than-usual scepticism until several clean, audited quarters accumulate. The dividend was cut to a token ₹1.50 as capital is conserved for the rebuild.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

Where this goes — and the honest case on both sides of an unusually uncertain name.

IndusInd in mid-2026 is a genuine paradox: a bank with a strong, differentiated, high-margin franchise wrapped around a broken record on the one thing a bank sells — trust. The engine (vehicle finance, microfinance, high NIM) is real and largely intact; the damage (a self-inflicted accounting scandal, a leadership vacuum since filled, a dented deposit base) is real too. The next two-plus years decide which fact wins. The bull owns the franchise and the below-book price; the bear owns the uncertainty about whether the accounts — and the culture behind them — can be trusted.

The bull case

  • A genuinely good, high-margin franchise. Vehicle finance (secured, moated, high-yield) and microfinance give IndusInd one of the best NIM profiles in Indian banking — and that engine kept running through the crisis.
  • Below-book, deep-value entry. At ~1.15× book (and at times near 1×), you're paying roughly net worth for a bank that, restored, historically earned ~1.8% RoA. If returns normalise, the re-rating is large.
  • Survivable, not existential. Strong capital (CRAR ~17.5%), RBI reassurance, and a committed Hinduja promoter meant no rescue was needed — the crisis was an accounting clean-up, not a solvency event.
  • Credible reset underway. New outside CEO (Rajiv Anand, ex-Axis), new chairman, restated and re-audited books, a return to Q4 profit and a restored dividend — the recovery has visibly begun.
  • Committed owner leaning in. The Hindujas are RBI-approved to raise their stake toward 26% and have discussed a $1bn+ injection — an owner adding capital in a crisis is the strongest vote of confidence there is.

The bear case

  • The accounts were found to be unreliable. The core problem was internal controls and governance culture — the hardest thing to fix and to trust. If these numbers were wrong, an investor must question all of them until many clean quarters pass.
  • Earnings are crushed and slow to recover. FY26 PAT down 65%; analysts expect 2+ years to fully restore profitability. The turnaround is early and unproven — a few good quarters is not yet a trend.
  • Twin credit-quality pressures. Elevated GNPA (3.43%) from the microfinance stress, and the ever-present risk in unsecured MFI and cyclical vehicle finance — reaching back into these for growth could re-import losses.
  • A dented, costlier funding base. Deposits shrank and CASA slipped; rebuilding cheap, sticky deposits amid a system-wide deposit war, while living down a scandal, is hard and slow.
  • Overhangs remain. An ongoing SFIO probe, the risk of further discrepancies surfacing, and the still-pending promoter stake-raise are unresolved tail risks. "Below book" can stay below book for a long time if trust doesn't return.
The bottom line

IndusInd is the purest governance-shock recovery stock in Indian banking — and a very different animal from Yes Bank. Yes Bank nearly died of bad loans and had to be rescued; IndusInd was never insolvent — it was caught with its own numbers wrong, and the wound is to credibility, not capital. Underneath the scandal sits a genuinely good, high-margin vehicle-and-microfinance franchise, a strong capital position, a committed promoter, and a credible new CEO — and the stock trades at or below its own book value, pricing in a lot of pessimism. That is the deep-value bull case. But be clear-eyed: this is a bank whose accounts were found to be unreliable, whose earnings are still crushed, and whose recovery will take years and must be proven quarter by quarter. Watch three things: the accounts (are the restated numbers holding up, with no new surprises?), the deposits and NIM (is the funding base rebuilding and the margin recovering?), and asset quality (is the microfinance stress genuinely behind it?). Get those right and a beaten-down franchise re-rates hard off a below-book base; get them wrong and it stays a cheap-looking bank you couldn't quite trust. In mid-2026 the honest verdict is that it is too early to be sure — which is precisely why the price is where it is.
