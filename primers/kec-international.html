00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

KEC International is a global infrastructure EPC contractor — a firm that wins large construction contracts, builds the projects over several years, and books the revenue as it executes. It is the flagship company of the RPG Group (the Harsh Goenka house), and it grew up doing one thing better than almost anyone in the world: making steel lattice towers for high-voltage power lines and stringing those lines across countries. That legacy business — power transmission & distribution (T&D) — is still roughly two-thirds of the company. Around it, KEC has bolted on four more legs: railways, civil (buildings, factories, water, metros), oil & gas pipelines, and cables, plus a growing solar/renewables line.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is an order-book business, and the order book is at a record. KEC exited FY26 (year ended March 2026) with a ~₹36,267 Cr order book after an all-time-high ₹25,280 Cr of new order intake during the year — well over a year and a half of revenue already booked. For an EPC firm, that backlog is the single most important forward indicator: it is the fuel the next two years of revenue burns.
  • The problem was never orders — it was margin. FY26 revenue rose ~8% to a record ₹23,506 Cr, yet EBITDA margin sat at just ~7.1%. That is the entire story in one line: KEC has plenty of work, but commodity inflation, some legacy low-priced contracts, and a heavy interest bill on its working capital have kept profitability well below the high-single/double-digit margins it used to earn. The bet is that margin claws back.
  • The engine is slowly deleveraging. Operating PAT grew ~18% to ₹650 Cr and net debt (including acceptances) eased to ~₹6,722 Cr. The stock trades near 31-32× earnings at a ~₹13,300 Cr market cap — a multiple that already prices in a margin recovery that has only partly arrived.
In plain terms

KEC is a contractor, not a product company. It does not sell a branded good off a shelf; it bids for enormous infrastructure projects — a 765 kV transmission line, a stretch of railway electrification, a metro viaduct, a refinery pipeline — wins some, and then spends two to four years building them. Its profit depends on three things happening in sequence: bidding at a sensible price, buying steel and aluminium at costs it can control, and executing on time. Get all three right and a thin margin turns into a decent profit on a very large revenue base. Miss on any one — a price war on bids, a spike in metal prices, a project that drags — and the same revenue produces almost no profit. The company at ₹13,300 Cr is a wager that the price war and the metal spike are behind it, and the margin is on its way back up.

01

EPC 101: how an order-book business actually makes money

No construction background needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole model.

Before any single KEC number makes sense, you need the shape of an EPC business. EPC stands for Engineering, Procurement and Construction — the firm designs the project (engineering), buys all the steel, cable, cement and equipment (procurement), and physically builds it (construction), usually handing over a finished, working asset on a fixed timeline. The customer is typically a government utility, a state power company, a railway board, a city metro authority, or a large private developer. The contract is often fixed-price or fixed-rate: KEC quotes a number to win the job, and then has to deliver inside that number.

That single feature — a price fixed at bid time, costs incurred over the years that follow — is why an EPC company behaves the way it does. Consider the life of one contract:

The five ideas that run an EPC business
IdeaWhat it means (and why it matters for KEC)

Order book / backlogThe stock of work won but not yet executed — ~₹36,267 Cr at March 2026. Divide by annual revenue and you get "revenue visibility": here, roughly 1.5 years of work already in hand before a single new order.
Book-to-billNew orders ÷ revenue in the same period. Above 1.0 means the backlog is growing faster than it is being consumed. KEC's ~₹25,280 Cr intake against ~₹23,506 Cr revenue is just above 1.0 — the book is holding.
EBITDA marginOperating profit as a share of revenue. In EPC it is thin — mid-single digits to low double digits. KEC's ~7.1% is the number the whole thesis hangs on, because on a huge revenue base a small margin move is a large profit move.
Working capitalThe cash tied up in a job between paying for materials and getting paid by the client. EPC is enormously working-capital-hungry, and the interest on that capital is a direct hit to profit. This is KEC's chronic pressure point.
The mechanism that decides an EPC's fate: the gap between bid and build

When KEC wins a ₹1,000 Cr transmission-line contract, it has effectively locked in its selling price for a job it will build over the next two-to-three years. But it buys the steel, aluminium and zinc for that job later, at whatever those metals cost then. If metal prices rise after the bid, the margin the company assumed at bid time quietly evaporates — the client still only pays the fixed price. This is why an EPC contractor is, underneath, a giant bet on input-cost discipline and execution speed. It also explains the counter-intuitive truth about KEC: the danger in a boom is not too few orders, it is winning too many orders at too-thin prices, which is exactly what happened in the crowded T&D bidding of recent years.

So the health of an EPC firm reads in a specific order. First, is the order book large and growing (demand and competitiveness)? Second, is the margin on that work adequate and rising (pricing and cost discipline)? Third, is the working capital under control so interest does not eat the profit (balance-sheet health)? KEC scores well on the first, poorly-but-improving on the second, and has spent years fighting the third. Everything that follows is really an elaboration of those three questions across five different businesses.

02

What KEC actually sells: one contractor, five verticals

The diversified model — read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

KEC's structure is best understood as a single EPC engine pointed at five different kinds of infrastructure. The skill it sells is the same across all of them — design, procure, build large projects on time and budget — but the end markets differ in growth, competition and margin. Diversification here is deliberate: for decades KEC was almost purely a T&D company, and when global transmission spending slowed, the whole business slowed with it. The other four legs exist to reduce that dependence.

Why the mix, not just the total, is the point

Two EPC firms can report identical ₹23,000 Cr revenues and be completely different animals depending on which verticals produced it. T&D is KEC's highest-competence, best-margin core — but it is also the most competitively bid. Civil is lower-margin historically but growing fast and diversifying the risk. Railways has been a margin problem child that KEC has deliberately shrunk. So when KEC's mix shifts — T&D revenue jumping 24% in FY26 while it prunes low-margin railway work — the quality of the revenue changes even if the headline growth looks modest. Reading KEC well means reading the mix, because the blended margin is just a weighted average of five very different businesses.

The next five sections take each vertical in turn. Keep the EPC lens from Section 01 on throughout: for every leg, ask the same three questions — how big and competitive is the demand, what does the margin look like, and how hungry is it for working capital?

03

The company, the RPG Group, and who controls it

A century-old engineering name inside Harsh Goenka's RPG house.

KEC International is the flagship of the RPG Group, the diversified conglomerate controlled by Harsh Goenka. The name "KEC" comes from Kamani Engineering Corporation, a business with roots stretching back to the mid-20th century that made India an early and formidable exporter of transmission towers and turnkey power lines. Under RPG ownership it has been rebuilt into a global multi-vertical EPC contractor while keeping the tower heritage at its core.

  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the RPG Group / Goenka family hold roughly 50.1% — a controlling but not overwhelming majority, leaving a large free float. Harsh Goenka chairs the group; the company is run by a professional management team (long led on the operating side by CEO Vimal Kejriwal and successors).
  • Group context: RPG is a diversified house spanning tyres (CEAT), pharma, plantations, IT and infrastructure. KEC is its infrastructure/EPC standard-bearer — do not confuse it with the unrelated power-utility "KEC" abbreviations or with other Kamani-named entities; this is the RPG-controlled, NSE-listed KEC International (NSE: KEC / BSE: 532714).
  • Key subsidiaries: SAE Towers (the Americas tower & line business) and KEC Asian Cables Ltd (the carved-out cables arm) are the two structurally important ones. SAE in particular is why "KEC builds power lines on multiple continents" is literally true.
A genuine surprise for the generalist: an RPG-owned Indian company has, for decades, been regarded as the world's largest maker of power-transmission towers — and through SAE Towers it manufactures towers and strings high-voltage lines in Brazil, Mexico and the United States. KEC is not a domestic infrastructure play that happens to export; it is a genuinely global T&D contractor with projects spread across roughly 100 countries. The Indian tricolour is on power lines in the Americas, Africa and the Middle East.
04

Transmission & Distribution: the legacy core

~68% of revenue, the highest-competence business, and the leg the whole grid build-out rides on.

Transmission & Distribution is what KEC is. It designs and manufactures the steel lattice towers you see marching across the countryside, and it builds the high-voltage transmission lines and substations that move electricity from where it is generated to where it is used — turnkey, meaning it does the whole job from foundation to energisation. In FY26 this segment delivered ~₹15,883 Cr of revenue, up 24% year-on-year, lifting its share of the company to roughly 68%. It is both the biggest and, in FY26, the fastest-growing leg.

The reason T&D is booming is structural, and it is worth spelling out because it is the strongest tailwind KEC has. The world is electrifying and decarbonising at once, and renewable power breaks the old grid map. Solar and wind farms get built where the sun and wind are — deserts, coasts, remote plateaus — not where the demand is. Connecting them requires vast new transmission: long high-voltage lines, new substations, and grid strengthening to carry variable renewable power to cities. India alone has committed to a massive expansion of its transmission network to hit its ~500 GW non-fossil target, and similar grid capex is underway in the Middle East, Africa and the Americas. Every kilometre of that is exactly what KEC builds.

The SAE Towers leg — KEC on three continents

Within T&D sits SAE Towers, acquired in 2010, which gives KEC manufacturing plants and project execution across Brazil, Mexico and the United States. SAE alone accounts for roughly 7% of the total order book. This matters for two reasons. First, it diversifies KEC's geography, so a slow tender year in India can be partly offset by the Americas (and vice versa). Second, the Americas — especially the US — are entering their own grid-modernisation super-cycle, ageing transmission infrastructure that has to be rebuilt to handle renewables and rising load. A tower-and-line specialist with local American plants is unusually well placed for that.

Why T&D is the best business but not the easiest one

T&D is KEC's highest-competence vertical — decades of tower engineering, in-house manufacturing, and a global execution track record are real, hard-to-copy advantages. But it is precisely because the demand is strong that the bidding gets crowded: when every EPC contractor sees the same grid super-cycle, they all chase the same tenders, and aggressive bidding compresses margins for everyone. KEC's T&D edge is not that it wins work — plenty of firms can win a transmission line — it is that its in-house tower manufacturing and global scale let it execute at a cost others struggle to match. The FY26 combination of 24% revenue growth and a rising order book suggests it is winning the volume; whether it is winning it at improving prices is the margin question of Section 08.

05

Railways: the vertical KEC learned to be picky about

A turnkey rail-infrastructure business riding India's rail capex — but one KEC has deliberately reshaped for margin.

KEC's railways business does turnkey rail infrastructure: laying and doubling tracks, overhead electrification, signalling and telecom, and building stations and rail systems. It rode the first wave of India's enormous railway modernisation push — record central budgets for track expansion, 100% route electrification, station redevelopment and dedicated freight corridors — and grew rapidly for several years.

But railways also taught KEC a hard lesson, and the way it responded is instructive. Rail EPC in India became intensely competitive and, for a stretch, low-margin: aggressive bidding, complex multi-agency execution, and slow client processes squeezed profitability. Rather than chase revenue for its own sake, KEC deliberately pulled back — becoming far more selective about which rail contracts it bids, and letting the segment shrink as a share of the mix in favour of better-priced T&D and civil work. That is why railways, once a large growth engine, is now a smaller (roughly high-single-digit percent, approx.) slice of revenue.

The discipline that reads as weakness but isn't

A shrinking segment usually looks like a problem. In railways it was the opposite — a signal that KEC would rather book less revenue than book it at margins that destroy value. For an EPC company, the temptation to fill the order book at any price is constant, because a big backlog looks impressive and keeps the workforce busy. KEC's willingness to walk away from underpriced rail tenders is one of the clearest pieces of evidence that management is now optimising for margin and cash, not headline size. The railway story is less about how big the segment is and more about what its deliberate shrinkage tells you about how the company is being run.

The underlying market is still real and large — Indian railway capex remains a multi-year tailwind, and KEC retains the capability to scale the segment back up if the pricing improves. But for now, railways is best read as a call-when-margins-make-sense option rather than a growth priority, a supporting leg that the company will lean into selectively.

06

Civil: the fast-growing new leg

Factories, buildings, water, metros and data centres — the diversification that is actually scaling.

If T&D is the heritage and railways the cautionary tale, Civil is the growth story of the diversification. KEC's civil business builds industrial plants and factories (for cement, metals, autos and more), commercial and residential buildings, water infrastructure, and urban transit such as metro viaducts — increasingly, it also targets high-value structures like data centres. It has grown from a standing start into KEC's clear second-largest vertical, and in FY26 the civil business secured a robust order intake of over ₹5,000 Cr.

The logic of civil is that it lets KEC redeploy the same core competence — managing large, complex construction projects on fixed contracts — into a market with far more customers and project types than T&D. When a cement major builds a new plant, when a state builds a water-supply scheme, when a city extends a metro, that is civil EPC, and it is largely uncorrelated with the transmission tender cycle. That diversification is exactly why KEC built the leg.

The reason civil matters more than its margin might suggest

Civil EPC is not usually a high-margin business — it is competitive and execution-heavy. So why lean into it? Because diversification of the order book is itself worth something. A company that is 90% T&D lives and dies on the transmission cycle; a company that is two-thirds T&D and one-third civil-plus-others has more places to deploy capacity when any one market slows. Civil's job in the KEC story is less to lift the blended margin and more to make the whole ₹36,000 Cr backlog steadier and less hostage to a single tender stream. It is the leg that turns KEC from a T&D specialist into a genuine multi-vertical EPC — and steadier revenue is what eventually earns a contractor a better multiple.

07

Oil & Gas, Cables & Solar: the specialist legs

Three smaller businesses that round out the engine — one of them a genuine manufacturer.

Beyond the big three, KEC runs three more focused businesses. Individually each is a modest slice of revenue; together they add both diversification and, in the case of cables, a real manufacturing franchise rather than pure contracting.

Oil & Gas pipelines

KEC executes oil & gas pipeline EPC — laying cross-country hydrocarbon and city-gas pipelines and associated facilities. It is a specialist, cyclical business tied to energy-infrastructure spending, and KEC deploys it selectively where the margins justify the effort. It broadens the company's exposure into the energy value chain without being a core dependence.

Cables — the one business that is also a product

Uniquely among KEC's legs, cables is a manufacturing business: KEC makes power and specialty cables in its own plants and sells them, rather than only building projects for clients. In January 2025 this business was carved into a wholly owned subsidiary, KEC Asian Cables Ltd, and in FY26 it delivered its highest-ever revenues, order intake and profitability. Cables give KEC a product line with its own demand drivers — electrification, industrial and infrastructure cabling — and, being a manufactured good, a somewhat different margin and working-capital profile from project EPC.

Solar & renewables EPC

KEC also builds solar and renewables EPC — engineering and constructing solar plants and associated evacuation. It is an emerging leg that plugs KEC directly into the same energy-transition wave that drives its T&D super-cycle: the more renewables get built, the more both solar EPC and the transmission to carry that power are needed. In effect, KEC can bid for the generation and the grid connection.

Why carving out cables is a tell worth noticing

Moving cables into its own wholly owned company is a small structural change with an outsized signal. A manufactured-product business and a project-EPC business have genuinely different economics — different inventory cycles, different margins, different capital needs — and blending them inside one set of accounts blurs how each is really doing. By ring-fencing cables, KEC makes it visible as a standalone performer (its record FY26 numbers would otherwise vanish into the consolidated blur) and gives itself the option to grow, partner or surface value in it separately. It is the kind of housekeeping a management focused on the quality of each rupee of profit does — and consistent with the same margin-and-cash discipline visible in the railway pullback.

08

The order book, the margin gap, and the ₹235-crore point

The single most important idea in the whole case — and it is specific to KEC's shape.

Here is the trap most first-time readers fall into with KEC: they watch the order book. And the order book is genuinely impressive — a record ₹25,280 Cr of intake in FY26, a ₹36,267 Cr backlog, book-to-bill just above 1.0. But KEC's stock has almost never been held back by too few orders. The thing that has kept the profit — and the share price — down is margin. To see why, you have to put KEC's own numbers next to each other and notice how a small percentage does an enormous amount of work.

The device that only fits KEC: the ₹235-crore point

On KEC's ~₹23,500 Cr revenue base, one single percentage point of EBITDA margin is worth roughly ₹235 Cr. Set that against operating PAT of ~₹650 Cr and the whole equity story snaps into focus: KEC does not need a bigger order book to transform its profit — it needs its margin to travel from ~7% back toward the 9–10% it used to earn. Two or three points of margin recovery is on the order of ₹500–700 Cr of extra EBITDA — as much again as the entire current operating profit — dropped onto a revenue line that barely has to grow. That is why a KEC investor should watch the margin line the way a T&D engineer watches voltage: it is the variable that carries all the energy. The order book tells you the work exists; the margin tells you whether the work is worth doing.

Why has the margin been stuck near 7% when KEC used to earn more? Three pressures, stacked:

  • Commodity inflation. Steel, aluminium and zinc are the raw materials of towers and lines. When their prices rose after contracts were bid at fixed prices, the assumed margin got eaten — the Section 01 bid-vs-build gap in action.
  • Legacy low-margin orders. A backlog of older contracts won in crowded, aggressively-bid tenders (especially in railways and some T&D) executed at thin margins, dragging the blended number down as they worked through.
  • Interest & working capital. A heavy finance cost on the working capital KEC ties up (Section 09) sits between EBITDA and profit — so even when operating margin holds, interest siphons off a large share before it reaches PAT.

The recovery thesis is simply that all three ease at once: commodity prices stabilise, the legacy low-margin book runs off and is replaced by better-priced new orders, and interest falls as debt comes down and rates ease. FY26's 18% operating-PAT growth on just 8% revenue growth is the first evidence that this is starting — profit growing more than twice as fast as revenue is the signature of margin beginning to claw back. Whether it continues is the entire debate.

The one number that settles it: not order intake, not backlog — the consolidated EBITDA margin. If it climbs quarter after quarter from ~7% toward 8%, 9%, 10%, KEC's profit compounds off a revenue base it already has, and today's ~31× multiple looks reasonable. If it stalls at 7%, the record order book just produces more thin-margin revenue and the multiple has nothing underneath it. Read the margin line first; everything else is supporting detail.
09

Working capital, debt & interest: the profit killer

The least glamorous section — and, for years, the most important one.

An EPC contractor is, financially, a machine for tying up cash. KEC buys steel, aluminium, cable and equipment, pays workers and subcontractors, and builds for months before a client — often a government utility or state body — pays it, sometimes slowly. That gap has to be funded with borrowed money, and the interest on that borrowing is a direct subtraction from profit. For KEC, this working-capital-and-interest burden has been the single biggest reason profit lagged revenue.

The scale of it is the point. Net debt including acceptances stood at ~₹6,722 Cr at March 2026 — down modestly (about ₹84 Cr) over the prior quarter, but still large relative to a ~₹650 Cr operating profit. The finance cost on borrowings of that size runs into the several-hundred-crore range each year (of the order of ₹750–850 Cr, approx.) — which, remarkably, is larger than the entire operating PAT. Sit with that for a moment: the interest bill is bigger than the profit. It means that every rupee KEC shaves off its interest cost — by reducing debt, by collecting receivables faster, by rates easing — flows almost directly to the bottom line.

Why deleveraging and margin are the same story told twice

KEC's profit is squeezed from two directions at once, and both are about a thin number being pressured. From above, a low EBITDA margin means little operating profit is generated per rupee of revenue. From below, a heavy interest bill takes a large bite of what operating profit there is. The encouraging read on FY26 is that both levers moved the right way — operating margin edged up (18% PAT growth on 8% revenue) and net debt ticked down. If that continues — margin toward 9–10% and debt/interest falling — the two levers compound: more profit generated, less of it lost to interest. That double-compounding is the mechanical heart of the bull case, and it needs no revenue heroics at all. The bear case is that a working-capital-heavy EPC in a competitive market rarely gets to keep both improvements at once.

There is a structural caution here too. Faster growth in an EPC business consumes working capital — winning and executing more projects means funding more materials and receivables in-flight. So KEC has to walk a line: grow the order book enough to keep the engine full, but not so fast that working capital balloons and the interest bill wipes out the margin gains. The company that manages that tension — growing profit while shrinking net debt — is a genuinely different, and better, business than the one that limped through the margin-squeeze years.

10

The industry and the rivals

A crowded EPC field with one giant benchmark and several sharp T&D specialists.

Infrastructure EPC in India is competitive by nature — large tenders, many capable bidders, and clients (often government) who award to keen prices. KEC competes on different fronts in different verticals, but the names to hold in mind are a mix of the diversified giant, the focused T&D peers, and global tower-and-line contractors.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two features matter. First, the closest mirror to KEC is Kalpataru Projects — same T&D heritage, same diversification playbook, same margin-and-working-capital pressures — so the two are read as a pair, and both re-rate on the same "grid super-cycle plus margin recovery" narrative. Second, the real competitive edge in T&D is not winning tenders (many can) but executing cheaply, and that comes from in-house tower manufacturing, global scale, and a century of project experience. KEC's moat, such as it is, sits in that execution cost and its worldwide footprint — not in any protection from competition, which the thin industry margins make plain does not exist. The grid super-cycle can lift the whole field; who profits from it depends on who executes at the best cost.

Financials & valuation

A record order book and a recovering profit — priced for the recovery to keep going.

FY26 was a record year on the top line and a turning point on profit: revenue up 8% to ₹23,506 Cr, EBITDA up to ₹1,659 Cr at a 7.1% margin, and operating PAT up 18% to ₹650 Cr — profit growing more than twice as fast as revenue, the early signature of margin recovery. The market has already re-rated the stock to reflect a good part of that. The question is whether the recovery keeps pace with the price.

The valuation puzzle, stated plainly

KEC looks expensive (~31× reported earnings) and cheap (~20× operating profit, ~0.6× revenue) at the same time, and the difference between those two numbers is the investment case. The high P/E exists because reported profit is being held down by interest and tax; the low sales multiple exists because a ₹23,500 Cr, ₹36,000-Cr-backlog contractor is worth only ₹13,300 Cr today. Resolve the tension one way — margin to 9–10%, interest falling — and reported earnings could roughly double off the same revenue, collapsing that 31× toward the teens without the share price moving. Resolve it the other way — margin stuck at 7%, working capital ballooning with growth — and the 31× is simply what a thin-margin EPC deserves. You are not betting on KEC winning more work. You are betting on the profitability of the work it already has.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A record backlog meeting a grid super-cycle — asked to prove it can earn a real margin on it.

KEC enters FY27 with the fullest order book in its history, a global grid-expansion wave behind its core business, and the first hard evidence — profit growing twice as fast as revenue — that the margin squeeze is easing. What it has not yet proven is that it can hold and extend that recovery while it grows, without working capital and interest clawing the gains back. That, and not order wins, is the open question.

The bull case

  • Grid super-cycle — renewables force massive new transmission worldwide; T&D (KEC's best business) grew 24% in FY26 with a large, rising backlog.
  • Margin recovery underway — 18% operating-PAT growth on 8% revenue is the signature; each point of margin is worth ~₹235 Cr on this revenue base.
  • Record, diversified order book — ~₹36,267 Cr, ~1.5× revenue, spread across five verticals — high visibility and less single-market risk.
  • Deleveraging optionality — net debt easing; interest (~half of EBITDA) is so large that even modest reductions flow hard to profit.
  • Global footprint — SAE Towers gives local execution in the Americas as US grid modernisation accelerates; ~100-country reach.

The bear case

  • Thin, fragile margins — 7% EBITDA leaves no cushion; fixed-price contracts mean a steel/aluminium/zinc spike can wipe out an assumed margin.
  • Working-capital & interest drag — ₹6,722 Cr net debt; an interest bill larger than operating PAT; faster growth consumes more working capital.
  • Competition & bidding discipline — crowded T&D tenders can re-compress margins; the record intake is only good if it was won at sensible prices.
  • Client-payment & execution risk — government and utility clients pay slowly; project delays and cost overruns hit a fixed-price book directly.
  • Rich on reported earnings — ~31× prices in a margin recovery that has only partly arrived; a stall re-rates the stock.
The bottom line

KEC spent decades proving it could build the world's transmission towers and win work on every continent — and none of that was ever the problem. Its lost profits were surrendered a percentage point at a time, to commodity inflation, aggressive bids and an interest bill bigger than its profit. FY26 is the first year in a while where the arithmetic bent back the right way: profit up 18% on revenue up 8%, net debt down, T&D compounding on a grid wave that has years to run. The case now rests on one line item, tracked every quarter — the consolidated EBITDA margin climbing off ~7% toward the 9–10% KEC used to earn, with net debt falling alongside it. Watch those two numbers move together and the record backlog finally becomes real money. Watch the margin stall, and KEC stays what it has been too long: a world-class builder that struggles to keep what it builds.
