00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Bajaj Finance is a non-banking financial company (NBFC) — a lender that is not a bank. It lends money to ordinary Indians and small businesses: financing the TV or phone you buy on EMI, personal loans, two-wheeler and gold loans, loans against property, small-business and SME credit, and (through its listed subsidiary Bajaj Housing Finance) home loans. It is India's largest and most valuable retail-focused NBFC, with ~₹5.10 lakh crore of assets under management (AUM) and ~119 million customers as of March 2026.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A relentless compounder. Consolidated AUM grew ~22% to ₹5.10 lakh Cr in FY26; net profit was ₹19,332 Cr; it booked ~52 million new loans in the year (+21%). Over the past decade it has compounded loan book and profit at ~25%+ a year — a rare consistency that built the most premium valuation in Indian financials.
  • It earns a "spread", not a fee. Like any lender, Bajaj Finance makes money on the gap between what it pays to borrow money and what it charges to lend it out, minus the loans that go bad and the cost of running the machine. In FY26 it earned a financing margin of ~33%, a return on assets (ROA) of ~4.6% and a return on equity (ROE) of ~19% — best-in-class for a lender.
  • The engine is the consumer-durable / EMI flywheel. Bajaj Finance sits inside ~1.5 lakh+ retail stores, financing electronics and appliances on "zero-cost EMI". That low-margin loan is really a customer-acquisition machine: once it has you, it cross-sells personal loans, insurance, gold loans and an EMI card — far higher-value products — over years. That cross-sell is the moat the ~5× book valuation is paying for.
In plain terms

Think of Bajaj Finance as India's most efficient lending and cross-selling factory. It uses cheap, ubiquitous credit — the EMI on your new fridge — as bait to acquire a customer at the checkout counter. Then, armed with that customer's repayment history and an app on their phone, it sells them progressively bigger and more profitable loans and financial products for years. The whole business is a balance between growing the loan book fast, keeping borrowing costs low, and — crucially — not letting too many loans go bad. Get all three right at scale, which it has for 15+ years, and you get one of the great compounding stories in Indian markets — which is exactly why it is priced like one.

01

NBFC economics 101: how a non-bank lender earns

No finance degree required — just the handful of numbers that drive everything.

A lender is, at its core, a spread business: it raises money at one cost and lends it out at a higher rate. The gap between the two — after the loans that go bad and the cost of running the operation — is the profit. Five terms tell you almost the whole story.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and Bajaj Finance's number)
AUMAssets Under Management — the total size of the loan book (all the money currently lent out). The headline scale number. Bajaj Finance: ~₹5.10 lakh Cr consolidated (Mar 2026), up ~22%.
Spread / NIMThe gap between the yield it earns on loans and its cost of funds (what it pays to borrow). Standardised against the book, this is the net interest margin. Bajaj's NII (net interest income) was ~₹11,781 Cr in Q4 FY26 (+20%).
Credit costThe share of the book written off as bad loans each year — the single biggest swing factor for a retail lender. Bajaj's was ~1.9–2.0% (annualised) in FY26 — elevated but managed.
GNPA / NNPAGross / Net Non-Performing Assets — the % of loans gone bad (borrowers not paying). The best gauge of underwriting discipline. Bajaj: ~1.0% / ~0.4% (Mar 2026) — very clean for unsecured-heavy lending.
ROA / ROEReturn on Assets / Equity — profit as a % of the book / of shareholder money. The headline quality scores. Bajaj: ~4.6% ROA, ~19% ROE — among the best of any large lender in India.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (Lending yield − Cost of funds) × Book − Bad loans − Operating costs − Tax. So Bajaj Finance grows profit in four ways — grow the book (AUM), protect the spread (lend at high yield while borrowing cheaply), keep losses low (underwriting), and spread fixed costs over more loans (operating leverage). The magic of a high-quality NBFC is that all four can move the right way at once: a bigger book on a stable spread with disciplined losses produces profit growth above book growth. That's the compounding the market pays up for — and credit cost is the one number that can wreck it overnight.

Why an NBFC is not a bank — and why it matters

  • Banks can take your savings deposit; most NBFCs cannot. A bank funds itself with cheap current/savings (CASA) deposits at near-zero cost. An NBFC like Bajaj Finance must borrow its money — from banks, bond markets, and (because it holds a deposit-accepting licence) fixed deposits from the public, which were a ~₹71,000 Cr book at Dec 2025. Its cost of funds is therefore higher than a bank's — which is why it lends at higher rates to higher-risk, higher-yield customers a bank often won't serve.
  • The trade-off: the NBFC accepts a costlier funding base in exchange for speed, reach and underwriting in segments banks find too small or too risky — the EMI on a ₹30,000 phone, the rural two-wheeler loan, the shopkeeper's working-capital line. That's the structural niche, and Bajaj Finance is the master of it.
  • Lighter (but tightening) regulation. NBFCs historically faced lighter rules than banks. That gap is closing — the RBI's "scale-based regulation" now treats Bajaj Finance as an Upper-Layer NBFC, with bank-like norms on capital, governance and disclosure (see §07).
02

How the money flows: borrow → lend → cross-sell

Read this once and the whole strategy clicks into place.

Bajaj Finance is a conveyor belt for money with a twist. It raises funds at one end, lends them out at a higher rate in the middle, and — this is the bit rivals can't copy — turns each borrower into a relationship it monetises again and again. Two engines run in parallel: the spread engine (borrow cheap, lend dear) and the cross-sell engine (acquire a customer cheaply, sell them more over time).

The spread, decoded

  • Cost of funds — what Bajaj pays to borrow. Its scale, AAA credit rating and diversified mix (bonds, bank lines, public deposits) keep this among the lowest for any NBFC — a genuine competitive advantage, because cheaper funding lets it either earn a fatter spread or under-price rivals.
  • Lending yield — what it charges. A blend of low-yield secured mortgages and high-yield unsecured consumer and SME loans. The mix is deliberately tilted toward higher-yield retail credit a bank won't touch.
  • Net interest income (NII) — yield minus cost of funds, in rupees. ~₹11,781 Cr in Q4 FY26, up ~20%. This is the core revenue line; fees and insurance commissions sit on top.
Why cross-sell is the real moat

Anyone with capital can offer a 0% EMI on a fridge — and many do, at a loss. What very few can do is turn that one transaction into a 10-year, multi-product relationship. Because Bajaj Finance already knows a customer's repayment behaviour, it can pre-approve them for a personal loan or insurance in seconds, at near-zero acquisition cost, with far lower risk than lending to a stranger. So the "loss-leader" durables loan is really the cheapest customer-acquisition channel in Indian retail finance, and the high-margin cross-sell that follows is where the money is made. This is why two numbers matter most: customer franchise (now ~119 million — the size of the funnel) and new loans booked (~52 million in FY26 — the rate it's deepening relationships).

03

The company and who controls it

From a two-wheeler financier to a ₹6-lakh-crore lending platform.

Bajaj Finance is the lending arm of the Bajaj group, one of India's oldest and most respected business houses. It began financing Bajaj scooters and motorcycles, then — under managing director Rajeev Jain from 2007 — transformed into a diversified, data-driven, multi-product retail lender. It is majority-held by the listed holding company Bajaj Finserv.

Ownership & structure

  • Holding company: Bajaj Finserv (itself listed) is the parent, holding just over half of Bajaj Finance; the broader Bajaj promoter group sits above that. Promoter group holding in Bajaj Finance is ~55%. A stable, long-term, conservative promoter is a real asset in lending, where governance and risk culture decide survival.
  • Leadership: long-time MD Rajeev Jain (now Vice-Chairman / executive leadership) is the architect of the modern company; the chairmanship sits within the Bajaj family (Sanjiv Bajaj). Deep institutional and management continuity.
  • Key subsidiary: Bajaj Housing Finance (~88.7% owned, separately listed) — the home-loan arm; plus Bajaj Financial Securities and the broader Bajaj Finserv financial-services stable (insurance, broking). See §08.
  • Balance sheet: well-capitalised (capital adequacy comfortably above RBI's requirement), AAA-rated, with a granular, fast-growing deposit and borrowing base funding the loan growth.

The EMI & cross-sell flywheel — the real moat

The single most important idea in the entire Bajaj Finance investment case.

Most lenders pay dearly to find borrowers — through advertising, branches and brokers. Bajaj Finance's masterstroke was to invert that cost: it acquires customers at the precise moment they're already spending, then keeps selling to them for years. This is the flywheel, and it explains both the company's profitability and its valuation.

The flywheel in one mechanism

Acquire a customer cheaply at a store checkout → capture their repayment data → use it to pre-approve them, in seconds, for higher-value loans and products → each new sale costs almost nothing and carries lower risk (you already know they pay) → profit per customer compounds → the surplus funds even wider distribution and better tech → which acquires more customers, more cheaply. Round and round. The ~119 million customer franchise is the flywheel's stored energy; the ~52 million new loans a year is its speed; the cross-sell ratio is its efficiency. No rival in Indian retail finance has built this at the same scale.

06

The omnichannel app & the FinAI bet

From a finance company at the checkout to a financial-services platform in your pocket.

For most of its history, Bajaj Finance met customers physically — at the electronics store, the dealer, the branch. The strategic transformation of the 2020s is to also own the digital relationship: the Bajaj Finserv app, a web platform, and proprietary marketplaces that let it sell loans, insurance, investments and payments directly, continuously, and at near-zero marginal cost.

  • The omnichannel idea. Bajaj aims to be "omnipresent" — reachable across physical stores, the app, the web and partner channels, with the customer moving seamlessly between them. It operates across 4,000+ locations and 190,000+ active distribution points, now fused with a digital front end.
  • Three proprietary marketplaces. Inside the Bajaj Finserv app sit an EMI store, an insurance marketplace and an investment marketplace — turning a lending app into a financial supermarket, and multiplying cross-sell surface area.
  • FinAI. In FY26 Bajaj rolled out a generative-AI programme ("FinAI") across underwriting, collections and service — aimed at lower operating cost, faster decisions and hyper-personalised cross-sell. The opex-to-income ratio sitting around the low-30s% is the scoreboard to watch.
Why this matters strategically

The omnichannel push does three things. First, it lowers acquisition and servicing cost — a digital cross-sell is far cheaper than a feet-on-street one, which protects margins as the book grows. Second, it defends the customer against fintechs and banks trying to peel them away — own the app and you own the relationship. Third, it generates data, the fuel for better underwriting and sharper cross-sell. The bet is that Bajaj can be both a low-cost balance-sheet lender (its historic strength) and a slick digital platform (the fintech challenge) — capturing the best of both. The risk: heavy tech investment, and well-funded digital rivals competing for the same screen.

07

The RBI, risk-weights & the unsecured-lending cycle

The structural force unique to an unsecured-heavy lender — and Bajaj sits in the eye of it.

Every lender lives or dies by the regulator and the credit cycle, but a business tilted toward unsecured consumer credit is unusually exposed to both. India's central bank, the RBI, swung hard at exactly this kind of lending in late 2023, then partly reversed in 2025 — and where that pendulum sits is one of the most important variables for Bajaj Finance. This is the bear's structural lever and the bull's reset button at once.

A higher risk weight forces a lender to hold more capital per loan — slowing growth and lowering returns. The Nov-2023 hike was a deliberate brake on a credit boom; the 2025 rollback eased it.

What actually happened — and why it matters

  • The boom (2022–23). Unsecured retail credit at NBFCs was surging ~50% a year. The RBI grew worried about over-leveraged borrowers and frothy growth.
  • The clampdown (Nov 2023). The RBI raised the risk weight on consumer credit from 100% to 125% — meaning lenders had to set aside ~25% more capital per unsecured loan. That mechanically slows growth and dents returns. It also signalled the regulator wanted the whole industry to cool unsecured lending.
  • The reversal (effective Apr 2025). With growth having moderated, the RBI rolled the risk weight back to 100% and eased bank lending to NBFCs — freeing up capital and reviving the growth runway. NBFC stocks, Bajaj included, rallied on the news.
  • The lived consequence. Through this cycle Bajaj's credit costs rose (a slice of unsecured borrowers over-extended), which is why FY26 credit cost sat elevated near ~1.9–2.0% and management trimmed growth guidance in parts of the book (notably MSME and captive two-/three-wheeler). The improvement in headline GNPA to ~1.0% by Mar 2026 suggests the worst of the stress is passing — but the cycle is the thing to watch.
Why this is serious — but not fatal

Four things keep the story intact despite the regulatory and credit-cycle risk. (1) Diversification. Unsecured consumer credit is a large but not dominant slice; mortgages, SME and secured lending cushion it. (2) Underwriting pedigree. Bajaj's losses through the stress stayed far below most peers — its data and scorecards are a genuine edge. (3) Capital strength. It is well-capitalised above RBI minimums, so a risk-weight hike pinches returns but doesn't threaten solvency. (4) The regulator can also help. The 2025 rollback shows the pendulum swings both ways; an NBFC that survives the tightening cleanly emerges with stronger share as weaker lenders retreat. Net: regulation caps the pace of unsecured growth, but a disciplined lender can keep compounding through the cycle.

The honest tension: Bajaj Finance's premium is built on fast, high-return unsecured growth — and that is precisely what the RBI watches most closely and what sours first in a downturn. The bull believes underwriting + diversification + scale ride the cycle; the bear believes the next credit-cost spike, or the next regulatory tightening, exposes how cyclical "consistent compounding" really is. Watch credit cost and GNPA together each quarter: if growth re-accelerates while losses stay contained, the machine is working.

Bajaj Housing Finance — the listed crown jewel

A separately-listed home-loan giant that Bajaj Finance still mostly owns.

One of the most important — and most misunderstood — parts of the Bajaj Finance story is its mortgage subsidiary, Bajaj Housing Finance Limited (BHFL). Built from 2018, it grew into one of India's largest housing-finance companies, and its September 2024 IPO was a blockbuster. Crucially, Bajaj Finance still owns ~88.7% of it — so BHFL's value flows straight back to Bajaj Finance shareholders.

How to think about the structure

When you buy Bajaj Finance, you are buying two things: a high-yield, high-return consumer-and-SME lender (the parent), and a ~88.7% stake in a separately-listed, fast-growing, low-risk mortgage lender (BHFL). Analysts often value the two pieces separately — a "sum-of-the-parts" — because the market puts its own price on the listed BHFL stake. The structure gives Bajaj Finance optionality: it can raise capital by selling down BHFL without losing the strategic benefit of a captive mortgage engine. The watch-item is simply that two listed entities in one group invite cross-holding-discount debates and the occasional overhang when stake sales happen.

How to think about the ~5× book multiple

Lenders are usually valued on price-to-book (price versus net worth), and most Indian banks trade at 1–3× book. Bajaj Finance trades around 5× book and ~30× earnings — a steep premium that only makes sense if you believe it can keep compounding AUM at ~20%+ on a ~19% ROE with contained losses for many more years, while the cross-sell flywheel lifts profit per customer. Bulls argue a debt-light, AAA-rated, structurally-advantaged retail-credit platform with a 119-million-customer moat and a huge under-penetration runway deserves a scarcity premium. Bears argue ~5× book leaves no room for error: a credit-cost spike, a regulatory tightening, a growth slowdown, or simply multiple compression as the law of large numbers bites, and a premium lender can de-rate hard. Both can be right at different points in the cycle — which is why AUM growth, credit cost and ROE are the numbers to track every quarter.

The rivals and the runway

A huge, under-penetrated credit market — contested by banks, NBFCs and fintechs.

India's retail-credit market is vast, fast-growing and still under-penetrated — household debt to GDP is low by global standards, and hundreds of millions of Indians are only now entering the formal credit system. That is Bajaj Finance's runway. But it is contested from three directions at once.

The growth runway, in three layers

  • Penetration — formal credit is still shallow across India; new-to-credit customers, rural reach and SME lending offer years of structural volume growth.
  • Cross-sell — deepening products-per-customer across the 119-million franchise lifts revenue with almost no extra acquisition cost — the highest-return growth lever.
  • New engines — payments, insurance and investment distribution via the Bajaj Finserv app, plus the mortgage runway at BHFL, extend the platform well beyond pure lending.
The next chapter is less about finding new borrowers and more about owning the entire financial life of the 119 million it already has — that's where the operating leverage and the moat both live.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely exceptional franchise — priced for it to keep executing.

Bajaj Finance is one of the highest-quality compounding stories in Indian financials: a diversified, well-capitalised, AAA-rated retail lender with best-in-class returns, a vast customer moat and a long penetration runway, run by a stable, conservative promoter. As with all great compounders, the investment debate is almost entirely about price and the credit cycle, not business quality.

The bull case

  • Cross-sell moat — a 119-million-customer flywheel and the lowest NBFC funding cost; no rival matches the scale + diversification + data combination.
  • Best-in-class returns — ~4.6% ROA / ~19% ROE with sub-1% blended GNPA, sustained through a stress cycle.
  • Long runway — under-penetrated credit market; rural, SME and payments barely tapped; cross-sell lifts profit per customer with little extra cost.
  • BHFL optionality — a ~88.7%-owned, separately-listed mortgage giant that both steadies the book and crystallises value.
  • Regulatory tailwind — the 2025 risk-weight rollback reopened the growth runway; disciplined survivors gain share.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~5× book / ~30× earnings leaves little margin for error; vulnerable to de-rating on any wobble.
  • Unsecured / credit-cycle risk — elevated credit costs in FY26 show how fast the unsecured book can sour; the next downturn is the real test.
  • Regulatory overhang — the RBI watches unsecured retail and Upper-Layer NBFCs closely; tightening can cap growth and returns at will.
  • Law of large numbers — sustaining 20%+ growth on a ₹5-lakh-crore base gets harder each year; competition from banks and fintechs intensifies.
  • Funding-cost & key-person risk — an NBFC is only as cheap as its access to funds; and the franchise has long leaned on a single architect's vision.
The bottom line

Bajaj Finance is a structurally advantaged, exceptionally well-run retail-credit platform with a genuine cross-sell moat, a long runway, and a balance sheet that can fund its own growth. The business risk is low by lender standards — but it is still a lender, and lenders are cyclical: the valuation risk and the credit-cycle risk are both real. The story over the next few years will be written in three numbers each quarter — AUM growth (is the runway still there?), credit cost / GNPA (is underwriting holding through the cycle?), and ROE (is the compounding intact?). Get those right and the premium is justified; stumble, and a 5× book stock has a long way to fall.
