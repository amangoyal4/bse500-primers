00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

State Bank of India is India's largest bank by almost every measure — deposits, advances, branches, customers and balance-sheet size. It is a 200-year-old, government-controlled institution that takes in savings from roughly a fifth of the country and lends them out to companies, farmers, homebuyers and the government itself. It is also a financial conglomerate: it controls listed market leaders in life insurance (SBI Life), credit cards (SBI Cards) and the country's largest mutual fund (SBI Mutual Fund).

Three facts frame everything:

  • Unrivalled scale, funded cheaply. SBI holds ~₹59.8 lakh crore of deposits (≈22% of all bank deposits in India) across a network of ~22,000+ branches and roughly half a billion customers. Crucially, ~40% of those deposits sit in low-/no-interest current and savings accounts (CASA) — the cheapest funding in banking. That low-cost deposit base is the moat.
  • A record year, after a brutal decade. FY26 (year ended Mar 2026) net profit crossed ₹80,032 Cr for the first time (+12.9%), on a 1.12% return on assets and ~18.6% return on equity. Gross bad loans fell to 1.49% — a world away from the ~10.9% peak of FY18, when the bank was at the centre of India's NPA crisis.
  • It is ~57% owned by the Government of India. That is a double-edged sword the whole investment case turns on: a sovereign guarantee that makes its deposits the safest in the country and funding ultra-cheap, versus political/PSU constraints, social-banking mandates and the ever-present risk of capital being directed to national priorities rather than shareholder returns.
In plain terms

Think of SBI as a giant, government-backed money utility. Half a billion Indians park their salaries and savings with it because they trust it can never fail — the state stands behind it. SBI pays them little or nothing for those deposits, then lends that money out at higher rates to companies, home buyers and the government, pocketing the spread. Because it is so big and so trusted, its raw materials (deposits) are cheaper than almost anyone else's — that is its single biggest advantage. The catch: the same government that makes it safe also tells it to open accounts for the poor, fund priority sectors, and occasionally absorbs its cheap capital — so SBI rarely earns the eye-watering returns of a nimble private bank, and trades at a discount to match.

01

Bank economics 101: how a bank actually earns

No finance degree required — just the handful of numbers that drive everything.

A bank is, at its core, a spread business built on trust. It borrows money cheaply (mostly from depositors), lends it out at a higher rate, and keeps the difference — while managing the risk that some borrowers won't pay it back. Five ideas tell you almost the whole story.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and SBI's number)
DepositsMoney the public lends to the bank (savings, current, fixed deposits). The bank's raw material and cheapest funding. SBI: ~₹59.8 lakh Cr.
Advances / loansMoney the bank lends out (to companies, retail, farmers, government). Its income-earning assets. SBI: ~₹49.3 lakh Cr gross.
NII & NIM"Net Interest Income" = interest earned minus interest paid. "Net Interest Margin" expresses it as a % of assets — the spread. SBI FY26: NII ₹1.73 lakh Cr, domestic NIM ~3.03%.
CASA ratioShare of deposits in Current & Savings Accounts — these pay little/no interest, so a high CASA means cheaper funding. SBI: ~39.5%.
GNPA / NNPA"Gross / Net Non-Performing Assets" — loans that have gone bad, as a % of advances. The single best gauge of credit risk. SBI FY26: 1.49% / 0.39%.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ (interest earned − interest paid) + fees − operating costs − loan losses − tax. So a bank gets richer in four ways — lend more (grow advances), fund cheaper (raise CASA, the key lever), widen the spread (NIM), and lose less to bad loans (credit cost). The brilliance and the danger are the same: a bank lends out ~10× its own capital, so it is enormously leveraged. When credit is good, a thin spread on a huge book is hugely profitable; when borrowers default, losses eat capital fast. That is why asset quality is the number that can make or break a bank — and why SBI's journey from a ~10.9% bad-loan peak to 1.49% is the story of the last decade.

Two more gauges every bank-watcher uses

  • ROA (Return on Assets) — profit as a % of the bank's total assets. For a big bank, anything above ~1% is healthy. SBI FY26: 1.12%. (It looks tiny because banks are so leveraged — but on ₹70+ lakh crore of assets, 1.12% is ₹80,000 Cr of profit.)
  • Capital adequacy (CAR / CET-1) — the shock-absorber. Regulators force banks to hold a minimum cushion of their own capital against risky loans so they can survive losses. SBI FY26: CAR 15.4%, CET-1 12.3% — comfortably above the regulatory floor, but thinner than top private banks.
02

The deposit franchise: the moat almost no one can copy

If you understand one thing about SBI, make it this.

Every bank competes for deposits, but SBI's liability franchise is in a class of its own. Half a billion customers, ~22,000+ branches reaching into villages no private bank will ever serve, and a sovereign guarantee in customers' minds combine to give SBI the largest and stickiest pool of low-cost deposits in the country. Cheap, stable funding is the foundation everything else is built on.

The value chain — how a deposit becomes a profit

SBI's businesses aren't silos; they form one connected money-flow. Cheap deposits feed loans, loans earn a spread, and the customer relationship is then cross-sold into the subsidiaries. The further down the chain, the higher the value SBI extracts per customer.

Why "cheap deposits" is the whole game

Imagine two banks that both lend at 9%. Bank A funds itself at 6%; Bank B, with a stronger deposit base, funds at 4.5%. Bank B earns a 4.5% spread vs Bank A's 3% — half as much again, on the same loans, with the same risk. That is the power of low-cost deposits, and SBI's ~40% CASA (₹22.6 lakh Cr of near-free current/savings money) plus its scale and trust hand it structurally cheaper funding than almost any rival. The flip side: SBI's NIM (~3.0%) is actually lower than top private banks' (~3.5–4%+) — because SBI also lends a lot to safe-but-low-yielding borrowers (large corporates, government, home loans). Its edge is safety and scale, not the fattest margin.

03

The company and who controls it

A 200-year-old institution, ~57% owned by the state.

SBI traces its roots to the Bank of Calcutta, founded in 1806 — making it one of the oldest banks in Asia. Nationalised in 1955 from the Imperial Bank of India, it has been the financial backbone of the Indian state ever since. Today the Government of India owns ~57.5%; the rest trades publicly, held by LIC, foreign and domestic institutions, and retail investors.

Timeline

  • 1806
    Bank of Calcutta founded; later merges into the Imperial Bank of India (1921).
  • 1955
    The Imperial Bank is nationalised and renamed State Bank of India — the country's flagship public-sector bank.
  • 2008–17
    Merges its associate banks; in 2017 absorbs five associate SBs + Bharatiya Mahila Bank in one of India's largest bank mergers, vaulting into the global top-50 by assets.
  • 2015–18
    RBI's Asset Quality Review forces honest recognition of hidden bad loans; SBI's gross NPA peaks near ~10.9% (FY18) and it posts a rare annual loss — the low point.
  • 2017–19
    Lists subsidiaries: SBI Life (2017) and SBI Cards (2020); recoveries and the IBC bankruptcy code drive the clean-up.
  • FY26
    Net profit crosses ₹80,032 Cr for the first time; GNPA down to 1.49%, ROE ~18.6%; raises ~₹25,000 Cr equity (FY25) to bolster capital; SBI Mutual Fund IPO being prepared.

Ownership & leadership

  • Majority owner: the Government of India (~57.5%), via the Ministry of Finance — notably the lowest government stake among public-sector banks, giving SBI relatively more operating room than its PSU peers.
  • Leadership: Chairman C. S. Setty (career SBI banker) leads a professional management team; the bank is run by career bankers, not political appointees, though the board includes government and RBI nominees.
  • Capital: CAR ~15.4% / CET-1 ~12.3% at FY26 — adequate and recently topped up (a ~₹25,000 Cr QIP in FY25), but a perennial question is whether the state will inject capital or dilute minority holders when growth demands it.
04

Where the money is lent

A balanced loan book — retail-led, with a large corporate and agri spine.

SBI lends across the whole economy, which makes its loan book a near-proxy for India itself. The mix has shifted over the past decade toward safer retail lending (especially home loans) and away from the lumpy corporate exposures that caused the last crisis — a deliberate de-risking. In FY26, gross advances grew a strong ~16.9% to ₹49.3 lakh crore.

Why the home-loan book matters so much

SBI is India's largest home-loan lender by a distance. Home loans are the gold standard of safe lending: secured by property, spread across millions of salaried borrowers, with very low default rates. As home loans and other retail rise in the mix, SBI's overall risk falls even as the book grows — a quieter but powerful reason its asset quality has been so resilient. The trade-off is yield: a home loan earns far less than an unsecured personal loan or a risky corporate exposure, which is part of why SBI's NIM sits below the most aggressive private lenders. It is, deliberately, a volume-and-safety bank, not a margin-maximiser.

05

Asset quality and the clean-up that defines the decade

The single most important transformation in SBI's modern history.

A decade ago, SBI was the poster child of India's bad-loan crisis. Aggressive corporate and infrastructure lending in the 2000s went sour; the RBI's 2015 Asset Quality Review forced banks to stop hiding it. SBI's gross NPA ratio peaked near ~10.9% in FY18 — meaning more than one rupee in ten lent had effectively gone bad — and the bank posted a rare annual loss. What happened next is the heart of the modern investment case: a multi-year, structural clean-up that took the gross NPA ratio all the way down to 1.49% in FY26.

Net NPA fell in parallel to just 0.39% in FY26, with a provision coverage ratio of ~74% (over 90% including written-off accounts) — meaning the residual bad loans are heavily provided for.
What actually fixed it — and why it should last

Four forces drove the turnaround. (1) Recognition — the AQR forced the bad loans into the open so they could be dealt with. (2) The bankruptcy code (IBC, 2016) gave banks real teeth to recover money from defaulting promoters, driving record recoveries. (3) A de-risked book — SBI shifted lending toward safe retail/home loans and tightened corporate underwriting, so the new loans are far less likely to go bad. (4) Heavy provisioning — it set aside capital against bad loans aggressively, so the cushion is real. The result is a credit cost of just ~0.4% in FY26 and management guiding to ~0.5% ahead. The bull case rests on the belief that this is structural, not luck; the bear case is that a downturn, or renewed political pressure to lend, could reopen old wounds.

06

The sovereign double-edge: shield and leash

The defining force unique to SBI — and the lens every other fact must pass through.

SBI is not just a bank; it is an instrument of the Indian state. The Government of India owns ~57.5% of it, and that single fact shapes everything — its funding cost, its mandate, its valuation and its risks. The honest way to read SBI is to see government ownership as simultaneously its greatest strength and its biggest structural drag. Both are true at once.

The shield — why state ownership helps

  • An implicit sovereign guarantee. Depositors believe SBI cannot be allowed to fail — so they accept low interest, giving SBI the cheapest, stickiest funding in India. This is the deepest root of its moat.
  • Crisis backstop. When PSU banks needed capital after the NPA crisis, the government recapitalised them. SBI has a lender of last resort that no private bank enjoys.
  • Preferred banker to the state. Government salaries, pensions, treasury business, sovereign schemes and flows naturally route through SBI — vast, captive, low-cost volume.
  • Scale & reach no private bank can match — the network the state mandated decades ago is now a commercial asset.

The leash — why state ownership costs

  • Social-banking mandates. Priority-sector lending (agriculture, weaker sections), Jan Dhan accounts and rural reach are obligations a pure profit-maximiser would avoid — they depress returns.
  • Directed lending risk. The state can lean on SBI to fund national priorities or stressed sectors — the original seed of the last NPA crisis.
  • Capital & dilution overhang. Dividends and equity decisions answer partly to the exchequer; the government may dilute minorities or extract capital.
  • A permanent valuation discount. Because of all the above, SBI trades at a fraction of private-bank multiples — even when its numbers are excellent.
The discount, quantified: SBI delivers an ROE (~18.6%) comparable to top private banks, yet trades around ~1.4–1.6× book value versus ~3×+ for the best private lenders. The gap is the sovereign leash priced in. Bulls argue the discount is too wide given how clean the book now is and how valuable the subsidiaries are; bears argue the discount is permanent and deserved, because the government will always, eventually, ask SBI to do something a private bank wouldn't.
07

The hidden value: SBI's listed subsidiaries

SBI is not just a bank — it controls a stable of market-leading financial businesses.

One of the most under-appreciated facts about SBI is that it is a financial conglomerate. It controls the leaders (or near-leaders) in life insurance, credit cards and asset management — two already listed, one heading for IPO. Add up SBI's stakes in these, and a large chunk of SBI's own market value is "covered" by businesses outside the core bank. Analysts call this a sum-of-the-parts (SOTP) story.

Why the sum-of-parts matters

Put crudely: SBI's stakes in just the listed subsidiaries (SBI Life + SBI Cards) plus a soon-to-list SBI MF can be worth well over ₹1.5 lakh crore — a meaningful slice of SBI's own ~₹9.6 lakh crore market cap. The bullish reading is that the core bank is being valued cheaply once you strip out the subsidiaries — you're getting India's biggest bank at a discount, with the insurance/cards/AMC businesses thrown in for less than they'd fetch alone (a classic "holding-company discount"). The SBI Mutual Fund IPO, expected in 2026, is a near-term catalyst that could crystallise some of this hidden value and put a hard market price on the largest of the three. The bearish reading: holding-company discounts rarely close, and SBI is unlikely to sell down these prized assets, so the value stays "trapped" on the balance sheet.

08

Financial inclusion, scale and the digital flywheel

The mandate that costs money today — and may be the deepest moat tomorrow.

No private bank would ever have built SBI's footprint: branches in remote villages, accounts for the poor, banking correspondents in places with no economics. As the state's chief instrument of financial inclusion, SBI carries the largest share of India's Jan Dhan (no-frills) accounts and reaches customers no rival will. That mandate does depress returns — but it has handed SBI a customer base of unmatched depth, which it is now monetising digitally.

How a cost centre becomes a moat

For years, financial inclusion looked like pure cost — millions of low-balance accounts that lose money to service. Digital changed the maths. Through YONO and India's UPI/digital-payments rails, SBI can now serve those half a billion customers at a fraction of branch cost, cross-sell them loans, insurance (SBI Life) and cards (SBI Cards), and lift business-per-employee. The flywheel: unmatched reach → unmatched data and relationships → cheap digital cross-sell → higher returns on a base no one else can replicate. If SBI executes, the very mandate that depresses its margins becomes the foundation of a durable, hard-to-attack franchise. If it doesn't, the cost stays and the value never shows up.

Financials & valuation

A record year — and a stock that still trades at a sovereign discount.

FY26 was the best year in SBI's history: profit crossed ₹80,000 Cr, returns are strong, and the book is clean. The debate isn't the recent performance — it's whether the market will ever pay a "private-bank" price for a state-controlled bank, and whether margins can hold as interest rates fall.

How to think about the valuation

Banks are usually valued on price-to-book (P/B) — the market price versus the bank's net worth — because their "assets" are loans, and a bank earning a high return on equity deserves a higher multiple of book. SBI now earns a ~18–20% ROE, yet trades around 1.4–1.6× book, while HDFC Bank or ICICI trade well above ~2.5–3×. That gap is the sovereign discount (Section 06). The bull math: a record-profit, cleanest-book-in-a-decade bank, with subsidiary value on top (Section 07), at well under 2× book and ~8–9× earnings, looks cheap if the clean-up holds and the discount narrows even slightly. The bear math: NIMs are under pressure as the RBI cuts rates (Q4 FY26 domestic NIM dipped to ~2.93%), government ownership caps the multiple permanently, and any growth surge could require dilutive capital. The dividend of ₹17.35/share for FY26 is a tangible return to shareholders in the meantime.

Watch the margin: SBI guides to FY27 domestic NIM above 3%, credit growth of 13–15% and credit cost ~0.5%. The single most important quarterly number to track is NIM — in a falling-rate environment, SBI's loans reprice down faster than its deposits, squeezing the spread before deposit costs catch up. A NIM that holds near/above 3% keeps the profit machine humming; one that slides toward 2.8% is the bear's early warning.
10

The industry and the rivals

A two-tier banking system — and SBI straddles it.

Indian banking splits into two camps: public-sector banks (PSBs) — state-owned, scale-heavy, return-light — and private banks — nimble, profitable, premium-valued. SBI is the king of the PSBs but, post-clean-up, increasingly competes on quality with the best private banks. The structural backdrop is a long, under-penetrated credit cycle in a fast-growing economy.

The competitive tension to watch

Two dynamics matter. First, the private banks keep out-earning SBI on margin and returns, and the market rewards them with far higher multiples — the question is whether SBI's clean book and digital push can narrow that gap, or whether the structural ceiling holds. Second, the whole sector is a leveraged bet on India's growth: as a $4-trillion-plus economy formalises and borrows more, the credit pie grows for years — and SBI, with the cheapest funding and the widest reach, is positioned to take a large, low-risk slice of it. The risk is symmetric: a sharp economic downturn would hit the biggest, most economy-correlated lender hardest.

The regulatory backdrop: SBI is regulated by the RBI (which sets capital, priority-sector and provisioning rules) and is partly answerable to the government as owner. Interest-rate cycles (the RBI's repo rate) directly move its NIM; priority-sector and inclusion mandates are permanent features; and as a "Domestic Systemically Important Bank" it carries extra capital buffers. Regulation here is not a tail risk — it is the weather.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cleaned-up giant — priced for its sovereign constraints, with hidden value on the side.

SBI in FY26 is a fundamentally stronger institution than the crisis-era bank of a decade ago: record profits, a clean book, ~18% returns, a digital franchise and valuable subsidiaries. The investment debate is almost entirely about valuation and the sovereign ceiling — not business quality, which has rarely looked better.

The bull case

  • Unmatched deposit moat — ~22% of India's deposits, ~40% CASA, a sovereign-trust funding edge no rival can copy.
  • Cleanest book in a decade — GNPA 1.49%, NNPA 0.39%, ~0.4% credit cost; the structural de-risking looks durable.
  • Record returns at a discount price — ~18.6% ROE yet ~1.4–1.6× book and ~8–9× earnings.
  • Subsidiary value on top — SBI Life, SBI Cards and a soon-to-IPO SBI Mutual Fund partly "cover" the market cap; the AMC listing is a catalyst.
  • Digital flywheel — YONO + half a billion customers turning the inclusion mandate into a cross-sell engine.

The bear case

  • The sovereign leash — government ownership caps the multiple, imposes social mandates, and can direct lending or extract/dilute capital.
  • Margin pressure — falling RBI rates squeeze NIM (Q4 domestic NIM dipped to ~2.93%); the spread is the profit engine.
  • Economy-correlated — the most India-exposed lender; a downturn or a return of corporate stress hits it hardest.
  • Permanent valuation discount — holding-company and PSU discounts rarely close, so the "hidden value" may stay trapped.
  • Capital & dilution overhang — thinner CET-1 than top private banks; heavy growth could need fresh equity.
The bottom line

SBI is a genuinely transformed, sovereign-scale bank with the country's best deposit franchise, a clean balance sheet, ~18% returns and a basket of valuable subsidiaries — trading at a fraction of private-bank multiples precisely because the government owns it. That is the whole investment case in one sentence: business quality high, valuation capped by ownership. The story over the coming years will be written in three numbers — NIM (does the spread hold as rates fall?), GNPA / credit cost (does the clean book stay clean?), and the SBI Mutual Fund IPO (does hidden subsidiary value get crystallised?). Get those right and a cheap multiple on a record-profit bank can re-rate; stumble on any of them, and the sovereign discount stays exactly where it is.
