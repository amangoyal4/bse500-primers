Lemon Tree Hotels, the mid-market chain learning to earn without owning

A complete walk through India's largest mid-priced hotel company — how a hotel actually makes money (average room rate × occupancy, and the ferocious operating leverage that follows), why the same brand earns two utterly different returns depending on who owns the building, and how a debt-funded owner of concrete-and-steel hotels is turning itself into a lighter, fee-collecting brand manager just as India's travel demand runs hot.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Lemon Tree Hotels runs India's largest chain of mid-priced hotels — the clean, functional, business-and-leisure hotels that sit below the five-star luxury names but a clear rung above unbranded budget lodgings. It operates a ladder of brands from economy to upscale, and it makes money two very different ways: it owns and leases hotels (taking all the room revenue but also all the capital cost and debt), and it increasingly just manages hotels that someone else paid to build (taking a slim management fee for putting its brand and operating system on the door). At FY26 close it ran 131 operating hotels with ~11,811 rooms, with a pipeline of roughly the same size again — and that pipeline is almost entirely the fee-based, capital-free kind.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The up-cycle is real and it shows in the rate. FY26 was a record year: gross average room rate (ARR) of ~₹6,875 and occupancy of 73.5% were both the highest the company has ever printed for a full year. Revenue rose ~13% to ~₹1,453 Cr, EBITDA ~10% to ~₹699 Cr (a ~48% margin), and profit after tax jumped ~19% to ~₹288 Cr. When Indian travel demand outruns new hotel supply, room rates rise straight into profit.
  • The business is quietly changing shape. Lemon Tree built its scale by owning hotels, funded with debt. Its growth engine now is managed and franchised rooms, where a third party owns the building and Lemon Tree collects a fee. FY26 management fees reached ~₹169.7 Cr, up 14%, and the signed pipeline is dominated by these asset-light rooms. It is a company mid-transition — an owned-hotel balance sheet plus a fast-compounding fee business bolted on top.
  • The debt is coming down and the mix is going up-market. Borrowings fell to ~₹1,500 Cr from ~₹1,699 Cr, and the cost of that debt dropped to 7.42%. At the top of the ladder, its new upscale brand Aurika — including a 669-room hotel at Mumbai airport, one of India's largest — pushes a mid-market chain into higher rates and a richer guest. The stock trades near ~₹8,500 Cr of market value, roughly 14–17× EV/EBITDA — priced for the fee business to keep scaling while the cycle stays warm.
In plain terms

Lemon Tree is what you get when a hotel company decides it would rather sell the flag than pour the foundation. For twenty years it grew by borrowing money to build and buy hotels, keeping every rupee of room profit but also carrying every rupee of debt. Now it is adding a second, lighter business on top: it lets other people fund the concrete, and simply runs the hotel under its brand for a fee — no land, no loan, almost no capital. The old business still dominates the revenue and still carries the debt; the new one barely registers in rupees yet but grows fast and needs no money to do it. The whole investment question is how quickly the light half grows relative to the heavy half — while India's travel boom keeps filling the rooms.

01

Hotel economics 101: how a room earns its keep

No finance background needed — just the handful of terms that drive the whole business.

A hotel is a machine for selling the same rooms over and over, night after night. Unlike a factory, it cannot store its product: an empty room on a Tuesday is revenue that is gone forever — you can never sell last night's room again. That single fact shapes everything about how hotels make (and lose) money. To follow Lemon Tree, you need four terms and one idea.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
ARR"Average Room Rate" — the average price a guest pays per room per night. Lemon Tree's FY26 gross ARR was ~₹6,875. When demand is strong, a hotel raises the rate rather than discount to fill — and rate rises flow almost straight to profit.
OccupancyThe share of available rooms actually sold. FY26 ran at 73.5% — high for the industry. Above roughly 70%, a hotel gains the pricing power to push ARR, because it is no longer desperate for the marginal guest.
RevPAR"Revenue Per Available Room" = ARR × occupancy. The single number that captures how hard each room is working. FY26 RevPAR was roughly ₹5,050 (₹6,875 × 73.5%). RevPAR growth is the pulse of the whole sector.
Keys / roomsThe count of sellable rooms — the industry's unit of size. Lemon Tree runs ~11,811 operational rooms across 131 hotels, with a pipeline of a similar number again. Scale is measured in keys, not hotels.
The one idea that decides everything: operating leverage

A hotel's costs are mostly fixed. The building, the loan interest, the front desk, the housekeeping supervisor, the lobby lights — most of that is paid whether the hotel is half-empty or full. So the first block of guests each night goes to covering those fixed costs and earns the hotel almost nothing. But once the hotel crosses its break-even occupancy, the next guest is nearly pure profit: the marginal cost of cleaning one more already-built room is tiny — a bit of laundry, some electricity, breakfast. This is why hotel profits swing so violently. In a good year, rising ARR and occupancy land on a fixed cost base and margins balloon; Lemon Tree's ~48% EBITDA margin is exactly this effect at work. In a bad year — a travel shock, a recession — the same fixed costs sit there while revenue falls away, and the swing runs brutally in reverse.

That leverage is the reason hotel stocks are so cyclical, and the reason the ownership model matters so much. If you own the hotel and its debt, you get the full upside of a boom — and the full downside of a bust, with interest payments that do not care how full your rooms are. If you only manage the hotel for a fee, you get a smaller, steadier slice with none of that debt. Hold that distinction; it is the axis the entire Lemon Tree story turns on.

02

The brand ladder: one company, many price points

From an economy room to an airport luxury suite — and why the spread is deliberate.

Lemon Tree does not sell one kind of hotel; it sells a ladder of them, each aimed at a different traveller and price point. That breadth lets it put a hotel on almost any decent site — a small city, a highway, an airport, a pilgrimage town — under whichever brand fits the local demand and land cost. The ladder runs from bare-bones economy to genuine upscale.

The logic of the ladder is coverage. A single brand can only chase a single slice of demand; a ladder lets Lemon Tree take the site that suits a Red Fox on a highway and the site that suits an Aurika at an airport, and everything in between, without turning business away for want of the right flag. It also lets the company grow a guest up the ladder — a traveller who knows the mid-market Lemon Tree name may trade up to a Premier or an Aurika as their budget rises, all within the same loyalty and booking system.

Why the mid-market is a good place to stand

India's branded-hotel market is barrelled at the two ends — glamorous luxury names at the top and a vast, chaotic ocean of unbranded budget lodgings at the bottom. The mid-market in between is under-supplied with quality branded rooms, yet it is exactly where India's growing middle class and travelling salesforce actually stay. That is the gap Lemon Tree was built to fill: hotels good enough to trust, cheap enough to expense, and branded enough to book on an app. Being the largest player in that specific band gives it something the luxury chains can't cheaply copy — a national network of consistent, mid-priced rooms that corporates and travel platforms can rely on in a hundred cities at once.

03

The company, the founder, and who controls it

A founder-built chain with a widely held register and institutional money behind the bricks.

Lemon Tree Hotels was founded in 2002 by Patanjali (Patu) G. Keswani — an IIT-and-IIM-trained former consultant and hotelier who set out to build a chain aimed squarely at the middle of the market, not the top. He remains Chairman and Managing Director, and is the face and architect of the company. Unusually for a founder-led Indian firm, the promoter stake is modest.

Timeline

  • 2002

  • 2004–2010

  • 2020–2021
    The pandemic slams the whole sector — occupancy collapses, losses mount, debt bites. Lemon Tree survives, cuts costs hard, and leans further into the asset-light, fee-based model.
  • 2023

  • FY26

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter: the Keswani family holds roughly 22–23% (approx.) — a controlling founder stake but well short of a majority, so the register is unusually widely held for a founder-run Indian company. Public shareholders, domestic funds and foreign institutions own the balance. Investors should track the promoter holding and any pledge directly in the shareholding filings.
  • The key subsidiary — Fleur Hotels: the group's owned-hotel vehicle, historically held ~58.9% by Lemon Tree and ~41.1% by APG (a Dutch pension-fund investor). Fleur holds many of the capital-heavy owned assets, including the Aurika Mumbai flagship. In a recent development, Warburg Pincus is buying out APG's stake in Fleur — a change of the outside owner of the owned portfolio, and a reshaping of how much of that bricks-and-mortar profit accrues to Lemon Tree's own shareholders (worth watching as it completes).
  • The estate: ~131 operating hotels, 11,811 rooms at FY26 close, spread across the brand ladder and across roughly 90+ Indian cities, with a handful of overseas properties. A signed pipeline of a similar room count again sits behind it — overwhelmingly managed and franchised.
Why the structure matters: Lemon Tree's economics are split across pockets. The heavy, debt-laden owned hotels largely sit in Fleur, part-owned by an outside investor; the light, fee-collecting management business sits in the listed parent. When you judge how much a boom year is worth to a Lemon Tree shareholder, you have to know how much of the owned-hotel profit is shared with Fleur's minority partner — and the APG-to-Warburg change is a live moving part in exactly that calculation.
04

The asset-light pivot: the same brand, two different returns

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — and it is specific to this company.

Here is the crux, and it is worth reading twice. A Lemon Tree Premier in Pune and a Lemon Tree Premier in Coimbatore can look identical to a guest — same sign, same rooms, same breakfast. But they can earn Lemon Tree Hotels wildly different amounts, because the number that matters is not the room; it is who owns the building. That is the entire pivot the company is executing, and no rival's story turns on quite the same hinge.

Play it out with the two models side by side. In the owned model, Lemon Tree buys land, borrows to build, and then keeps essentially all of the room revenue — a big rupee number, but earned on a big pile of capital and shadowed by interest payments. In the managed model, someone else — a wealthy individual, a developer, a family office — puts up the capital to build the hotel; Lemon Tree simply operates it under its brand and takes a management fee, typically a small percentage of the hotel's revenue plus a slice of its profit. The fee is a fraction of what the owned hotel earns in absolute rupees. But it required Lemon Tree to invest almost nothing, so the return on Lemon Tree's own capital is enormous, and each extra managed hotel adds fee income at close to 100% incremental margin.

The device that only fits Lemon Tree: the same flag, priced two ways

Picture two Lemon Tree Premiers that are physically identical. On the owned one, the company might collect the hotel's entire operating profit — say a large seven-figure rupee sum a year — but it sank tens of crores of its own and the bank's money into the walls to get it, and a bad year still owes the bank. On the managed one next door, Lemon Tree collects only a management fee — a small slice of revenue — a far smaller rupee figure. Yet it spent essentially nothing to earn that fee, so the return on its capital is near-infinite, the income is steady, and a downturn cannot bankrupt it because there is no loan against the building. Same brand. Same rooms. Two completely different businesses hiding behind one sign. The whole Lemon Tree thesis is the mix shifting from the first hotel to the second — which is why the pipeline being ~all managed is the tell that matters.

In rupee terms the fee business is still small — ~₹170 Cr against ₹1,453 Cr of group revenue. But it grows without swallowing capital, it does not add debt, and it is far steadier through a downturn than owned-hotel profit. The signed pipeline — roughly as many rooms again as the company already operates, almost all of it managed and franchised — is the shape of things to come. Lemon Tree is not abandoning owned hotels; it is letting them dilute in the mix while the fee layer compounds on top.

A genuine surprise: a chain famous for owning its hotels now signs almost nothing but managed and franchised rooms — the pipeline is the near-inverse of the operating estate. The company you can see today (mostly owned, debt-carrying) is not the company being built in the pipeline (mostly fee-based, capital-free). You are watching two different Lemon Trees overlap in real time.
05

Fleur Hotels, APG, and the long deleveraging

Where the owned bricks live — and why cutting their debt is half the story.

If the fee business is the future, the owned hotels are the present — and they come with a balance sheet. Most of Lemon Tree's capital-heavy assets sit inside Fleur Hotels, a subsidiary set up specifically to hold owned properties and funded partly by outside patient capital. Understanding Fleur, and the debt it carries, explains why deleveraging is not a footnote here but a central plot line.

Fleur was built to solve a real problem: owned hotels devour cash up front and only pay it back over decades, which is more than the parent's balance sheet could comfortably fund alone. So Lemon Tree brought in APG, a large Dutch pension-fund investor, to co-own Fleur — Lemon Tree holding roughly 58.9%, APG about 41.1%. APG's money helped fund the owned estate, including the flagship Aurika Mumbai. The trade-off is that a chunk of the owned-hotel profit belongs to APG (now, as its stake is bought out, to Warburg Pincus), not to Lemon Tree's own public shareholders.

Borrowings fell ~₹200 Cr year-on-year to ~₹1,500 Cr while the cost of that debt dropped to 7.42% — a double win, less debt at a lower rate. Management has signalled it intends to keep pushing borrowings down as owned-hotel cash flow and fee income build.

Two forces are cutting the debt. First, the owned hotels are throwing off strong cash in this up-cycle — high occupancy and record ARR on a fixed cost base means fat operating cash flow, and much of it goes to repaying loans rather than building new owned hotels. Second, the growth capital is going into fee-based rooms that need no borrowing at all. So debt falls from both ends: existing loans get repaid, and new growth doesn't add new loans. Cash profit of ~₹443 Cr in FY26 (well above the ~₹288 Cr reported profit) is the fuel doing this work.

Why deleveraging changes the risk, not just the returns

An owned-hotel company with heavy debt is a leveraged bet on the travel cycle — magnificent in a boom, dangerous in a bust, because interest is owed whether or not the rooms fill. Every crore of debt Lemon Tree repays does two things: it lowers the interest bill (lifting profit directly), and it shrinks the size of the hole a future downturn could dig. A less-indebted Lemon Tree keeps more of each boom-year rupee and survives a lean year more easily. Pair that falling debt with a growing fee business that carries no debt of its own, and the company's whole risk profile drifts from "leveraged cyclical" toward "capital-light brand with a shrinking loan book." That drift, more than any single quarter's rate, is what a patient owner is really buying.

06

Aurika and the reach up-market

Why a mid-market chain built one of India's largest hotels — at the top of its range.

For most of its life Lemon Tree was defined by the middle — dependable rooms at an expensable price. Aurika is the deliberate exception: a genuine upscale-to-luxury brand, positioned above even Lemon Tree Premier, and headlined by a hotel that is anything but mid-market — the 669-room Aurika, Mumbai International Airport, among the largest hotels in India by room count.

The strategic logic is threefold. First, rate: an upscale hotel commands a far higher ARR than a mid-market one, and on the same fixed cost base that higher rate drops disproportionately into profit. Second, the guest: Aurika lets Lemon Tree capture the traveller who has out-grown its mid-market rooms without losing them to a luxury rival — trading a guest up the ladder rather than out of the group. Third, signalling: a chain that can build and run a 669-room airport flagship has proven it can operate at a scale and standard that makes owners of would-be luxury hotels comfortable handing it a management contract. Aurika is a brand and a business card at once.

The airport bet, decoded

An airport hotel is a specific kind of machine. It runs on a torrent of transit demand — crew layovers, connecting passengers, delayed flights, early-morning departures — that fills rooms at odd hours a city hotel cannot. Put a very large hotel next to a very large airport and you can run high occupancy at a strong rate almost regardless of the city's leisure season. For a company whose whole edge is squeezing profit from occupancy on a fixed cost base, a 669-room airport flagship is that edge dialled to its maximum — a lot of rooms, a reliable stream of guests to fill them, and an upscale rate on every one. It is also the single biggest concentration of capital in the owned estate, which is why it sits inside Fleur and why its performance moves the group's numbers.

The nuance to hold: Aurika is a bet that a mid-market operator can be trusted up-market. That is not automatic — luxury guests are unforgiving, and a brand built on functional-and-affordable has to earn permission to charge upscale rates. If Aurika delivers, it re-rates the whole group's average room rate and opens a richer band of management contracts. If it disappoints, it is a large, capital-heavy hotel at the top of a cycle. The airport flagship's occupancy and rate are the early scoreboard on whether the reach up-market is working.
07

The people it hires: an operating model, not a CSR line

The genuinely unusual thing about this company — hiding in plain sight in its workforce.

Here is the fact about Lemon Tree that surprises almost everyone who hears it, and it is not on any valuation screen. Roughly one in five of its employees comes from what the company calls "Opportunity Deprived Indians" — a group that centres on people with disabilities (including those who are speech- and hearing-impaired, visually impaired, autistic, or orthopaedically disabled) alongside the economically and socially marginalised. This is not a charity programme run on the side. It is woven into how the hotels actually operate.

Walk into many Lemon Tree hotels and some of the staff cleaning rooms, working the kitchens, or handling back-of-house tasks are deaf or speech-impaired, working with adapted signage, visual systems and colleague sign-language — integrated into the same service standards as everyone else. The company recruits deliberately from these groups, trains for the roles, and builds the workplace around making the hiring succeed rather than treating it as an exception.

Why this is a business decision, not just a good one

It is tempting to file inclusion under "nice to have." At Lemon Tree it doubles as operating advantage. Hospitality is plagued by high staff turnover — people leave for a marginally better wage down the road, and constant re-hiring and re-training is a real cost that quietly erodes service quality. Employees hired from opportunity-deprived and disabled backgrounds, given a stable, dignified role that few other employers offer, tend to stay — lowering attrition, deepening experience, and steadying service in a business where consistency is the product. A more loyal, lower-churn workforce is a genuine edge in an industry that runs on it. That the same policy also changes lives is the point; that it is defensible on a spreadsheet is what makes it durable.

For an investor, the takeaway is narrow but real: this is a structural quirk of how Lemon Tree runs, one a rival cannot copy overnight because it took two decades to build the recruitment pipelines, training systems and workplace design that make it work. It will not show up as a line in the results. It shows up as slightly stickier staff, slightly steadier service, and a brand identity — "we hire differently" — that mid-market travellers and corporate booking desks increasingly notice.

08

India's hotel up-cycle: demand ahead of supply

The structural tailwind — and why it is a supply story as much as a demand one.

Lemon Tree's record ARR and occupancy are not just good management; they ride a genuine up-cycle in Indian hotels. And the most important thing about this cycle is that it is being driven from both sides — surging demand meeting a supply base that cannot expand fast enough. When rooms are scarce and travellers plentiful, rates rise, and rate rises land almost entirely in profit.

What's powering the cycle
DriverWhat it isWhy it helps Lemon Tree
Domestic travel boomA large, richer middle class travelling more — leisure, weddings, pilgrimage, and a return of business travel — with spending increasingly on experiences.Fills mid-market rooms, the exact band Lemon Tree leads. More travellers, more nights, higher occupancy.
Tight new supplyBuilding a hotel takes years; land, approvals and capital are slow. Branded mid-market supply has lagged demand badly.Scarcity is pricing power — with few new rooms opening, existing hotels raise ARR into a demand wall. This is the crux of the up-cycle.
FormalisationTravellers shifting from unbranded lodgings to trusted, app-bookable branded hotels.A structural share gain for branded chains like Lemon Tree at the expense of the unorganised market.

The insight that separates this cycle from a normal recovery

The powerful part is not simply that Indians are travelling more — it is that supply cannot respond quickly. A factory can add a shift; a hotel cannot conjure rooms. It takes three-to-five years to find land, get approvals, raise capital and build a hotel, so when demand jumps, the room count stays roughly fixed for years — and the only release valve is price. That is why occupancy above ~70% turns into rising ARR, and rising ARR on a fixed cost base turns into surging margins. The scarcity is the engine. The risk buried in the same sentence: high rates eventually do pull new supply into the market, and the mid-market band is the easiest to build — so the very success of the up-cycle plants the seed of the next over-supply.

For now, the balance sits firmly in the operators' favour, which is why Lemon Tree could print its best-ever ARR and occupancy in the same year. The question an investor has to keep live is not whether the cycle is real — it plainly is — but how many years of runway remain before new mid-market rooms start competing the rate back down.

09

The industry and the rivals

A crowded field — but few doing the same job at the same price point at the same scale.

Lemon Tree competes with almost everyone in Indian hospitality on some site or other, but its true peer set is narrow: national chains fighting for the branded mid-market. Above it sit the luxury names; below and beside it sit the budget aggregators; the mid-market itself is where the real contest is.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two contests run in parallel. The first is for guests — and here Lemon Tree's national mid-market network and brand ladder are a real moat that a fragmented field of smaller chains cannot match, with IHCL's Ginger the one genuinely comparable challenger. The second, and increasingly the more important, is for management contracts — the right to run someone else's hotel under your flag. That fight pits Lemon Tree not just against Indian peers but against Marriott, Accor and IHG, whose global loyalty programmes and reservation systems are powerful lures for an owner deciding whose brand to put on the door. Lemon Tree's counter is local depth, a mid-market specialism the globals treat as a sideline, and a willingness to sign the mid-sized Indian hotel a global chain might overlook. Winning asset-light rooms is where the next decade is decided.

Financials & valuation

A record year on a fixed cost base — priced for the cycle to hold and the fee book to grow.

FY26 was, on nearly every operating line, the best year in Lemon Tree's history: record ARR and occupancy, double-digit revenue growth, ~48% EBITDA margins, rising profit, and falling debt. The market has re-rated the stock to reflect much of that. The debate is whether the cycle holds long enough — and the fee business grows fast enough — to justify the multiple.

The valuation is where views split. At roughly ₹8,500 Cr of market value and ~14–17× EV/EBITDA, Lemon Tree is not priced as a cheap, forgotten cyclical; it is priced as a company delivering record numbers with a credible asset-light growth path. Bears note the obvious: those record ARR and occupancy figures are, almost by definition, near a cyclical peak, and paying a full multiple on peak-cycle earnings for a leveraged owner of hotels is how investors get hurt when the cycle turns. Bulls counter that the fee business — capital-free, steadier, compounding at ~14% — deserves a higher multiple than owned hotels, and that a shrinking debt load lowers the risk in every future year.

The one number that settles the thesis: fee income as a share of EBITDA

Watch three things, in order. RevPAR growth is the pulse — it tells you whether the cycle is still warm, and it is the first thing to soften when demand rolls over. Net debt is the gate — every crore repaid lowers the risk that a downturn does real damage. But the metric that actually settles the asset-light thesis is the share of EBITDA coming from management fees rather than owned rooms. If fee income keeps growing ~14%+ while the owned estate's contribution flattens, the mix is genuinely shifting — Lemon Tree is becoming the lighter, higher-return, less-cyclical brand manager the valuation is paying for, and it earns the multiple. If fee income stalls and the story is really just a leveraged owner riding a good ARR year, then the same multiple is being paid on peak-cycle owned-hotel profit, and the re-rating is borrowed against a cycle that will eventually turn. Fee-mix is the scoreboard; RevPAR and net debt are the read-outs on the way there.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A record cycle meeting a deliberate model shift — with the question of what's cyclical and what's structural.

Lemon Tree enters FY27 at the strongest operating point in its history — record rate, record occupancy, falling debt, and a pipeline that is almost all capital-free fee rooms. The debate is not whether the business works; it plainly does. It is how much of today's profit is a durable, structurally-lighter company and how much is simply a very good year in a cyclical industry.

The bull case

  • Structural demand-supply gap — Indian mid-market rooms are scarce and travel demand is surging; slow new supply hands existing hotels pricing power for years.
  • The asset-light flywheel — a ~14%-growing fee book that needs no capital and no debt, on a pipeline dominated by managed and franchised rooms.
  • Deleveraging — borrowings down to ~₹1,500 Cr at a lower 7.42% cost, lowering risk and lifting profit each year.
  • Mix going up-market — Aurika lifts blended ARR and opens richer management contracts a mid-market chain couldn't previously chase.
  • A moat rivals can't quickly copy — national mid-market network, brand ladder, and a two-decade-built inclusive-hiring model that steadies service and staff.

The bear case

  • Cyclicality near a peak — record ARR and occupancy are, by definition, close to a cycle top; RevPAR can fall hard in a travel or economic shock, and fixed costs make the reverse leverage brutal.
  • Owned-hotel leverage — ~₹1,500 Cr of debt still amplifies the cycle; the deleveraging is real but incomplete.
  • Supply risk — high rates draw new mid-market rooms, the easiest segment to build; today's scarcity seeds tomorrow's competition on ARR.
  • Execution up-market & on the pipeline — Aurika must prove a mid-market operator can hold upscale rates; and a signed managed hotel is not an operating one until it opens.
  • Valuation & competition — a full multiple on peak-cycle earnings, against IHCL's Ginger, Marriott/Accor/IHG for contracts, and OYO/budget below; plus owned profit shared with Fleur's minority partner.
The bottom line

Lemon Tree is two companies wearing one uniform: a debt-carrying owner of concrete-and-steel hotels enjoying a spectacular cyclical year, and a capital-free brand manager quietly compounding fee income underneath it. The bricks are what you see in the revenue and the debt; the fees are what the pipeline is being built from. In an up-cycle, both look wonderful — record rooms revenue and a growing fee book. The test comes when the cycle cools, because that is when the two halves diverge: owned-hotel profit will fall with RevPAR, but management fees should hold, and the debt will be lower to weather it. So the milestone to mark is not next quarter's ARR — it is the first year fee income climbs as a share of EBITDA while owned-room profit softens. That is the year Lemon Tree proves it has genuinely changed shape rather than just caught a good wind. Until then, watch the fee-mix line every quarter; it is the difference between a re-rated cyclical and a company that earned its new multiple.
