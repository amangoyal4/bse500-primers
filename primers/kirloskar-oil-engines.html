00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Kirloskar Oil Engines Limited (KOEL) is India's leading maker of diesel engines and generating sets ("gensets") — the machines that make electricity when the grid fails. It is the manufacturing flagship of the century-old Kirloskar Group, headquartered in Pune, and it sells into four end-markets: backup power (gensets), industrial engines, agriculture, and an aftermarket spares-and-service business — plus it owns a growing non-bank lender, Arka Fincap.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A backup-power champion riding a regulatory upgrade. Consolidated revenue reached ~₹7,701 Cr in FY26 (up ~22%), with a standalone (engine-business) EBITDA margin of ~13.1% and consolidated profit of ~₹558 Cr. The single biggest driver is Power Generation, where the shift to stricter CPCB-IV+ emission norms forced customers to buy cleaner, costlier, higher-value gensets — lifting both volumes and price per unit.
  • The moat is the aftermarket, not just the engine. Like a printer-and-ink business, KOEL sells the engine once but earns for 15–20 years on spares, service and annual maintenance. Its distribution / aftermarket arm is a high-margin annuity that smooths out the cyclicality of new-equipment sales — and it is the reason a genset customer stays a KOEL customer for decades.
  • An engine-maker that also runs a lender. KOEL owns Arka Fincap, an RBI-registered NBFC with an ~₹7,947 Cr loan book. It is a genuine second value-driver — but also the sharpest debate around the stock: why does a diesel-engine company run a financial-services business, and how should you value the two together? The market currently pays a rich ~55× earnings for the combination.
In plain terms

Think of KOEL as the company that keeps the lights on when the power cuts out — in hospitals, data centres, telecom towers, factories, malls and farms. Every one of those customers needs a reliable machine that starts instantly and runs for years, plus someone to service it forever. KOEL makes the machine, sells it through a dense dealer network, and then earns a steady stream from keeping it running. On top of that engine business it has bolted a lending arm (Arka) — a separate bet on India's credit growth. The recent excitement is a rule change: India tightened genset pollution limits (CPCB-IV+), which junked cheap old designs, pushed prices up, and handed share to the few players — KOEL among them — with the engineering to comply.

01

Engines & gensets 101: how the business earns

No mechanical degree required — just how a "sell once, service forever" machine business works.

At its core, KOEL is an engineering business that turns fuel into rotation, and rotation into electricity or work. To understand the investment case you only need to grasp three things: what a genset is, why the aftermarket matters more than the sale, and why "content per unit" (the price of each machine) is as important as how many it sells.

What is a genset, and where does the money come from?

  • The engine — a diesel (or gas) engine is the heart: it burns fuel to spin a shaft. KOEL designs and manufactures these in-house, from tiny farm engines to high-horsepower industrial units.
  • The genset — bolt an alternator onto that spinning shaft, wrap it in a control panel and an acoustic enclosure, and you have a "generating set" — a self-contained power station that switches on within seconds of a grid failure. This is KOEL's biggest product line.
  • The aftermarket — every engine needs filters, spare parts, lubricants, overhauls and annual maintenance contracts for its whole 15–20-year life. This recurring, high-margin revenue is the real prize — it dwarfs the one-time sale over the asset's lifetime.
The "razor-and-blades" mechanism

A genset is sold once, at a modest margin, in a competitive tender. But it then consumes spares and service for two decades — and the customer almost always buys those parts from the original maker, because a genset that fails during a power cut in an operating theatre or a data hall is a catastrophe. So the manufacturer with the largest installed base and the densest service network earns a long, sticky, high-margin annuity long after the sale. KOEL's ~decades-old installed base across India is exactly that annuity engine — and it's why market share today compounds into aftermarket profit for years.

Why "content per unit" is the second lever

KOEL grows in three ways at once, and they stack: (1) sell more units (volume, driven by power-backup demand); (2) sell higher-value units (a bigger, cleaner, more feature-rich genset carries a much higher price and margin than a basic one); and (3) earn more aftermarket as the installed base grows. The CPCB-IV+ upgrade (Section 05) is powerful precisely because it pulls all three levers together — it forces old machines to be replaced, raises the price of each new one, and expands the base of complex machines that need specialist servicing.

Why running the same engine into four markets matters

Designing and certifying an engine is expensive and slow. By amortising one engineering base across power, industry and agriculture, KOEL spreads its R&D, tooling and castings cost over far more units than a single-market rival — and each market cycles differently (backup power is infrastructure-led, agriculture is monsoon-led, exports are global-led), so the mix smooths the ride. The aftermarket sits on top of all of them: every engine sold in any segment becomes a future spares-and-service customer. That's the quiet compounding underneath the headline genset story.

03

The company and who controls it

A century-old industrial house, a listed flagship — and a famous family feud in the background.

KOEL is the engine arm of the Kirloskar Group, one of India's oldest industrial families, founded by Laxmanrao Kirloskar in 1888. The modern listed entity was carved out of the group's pump-and-engine business and now runs as an independent, professionally managed company chaired by the Atul Kirloskar branch of the family.

Ownership & the family dispute

  • Promoters (Kirloskar group): ~41.1%, with no shares pledged. Domestic institutions (DIIs) hold ~27%, FIIs ~11%, and the public ~21% — so the free float is large and institutionally owned.
  • Leadership: chaired by Atul Kirloskar (with Rahul Kirloskar and Nihal Kulkarni on the board), and run by a professional management team; Arka is led separately. Sanjeev Nimkar has served as Managing Director of the engine business.
  • The family feud — worth knowing, not panicking about: the 2009 settlement has been contested for years. Sanjay Kirloskar (Kirloskar Brothers) alleged KOEL breached a non-compete clause via its acquisition of a pump-maker (La Gajjar Machineries), and the branches have since sparred over disclosures — including a 2025–26 fight with SEBI over Regulation 30A and the family-settlement deed, now before the courts. It is a governance/overhang risk to monitor, but it has not disrupted KOEL's day-to-day operations or its record financial run.
Why the dispute matters for a shareholder: family disputes at Indian promoter groups can create uncertainty — over strategy, related-party dealings, and disclosures — even when the operating business is thriving. KOEL's clean balance sheet and independent listing insulate it operationally, but the multi-year litigation is a reason the "Kirloskar" name carries a small governance discount in some investors' minds. Track it, but weigh it against a business that has kept compounding straight through the noise.

Power Generation — the biggest driver

Backup power is the growth engine — and data centres just changed the ceiling.

Power Generation is KOEL's largest and fastest-growing business. It makes and sells diesel (and increasingly gas) gensets that provide backup power when the grid fails — and in an economy with rising digital infrastructure, patchy grid reliability in places, and zero tolerance for downtime, demand for reliable backup keeps climbing. Powergen grew ~32% in FY26.

The data-centre inflection — breaking into a Cummins stronghold

Historically the very large, high-horsepower (HHP) genset segment — the kind data centres buy — was dominated by Cummins India, estimated to hold ~80% of that niche. KOEL has been climbing the horsepower ladder to compete, and in 2026 it landed a marquee proof-point: a 192 MW order from HyperNext (a digital-infrastructure provider) for 96 units of its 2,500 kVA OptiPrime Dual Core power systems — its formal entry into the data-centre power market. Analysts size the HHP power-generation opportunity at roughly ₹15,000 Cr of capex over FY26–30, a segment where even modest share gains move KOEL's needle materially.

Why moving "up the kVA curve" is such a big deal

A small genset is a commodity — many players make them and margins are thin. A high-horsepower genset for a data centre is the opposite: it demands serious engineering, emission compliance, reliability track record and after-sales muscle, so few suppliers qualify and pricing is far richer. By proving it can build and deliver 2,500 kVA-class machines at data-centre scale, KOEL isn't just adding revenue — it's entering a higher-margin, higher-barrier segment and challenging the incumbent's near-monopoly. The 192 MW order is important less for its size than as a reference win that opens the door to more.

05

The CPCB-IV+ upgrade cycle — the defining tailwind

A pollution rule became a multi-year growth engine. Here's the mechanism, in plain English.

The single most important recent force in KOEL's story is not a product or a customer — it's a regulation. India tightened its genset emission standard from the old CPCB-II to a far stricter CPCB-IV+ norm (for gensets from ~19 kW up to 800 kW). A tightening emission standard sounds like a cost — but for a capable manufacturer it is one of the most powerful tailwinds there is. Understanding why unlocks the whole bull case.

What CPCB-IV+ actually changed

  • It made old designs illegal. A CPCB-IV+ genset must slash NOₓ and particulate emissions versus CPCB-II — which requires a genuinely new engine plus after-treatment electronics (sensors, control units, emission-control hardware). You can't just tweak the old model.
  • It raised the price of every genset. The new technology adds an estimated 15–20% to factory cost (and price) — so the same unit of demand now generates materially more revenue and, if managed well, more absolute profit per machine.
  • It knocked out the small assemblers. Compliance needs embedded electronics, type-approval certification and R&D that small, unbranded genset assemblers simply can't fund — so the norm consolidates share toward the few engineering-led players (KOEL, Cummins, a handful of others).

The "pre-buy then replacement" wave

Emission-deadline transitions typically create a two-part demand wave. First a pre-buy: customers rush to buy under the old, cheaper norm before it lapses. Then, as the new norm bites, a long replacement cycle: the entire installed base of old CPCB-II gensets gradually gets swapped for compliant CPCB-IV+ machines — at higher prices. For a maker like KOEL, that's years of elevated demand at richer realisations. KOEL has rolled out a full CPCB-IV+ range (its OptiPrime family) across capacity bands, plus retrofit emission-control kits for existing CPCB-II gensets — capturing both the new-machine and the upgrade markets. Management noted CPCB-IV+ pricing has now stabilised and demand is broadening across kVA ranges — the sign of a cycle maturing from disruption into a durable, higher base.

The honest caveat: the sharpest part of a pre-buy/transition tailwind is, by nature, partly one-time — a step-change in the base rather than a permanent growth rate. The bull case rests on the tailwind converting into a structurally larger, higher-value, higher-share business (plus the underlying secular growth in backup-power demand), not on the transition spike repeating. Watch whether Powergen growth stays healthy after the initial replacement surge normalises.
06

Industrial & Agriculture

The two other engine markets — cyclical industry, and KOEL's rural heritage.

Beyond gensets, KOEL's engines power machines and farms. These businesses matter both as revenue in their own right and as diversification — they cycle on different drivers (capex and monsoon) than backup power, and they broaden the installed base that feeds the aftermarket.

Industrial — engines inside other people's equipment

KOEL supplies engines that sit inside other manufacturers' machines: construction and earth-moving equipment, mining machinery, air compressors, fire-fighting pumps, road-building equipment and material handling. It's a classic B2B "engine-under-the-hood" business — KOEL wins by offering reliable, emission-compliant, well-serviced power units to OEMs (original-equipment makers), and rides India's infrastructure and construction cycle. The Industrial segment grew ~24% in Q4 FY26, reflecting the capex upswing.

Agriculture — KOEL's oldest, most rural business

  • Farm engines & pumpsets — diesel engines that drive irrigation pumps, plus small engines for power tillers and farm equipment. This is KOEL's heritage market, deeply embedded in rural India through the Kirloskar brand.
  • Highly seasonal & monsoon-linked — agri demand swings with the rains, crop cycles, rural income and diesel economics; it is lower-margin and more cyclical than gensets, but it is a large, sticky base and a channel into deep rural markets.
  • An electrification headwind — over the long run, government solar-pump schemes and rural electrification chip away at diesel agri-pump demand — a structural reason KOEL leans into gensets, industry and the aftermarket for growth.
Exports — the fourth engine, quietly growing

KOEL also sells engines and gensets internationally (Middle East, Africa, South-East Asia and beyond), where diesel gensets remain essential infrastructure. International business grew strongly in FY26 (gross sales up ~37%). Exports matter because they widen the addressable market beyond India's own cycles and let KOEL keep its plants full — but they also expose it to currency swings, competition from global majors, and country-specific demand. It's an optionality layer on top of the domestic core rather than the main event.

07

Distribution & the aftermarket annuity

The quiet, high-margin business that makes the whole model sticky.

If Power Generation is the growth story, Distribution / B2C is the quality story. This is the spares, service, maintenance contracts and pumps ("Fluid Dynamics") business — the recurring revenue that a large installed base throws off year after year, at margins the one-time equipment sale can't match. It grew ~20% in Q4 FY26 and reached roughly ₹1,139 Cr in FY26.

Why the aftermarket is the strategic prize

  • Recurring & predictable — spares and service demand is tied to the running installed base, not to this year's new-equipment orders, so it's far steadier than genset sales and cushions the cyclicality of the B2B segments.
  • High-margin & captive — genuine spare parts and factory-trained service command premium pricing, and customers overwhelmingly buy from the original maker because reliability during an outage is non-negotiable.
  • A moat that compounds — every genset, industrial engine or farm engine KOEL sells today becomes an aftermarket customer for 15–20 years. The larger and more complex the base (CPCB-IV+ machines especially), the bigger and stickier the annuity.
  • The pumps / Fluid Dynamics layer — KOEL's B2C also includes water pumps and related products (built up partly via acquisition), a consumer-facing channel that broadens the brand's rural and retail reach and cross-sells with the engine base.
The flywheel in one sentence

More gensets sold today → a bigger installed base → more high-margin spares, service and AMC revenue for two decades → steadier profits and cash to fund the next round of growth. Market share won now is aftermarket profit banked for the future — which is exactly why the CPCB-IV+ share gains matter beyond this year's revenue line.

08

Arka Fincap — why an engine-maker runs a lender

The stock's sharpest debate — a genuine second value driver, or a capital-allocation distraction?

Here is the part of KOEL that surprises first-time readers: it owns Arka Fincap, a wholly-owned, RBI-registered non-banking financial company (NBFC) — a lender. Set up in 2018, Arka has grown into a real business with an ~₹7,947 Cr loan book (AUM) at March 2026 (up from ~₹7,256 Cr a year earlier) and FY26 income of roughly ₹877 Cr. It is both a legitimate second engine of value and the single most-debated element of the KOEL story.

What Arka actually does

  • MSME & SME lending — loans to small and medium businesses; now the largest and increasingly retail-tilted part of the book (retail-oriented AUM has crossed ~64%).
  • Corporate lending — structured loans to companies.
  • Real-estate & urban-infrastructure finance — project and developer lending.
  • Capital-markets / syndication — arranging and distributing debt.

Arka carries strong credit ratings (CRISIL AA long-term / A1+ short-term) and has been de-levering — its debt-to-equity ratio improved from ~4.4 (FY25) to ~3.7 (FY26) as it scales more conservatively.

Why does an engine company run an NBFC? The honest answer

There are two ways to read it. The charitable case: the Kirloskar brand carries deep trust, KOEL generates cash, and deploying some of it to build a credit franchise creates a second, uncorrelated growth vertical with its own value — a "sum-of-the-parts" (SOTP) asset the market can eventually value separately (even list). The skeptical case: lending and engine-making are utterly different businesses; an NBFC consumes capital, adds balance-sheet leverage and risk, and can distract management from the core — investors who want NBFC exposure can buy a pure-play lender rather than get it bundled inside an engine stock. This tension is real, and reasonable people land on different sides.

The balance-sheet optical illusion to understand: KOEL's consolidated accounts show large borrowings (~₹5,374 Cr at FY26) and a lower consolidated ROCE (~15%) — which can look alarming for a "cash-rich engine company." Almost all of that debt is Arka's: an NBFC funds itself with borrowings to on-lend, so debt is its raw material, not distress. Strip Arka out and the core engine business is effectively net-cash / debt-light with a much higher return on capital. When you read KOEL's numbers, always separate the two: an industrial business and a finance business have completely different "healthy" balance sheets. This is also why a proper valuation values KOEL on a SOTP basis — engine business on an industrial multiple, Arka on a book-value/financials multiple.

The industry and the rivals

A consolidating genset market where a handful of engineering-led players take the spoils.

India's diesel-genset market is large and, thanks to CPCB-IV+, consolidating toward the organised, engineering-capable players. The top five — Cummins India, KOEL, Caterpillar (FG Wilson), Mahindra Powerol and Greaves Cotton — together hold an estimated 55–60% of organised-market revenue, and the emission-norm barrier is steadily squeezing out small assemblers.

The competitive tension to watch

Two dynamics define the next few years. First, consolidation is a tailwind for KOEL: CPCB-IV+ adds 15–20% to cost and requires electronics that small players can't fund, so organised leaders keep taking share. Second, the battleground is moving up the horsepower curve: the richest growth is in large gensets for data centres and infrastructure, a segment Cummins has dominated. KOEL's strategic bet is to climb into that high-value niche (the 192 MW HyperNext win is the flag in the ground) while defending its strongholds in medium gensets, agriculture and the aftermarket. Whether it can meaningfully dent Cummins' HHP lead — profitably — is the key competitive question.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A structurally advantaged industrial with real optionality — priced for continued execution.

KOEL is one of the cleaner plays on two durable Indian themes at once: the relentless need for reliable backup power and the formalisation of an industry via tighter standards. It also carries genuine optionality in alternate fuels and in Arka. The debate is about price, cyclicality and governance — not whether the business is good.

The optionality layer — beyond diesel

  • Alternate & cleaner fuels — KOEL is building gas / CNG, dual-fuel and even hydrogen and fuel-cell capability, hedging the very-long-term risk that diesel loses favour. Its gensets are increasingly marketed as fuel-agnostic and materially more efficient.
  • Data-centre & HHP scale-up — the 192 MW win is a template; each further hyperscale reference win compounds credibility in the richest genset niche.
  • Arka Fincap SOTP — a maturing NBFC that could, in time, be valued (or even monetised) separately.
  • Exports & B2C — international growth and the pumps/aftermarket franchise widen the base beyond domestic gensets.

The bull case

  • Regulatory tailwind — CPCB-IV+ drives replacement demand, higher price per genset, and share gains to capable players like KOEL.
  • Structural backup-power demand — data centres, hospitals, telecom, infra and industry all need reliable standby power; the grid isn't perfectly reliable.
  • Data-centre entry — the 192 MW HyperNext win cracks open a high-value, Cummins-dominated niche.
  • Aftermarket annuity — a large, growing installed base throws off sticky, high-margin service revenue for decades.
  • Margin & optionality — a genuine mix-led margin journey, plus Arka SOTP, exports, and alternate-fuel optionality; strong cash generation in the core.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~55× earnings leaves little margin for error; vulnerable to de-rating on any stumble.
  • Cycle normalisation — part of the CPCB-IV+/pre-buy tailwind is one-time; growth must prove durable after the replacement surge.
  • Cummins & competition — dislodging the incumbent in high-horsepower/data-centre gensets profitably is unproven at scale.
  • Capital-allocation & governance — the NBFC bundles finance risk into an engine stock; the long-running Kirloskar family/SEBI dispute is an overhang.
  • Cyclical & structural drags — industrial/agri demand cycles (capex, monsoon), diesel's long-term substitution risk, and commodity/currency swings.
The bottom line

KOEL is a high-quality, structurally advantaged industrial — the leading Indian engine-and-genset maker, riding a regulatory upgrade and a secular backup-power boom, with a sticky aftermarket, real alternate-fuel optionality and a maturing finance arm. The business risk is modest; the valuation and cyclicality risks are real. The story over the next few years will be told in a few numbers: Powergen growth after the CPCB-IV+ surge normalises (is it durable?), data-centre/HHP order wins (does it dent Cummins?), margin (does the mix keep richening?), and how the market values Arka. Get those right and the compounding justifies the premium; disappoint on them, and a 55× industrial has room to fall.
