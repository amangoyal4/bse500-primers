00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

National Fertilizers Limited (NFL) makes urea — the cheap, near-pure-nitrogen fertiliser that is by far India's most-used plant food — and sells it to farmers across the country under long-familiar brands like "Kisan Urea." It runs five gas-based urea plants: Nangal and Bathinda in Punjab, Panipat in Haryana, and two units at Vijaipur in Madhya Pradesh, with a combined revamped capacity of about 35.7 lakh tonnes a year. That makes it India's second-largest urea producer and the largest among the central public-sector fertiliser companies. It is a Navratna undertaking, and the Government of India owns 74.71% of it.

Three facts frame everything:

  • The farmer pays a tiny sliver of urea's real cost; the government pays the rest. Urea's shelf price is statutorily fixed by the government at roughly ₹242 for a 45 kg bag — around ₹5,400 a tonne — and has barely moved in years. The true cost of making or importing a tonne of urea is several times that. The gap is met by a government subsidy paid to NFL. So NFL's dominant "customer," by rupees collected, is not the farmer at all — it is the State.
  • Because the subsidy is cost-plus, the margin lever is efficiency, not price. Urea subsidy is worked out against fixed energy norms per plant. NFL is largely reimbursed for the gas it is "allowed" to burn per tonne under the norm; if a plant runs more efficiently than its norm it keeps the saving, and if it runs less efficiently it eats the excess. The gas price itself is mostly a pass-through to the subsidy — the real profit comes from beating the norm and from a fixed conversion return, which is why margins are structurally thin.
  • FY26 was a solid recovery year on a thin base. Standalone revenue was about ₹21,519 Cr and net profit roughly ₹170 Cr — more than double FY25's ~₹76 Cr — on an operating margin near 3.9%. Return on equity sits in the mid-single digits, and the stock trades on a low-teens earnings multiple. This is the profile of a regulated, working-capital-heavy PSU utility of a company, not a growth machine.
In plain terms

Picture a government-owned factory that turns natural gas into urea and then sells it at a price the government itself fixes far below cost — so the government hands the factory a cheque for the difference on every tonne. NFL does not set the shelf price, does not set the subsidy, and cannot even fully choose what it pays for gas. What it can do is run its plants leanly enough to burn less gas than the official norm allows, keep them running near full capacity, and collect its money from Delhi on time. That is the whole art of the business: a low-margin, high-volume conversion job wrapped inside a subsidy the company doesn't control, with the State as owner, price-setter and largest paymaster all at once.

01

Urea 101: what NFL actually makes

No chemistry degree needed — just the nutrient, the feedstock, and why urea is a special case.

Crops need three main nutrients: Nitrogen (N) for leafy growth, Phosphorus (P) for roots and grain, and Potassium (K) for overall health. NFL lives almost entirely on the N side — it makes urea, which is about 46% nitrogen and is the cheapest, most concentrated way to put nitrogen into Indian soil. To follow the company you need only a handful of terms.

The only fertiliser terms you need here
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for NFL)
UreaThe workhorse nitrogen fertiliser (~46% N) — India's single most-consumed fertiliser by a wide margin. It sits under a separate, tighter price-and-subsidy regime from all other fertilisers. This is essentially NFL's whole business.
Natural gasThe feedstock and the fuel for making urea. Gas is turned into ammonia, which is then combined with CO₂ to make urea. Gas is the dominant cash cost of a urea plant — routinely 70–80% of it — so a urea maker is, at heart, a gas-conversion business.
Neem-coated ureaSince 2015 the government has required all urea sold in India to be coated with neem oil. It slows nitrogen release (a little better for the crop) and, crucially, makes the urea unpalatable for diversion to industry — plugging a subsidy leak. All of NFL's urea is neem-coated.
Fixed MRPUnlike almost any other product, urea's Maximum Retail Price is set by statute — about ₹242 per 45 kg bag — not by the maker or the market. The farmer pays this and only this; everything above it is the government's problem.
SubsidyThe government payment that bridges urea's real cost and its fixed MRP. For NFL it is the largest single line of cash coming in — larger than everything farmers pay combined.
Why urea is regulated far more tightly than any other fertiliser

Phosphatic and potassic fertilisers (DAP, NPK, MOP) sit under a lighter-touch scheme called Nutrient-Based Subsidy (NBS), where the maker can move the shelf price and the government pays a fixed per-nutrient top-up. Urea is different: its price is frozen by law and the government instead reimburses the maker's cost under a detailed formula. The reason is political weight — urea is the fertiliser the largest number of farmers buy in the largest quantity, so no government wants its price to move. The consequence for NFL is profound: it has almost no pricing power at the shop, and its economics are dictated by a government cost-plus formula rather than by a market. Understanding that formula (next section) is understanding the company.

Who buys, and when

  • The farmer — the end user, who buys a neem-coated urea bag at the fixed MRP from a local dealer. Demand is seasonal, tracking the two big cropping seasons: kharif (monsoon-sown, roughly June–October) and rabi (winter, roughly October–March).
  • The dealer channel — a vast retail network, with sales tracked bag-by-bag on the government's Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) system, which only releases subsidy once a sale to a farmer is actually recorded at the point of sale.
  • The government — not the buyer of the bag, but the payer of most of its value, and therefore NFL's largest source of cash. It is also NFL's controlling owner. That double role is the defining oddity of the company.
02

The urea subsidy contract: how the price is really set

The single most important structural fact about this company — understand it and the rest follows.

A bar of soap has one price, set by its maker. A bag of urea has a price set by Parliament, and the maker is paid the rest by the government under a formula. This is the defining feature of Indian urea, and every line of NFL's accounts flows from it. Two mechanisms matter: the fixed MRP the farmer pays, and the cost-plus subsidy the government pays on top, governed by energy norms and gas pooling.

Unlike a phosphatic maker, NFL's realisation is a cost-plus formula: the subsidy is calibrated to a recognised cost, so a gas spike is mostly passed through — it does not directly crush the margin the way it crushes a DAP maker's. The catch is in the word "recognised."

Here is the machinery, in plain steps. The government fixes urea's farm-gate MRP by law. It then calculates each plant's recognised cost of production using a fixed energy norm — an allowed quantity of energy (gas) per tonne of urea — plus recognised conversion costs and a fixed capital-related return. The subsidy paid to NFL is, broadly, that recognised cost minus the MRP the farmer already paid. Two policy tools sit underneath this: gas pooling, which blends domestic gas and imported LNG into a single uniform delivered price for all gas-based urea plants so that no plant is penalised for its location on the pipeline; and energy-efficiency norms (set under the New Urea Policy 2015 and successors), which reward plants that burn less gas than their norm and punish those that burn more.

The subtle point that separates NFL from a DAP maker

For a phosphatic-fertiliser company, a spike in global input prices ahead of a subsidy reset directly compresses the profit spread — the pain lands on the margin. For NFL, because urea subsidy is cost-plus against a recognised cost, a gas-price spike is largely passed through to a bigger government subsidy rather than to a smaller margin. That sounds comforting, and on the margin line it partly is. But the pain does not vanish — it moves. A gas spike inflates the subsidy the government owes NFL, which swells the subsidy receivable and forces NFL to borrow more to fund the gap while it waits to be paid (section 06). So NFL's exposure to gas prices shows up less in the margin and more in the balance sheet — a crucial distinction a first-timer will miss.

Why does the government run such an elaborate system? Because urea is politically untouchable and fiscally enormous — fertiliser subsidy is one of the largest lines in the Union budget. Freezing the farmer's price keeps rural India stable; reimbursing the maker's recognised cost keeps the plants running; and the energy norms are the lever the State uses to stop reimbursing inefficiency and to push makers to modernise. Every capex decision NFL makes — revamping Vijaipur, planning ammonia upgrades — is ultimately about staying on the right side of those norms.

03

What NFL makes, and where it makes it

Five plants, one dominant product, and a small but growing tail of everything else.

NFL is overwhelmingly a urea manufacturer-and-marketer, but it is not only urea. Around the core it sells bought-in fertilisers to keep dealers stocked, a rising line of industrial chemicals, and a scatter of agri-input products. The revenue is dominated by urea, but the interesting growth is in the tail.

The five plants

NFL's urea comes from five gas-based plants of very different vintages. The two Vijaipur units in Madhya Pradesh are the modern, efficient core; the older northern plants at Nangal, Bathinda and Panipat were originally built on fuel oil and later converted to natural gas, and they carry higher energy consumption — which, under the energy-norm regime, matters directly to profitability.

Why the tiny tail gets outsized management attention

Roughly 95% of NFL's revenue is urea and traded fertiliser — regulated, cost-plus, capped. The tail — industrial chemicals and agri-inputs — is small, but it is the part of the business where NFL sets its own prices and keeps its own margin without asking Delhi. Every rupee of industrial-products profit is a rupee that does not depend on the subsidy cheque arriving, the energy norm holding, or the gas pool behaving. That is why a company this dominated by urea keeps talking up nitric acid and bentonite sulphur: not because they will move the needle this year, but because they are the only levers on the whole dashboard that NFL turns entirely by itself.

04

The company, the group, and who controls it

A Navratna PSU three-quarters owned — and price-controlled, and paid — by its own regulator.

National Fertilizers is a central public-sector undertaking: promoted by the Government of India in 1974, listed but still 74.71% government-owned, and granted Navratna status (a designation giving larger PSUs more operational and investment autonomy). Understanding that the State is simultaneously NFL's owner, its price regulator and its largest paymaster explains almost everything about how the company behaves and how its stock is valued.

Timeline

  • 1974
    National Fertilizers Limited incorporated by the Government of India to build fuel-oil-based urea plants at Panipat (Haryana) and Bathinda (Punjab).
  • 1978
    The Nangal plant of Fertilizer Corporation of India is transferred to NFL, adding a third northern base.
  • 1980s–90s
    NFL builds the natural-gas-based Vijaipur complex in Madhya Pradesh (later expanded to two units), giving it modern, efficient capacity on the HBJ gas pipeline.
  • 2013–15
    Northern plants converted from expensive fuel oil to natural gas; the New Urea Policy 2015 and mandatory neem coating reshape the subsidy and anti-diversion regime.
  • FY23
    A global energy shock sends imported LNG and gas prices soaring; urea subsidy balloons and working-capital borrowings across the sector spike as makers wait to be reimbursed.
  • FY25–FY26
    Gas prices normalise, subsidy releases stay timely, industrial-products revenue jumps ~43%, and NFL's rating is upgraded to IND AA / Stable. FY26 net profit more than doubles to ~₹170 Cr.
  • FY27 onward
    A large multi-year capex programme is framed — green ammonia, a Ramagundam urea greenfield, ammonia revamps, a Vijaipur debottlenecking, nano-urea at Nangal, and a stake in the Namrup (Assam) urea JV.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter: the President of India (i.e. the Government of India), holding 74.71%. The remaining ~25% is public float. As a majority-government company, NFL's board, leadership and major decisions run through the Department of Fertilizers and the usual PSU governance machinery.
  • Navratna status: gives NFL more freedom to invest and form joint ventures without case-by-case clearance — relevant now that it is planning tens of thousands of crore of capex.
  • Associates & JVs: NFL holds ~26% in Ramagundam Fertilizers & Chemicals (RFCL) — a gas-based urea plant in Telangana it co-promotes with Engineers India and FCIL — which is equity-accounted, so RFCL's profits lift NFL's consolidated earnings above its standalone figure. NFL is also taking a minority stake in the Namrup (Assam) urea JV, a large brownfield revival.
The three-hats problem, stated plainly: the Government of India owns NFL, fixes the price of its only real product, writes the subsidy formula that determines most of its revenue, and is the counterparty that owes it money. For a minority shareholder this cuts both ways. The upside is safety — a sovereign owner-and-paymaster is not going to let the company fail, and subsidy is ultimately reliable. The downside is that NFL is run to serve national food-security and fiscal goals first and shareholder returns second, which is a large part of why its return on equity is low and its multiple is modest. You are buying a regulated utility whose regulator is also its owner.
05

The gas-cost pass-through and the energy-norm spread

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a cost-plus urea maker.

Most people instinctively track a fertiliser company by the price of fertiliser, or by the price of gas. For NFL, both are largely the wrong number. Because urea subsidy is cost-plus, NFL is broadly reimbursed for the gas it burns up to a fixed norm — the gas price mostly passes through. The profit that NFL actually keeps comes from two much smaller things: beating the energy norm (using less gas per tonne than the government allows), and a fixed conversion-and-capital return. That is why margins are thin by design, and why efficiency — not price — is the whole game.

The profit lives in the thin slice on the bottom: the recognised fixed return plus whatever the plant saves by beating its energy norm. Proportions are illustrative of the mechanism.

Now look at the lever NFL genuinely controls. Two plants of identical size can earn very different profits on the same urea, because the government pays each against its own energy norm and reimburses at a group rate. A plant that consumes, say, 5.5 gigacalories of energy per tonne against a norm of 6.0 keeps the value of that half-gigacalorie of saved gas. A plant that consumes 6.5 against a 6.0 norm has to absorb the extra half from its own margin. This is precisely why NFL's Vijaipur units (modern, efficient) are its profit engine and its older northern plants are the drag — and why every rupee of energy-saving capex is really a bet on widening this spread.

The device that only fits a cost-plus urea maker: fixed return + norm saving

Forget "the price of urea" and even "the price of gas." The number that runs NFL is (recognised fixed return) + (energy actually saved against the norm) − (energy burned above the norm), tonne by tonne, plant by plant. The gas price barely touches this — it is reimbursed both ways. What touches it is how efficiently NFL runs relative to a benchmark the government sets. In a good year, high plant utilisation and energy consumption comfortably under norm across the fleet produce a decent aggregate saving on top of the fixed return; in a bad year, a plant trip, a shutdown, or an ageing unit drifting above norm eats into it. This is a fundamentally different profit engine from a phosphatic maker's global-price spread — quieter, more regulated, and won or lost on operational discipline rather than on commodity timing.

06

The subsidy receivable: NFL's biggest debtor is the State

The working-capital reality that a first-timer never sees coming.

Here is the fact that surprises almost everyone reading a fertiliser balance sheet for the first time: NFL's largest source of cash is the Government of India, via the subsidy — and because the government pays that subsidy with a lag, one of NFL's largest short-term assets is money the government owes it. A big slice of the balance sheet is, in effect, an IOU from the State that also happens to own the company.

The mechanism is unavoidable. NFL sells a bag, collects the small fixed MRP from the farmer, and then waits to be reimbursed the much larger subsidy — a wait that depends on budget releases and DBT verification of actual retail sales. Meanwhile it has already paid for the gas and run the plant. To bridge the gap it borrows short-term. That is why a profitable urea company can carry sizeable working-capital borrowings — they exist mainly to fund the subsidy float, not to build anything. When gas prices rise, the subsidy owed per tonne rises too, so the receivable and the borrowing swell even if nothing else changes.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with

A urea maker can post better profits and simultaneously see its borrowings climb — and both can be genuine. The reason is that its biggest debtor is the government: growth, or a gas-price rise, means carrying a larger pile of subsidy receivables while paying cash-on-the-nail for gas. So the health of this business is never captured by profit alone. Every year you must ask a second question: how big is the subsidy receivable, and is the government paying on time? A budget crunch that delays subsidy releases can turn a decent operating year into a working-capital headache — interest costs rise, cash flow lags reported profit, and the balance sheet swells. The profit is real; so is the IOU behind it.

Why this is a rare kind of comfort: unlike a private company chasing scattered debtors, NFL's dominant receivable is owed by a sovereign — and by the very sovereign that owns it. The ultimate payer is as reliable as they come, which is a big part of why NFL earns strong credit ratings on modest profits. The risk is not that the money never arrives; it is that the timing and the pooled-gas assumptions behind it are set by fiscal politics, and they move NFL's cash and interest bill directly. Read the subsidy receivable and the interest line together; they tell the story the profit number hides.
07

Diversification and the capex pivot

The attempt to earn rupees the subsidy doesn't govern — and a very large building programme behind it.

A company whose core is a capped, cost-plus, single-product business has one obvious strategic problem: there is a ceiling on how good the core can ever be. NFL's answer is two-pronged — diversify into un-subsidised products it prices itself, and invest heavily in new and greener capacity to secure its place in India's long-term urea and ammonia map. Both are slow-burn, but both are where the equity story, such as it is, has to come from.

Diversification — small lines NFL fully controls

  • Industrial chemicals — nitric acid, ammonium nitrate, sodium nitrate/nitrite, sold to industry at market prices. ~₹673 Cr in FY25, +~43% YoY, and higher-margin than urea.
  • Agri-input specialities — bentonite sulphur, water-soluble fertilisers, bio-fertilisers, potash-derived-from-molasses, city compost and certified seeds. Small, but they widen the farmer relationship beyond a single bag.
  • Nano urea — NFL is setting up nano liquid-urea capacity (reportedly at Nangal, ~1.5 lakh bottles/day of 500 ml), a government-backed push to shrink conventional urea's subsidy and logistics footprint. Strategically important, commercially still nascent.

The capex pivot — a much bigger build than the company's size suggests

NFL has framed a large multi-year investment programme — reported in the order of ~₹15,000 Cr across roughly 2026–2031 — aimed squarely at the next decade of policy priorities:

Two things make this credible-but-cautionary. It is credible because the direction matches exactly what the government wants — reduce urea imports, green the ammonia feedstock, modernise ageing plants — and because assured-return urea investment policies (the successor to earlier new-investment schemes) are designed to make such capacity bankable. It is cautionary because ₹15,000 Cr is enormous relative to NFL's ~₹3,000-odd-crore market value and its modest profits, and PSU mega-projects have a long history of running late and over budget. The pending Namrup (Assam) JV stake and the RFCL Ramagundam experience are the templates — and the warnings.

Why NFL keeps investing in a capped business

You might ask: if urea returns are capped by a formula, why pour capital into more of it? Three reasons. First, new urea capacity built under assured-return investment policies earns a guaranteed post-tax return — modest but bankable, and it displaces imports the country wants gone. Second, efficiency and ammonia-revamp capex directly widens the energy-norm spread that is NFL's profit (section 05), so it pays for itself in the reimbursement math. Third, green ammonia and nano urea are option value on the next regime — if the subsidy framework tilts toward low-carbon production, the early movers keep their place in the plan. None of this promises a re-rating. It is the patient, policy-aligned capital allocation of a company whose job is to stay essential to national food security while quietly earning a little more per tonne.

08

The three forces — and the PSU realities

Subsidy policy, gas prices, and the monsoon: the trio that writes the year, plus the structural facts of a State-owned utility.

Strip NFL to essentials and its year is decided by three external forces, none of which it authors. Layered on top are the permanent realities of being a government-controlled, cost-plus utility. Take them straight — no spin — and the risk profile is clear.

The three swing factors
ForceWhat it isHow it hits NFL
1 · Subsidy policy & timingThe energy norms, pooled-gas assumptions and fixed returns the government sets, and how promptly it pays.Sets most of realisation and the size of the receivable. Tighter norms squeeze the efficiency spread; a payment delay swells working capital and interest cost.
2 · Natural gas priceThe pooled cost of domestic gas plus imported LNG that feeds every plant.Mostly a pass-through to the subsidy, so it hits the balance sheet (receivable, borrowings, interest) more than the margin — the opposite of a phosphatic maker.

The PSU realities, stated without gloss

  • Low return on equity. NFL's ROE sits in the mid-single digits (around 6%), and ROCE around 8%. That is structural, not a one-off: a cost-plus regime deliberately caps the return, and the fixed formula leaves little room to earn more per tonne. This is normal for a PSU urea maker and abnormal for almost anything else.
  • Lumpy, loss-prone quarters. Because subsidy recognition and plant shutdowns fall unevenly, individual quarters swing hard — NFL has reported outright losses in weak quarters (for example the June-2025 quarter) and healthy profits in strong ones. The full-year figure is the only fair read.
  • Working-capital-heavy, thin-margin. A large chunk of the balance sheet is subsidy float funded by borrowings; the operating margin is ~3–4%. Small changes in gas, norms or payment timing move the reported numbers a lot.
  • Government-first priorities. Decisions serve food security and fiscal policy first. That underwrites survival but caps ambition, and it means capital allocation (like the ₹15,000 Cr plan) answers to national goals as much as to shareholder returns.
The uncomfortable summary a first-timer should internalise

NFL's job is to run five ageing-to-modern plants at high utilisation, beat its energy norms, sell every tonne it can at a price it doesn't set, and collect a subsidy it doesn't decide from an owner that also regulates it. It can improve its odds — modernise the older plants, grow un-subsidised industrial products, invest in efficiency and green ammonia. What it cannot do is escape the ceiling: a cost-plus urea utility is built to be safe and essential, not to compound wealth quickly. FY26's recovery was real and welcome, but it happened inside those walls, not outside them. Price the company as what it is — a low-return, sovereign-backed, working-capital-heavy utility having a better stretch — and the numbers make sense.

09

The industry and the rivals

A regulated, subsidy-shaped market of PSUs, cooperatives and a few private players.

Indian urea is a large, tightly regulated market where the shelf price is common to all and the competition is really about cost position, plant efficiency and reliability — because no maker competes on the frozen MRP. NFL is one of the biggest producers, but the field is a mix of fellow PSUs, a giant cooperative, and private players who straddle urea and the freer NBS fertilisers.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, this is not a price war — the MRP is common and fixed, so makers compete on cost, energy efficiency, plant uptime and dealer reach. The lowest-energy, best-run plant earns the widest spread on the same bag, which is exactly why NFL's efficient Vijaipur units matter and its older northern plants are a relative handicap versus a modern private plant like Chambal's. Second, the most useful mirror for NFL is RCF — another listed PSU urea maker under the identical regime — while the NBS players (Coromandel and peers) are best read as a reminder of the pricing freedom urea makers give up in exchange for a guaranteed cost-plus return.

10

Financials & valuation

A recovering cyclical utility on a modest multiple — the question is durability and returns, not richness.

FY26 was a clear step up: standalone revenue ~₹21,519 Cr and net profit roughly ₹170 Cr, more than double FY25's ~₹76 Cr, with the operating margin rising to ~3.9% from ~3.1%. But context matters — these are small profits on a large, subsidy-inflated revenue, and returns remain low by design. NFL carries no heroic expectations in its price; it trades on a low-teens earnings multiple and near book value, exactly what the market assigns a cost-plus PSU utility. The debate is not whether the price bakes in optimism; it is whether the recovery and the capex can lift returns off a structurally low base.

The numbers that settle the thesis: efficiency spread, the receivable, and returns

At a low-teens multiple and near book, NFL is priced as the utility it is — the case does not need a re-rating, it needs returns to rise off the floor. So the metrics that prove or break it are: the full-year profit and operating margin (is the efficiency spread widening as plants modernise?), the subsidy receivable and interest bill (is the government paying on time, or is profit turning into an IOU and borrowings?), and eventually return on the new capex (do the ₹15,000 Cr of projects earn their assured returns without ballooning debt?). If FY26's recovery holds and the modernisation lifts ROE even modestly, a near-book PSU utility can re-rate gently. If gas spikes swell the receivable, or the mega-capex runs late and levers up the balance sheet, the low multiple is simply correct. 11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, recovering PSU utility with a big building programme — asked whether it can lift returns off the floor.

National Fertilizers enters FY27 in better shape than it has been in years: a doubled profit behind it, a June-quarter turnaround, timely subsidy releases, an upgraded credit rating, and a large capex map aimed exactly where policy is pointing. What no cost-plus urea maker can promise is the one thing that would change its character — a structurally higher return. That tension frames both cases.

The bull case

  • Cheap and recovering — low-teens earnings, near book value, for a scaled second-largest urea maker whose profit just more than doubled; little optimism is priced in.
  • Sovereign safety net — 74.7% government-owned, with the State as reliable ultimate paymaster; survival is not in question and credit ratings are strong (IND AA).
  • Efficiency & diversification self-help — modernising older plants widens the energy-norm spread, and growing un-subsidised industrial products adds margin NFL fully controls.
  • Policy-aligned capex optionality — a ~₹15,000 Cr plan in green ammonia, a Ramagundam urea greenfield and ammonia revamps, much of it under assured-return frameworks.
  • Non-discretionary demand — India must keep feeding itself; urea is the most essential fertiliser, and volumes are structurally stable.

The bear case

  • Structurally low returns — a cost-plus cap keeps ROE in the mid-single digits; without a policy change, the ceiling on profitability is real and permanent.
  • Subsidy-timing & gas risk on the balance sheet — a gas spike or delayed release swells the receivable and borrowings, lifting interest costs even in a "good" operating year.
  • Ageing northern plants — Nangal, Bathinda and Panipat are older and energy-hungry, a drag on the efficiency spread until modernised.
  • Mega-capex execution risk — ₹15,000 Cr is huge against a ~₹3,000-crore market value; PSU projects have a history of delays, cost overruns and balance-sheet strain.
  • Owner-and-regulator conflict — decisions serve fiscal and food-security goals first; minority shareholder returns are a secondary consideration, and quarters can swing to outright losses.
The bottom line

National Fertilizers is a well-run, State-owned urea utility having its best stretch in years, priced as if the market doubts that low returns can rise much from here. The self-help is genuine — modernise the plants to widen the energy-norm spread, grow the un-subsidised industrial and speciality lines, and invest along the grain of national policy — and FY26's doubled profit shows it can move the needle. But the forces that write each year, the subsidy formula, the gas price and the monsoon, sit outside the company, and its balance sheet quietly carries a large IOU from the State that grows when gas does. The next test is concrete and datable: whether full-year profit and operating margin keep climbing through FY27 as the older plants modernise and the receivable stays under control, and whether the first tranches of the mega-capex earn their returns without levering up. Deliver both and a near-book PSU utility can re-rate gently; miss on execution or get caught by a gas spike, and the modest multiple will read as exactly right.
