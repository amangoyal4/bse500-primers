00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

CRISIL does two very different things under one roof. First, it is India's original and largest credit-rating agency — the umpire that grades how likely a company is to repay its bonds and loans, and it rates roughly one in every two rated companies in India. Second — and this is now the bigger business — it runs a huge offshore research-and-analytics engine that does credit research, risk modelling, benchmarking and data-analytics work for its US parent S&P Global and for banks and financial institutions around the world. It is, in one company, a regulated Indian ratings oligopolist and a global "knowledge-process" exporter.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Controlled by S&P Global. The US ratings-and-data giant S&P Global owns ~66.6% of CRISIL (via S&P India LLC ~42.7% and S&P Global Asian Holdings ~15.8%). CRISIL is both a subsidiary of, and a major offshore supplier to, its parent — a relationship that is a strength and a concentration risk at the same time.
  • Research is now larger than Ratings. In CY2025 the Research, Analytics & Solutions segment earned ~₹2,572 Cr (~70% of income), while the higher-margin Ratings segment earned ~₹1,079 Cr (~30%). Ratings is the crown jewel on margin (~44%); Research is the growth and scale engine.
  • A capital-light quality compounder — at a premium price. CRISIL earns a ~24.5% return on equity and ~33% return on capital, needs almost no fixed assets, and pays out most of its profit as dividends (₹61/share in CY2025). Net profit rose ~12% to ₹766 Cr. The market rewards that quality with a rich ~35× earnings multiple — the crux of the investment debate.
In plain terms

Think of CRISIL as two businesses that both sell trust and analysis. The famous one — Ratings — is like a licensed examiner: companies that want to borrow money pay CRISIL to grade their creditworthiness, because lenders trust that grade. It's small in revenue but very profitable, and it's a protected club (only a handful of firms are allowed to do it). The bigger, quieter one — Research & Analytics — is like a giant back-office brain-for-hire: thousands of Indian analysts do research, build risk models and crunch data for S&P and for global banks who would rather rent that expertise from low-cost, high-skill India than build it in New York or London. CRISIL is a bet on both India's credit market growing and the world outsourcing its financial analysis to India.

01

What a credit rating actually is

No finance degree required — the one idea the whole ratings business rests on.

When a company (or a bank, or a government body) wants to borrow money by issuing a bond or taking a large loan, a lender's central question is simple: will I get my money back? A credit rating is a professional, standardised answer to exactly that question — a letter grade, from the safest AAA down through AA, A, BBB and into speculative territory (BB, B, C) and finally D (default) — that summarises how likely the borrower is to repay on time and in full.

The rating ladder, decoded
GradeWhat it signals
AAA / AAHighest safety. Very low chance of default. The borrower pays the lowest interest rate. (Written "CRISIL AAA".)
A / BBBAdequate safety, "investment grade". Still bankable, but the lender wants a bit more interest for the extra risk.
BB / B / C"Sub-investment / speculative grade" — rising risk of default; much higher interest demanded, if the borrower can raise money at all.
DIn default — payments have been missed.

Why does a grade from a private company matter so much? Because it lets the whole credit market function without every lender doing its own deep-dive on every borrower. Regulators lean on it too: banks, insurers, mutual funds and pension funds are required by rules to hold assets of certain minimum ratings, and to set aside more capital against lower-rated loans. A rating is therefore not just an opinion — it is a regulatory passport that decides who can buy your bond and at what price.

The crucial quirk: the issuer pays

Here is the feature that makes ratings such a good business — and such a sensitive one. The company being rated pays for its own rating ("issuer-pays"). That sounds like a conflict of interest, and it is one the industry manages carefully, because a rating is only worth anything if investors trust it to be independent. So the entire franchise rests on reputation: CRISIL's grades are valuable precisely because the market believes they are honest and rigorous. Build that trust over decades and you have a near-unassailable moat; lose it in a single scandal and the business can unravel. Every ratings agency lives and dies on this one intangible.

Why it's an annuity, not a one-off sale

A rating isn't sold once. Once CRISIL rates a bond or a bank loan, it must keep watching it ("surveillance") for the entire life of that debt — re-checking the borrower's health every year and changing the grade if things deteriorate or improve. So a single rated instrument produces an initial fee plus a recurring annual surveillance fee for years. Stack up tens of thousands of live ratings and you get a large, sticky, recurring revenue base that renews largely on its own — the closest thing in finance to a subscription.

Read this once and the whole company clicks into place.

CRISIL reports in two accounting segments — Ratings, and Research, Analytics & Solutions — but it is easiest to understand as three businesses with three different economic characters. One is a regulated Indian franchise; two are global analytics-for-hire operations.

The one-line version of each

  • 3 · CRISIL Intelligence (formerly Market Intelligence & Analytics / Research) — India-facing market research, industry reports, benchmarking, ESG scoring and consulting for corporates, banks and government.

Why group them like this

The accounting puts Ratings in one box and everything else in a second box called "Research, Analytics & Solutions". But economically the important split is regulated-domestic vs. global-analytics-export. Ratings is a protected, high-margin Indian licence tied to India's bond market. Everything else is a skill-arbitrage export business — selling the brains of Indian analysts to the global financial industry, growing with global banks' budgets and the worldwide trend of moving research and risk work offshore. Two engines, two entirely different sets of tailwinds and risks — which is exactly why CRISIL is more interesting than a "pure" rating agency like CARE or ICRA.

03

The company and who controls it

From India's first rating agency to an S&P Global subsidiary.

CRISIL — originally Credit Rating Information Services of India Limited — was set up in 1987 by ICICI and UTI (with SBI, LIC and others), becoming India's first credit-rating agency. Over nearly four decades it built the ratings franchise, added an equity-research and analytics arm, and gradually passed into the control of the US ratings-and-data major S&P Global, which took a majority stake in 2005 and steadily raised it.

Timeline

  • 1987–88
    Incorporated as India's first credit-rating agency, promoted by ICICI, UTI and others; assigns India's first corporate credit ratings.
  • 1993
    Lists on Indian stock exchanges (today BSE: 500092, NSE: CRISIL).
  • 2005
    US-based Standard & Poor's (S&P) acquires a majority stake; CRISIL also acquires Irevna, a global equity-research and analytics firm — the seed of today's offshore research engine.
  • 2013
    Acquires Coalition, the London-based investment-banking analytics/benchmarking specialist — deepening the global-analytics footprint.
  • Feb 2020
    Completes acquisition of Greenwich Associates (US financial-services benchmarking); merges it with Coalition to form Coalition Greenwich.
  • 2024–25
    Group-wide rebrand: businesses realigned as CRISIL Ratings, CRISIL Intelligence (formerly MI&A), CRISIL Coalition Greenwich, and CRISIL Integral IQ (formerly GR&RS) under one connected identity.
  • CY2025
    Total income ~₹3,756 Cr, PAT ₹766 Cr (+12%); Research now ~70% of income; S&P Global holding ~66.6%.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter — S&P Global (~66.6%): held through S&P India LLC (~42.7%) and S&P Global Asian Holdings Pte. Ltd. (~15.8%), plus a small additional stake. S&P Global is one of the world's "big three" credit-rating and financial-data companies — so CRISIL sits inside a global ratings-and-data empire.
  • The rest of the register: mutual funds (~7%), foreign institutional investors (~7%), insurers (~5%) and retail (~13%) hold the ~33% free float. The low free float and marquee parent are part of why the stock trades at a scarcity premium.
  • Legal structure: the regulated ratings business sits in a ring-fenced subsidiary, CRISIL Ratings Limited — SEBI rules require the rating activity to be separated from the commercial analytics businesses to protect rating independence.
  • Calendar-year reporting: unusually for an Indian company, CRISIL reports on a January–December financial year (following its US parent), not the April–March year most Indian firms use. "CY2025" here means the year ended 31 December 2025.
The S&P relationship, in one line: S&P Global is simultaneously CRISIL's controlling owner, its biggest single client (routing global ratings-support and research work through CRISIL's Indian centres), and its global brand parent. That triple role drives a lot of CRISIL's growth — and concentrates a lot of its risk in one counterparty.
04

Ratings — the annuity oligopoly

Small in revenue, mighty in margin — and protected by regulation and reputation.

The Ratings segment earned ~₹1,079 Cr in CY2025 (up 18.4%) — only ~30% of CRISIL's income, but the most profitable part of the company at a ~44% segment margin (segment profit ~₹478 Cr). It is a textbook high-quality franchise: reputation-based, regulated, recurring, and capital-light.

What makes ratings such a good business

  • A regulated oligopoly. Only firms licensed by SEBI can issue ratings, and CRISIL is the market leader with an estimated >60% share — it says it rates about one in every two rated companies in India and has rated 35,000+ entities. New entrants can get a licence, but not the decades of trust that make a rating bankable.
  • Recurring surveillance revenue. As covered in §01, each rating generates an upfront fee plus annual surveillance fees for the life of the debt — a large, self-renewing base rather than one-off sales.
  • Capital-light and high-margin. The "factory" is analysts and a methodology, not plant and machinery. So incremental ratings drop a lot of revenue to profit, and the business throws off cash.
  • Embedded in the plumbing. Because regulators require ratings for banks' capital rules, bond issuance and money-market instruments, demand is structural, not discretionary — you cannot issue a rated bond in India without a rating.
The catch: it's tied to the credit cycle

Ratings revenue rises and falls with how much borrowing is happening. In a boom, companies issue lots of bonds and take lots of bank loans — every new instrument is a new rating fee. In a downturn, or when interest rates spike and issuance dries up, new rating volumes shrink (though the recurring surveillance base cushions the fall). So Ratings is high-quality but cyclical: it's geared to India's bond-market and bank-credit growth. The good news for CRISIL is the structural story — India's corporate bond market is still small relative to the economy and is being deliberately deepened by regulators, which should mean years of rising issuance to rate.

There's a second, less obvious piece inside the Ratings segment: the Global Analytical Center (GAC). This is an offshore team in India that provides ratings-support analytics to S&P Global Ratings — data, research and surveillance support for the parent's worldwide rating operations. GAC's expansion was a specific growth driver in CY2025, and it blurs the line between the "domestic ratings" story and the "global offshoring" story: even inside Ratings, part of the growth is S&P routing global work to India.

05

Research & Analytics — the offshore engine

The biggest, least-understood part of CRISIL — and the real growth story.

If Ratings is the famous business, Research, Analytics & Solutions (RAS) is the large one: ~₹2,572 Cr in CY2025 (~70% of income, up 9.4%), at a ~22% segment margin. Its centrepiece is CRISIL Integral IQ (formerly "GR&RS", Global Research & Risk Solutions) — a global-delivery operation that does credit research, risk modelling and analytics for the world's banks and financial institutions from India. Think of it as an engineering-services / KPO business, but for finance.

What the offshore analysts actually do

  • Credit & equity research — writing the research reports, financial models and company analysis that global investment banks and asset managers used to produce in-house in London or New York.
  • Risk & regulatory analytics — building and validating the risk models banks must run for capital adequacy, stress testing, credit risk and regulatory compliance (Basel, IFRS 9, model validation).
  • Data & quantitative analytics — data engineering, valuations, portfolio analytics, and increasingly AI/machine-learning-driven tools (CRISIL has pushed "Integral IQ" branding around AI-enabled analytics).
  • Support for S&P Global — a meaningful share of RAS work serves the parent's own research and data operations.
Why this exists — the arbitrage in plain English

A global bank needs enormous amounts of research and risk analysis done. Doing it with expensive analysts in New York or London costs a fortune. India offers a deep pool of highly qualified, English-speaking finance graduates at a fraction of Western salaries. CRISIL's pitch: send us the work — we'll do it in India, to a high standard, far cheaper, at scale, around the clock. It's the same logic that built India's IT-services and engineering-R&D industries, applied to financial research and risk. That's why RAS is often described as a "finance ER&D / KPO" business. It grows with two things: the size of global banks' budgets, and the share of that work the world decides to move offshore.

Within RAS sits a jewel: Coalition Greenwich, built from the Coalition (2013) and Greenwich Associates (2020) acquisitions. It benchmarks the performance of investment banks (which bank is winning share in which trading or advisory business) and the experience of banks' clients — proprietary data that the banks themselves buy to see how they stack up against rivals. It rides the health of the global capital-markets and investment-banking cycle: strong when trading and deal-making budgets are high, softer when banks tighten belts.

The concentration to keep in view: a large chunk of RAS revenue depends on (a) S&P Global and (b) a relatively small set of global banks. That's efficient and sticky when times are good — but it means CRISIL's fastest-growing segment is exposed to global-bank spending and to the fortunes of one parent-client. When Wall Street cuts research and analytics budgets, CRISIL feels it.
06

CRISIL Intelligence — India's market brain

The part you have probably actually read — the reports and the numbers everyone quotes.

The third engine, CRISIL Intelligence (formerly Market Intelligence & Analytics, and before that CRISIL Research), is the India-facing knowledge business. When a newspaper cites a forecast for India's GDP, steel demand, bank credit growth or a sector outlook "according to CRISIL", this is usually the source. It sells research, data and advice about the Indian economy and its industries to corporates, banks, investors and government.

What it sells

  • Industry & economy research — subscription reports and forecasts on 80+ sectors, the macroeconomy, commodity prices and credit trends — a recurring, subscription-like revenue base.
  • Benchmarking & analytics — data products that let banks and companies compare themselves against peers and against sector norms.
  • Consulting & risk solutions — bespoke advisory: infrastructure and policy advisory (often for government and multilateral bodies), risk frameworks, and go-to-market analysis.
  • ESG & sustainability — ESG scores, assessments and climate-risk analytics — a fast-growing adjacency as Indian companies face sustainability disclosure norms.
Why it matters more than its size

CRISIL Intelligence is not the biggest revenue line, but it is strategically central: it is where CRISIL's brand as "India's authority on credit and the economy" is built and reinforced. That authority is what makes issuers want a CRISIL rating and makes clients trust CRISIL's global analytics. It's also a natural cross-sell hub — a company that buys CRISIL's industry research is a warm lead for its ratings, its benchmarking and its consulting. In other words, Intelligence is partly a business and partly the marketing-and-credibility flywheel for the whole group.

07

The two growth stories, stacked

Why CRISIL is a bet on two independent structural trends at once.

Most companies have one growth story. CRISIL has two, and they are largely uncorrelated — which is a big part of the appeal. One is domestic and one is global; one drives Ratings, the other drives Research.

Story 1 — India's credit market deepens

India finances itself far more through banks than through a bond market, unlike developed economies. Regulators (SEBI, RBI) have spent years trying to deepen the corporate bond market — pushing large borrowers to raise a share of funding via bonds, widening the investor base, and improving market infrastructure. Every rupee of that shift from bank loans to rated bonds creates rating fees. Add rising infrastructure financing, more first-time issuers, securitisation and municipal/InvIT/REIT issuance, and the structural direction of travel is more instruments to rate. This is the tailwind under the Ratings segment (and it's why Ratings grew ~18% in CY2025 even though it's the "mature" business).

Story 2 — the world offshores its financial analysis to India

Global banks and financial institutions are under permanent cost pressure and are steadily moving research, risk and analytics work to India — either into their own captives (GCCs) or to specialists like CRISIL. The addressable pool is enormous: research, model validation, regulatory reporting, ESG, and now AI-enabled analytics. CRISIL, with its S&P pedigree and decades of financial-domain expertise, is one of the premier destinations for that work. This is the tailwind under the Research/RAS segment — an "ER&D-for-finance" export story that grows with global-bank budgets and offshoring intensity.

Why two engines is the point

Because the India-credit story and the global-analytics story respond to different drivers — one to Indian interest rates and bond issuance, the other to global-bank budgets and offshoring — they tend not to slump at the same time. When Indian credit is soft, global analytics can carry the load, and vice-versa. That diversification is a genuine quality feature: it makes CRISIL's overall growth steadier than a pure-play rating agency or a pure-play KPO. The flip side is that neither engine is entirely in CRISIL's own hands — both depend on cycles it doesn't control.

How CRISIL makes money — the economics

Capital-light, cash-generative, and built to pay dividends.

CRISIL's financial character is that of a high-quality services compounder: it sells analysis and reputation, not physical products, so it needs very little capital to run and converts most of its profit into cash. The two segments have different margins — Ratings ~44%, Research ~22% — and blend to a group operating margin in the high-20s.

Why the numbers look this good

Three structural features drive the returns. (1) No factories. The main cost is people — employee expense is roughly half of total costs — and there is almost no capital tied up in plant, so returns on capital are naturally high. (2) Recurring revenue. Surveillance fees (Ratings) and research subscriptions (Intelligence) renew year after year, giving a stable base on top of which new work adds. (3) Little need to reinvest. Because growth doesn't require heavy capex, CRISIL can hand most of its profit back to shareholders — hence the large, steady dividends and the occasional buyback. The trade-off, which every people-business shares: the assets go home every night, so the model depends on attracting and retaining skilled analysts, and wage inflation directly compresses margins.

One nuance worth flagging: CRISIL is export-heavy. A large share of Research revenue is billed to overseas clients in dollars and pounds, while costs are largely in rupees. That makes CRISIL a net beneficiary of a weaker rupee — but also exposes it to currency swings and to the risk that a stronger rupee, or FX volatility, dents reported margins.

The industry and the rivals

Two competitive arenas — a cosy ratings club, and a crowded analytics market.

Because CRISIL straddles two businesses, it faces two very different competitive maps. In ratings, it is the leader of a small, regulated club dominated by the local arms of the global "big three". In analytics, it competes in a large, fragmented, and increasingly contested global market.

For an incumbent leader like CRISIL, heavier regulation is a mixed blessing that mostly protects the top of the market.

How to think about the ~35× multiple

At ~₹29,700 Cr market cap on ~₹766 Cr of CY2025 profit, CRISIL trades around 35× trailing earnings — a clear premium to the market and to peers like CARE and ICRA. That price is what the market pays for a capital-light, ~24% RoE, debt-free, dividend-rich franchise with two structural growth engines and a global parent. Bulls argue that quality this durable, with a regulated ratings moat plus a secular offshoring tailwind, deserves a scarcity premium — especially with a low free float. Bears argue the multiple leaves no room for error: single-digit growth in the big Research segment, any wobble in global-bank spending, an FX swing, or a ratings-quality scandal, and a 35× stock can de-rate sharply. Both can be right at different points in the cycle — which is why the growth rate of the Research segment and the health of the credit cycle are the numbers to watch each quarter.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely wonderful franchise — priced for it to keep performing.

CRISIL is one of the cleaner quality-compounding stories in Indian financials: a regulated ratings moat, a large and growing global-analytics business, a debt-free balance sheet, high returns, and generous dividends, all under the wing of S&P Global. As with most such names, the investment debate is about price, cyclicality and concentration, not business quality.

The bull case

  • Two structural tailwinds — India's deepening bond/credit market (Ratings) plus the world offshoring financial research and risk to India (Research), rarely slumping together.
  • Ratings moat — a regulated, reputation-based oligopoly with >60% share, recurring surveillance revenue and ~44% margins.
  • Capital-light quality — ~24% RoE, ~33% RoCE, debt-free, high cash conversion, a reliable ~55–60% dividend payout.
  • S&P parentage — global brand, methodology, and a steady pipeline of offshored analytics work from the parent.
  • Optionality — AI-enabled analytics (Integral IQ), ESG, and Coalition Greenwich data as higher-value adjacencies.

The bear case

  • Premium valuation — ~35× earnings leaves little margin for error; vulnerable to de-rating on any growth stumble.
  • Client & parent concentration — the big Research segment leans on S&P Global and a small set of global banks; their budget cuts hit hard.
  • Cyclicality — Ratings tracks the credit/issuance cycle; Coalition Greenwich tracks investment-bank spending — both can turn down.
  • FX & wage exposure — export-heavy revenue means currency risk; a people-business means wage inflation squeezes margins.
  • Reputational / regulatory risk — a ratings agency lives on trust; a missed default or SEBI action can damage the franchise and volumes.
  • Competition — ICRA (Moody's) and India Ratings (Fitch) in ratings; captives and KPOs (Evalueserve, Acuity, eClerx) in analytics.
The bottom line

CRISIL is a rare double: a protected, high-margin Indian ratings franchise and a scaled global financial-analytics exporter, run inside a world-class ratings-and-data group. The business quality is high and the twin-engine design makes growth unusually steady. The risks are equally clear: the fastest-growing engine is concentrated on one parent-client and on global-bank budgets it cannot control; the whole thing is cyclical; and it is priced at a premium that assumes the compounding continues. Watch two numbers each quarter — the Research segment's growth rate (is the offshoring engine re-accelerating?) and Ratings volumes (is India's credit cycle expanding?). Get those right and the ~35× multiple keeps compounding; disappoint on either and a richly-valued quality stock has room to fall.
