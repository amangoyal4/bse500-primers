00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

SAMHI Hotels Limited is a branded-hotel ownership and asset-management company — one of India's largest by number of rooms. It owns a portfolio of about 31 hotels and ~4,900 rooms concentrated in India's big office-and-business cities (Bengaluru, Hyderabad, the Delhi-NCR belt, Pune, Chennai), and it lets global brands run them under management and franchise contracts — Marriott, Hyatt and IHG names such as Courtyard, Fairfield, Sheraton, Hyatt Regency and Holiday Inn. SAMHI is the investor and landlord; the brand is the operator. Its distinctive strategy is acquisition-led: rather than build hotels from the ground up, it buys existing — often underperforming or financially stressed — hotels and built platforms, re-brands and renovates them, and lifts their margins.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It owns the asset but rents the flag. A generalist assumes a "hotel company" runs hotels. SAMHI, at heart, does not — it owns the real estate and the licence, and pays a global operator a fee to run the front desk, the loyalty programme and the brand standards. That split is deliberate: SAMHI captures the property's cash flows and any rise in its value, while outsourcing the day-to-day hospitality it has no edge in.
  • The tailwind is a genuine room-rate up-cycle. Since COVID, Indian hotel demand — corporate travel, weddings, tourism — has run ahead of a thin pipeline of new supply. That imbalance pushes up room rates (ADR) and occupancy together, and the resulting RevPAR (revenue per available room) has been climbing hard: SAMHI's rose ~16.5% in FY25 and a further ~9.5% in FY26. Because a hotel's costs are largely fixed, most of that extra revenue drops to profit.
  • The flip side is a heavy debt load and thin real profit. Buying hotels with borrowed money leaves SAMHI carrying big interest and depreciation. The company only turned its first full-year net profit in FY25 (~₹86 Cr); FY26's headline ~₹567 Cr looks spectacular but is mostly a one-time accounting gain, not operating cash. The real story of the next few years is deleveraging — using rising RevPAR and asset sales (a GIC partnership) to pay down debt.
In plain terms

SAMHI is a private-equity-style hotel investor wearing a hotelier's uniform. It goes shopping for hotels that someone else built and couldn't make work — a half-empty business hotel, a defaulted developer's asset, a portfolio a fund wants to exit — and buys them for less than it would cost to pour the concrete itself. Then it puts a Marriott or Hyatt sign on the door, spends on a refit, and lets the brand fill the rooms while SAMHI collects the property's profit. In a market where room rates are rising faster than new hotels are opening, each of those fixed-cost buildings throws off more and more cash every year. The catch is the mortgage: SAMHI bought all this with debt, so the value it is creating is racing against the interest bill. Whether it wins that race is the entire investment question.

01

Owner, not operator: the two halves of a hotel

The single structural fact that most people get wrong about SAMHI.

Every branded hotel is really two businesses stapled together. There is the asset — the land, the building, the rooms, the balance-sheet and the debt behind them — and there is the operation — the brand, the reservation system, the loyalty programme, the staff and the service standards. In the modern hotel industry these two are usually owned by different companies. SAMHI sits firmly on the asset side. It owns the buildings; it does not carry the flag.

The only hotel-structure terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
OwnerOwns the physical hotel and its balance sheet — takes the property's profit, the depreciation and the debt. This is SAMHI. The owner's return rides on how much cash the hotel throws off and what the building is worth.
Operator / brandMarriott, Hyatt, IHG and the like. They run the hotel to brand standard under a contract and take a management or franchise fee (a slice of revenue/profit) — but put in little or no capital. Asset-light by design.
Management contractThe brand actually staffs and runs the hotel for the owner, for a fee. The owner keeps the residual profit and the risk.
FranchiseThe owner (or a third-party operator) runs the hotel but licenses the brand name, systems and bookings for a fee. More control for the owner, still the brand's reach.
Upper-upscale / upscaleThe quality tiers SAMHI lives in — a notch below true luxury (Taj/Oberoi), aimed squarely at the business traveller. Full-service enough to command a real rate, efficient enough to run at a good margin.
Why a hotel owner outsources the hotel

It sounds odd that a hotel company would pay someone else to run its hotels. The logic is sharp. A global brand brings three things SAMHI could never build alone: a booking engine and loyalty base that fills rooms (a large share of a Marriott hotel's guests arrive through Marriott's own channels), a trusted name that lets the room charge a premium, and operating discipline honed across thousands of properties. SAMHI's edge is a different one — spotting a mispriced building, financing the purchase, and turning the asset around. By marrying its capital to the brand's distribution, SAMHI gets a hotel that fills up and charges well without having to become a hotelier. The fee it pays the brand is the price of that fill — and on a rising-rate asset, it is money well spent.

Where the money actually lands

  • Room revenue — the bulk of the take, and the most rate-sensitive. This is where the RevPAR up-cycle shows up first and hardest.
  • Food, beverage & banqueting — restaurants, bars and especially the wedding/conference business, which in India can be a large and profitable slice for a full-service hotel.
  • The brand fee (an outflow) — a percentage of revenue and profit paid to Marriott/Hyatt/IHG for running the property. It is a real cost, but it buys the demand that fills the rooms.
  • The residual — SAMHI's — what's left after operating costs and the brand fee is the owner's hotel-level (asset) EBITDA, out of which come interest, depreciation and, finally, the shareholder's profit.
02

Hotel economics 101: ADR, occupancy, RevPAR and the drop-through

The four numbers that run a hotel — and the reason a good year is a very good year.

A hotel is one of the purest fixed-cost businesses there is. The building, the staff, the electricity and the brand team cost roughly the same whether the hotel is half full or nearly full. So the whole art of hotel investing is captured in a handful of metrics, and in one consequence of them — operating leverage — that makes hotel earnings swing violently with the cycle. Learn four terms and you can read SAMHI.

The four hospitality metrics
MetricWhat it is
ADRAverage Daily Rate — the average price a sold room fetches per night. The pricing lever: it moves with demand, brand, city and how full the hotel already is.
OccupancyThe share of available rooms actually sold (SAMHI runs around 74%). The volume lever. Above ~70–75% a hotel gains real pricing power — it can push ADR because it no longer needs the marginal guest.
RevPARRevenue Per Available Room = ADR × occupancy. The single number that matters, because it blends price and volume into one. It is measured against every room the hotel has, sold or not — so an empty room drags it down.
Asset EBITDA marginHotel-level operating profit as a share of revenue. As RevPAR climbs on a fixed cost base, this margin expands fast — the drop-through effect below.

The drop-through: why a rising rate is worth so much

Here is the mechanism that makes hotels a leveraged bet on their own cycle. When a hotel is already ~74% full, selling one more room — or charging ₹500 more for a room already sold — adds revenue at almost zero extra cost. The room exists, the staff are rostered, the lights are on. So an incremental rupee of room revenue drops to hotel EBITDA at a very high rate — often 60–70 paise or more in the rupee. That is "drop-through," and it runs both ways: in a downturn, when RevPAR falls, the same fixed costs mean profit collapses far faster than revenue.

The device that is specific to SAMHI: the same room, worth more each year

SAMHI's ~4,900 rooms are, physically, almost the same rooms they were three years ago — the same buildings, the same fixed cost to keep the lights on. What changed is the price each one commands. RevPAR moving from roughly ₹4,300 to ₹5,490 in two years is a ~28% rise in revenue per room with barely any rise in the cost of having that room. Multiply a high-single-digit RevPAR gain across a fixed base and let two-thirds of it drop through, and hotel EBITDA can grow at a double-digit clip off a flat asset. This is the engine: SAMHI does not need to build a single new hotel to grow profit — it just needs the rooms it already owns to keep pricing higher into a supply-starved market. The whole thesis is a fixed number of rooms meeting a rising rate.

03

The India hotel up-cycle: demand outrunning supply

The structural tailwind that has re-rated the whole listed hotel sector.

SAMHI's rising RevPAR is not a company trick; it is riding a genuine, structural imbalance in Indian hospitality. After the COVID collapse, hotel demand came back hard — corporate travel, conferences, a wedding boom, domestic tourism and inbound visitors — while the supply of new branded rooms stayed thin, because the pandemic froze construction and because new hotels take years and heavy capital to build. When demand grows faster than rooms are added, the market tightens, hotels fill up, and rates rise. That is the up-cycle, and it has lifted every listed Indian hotel owner.

The scarcer new rooms are, the longer pricing power lasts — which is why the length of the up-cycle, not just its existence, is the real debate for hotel investors.

Two features of this cycle matter especially for SAMHI. First, its hotels sit in the business micro-markets of the big cities — the office corridors of Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Gurugram, Pune and Chennai — where demand is driven by corporate India and the IT/GCC (global capability centre) build-out, a deeper and steadier source than pure leisure. Second, because SAMHI's assets were often underperforming when it bought them, they had room to improve occupancy and rate faster than a mature, already-full hotel — the cycle and the turnaround compound.

The subtlety a first-timer should hold: this is a cyclical dressed as a growth story

An up-cycle is, by definition, a cycle — it turns. Room rates cannot rise faster than supply forever; eventually the pipeline of new hotels catches up, demand softens in a slowdown, and RevPAR flattens or falls. Because of drop-through, a falling RevPAR would hit SAMHI's profit hard. So the honest way to read the India hotel story is not "rates go up forever" but "rates are unusually tight now, and the question is how many years the supply shortage lasts." SAMHI is a good vehicle for the up-cycle precisely because it is high-octane — leveraged assets in the tightest markets — which is a virtue on the way up and a danger on the way down.

04

The buy-and-fix playbook: consolidation, not construction

SAMHI's defining strategy — and the reason it is not just another hotel chain.

Most hotel companies grow by building. SAMHI grows by buying. Its founding idea is that in a fragmented, capital-starved Indian hotel market, the cheapest way to own a good hotel is to acquire one someone else already built and got wrong — an underperforming, over-leveraged or orphaned asset — for less than it would cost to construct the same rooms today. Buy at a discount to replacement cost, put a global brand on it, renovate, professionalise the operation, and let the up-cycle plus the turnaround lift its value. It is a consolidator's model, closer to private equity than to traditional hoteliering.

The ACIC deal — the playbook at scale

The clearest example is the ACIC portfolio. In 2023 SAMHI absorbed a set of operating and under-construction hotels (the Asiya Capital / GIC-backed built-asset platform) largely in an all-stock deal, adding thousands of rooms in its target business markets in one stroke and bringing a partnership with the Singapore sovereign fund GIC. It is the essence of the model: rather than spend years building, SAMHI folded in a whole platform of assets — some to be rebranded and ramped — and used its listed equity as the currency. Earlier bolt-ons and greenfield-then-flip projects (a Hyatt Regency in Pune, Courtyard and Fairfield conversions, Sheraton Hyderabad) fit the same template.

The arithmetic is what makes it work. Even after paying to renovate a stressed asset to brand standard, SAMHI typically lands below the cost of building the same rooms new — and the hotel opens years sooner, capturing the up-cycle immediately rather than after a long construction wait. That gap — acquisition-plus-refit cost versus today's inflated replacement cost — is the margin of safety the whole model is built on, and it is why a rival cannot simply construct its way to SAMHI's returns at current building prices.

Why the buy-cheap model is powerful and dangerous at once

Buying below replacement cost is a real edge: it means SAMHI's rooms are cheaper than a rival's newly-built ones, so at the same RevPAR its return on capital is higher, and a new competitor cannot undercut it without building at today's inflated cost. But the same model has a sting — you buy stressed assets with debt, and you inherit their problems along with their discount. A hotel is cheap because it was failing; turning it around takes capital and time, and until it stabilises it may earn little while the interest clock ticks. SAMHI's history of losses is the ledger of that lag: years of buying and fixing before the portfolio matured enough, and the cycle turned kind enough, to cover the financing. The model works — but only if the turnarounds land before the debt bites.

A PE-founded platform, listed in 2023, with marquee financial owners.

SAMHI is not an old family hotel house; it is a professionally-founded, institutionally-backed platform built for exactly the buy-and-fix strategy above. Founded by Ashish Jakhanwala, it was assembled over more than a decade with capital from global private-equity and sovereign investors, listed on the NSE and BSE in September 2023, and continues to be shaped by its marquee backers rather than a promoter family.

06

The debt, and the deleveraging journey

The flip side of buying hotels with borrowed money — and the profit number that isn't what it looks like.

Every acquisition in section 04 was paid for substantially with debt. That is the unavoidable cost of an asset-heavy, buy-and-fix model: SAMHI carries a large borrowing pile, and against it a heavy load of interest and depreciation that has, for most of its life, kept the bottom line thin or negative. Understanding the debt — and how SAMHI is now paying it down — is as important as understanding the RevPAR up-cycle. The two are in a race.

Start with the surprise, because it reframes FY26 entirely. SAMHI's reported net profit for FY26 was roughly ₹567 Cr — a ~5.6× jump that looks like a blow-out year. It is not what it seems. The bulk of that figure comes from one-time, non-cash accounting gains — chiefly the recognition of a large deferred tax asset (banking years of past losses as a future tax shield) and reversals of earlier asset impairments. Strip those out and SAMHI's underlying operating profitability is far more modest — a company that only reached its first real full-year profit in FY25 (~₹86 Cr) and is still early in earning its way clear of its financing costs. The headline flatters; the operating story is "improving, not yet rich."

SAMHI is deleveraging by three routes at once. The up-cycle lifts EBITDA (more profit to service the same debt). The GIC capital infusion — ~₹752 Cr for a 35% subsidiary stake — brings in outside cash to repay borrowings without heavily diluting listed shareholders. And capital recycling — selling stakes in stabilised assets to fund growth or cut debt — is now a stated part of the model, PE-style. Together these have pulled net debt down toward ~₹1,430 Cr and leverage toward the ~2.5× the company is steering for.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with

SAMHI can post a record "profit" and still not be a highly profitable company — because that profit was mostly an accounting entry, not cash from renting rooms. And it can be genuinely improving its economics while its shares still look cheap on a headline P/E that the one-off gain has distorted. The clean way to judge SAMHI is to ignore the reported net-profit line almost entirely and watch two real numbers: hotel-level (asset) EBITDA, which tells you if the rooms are earning more, and net debt / EBITDA, which tells you if the borrowings are coming under control. A hotel owner is a spread between the cash its buildings throw off and the interest on the debt that bought them. For years that spread was negative or thin. The FY25–FY26 turn is the first stretch where the cash is comfortably outrunning the interest — and sustaining that, not the flashy PAT, is the whole game.

Why the debt reframes the risk: operating leverage (section 02) and financial leverage stack on top of each other here. A rising RevPAR is amplified twice on the way up — once by drop-through, again by falling interest as debt shrinks. But a falling RevPAR would be amplified the same way on the way down, against a fixed interest bill. That double gearing is exactly why SAMHI is a higher-beta way to own the Indian hotel cycle than a debt-light peer — thrilling in a boom, unforgiving in a bust.
07

Owned vs asset-light: two ways to own a hotel stock

Why SAMHI is a leveraged property bet, not a fee-collecting brand — and why that is a choice, not a flaw.

Not all "hotel stocks" are the same animal. There is a spectrum from asset-heavy owners, who put their own capital and debt into buying the buildings and keep the property's full profit and risk, to asset-light operators, who own little real estate and instead collect fees for running or franchising other people's hotels. SAMHI is firmly at the asset-heavy owner end. Knowing where a company sits on this spectrum tells you almost everything about how its earnings will behave.

Asset-light operator (e.g. a Lemon Tree-style manager)Owns few buildings; earns fees for operating/franchising hotels owned by others.Low capital, steadier fee income, less debt — but gives up the property upside SAMHI keeps in a boom.

SAMHI does carry a small, growing asset-light strand of its own — a management arm (built partly around the "Caspia" and third-party operating capability) that runs some hotels for fees — but it is a minor part of the story today. The company is, and for the foreseeable future will be, an owner. That is a deliberate bet: in a rising market, owning the building captures far more than collecting a fee on it. SAMHI has chosen to keep the property upside — and the property risk — for itself.

How to place SAMHI against its peers without confusing them

A common mistake is to lump SAMHI with an asset-light operator because both have hotel brands on their buildings. They are opposite bets. An asset-light manager sells you steady fee income with little debt — a lower-octane, more defensive way to play hospitality. SAMHI sells you the full swing of the property cycle, geared with debt — you get the whole RevPAR up-cycle dropping through to your EBITDA, and you carry the mortgage. Neither is better in the abstract; they suit different views. If you believe the Indian up-cycle has years left and SAMHI's deleveraging holds, the owner model gives you more of it. If you fear the cycle is late, the asset-light model is where you hide. SAMHI is the accelerator, not the airbag.

The portfolio: business hotels in the right cities

Where the ~4,900 rooms are, which flags they fly, and why the mix is deliberate.

SAMHI's portfolio is not scattered leisure resorts; it is a focused book of upscale and upper-upscale business hotels in the office micro-markets of India's largest cities, run under a stable of global brands. The concentration is intentional — corporate demand in a handful of deep markets is steadier and easier to asset-manage than a spread of one-off tourist properties.

What makes the portfolio work

  • City concentration in demand centres — Bengaluru, Hyderabad, the Delhi-NCR belt, Pune, Chennai: the corporate and GCC engines of the economy, where business travel is deep and recurring.

Leisure and luxury hotels grab attention, but SAMHI's book is deliberately unglamorous: mid-to-upper business hotels next to office parks. That is a feature. Corporate demand is repeat, contracted and less seasonal than leisure; it fills a hotel on weeknights all year, not just in a holiday spike. In the office cities SAMHI targets, that demand is also structurally growing as global firms pile capability centres into India. So the portfolio is tuned to the most durable, least faddish slice of the up-cycle — the business traveller who has to be in Bengaluru or Hyderabad on a Tuesday, at whatever rate a tight market sets.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, SAMHI does not really compete on guests the way it competes on assets — because the brands (Marriott, Hyatt, IHG) fill the rooms, SAMHI's real contest is with other buyers of hotels: who can acquire and turn around assets at the best price. Its PE-native, buy-cheap model is a genuine edge there. Second, on the stock, SAMHI is the high-beta name in the group — more leverage and a pure-owner model make it the sharpest way to play a continuing up-cycle, and the most exposed if it fades. Chalet is the closest like-for-like on the asset model; Indian Hotels is the quality anchor; the asset-light names are the defensive alternatives. SAMHI's case is not "better than Taj" — it is "more torque on the same cycle, if you can stomach the debt."

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A geared owner riding a strong cycle — asked whether it deleverages before the cycle turns.

SAMHI enters FY27 in its best-ever operating shape: RevPAR still rising, hotel EBITDA growing, a GIC partnership funding both growth and debt reduction, and a pipeline of assets to rebrand and ramp. What it cannot control is the one thing that decides the outcome — how long the Indian hotel up-cycle runs, because its leverage magnifies whichever way RevPAR moves. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • Leveraged play on a real up-cycle — tight supply and growing corporate demand keep RevPAR rising, and drop-through turns each rise into outsized EBITDA growth.
  • Bought below replacement cost — an acquisition-led book earns a higher return per rupee than newly-built rivals, and new supply can't undercut it cheaply.
  • Deleveraging in motion — rising EBITDA plus the GIC infusion and capital recycling are pulling net debt/EBITDA toward ~2.5×, de-risking the equity as it falls.
  • Turnaround runway — acquired assets still ramping (ACIC portfolio, rebrands, renovations) add growth on top of the cycle, largely without new construction.
  • Right cities, right tier — business hotels in Bengaluru/Hyderabad/NCR catch the deepest, most durable slice of demand as GCCs expand.

The bear case

  • Double gearing cuts both ways — operating and financial leverage stacked; a RevPAR downturn would hit EBITDA and equity hard against a fixed interest bill.
  • Cycle risk — room rates can't outrun supply forever; a new-hotel pipeline or a slowdown could flatten RevPAR just as the market prices in more.
  • Thin underlying profit — the flashy FY26 PAT is mostly one-off; the real operating bottom line is still modest and financing-heavy.
  • Execution & capex — turnarounds, rebrands and renovations cost capital and time; a stumble delays deleveraging.
  • Institutional overhang & brand dependence — large PE/sovereign holders may sell blocks, and profitability leans on fees paid to brands SAMHI does not control.
The bottom line

SAMHI Hotels is a disciplined, PE-native buyer of Indian business hotels, catching the strongest room-rate cycle in a generation with a portfolio it acquired cheaply and a balance sheet it is finally paying down. The engine is elegant — a fixed set of rooms worth more each year, with two-thirds of every extra rupee dropping to profit and a global brand doing the filling. The weight is equally real — the debt those rooms were bought with, and a reported profit that flatters an operating story still in its early innings. The next test is concrete and datable: whether net debt/EBITDA reaches the ~2.5× target over the coming few quarters while like-for-like RevPAR keeps climbing through FY27. Get the room rate and the debt paydown moving in the same direction and SAMHI compounds as the cycle's sharpest owner; let RevPAR stall with leverage still high, and the same gearing that thrilled on the way up will bite on the way down.
