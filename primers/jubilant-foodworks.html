00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Jubilant FoodWorks (JFL) is India's largest quick-service-restaurant (QSR) company. It does not own the Domino's Pizza brand — it is the master franchisee: the company that pays royalties to the American brand owner in exchange for the exclusive right to build and run Domino's stores across India, Sri Lanka and Bangladesh. It runs the same model for the fried-chicken chain Popeyes in India, owns the Domino's business in Turkey, Azerbaijan and Georgia (via its subsidiary DP Eurasia), and has a homegrown Chinese brand, Hong's Kitchen.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Scale leadership in a growing habit. JFL runs 3,636 restaurants across six countries, of which ~2,455 are Domino's stores in India — far ahead of any other single QSR chain in the country. FY26 revenue was ~₹9,513 Cr, up ~17%, riding India's long-term shift toward eating out and ordering in.
  • Profit rebounded, but the market is worried. FY26 net profit more than doubled to ~₹444 Cr (helped by a soft prior-year base and the international turnaround), yet the stock is down ~37% over the year. The reason: a broad demand slowdown has kept same-store sales growth (SSSG) in the low single digits, and margins are under pressure.
  • The whole debate is delivery vs disruption. Domino's India is a delivery-first machine — its own app and own rider fleet let it promise fast delivery and bypass the food-aggregator commission. That is the moat. The threat is that Zomato, Swiggy and the new 10-minute quick-commerce food players are changing how Indians order, while JFL still trades at a premium ~70× earnings. Bulls buy the dominant franchise; bears fear the disruption and the price.
In plain terms

Think of JFL as a pizza-and-chicken factory network run as a franchise business. Domino's, the global brand, licenses its recipes, menu and marketing to JFL; JFL puts up the capital, builds thousands of small kitchens, staffs them, runs the supply chain, and keeps most of the profit after paying the brand a royalty (a slice of every rupee of sales). It grows in two ways: open more stores, and sell more from the stores it already has (that second one is "same-store sales growth", the metric analysts live and die by). Right now the store count keeps climbing, but Indians are eating out a bit more cautiously — so the "sell more per store" engine has slowed, and that is the story of the last two years.

01

QSR and the master-franchise model 101

No jargon left standing — the handful of ideas that explain the whole business.

"QSR" means Quick-Service Restaurant — the standardised, fast-turnaround, value-led format the world knows as Domino's, McDonald's or KFC. It is the opposite of a fine-dining restaurant: a small menu, a repeatable kitchen process, fast service, and heavy reliance on takeaway and delivery. The business is built to be copied hundreds of times over — which is exactly why the franchise model exists.

What "master franchisee" actually means

A global brand like Domino's Pizza (owned by Domino's Pizza Inc. in the US) does not want to operate thousands of kitchens in every country itself. Instead it appoints a local partner — a master franchisee — and grants it the exclusive rights to a territory. In return the local partner agrees to build a minimum number of stores and to pay royalties. That local partner, for India, Sri Lanka and Bangladesh, is Jubilant FoodWorks.

Why the franchise model is clever — and its catch

The master-franchise model lets JFL ride a world-class brand without inventing it: it gets Domino's recipes, its 30-years-refined store operating system, and instant customer trust, and it focuses its energy on the things that actually win in India — supply chain, real estate, technology and delivery speed. The catch is threefold. First, JFL pays a royalty forever on every sale. Second, it does not control the brand — a global misstep, or a change in franchise terms, is out of its hands. Third, franchise rights are renewable, not permanent: the recent decision to walk away from Dunkin' (see §04) is a reminder that these agreements can be started and ended. For the crown jewel, though — Domino's India — JFL's rights run for decades and are the foundation of the whole company.

02

How JFL actually earns — the store P&L

Follow one store's rupee from menu price down to profit.

A QSR company is fundamentally a collection of small kitchens, each with its own tiny profit-and-loss statement. Understand one store's economics and you understand the whole company — because JFL's job is simply to run that store profitably and then repeat it thousands of times.

The three margins that matter

  • Gross margin (after food cost). Start with the menu price; subtract the cost of ingredients — cheese, wheat/flour, chicken, vegetables, packaging. Pizza has a famously high gross margin (dough and cheese are cheap relative to the price), which is why Domino's can run heavy value offers and still make money. JFL's gross margin runs in the high-60s to ~75% range. The enemy here is input inflation, especially cheese and wheat.
  • Store operating margin. From gross profit, subtract the costs of running the physical store — staff wages, rent, electricity/energy, delivery-rider cost. What's left is the store's own operating profit. This is where the recent squeeze bites: wage and energy inflation have pressured store margins even as sales grow.
  • Company EBITDA margin. Aggregate every store, subtract head-office cost, commissary (central-kitchen) cost, technology and marketing, and the royalty — and you get the group EBITDA margin. FY26 group EBITDA margin was ~19.8% on a post-IndAS-116 basis (see the callout on why that number is flattered).
Read this before you compare margins — IndAS-116: Since FY20, Indian accounting rule IndAS-116 requires companies to stop treating shop rent as a simple operating expense. Instead, leases are capitalised: rent disappears from EBITDA and reappears lower down as depreciation and interest. The effect is to inflate the headline EBITDA margin and load up "debt" on the balance sheet that is really just future rent. So JFL's ~19.8% reported EBITDA margin is not comparable to a pre-IndAS number: management's India business pre-IndAS EBITDA margin was ~12% in Q4 FY26. Always check which basis a QSR margin is quoted on.

Operating leverage — the whole point of scale

Because a big share of a store's costs are fixed (rent, base staffing, the manager), each extra order that comes through the door is highly profitable — the ingredients cost something, but the rent doesn't rise. So when a store's daily sales climb, its margin climbs faster. That is operating leverage, and it works in both directions: in a demand slump, soft sales mean margins fall faster than revenue. JFL's entire margin story — management guides to ~200 bps of margin improvement over time — rests on sales-per-store recovering so the fixed cost base gets better utilised.

03

The company and who controls it

From one pizza store in Delhi to a six-country QSR platform.

Jubilant FoodWorks is the food-services arm of the Jubilant Bhartia Group, the diversified business house of brothers Shyam S. Bhartia and Hari S. Bhartia (also known for pharma/life-sciences group Jubilant Pharmova and ingredients group Jubilant Ingrevia). The company began life in 1995 as "Domino's Pizza India" and opened its first store in New Delhi in January 1996 — it has been building the network ever since.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter: the Jubilant Bhartia group holds ~40.3%. A stable, long-term promoter that has controlled the business since inception — but not a majority stake, so institutional investors (who hold the bulk of the rest) have a real voice.
  • Institutions: mutual funds and foreign investors together own the majority (>50%) of the float — JFL is a core "India consumption" holding for many funds, which is part of why its valuation stays elevated and its stock moves sharply on sentiment.
  • Management: CEO & MD Sameer Khetarpal (since 2022) has pushed a technology-and-value agenda — cheaper store formats, an app/loyalty focus, aggressive value pricing and the portfolio clean-up (exiting Dunkin', doubling down on Domino's and Popeyes).
  • Balance sheet: unlike the debt-free Indian-only business of old, JFL now carries meaningful borrowings (~₹4,900 Cr of total liabilities that include lease obligations and DP Eurasia acquisition debt) — a change worth understanding (see §10).

Brand by brand

  • Domino's Pizza (India, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh) — the crown jewel: ~2,455 stores in India alone, the #1 pizza and #1 QSR brand in the country, delivery-led and deeply penetrated into smaller towns. Everything else is a rounding error next to it.
  • Popeyes (India) — the American fried-chicken chain, JFL's bet on the fast-growing chicken/QSR segment where KFC dominates. Small today (~78 stores) but posting ~28% same-store growth in FY26 — the clearest new-brand success and the most credible second growth leg.
  • DP Eurasia (Turkey, Azerbaijan, Georgia) — the international acquisition: Domino's master franchise for Turkey and the Caucasus.   • Dunkin' (exiting) — the coffee-and-donuts chain JFL ran for ~15 years. It never scaled profitably in India; in FY26 JFL announced it will not renew the franchise (agreement ends December 2026), redirecting capital to Domino's and Popeyes. A textbook example of the franchise model's built-in exit door.
Why the portfolio clean-up is a good sign

A multi-brand QSR company faces constant temptation to chase every trendy format. JFL's FY26 decision to kill Dunkin' and concentrate on Domino's + Popeyes signals capital discipline: rather than subsidise a sub-scale coffee brand indefinitely, management is pouring resources into the two concepts that are working. Popeyes' ~28% same-store growth suggests the fried-chicken bet is landing, giving JFL a genuine second engine beyond pizza — important, because a one-brand company is one demand-slump away from a stalled story.

05

The Domino's delivery moat

The one structural advantage the whole investment case leans on.

What makes Domino's India special isn't the pizza — it's the delivery system. For two decades Domino's has built its brand around a simple promise (fast, reliable home delivery) and, crucially, has done it with its own app and its own riders rather than depending on a third-party aggregator. In an era where Zomato and Swiggy take a hefty commission and own the customer relationship, that is a rare and valuable position.

On its own app, JFL keeps the full margin, owns the customer data and controls the delivery experience. On an aggregator, it pays a commission and rents the customer. Domino's delivery being predominantly on its own channel is the core structural edge.

What the moat buys JFL

  • Margin retention. Every order on Domino's own app avoids the aggregator commission — a direct margin advantage over rivals who lean on Zomato/Swiggy for the bulk of their delivery.
  • Customer ownership & data. JFL sees who orders, how often and what — powering its loyalty programme, targeted offers and menu decisions. An aggregator-dependent brand is largely blind to this.
  • Speed and reliability. Own riders stationed at own stores enable the fast-delivery promise Domino's has trained Indian customers to expect — a habit and trust that took 20+ years to build.
  • Dense store network = short delivery radius. ~2,455 stores mean most Indian urban customers are minutes away from a Domino's kitchen — the physical precondition for fast delivery, and something a new entrant can't replicate cheaply.

In FY26 the delivery engine was the bright spot: Domino's India delivery revenue grew ~10%+ and delivery order volumes grew in the high teens, even as dine-in and takeaway stayed soft. The delivery moat is doing exactly what it's meant to — cushioning the business when the walk-in economy is weak.

The nuance — the moat is real, but it's being tested

Owning delivery has always been Domino's superpower. But two things complicate it now. First, JFL also lists on Zomato and Swiggy to capture demand that starts on those apps — so it isn't purely "own channel", and it does pay commissions on that slice. Second, the definition of "fast" is being rewritten: quick-commerce platforms now promise food in 10–15 minutes, resetting customer expectations (see §08). JFL's answer is to lean harder into its own app, loyalty and value offers — but the moat that looked impregnable five years ago now has to be actively defended.

06

Store unit economics and the expansion drag

Why opening stores can slow growth before it speeds it up.

JFL grows by adding stores — it added a net ~351 stores group-wide in FY26, including ~289 net in India. But store expansion is a double-edged sword, and understanding why is central to reading the numbers.

The new-store J-curve

A brand-new Domino's store opens with its full cost base — rent, staff, equipment — but with a customer base it has to build from scratch. So it typically runs at low sales and thin (or negative) margins in year one, then ramps over 2–3 years as awareness builds. Two consequences follow:

  • New stores dilute SSSG optics. Because SSSG only counts stores open more than a year, a wave of young stores sits outside the SSSG base while dragging on overall margins — so aggressive expansion can make the "same-store" number look worse before the cohort matures.
  • New stores dilute blended ADS and margin. Average sales-per-store and blended margin fall when a lot of low-volume young stores enter the mix, even if each is on track. The payoff comes later, as they fill.
Management's smart response — cheaper stores

JFL has deliberately shrunk the cost of a new store: capex per Domino's store is down ~20% over three years, and new openings increasingly use small 600–700 sq ft "delivery/carry-out" formats rather than large dine-in restaurants. The logic is elegant: since Domino's is delivery-led, it doesn't need expensive dine-in real estate to serve most orders. Smaller, cheaper stores mean a faster payback, lower break-even sales, and a denser network (shorter delivery radius) — letting JFL keep expanding into smaller towns without the margin drag a big-box format would impose. This is the single most important operational lever management is pulling.

The bet is simple: build cheaper kitchens, closer together, and let a recovering same-store sales cycle turn fixed costs into operating leverage.

The risk embedded in the same mechanism: if the demand slump persists, the young-store cohort ramps slowly, the SSSG base stays soft, and the operating leverage that justifies the valuation simply doesn't show up on schedule. Expansion and demand recovery have to work together — one without the other disappoints.

07

The demand slump and the SSSG cycle

The cyclical force that has defined JFL's stock for two years — read this to understand the last chart.

For a QSR company, same-store sales growth (SSSG) is the pulse. It tells you whether existing customers are spending more — the truest read on demand. And the pulse has been weak: after the post-Covid boom, India's discretionary/eating-out spend cooled, and JFL's SSSG dropped from double digits into the low single digits. FY26 Domino's India SSSG was ~5.2% — respectable versus struggling peers, but a long way from the ~15–20% prints of the boom years.

Directional, to show the shape of the cycle. The story of JFL's de-rated stock is precisely this: SSSG collapsed from the re-opening highs and has only tentatively recovered — held up mainly by delivery volumes and value offers, while dine-in stays soft.

What's driving the slump

  • Weak discretionary spending. Urban middle-class consumers turned cautious after a period of high inflation; eating out and ordering in are among the first things a stretched household trims.
  • A hard comparison base. The FY22–23 re-opening boom set very high sales bases, making subsequent growth optically weak.
  • Intense competition for "share of stomach". More QSR chains, cloud kitchens, local players and — critically — quick-commerce food are all chasing the same eating-out rupee (see §08).
  • Input-cost inflation. Cheese, wheat, energy and wages have all risen, squeezing store margins just as sales growth slowed — the worst of both worlds.
How JFL is fighting back: aggressive value pricing (entry-price pizzas, combo offers) to defend order volumes; leaning on the delivery channel (growing high-teens) to offset soft dine-in; the fast-growing Popeyes as a second engine; and menu innovation plus loyalty to lift order frequency. Management has signalled that like-for-like growth should improve — the bet is that the cycle turns and the operating leverage kicks in. But the honest read is that SSSG recovery, not store count, is the swing factor for the stock, and it remains unproven.
08

Aggregators, quick-commerce and the 10-minute question

The structural disruption unique to this moment — both a threat and an opportunity.

The biggest debate around JFL isn't the pizza market — it's how India will order food in five years. Two forces are reshaping the channel: the food-delivery aggregators (Zomato, Swiggy) that already intermediate a huge share of restaurant delivery, and the newer quick-commerce platforms (Blinkit, Swiggy Instamart, Zepto) that deliver in 10–15 minutes and are now pushing into food. For a delivery-led company like JFL, this is existential in both directions.

The aggregator relationship — frenemy, not enemy

Zomato and Swiggy are simultaneously JFL's rival and its channel. They compete for the customer's attention and take commission on orders placed through them — a margin cost and a loss of the customer relationship. But they also aggregate enormous demand, so JFL lists on them too, to capture orders it would otherwise miss. The strategic tension: how much volume to concede to the aggregators (and their commission) versus how hard to push customers onto Domino's own app. JFL's answer has been to defend its own-app share fiercely with loyalty and pricing — its delivery moat is precisely the thing that lets it avoid becoming a commoditised listing on someone else's platform.

Threat vs opportunity — the honest two-sided view

The threat: quick-commerce is training Indians to expect anything in 10 minutes, including food. If a Blinkit or Instamart can deliver a hot meal or snack faster and cheaper, Domino's 20-minute promise loses its edge, and JFL's hard-won own-delivery advantage erodes into just another option. Worse, these platforms have deep-pocketed backers subsidising speed and price — a war JFL can't outspend. The opportunity: JFL already owns the two things quick-commerce needs — a dense network of kitchens close to customers, and a proven own-delivery operation. It can partner with quick-commerce platforms (using them as an extra demand funnel), pilot faster formats, and use its supply chain to serve instant-food demand others can only aspire to. The bull says JFL is uniquely positioned to win the instant-food era; the bear says it is a legacy QSR about to be disrupted by better-funded, faster rivals. This is the defining uncertainty in the stock.

The bottom line on disruption: JFL is not a passive victim — it has the network, the brand and the delivery DNA to compete. But the channel is shifting under its feet faster than at any point in its history, and the market is no longer willing to pay peak-boom multiples for a business whose delivery moat is being actively contested. Watch own-app order share and delivery volume growth — if they hold up, the moat is intact; if they slip, the disruption thesis is winning.
09

The competitive arena

India's organised QSR market is a handful of listed franchisees fighting for share of stomach.

India's branded QSR market is projected to be large and fast-growing — often sized toward ₹1 lakh crore by 2027, growing ~18–20% a year — as organised chains take share from unbranded eateries. It is dominated by a small set of listed master-franchisees, each running a global brand. JFL is the biggest, but the field is crowded and well-capitalised.

The competitive tension to watch

Two dynamics matter. First, pizza vs everything else: JFL owns pizza (Domino's is the runaway category leader), but the fastest-growing QSR segment is chicken — which is why Popeyes matters, pitting JFL directly against KFC (Devyani, Sapphire). Second, the demand slump hits everyone: every listed QSR is fighting soft SSSG and margin pressure at once, so the winner is whoever has the best unit economics and channel control. JFL's edges — scale, supply chain and own-delivery — are real, but rivals are cutting store costs and pushing value just as hard. The next few years are a share-of-stomach war fought on price, speed and store density.

10

Financials & valuation

Fast revenue growth, a profit rebound — and a still-premium price the market is now questioning.

JFL's top line keeps compounding — helped enormously by the consolidation of the acquired Turkey business — and FY26 profit more than doubled off a soft base. But the debate has never been about whether JFL grows; it's about what you should pay for that growth given the demand slump, the margin pressure and the disruption risk.

How to think about the ~70× multiple

At ~₹28,900 Cr on ~₹444 Cr of FY26 profit, JFL trades around 70× trailing earnings — a rich price that only makes sense if you believe SSSG recovers, margins climb the guided ~200 bps, Popeyes scales, and the delivery moat holds against quick-commerce. Bulls argue you are buying the category-defining, scale-leading QSR franchise in a structurally growing market — a rare consumer compounder that deserves a scarcity premium. Bears point out the multiple leaves no room for error: with SSSG stuck in single digits, margins squeezed, disruption rising and the stock already down 37%, a 70× multiple can keep de-rating if the recovery keeps slipping. Both can be right at different points in the cycle — which is exactly why same-store sales growth and margin trajectory are the two numbers to track every quarter.

11

The growth runway

Cheaper stores, small-town penetration, a chicken second-leg and an international turnaround.

Despite the near-term slump, the long-term growth levers are genuinely large — India remains dramatically under-penetrated in organised QSR versus other emerging markets, and JFL has several distinct engines to pull.

The structural tailwind

  • Under-penetration. India's per-capita QSR spend is a fraction of other emerging markets; as incomes rise and urbanise, the organised eating-out market can compound for a decade-plus.
  • Formalisation. Branded chains keep taking share from unorganised eateries — a penetration tailwind independent of any single year's demand.
  • The aspirational habit. Domino's is, for many young Indians, the default "treat" order — a brand-and-habit advantage that is hard to dislodge and travels well into smaller towns.
The next chapter is less about whether JFL can open stores — it clearly can — and more about whether it can make the existing ones sell more, faster, without giving the customer away to an aggregator.
12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A dominant franchise with a real moat — priced for a recovery that has to actually arrive.

JFL is the clearest way to own India's QSR growth: the scale leader, the delivery moat, a stable promoter and a genuine second engine in Popeyes. The investment debate is almost entirely about the demand cycle, the disruption risk and the price — not whether the business is good.

The bull case

  • Category leadership — ~2,455 Domino's India stores; the #1 QSR brand with unmatched scale and network density.
  • The delivery moat — own app + own fleet keep the customer, the data and the margin in-house; delivery volumes grew high-teens even in a slump.
  • Operating leverage ahead — cheaper store formats + a recovering SSSG cycle = the guided ~200 bps margin expansion.
  • A real second engine — Popeyes at ~28% same-store growth in the fast-growing chicken segment.
  • Long runway — India QSR under-penetrated, formalising, growing ~18–20%; international turnaround adds scale.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~70× earnings leaves no room for error; the stock is already de-rating (−37% in a year).
  • Demand slump / soft SSSG — like-for-like growth stuck in single digits; dine-in weak; recovery unproven.
  • Quick-commerce disruption — 10-minute food players reset delivery expectations and out-spend JFL on speed/price.
  • Margin pressure — cheese/wheat/energy/wage inflation squeezing store economics; true India margin only ~12%.
  • Added risk on the balance sheet — no longer debt-free; DP Eurasia brings lira/FX and geopolitical exposure.
The bottom line

Jubilant FoodWorks is a genuinely dominant QSR franchise with a rare own-delivery moat — the kind of scaled consumer platform that is hard to build and harder to replace. But it sits at the intersection of three live uncertainties: a demand slump that has flattened its most important metric, a channel disruption (aggregators + quick-commerce) that is contesting its core advantage, and a valuation that still bakes in a strong recovery. The business risk is moderate; the timing-and-price risk is real. The story over the next few years will be written in two numbers each quarter — same-store sales growth (is the demand cycle turning?) and own-app delivery share/margin (is the moat holding against quick-commerce?). Get those right and the compounding justifies the premium; keep missing them and a 70× stock still has room to fall.
