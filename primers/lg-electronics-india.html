LG Electronics India, the leader in India's living room

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

LG Electronics India (LGEIL) is the Indian arm of Korea's LG Electronics — it makes and sells home appliances and consumer electronics (everything except mobile phones) and is India's largest player in the space. If you have ever bought a refrigerator, washing machine, air conditioner or television in India, there is a very good chance the market leader in that aisle was LG. Its identity is #1-or-#2 category leadership across almost every large-appliance category, sold through one of the deepest dealer-and-service networks in the country, under a brand Indians trust deeply.

Four facts frame everything:

  • The clear category leader. LG holds roughly ~30% of refrigerators, ~34% of washing machines, ~28% of panel TVs and ~21% of inverter ACs — and its lead is even bigger in the premium end (~63% of OLED TVs, ~43% of side-by-side fridges, ~37% of front-load washers). No rival leads across as many categories.
  • A cash-rich, debt-free, high-return business. FY26 revenue was ₹24,605 Cr (up just ~1% in a tough year) with an ~10% operating margin and ₹1,685 Cr of profit — down 24% as commodity costs and the rupee squeezed margins — but with ~32% ROCE, essentially no debt, and strong cash generation. FY25 was the stronger comparison (₹2,203 Cr PAT, 13% margin).
  • It sits on top of a huge penetration runway. India's household ownership of appliances is strikingly low — air conditioners in only ~7–12% of homes, washing machines ~13%, refrigerators ~33%. As incomes rise and homes electrify, that is a multi-decade volume tailwind, and LG is positioned to capture a large slice of it.
  • It listed via a landmark, all-OFS IPO. The October-2025 IPO raised ~₹11,607 Cr and popped ~50% on debut — but it was a 100% offer-for-sale by the Korean parent, which sold 15% and pocketed every rupee; the company raised nothing. The parent still owns ~85%, and LGEIL pays it a ~1.9%-of-sales royalty — the two facts that define the minority-shareholder debate.
In plain terms

Think of LG India as the default brand in the Indian home. For 25 years it has quietly become the market leader in fridge, washer, AC and TV aisles by combining three things ordinary buyers care about: a trusted brand, a shop somewhere nearby that stocks it (a vast dealer network), and — crucially for a big-ticket machine you keep for a decade — a service engineer who actually shows up when it breaks. That last point is the underrated moat: people buy the appliance and the reassurance. On top of that sits a genuinely long growth story — most Indian homes still don't own an AC or a washing machine, and as they get richer they will. The catches: it's ~85%-controlled by its Korean parent, which it pays a royalty to; it just listed via a pure share-sale by that parent (so minorities effectively bought a slice of a great business at a full price rather than funding its growth); and FY26 showed the business is cyclical — a cool, rainy summer and dearer commodities can dent a year hard.

01

Durables economics 101: how an appliance leader earns

No background needed — just the handful of levers that drive the whole P&L.

A consumer-durables company is a volume-plus-mix-plus-brand business. It designs and builds appliances, sells them through a huge web of dealers and modern-retail chains, and earns a modest margin on each unit — but a margin it can protect because brand and service let it charge a premium. Because it competes on trust and shelf presence as much as on price, a handful of levers explain almost the whole picture.

The levers that move a durables company's profit
LeverWhat it means (and LG's number)
Volume (units)How many appliances it sells a year, across many categories. High volume spreads fixed factory, brand and R&D cost thinner per unit. LG's ~₹24,600 Cr of revenue rides on millions of units across four big categories.
Realisation / ASP"Average selling price." A premium, feature-rich mix (inverter ACs, front-load washers, OLED TVs, side-by-side fridges) lifts revenue per unit far above a bare-bones product. Premiumisation is LG's biggest lever on realisation.
MixWhich products, and which trims, sell. A high-end OLED TV or a side-by-side fridge carries a much richer margin than an entry model. Mix — not just volume — decides profitability.
Gross & operating marginWhat's left after the cost of steel, compressors, panels, plastics and copper — then after ad-spend, logistics and service. LG ran a ~10% operating margin in FY26 (down from ~13% in FY25) when commodities and the rupee bit.
Working capital & serviceDurables is seasonal (ACs sell in summer), so inventory and channel financing matter; and an after-sales network is both a cost and a moat (see §05). Run well, it turns cash quickly and stays near debt-free — as LG does.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Revenue ≈ Units × Realisation, and Profit ≈ Revenue × Margin. So an appliance leader grows profit three ways — sell more units (ride penetration and share), sell pricier units (premiumise the mix), and protect the margin (brand pricing power, scale in buying steel and compressors, efficient logistics). The vulnerability is on the cost side: the big raw materials — steel, copper, aluminium, plastics, display panels — and imported components are priced in dollars, so a jump in commodity prices or a weaker rupee squeezes margin fast, exactly as it did in FY26. And because a big chunk of the year (the AC season) rides on the summer, weather is a real swing factor — a cool, early monsoon can knock a whole quarter.

Why "brand + distribution + service" is the real asset

  • Brand lets LG charge a premium and be the default choice — buyers reach for a name they trust for a machine they'll keep 8–10 years.
  • Distribution — being physically present, in stock, in tens of thousands of shops from metros to small towns — is what converts brand into sales. You can't buy what isn't on the shelf near you.
  • Service — the after-sales network — is what makes buyers comfortable spending on a big-ticket item, and what earns repeat purchases. It is expensive to build and very hard for a new entrant to replicate. This trio is the moat; the rest is execution.
02

The business model: two segments, one national machine

Read this once and the strategy clicks.

LG India reports as two segments: Home Appliances & Air Solutions (the big one — refrigerators, washing machines, air conditioners, microwaves and a growing B2B/commercial air-conditioning business) and Home Entertainment (televisions, monitors, audio/soundbars). Underneath, the whole model rests on two strengths: a deeply-localised Indian manufacturing base feeding a vast national distribution-and-service network. Scale in both is what turns category leadership into durable profit.

The value chain — from compressor to living room to call-back

LG doesn't just assemble appliances; it runs a heavily localised Indian supply chain and pairs it with a distribution-and-service web that reaches deep into the country. The same production and logistics base feeds two very different demand engines — the appliance/AC buyer and the TV/entertainment buyer — and, increasingly, exports.

Why "asset-medium but network-heavy" is the right frame

Unlike a pure asset-light services business, LG does sink capital into plants (and is about to sink ₹5,000 Cr into Sri City). But its truly valuable assets are less the factories than the intangible network — the brand, the dealer relationships, and the service infrastructure. Those took decades and enormous spend to build, they compound with scale (more units in the field → denser service coverage → more trust → more units), and a challenger with a better product but no network still can't reach the customer or reassure them. That's why LG can be the leader in category after category and defend it: the moat isn't a single product, it's the whole go-to-market machine. The flip side is that the same seasonality and commodity exposure that hit FY26 are structural — this is a good, but cyclical, business.

03

The company and who controls it

A near-30-year Indian success story — still ~85% owned by Korea.

LG has been in India since the late 1990s, and over a quarter-century it has become the country's leading appliance-and-electronics brand. It listed in October 2025, but its controlling shareholder remains LG Electronics Inc., Korea, with ~85% after the IPO. Understanding that parent relationship is essential to understanding the stock.

Timeline

  • Late 1990s–2000s

  • 2010s
    Becomes the category leader across refrigerators, washing machines, panel TVs and inverter ACs; pushes hard into premium (OLED TVs, front-load washers, side-by-side fridges).
  • FY25

  • Oct 2025
    IPO — ~₹11,607 Cr, a 100% offer-for-sale by the Korean parent (stake 100%→~85%). Subscribed ~54×; issue price ₹1,140; lists ~50% higher (~₹1,710) on 14 Oct on NSE & BSE.
  • FY26
    A tougher year — revenue flat at ~₹24,605 Cr, PAT −24% to ₹1,685 Cr on commodity/rupee margin pressure and a weak AC summer; announces ₹5,000 Cr Sri City (Andhra Pradesh) plant, its third factory.

Ownership & the parent relationship

  • Promoter: LG Electronics Inc. (Korea) holds ~85%. That means the parent controls the board, strategy, product roadmap and technology; the public float is ~15%.
  • What the parent provides: world-class R&D and technology (OLED panels, inverter compressors, AI/IoT features), global scale in buying components, and the LG brand itself — genuine, valuable assets that a standalone Indian company could not replicate. LGEIL is, in effect, the best-in-class execution arm of a global electronics major.
  • What minority shareholders should watch: because the parent is both controller and counterparty (royalties, related-party component purchases — see §08), Korea's interests and Indian minority interests are usually aligned but not identical. This is the central governance nuance of the stock.
  • Balance sheet: essentially net cash / debt-free (borrowings ~₹459 Cr against ~₹7,000 Cr of reserves), generating strong operating cash flow — funding its own Sri City expansion entirely from internal accruals.

The product portfolio, broken into parts

Four big categories, each with a premium halo — and one seasonal swing factor.

LG's revenue is spread across a genuinely broad portfolio, which is itself a strength — no single product makes or breaks a year. Four categories do the heavy lifting: refrigerators, air conditioners, washing machines and televisions, each roughly a fifth-to-a-quarter of sales, plus microwaves, audio and a growing commercial/B2B line. In every one, LG plays across the ladder — from mass models up to a strong premium tier where its share and margins are highest.

The portfolio decoded

  • Refrigerators (~27%) — LG's biggest line and the #1 brand (~30% share). Its inverter-compressor and side-by-side/French-door premium models (~43% of that premium sub-segment) carry high realisation, and the parent's compressor technology is a real edge.
  • Air conditioners & Air Solutions (~22%) — the fastest-growing structural opportunity and the most seasonal line. This is the reported "Home Entertainment" segment; it's more competitive and price-deflationary than appliances, but the premium OLED franchise protects margin.
The breadth advantage: because LG leads across four large, largely independent categories, a soft patch in one (a weak AC summer) can be cushioned by another (a strong festive TV/fridge season). That diversification — plus the premium halo in each category — is why the portfolio, taken as a whole, is more resilient than any single product line. The risk is that the leadership has to be defended everywhere at once, against different rivals in each aisle.
05

The leadership moat: brand, distribution & service

The single most important idea in the whole investment case — why LG's leadership is hard to dislodge.

The one thing to understand about LG India is why it leads — and why that lead is durable. It isn't one clever product; it's a three-part moat that took 25 years and huge spend to build: a brand Indians trust, a distribution network that puts LG on the shelf everywhere, and an after-sales-service network that makes buyers comfortable spending big and keeps them loyal. In Indian consumer durables, this trio — not features alone — is what wins and holds share.

Why the durables moat is real — the mechanism, in plain English

Buying a fridge or AC is a high-stakes, infrequent decision. It's expensive, you keep it for a decade, and if it breaks you need someone to fix it — fast. That psychology is what makes the moat. First, brand trust lowers the buyer's perceived risk, so LG becomes the safe default and can charge a premium. Second, distribution means that when a family in a Tier-2 town walks into the local appliance shop, an LG unit is there, in stock, with a salesperson who knows it — you cannot sell what isn't within reach. Third — and most under-appreciated — service. A vast network of 1,000+ centres and 13,000+ engineers means the buyer believes "if it breaks, LG will fix it," which is often the deciding factor at the point of sale, and which earns the next purchase (and the neighbour's). A challenger can build a better AC in a lab; it cannot quickly build 30,000 dealer relationships and a national service army. That's the moat — and it's why LG's leadership has proved sticky category after category.

Why a new entrant can't just buy in

  • Network economics compound. More units in the field justify denser service coverage, which builds more trust, which sells more units — a flywheel a small player can't spin.
  • Service is a cost and a weapon. Running 1,000+ centres is expensive, but it's precisely the expense rivals under-invest in — and the reason LG keeps repeat buyers. It's a moat you pay for.
  • Brand trust is earned over decades, not bought. LG's reputation for durable, reliable appliances is the accumulated result of 25 years of products that worked and service that showed up — not something a marketing budget replicates in a year.
  • The premium halo reinforces the whole thing. Dominating OLED TVs and front-load washers signals technology leadership that lifts the perception of the entire brand, right down to the mass models.
In Indian durables, the product gets you considered; the network gets you bought — and the service gets you bought again. LG owns all three.
06

The low-penetration & premiumisation supercycle

The core growth story — a runway most developed-market investors struggle to grasp.

Here is the fact that frames LG India's long-term case: most Indian homes still don't own the appliances LG sells. Air conditioners are in only ~7–12% of households, washing machines ~13%, and even refrigerators only ~33%. Compare that with near-saturation in developed markets (and even in China), and you see the runway: as incomes rise, electricity reaches more homes, and summers get hotter, tens of millions of first-time buyers will enter these categories — and then trade up to better models over time.

The two-part growth engine — new buyers, then better buyers

This story has two stacked tailwinds. Part one: first-time penetration. Every year, millions of Indian households cross the income threshold where a fridge, a washing machine, or (increasingly) an AC becomes affordable and desirable. Electrification, urbanisation, rising incomes, dual-income households and — for ACs — genuinely rising heat all push in the same direction. This is a volume story that can run for decades. Part two: premiumisation. Once a household owns a basic model, its next purchase trades up — from a semi-automatic to a fully-automatic washer, from a single-door to a side-by-side fridge, from a fixed-speed to an inverter AC, from an LED to an OLED TV. Trading up lifts the average selling price and, because premium models carry richer margins, lifts profit faster than volume alone. LG sits in the sweet spot of both: it's the trusted mass brand first-time buyers reach for, and the premium leader they trade up into. That's why the penetration supercycle is the heart of the bull case.

The catch: it's a runway, not a straight line

  • Seasonality & weather. The AC business — the lowest-penetration, highest-opportunity line — is also the most weather-dependent. A cool, early monsoon (as in FY26) can wipe out a summer's demand and dent a whole year, even as the multi-decade trend stays intact.
  • Affordability & the cycle. Big-ticket appliances are discretionary; when incomes wobble or inflation bites, purchases get deferred. The runway is structural, but any single year is cyclical.
  • Everyone can see it. The penetration story is well-known, so rivals — global and Chinese — are chasing the same buyers. LG's answer is the moat in §05: get to the new buyer first, through the widest network, and keep them with service.
07

Make-in-India, localisation & the Sri City bet

The margin-and-capacity story — building deeper in India, and building more.

LG India is not an importer; it is a manufacturer. It builds most of what it sells in India, at two long-standing plants — Greater Noida (UP) and Ranjangaon, near Pune (Maharashtra) — with deep local sourcing of components. That localisation lowers cost, shortens supply lines and cuts currency risk. The clearest growth lever now is capacity: a third plant at Sri City, Andhra Pradesh, a ~₹5,000 Cr greenfield investment funded entirely from internal cash.

The manufacturing footprint
PlantRoleWhy it matters
Greater Noida (UP)Corporate office + manufacturing (appliances, TVs)The mature northern base; high utilisation (~84% in Q1 FY26) keeps unit cost low.
Ranjangaon / Pune (Maharashtra)Refrigerators & appliances manufacturingThe western base — closer to western/southern demand and ports for logistics and export.

Why localisation and a new plant are both a margin story

Two things make this matter. First, localisation protects margin. The big cost inputs — steel, compressors, panels, plastics, copper — swing with commodity prices and the rupee (which is exactly what squeezed FY26). The more LG makes and sources inside India, the less exposed it is to import duties and dollar-priced components, and the more it benefits from Make-in-India incentives. Deeper localisation is, over time, a margin cushion. Second, the new plant is the classic operating-leverage story. At first, Sri City will hurt: it carries fixed costs (depreciation, staff, overhead) while it ramps and runs half-empty — a drag on margin. Then it helps: as the penetration runway fills it with volume, those fixed costs spread over more units and the plant turns profitable, adding both capacity and eventually margin. The investment question is simply how fast Sri City fills — a well-timed ramp into rising demand is highly accretive; a ramp into a soft year is a temporary margin anchor.

The export angle

  • India is increasingly a manufacturing and export base for LG's group — a low-cost, high-quality location from which to serve neighbouring and emerging markets. Sri City, with southern port access, is part of that logic.
  • Exports are a secondary engine today (LG India is primarily a domestic play), but a growing one — and, like Hyundai's export hub, a potential future hedge against domestic cyclicality and a way to keep new capacity full.
  • The bigger near-term prize is simply domestic capacity for the penetration runway: LG needs to be able to build enough units to serve the tens of millions of new buyers coming — and Sri City is how it gets there.
The funding signal: that LG is funding a ₹5,000 Cr plant entirely from internal accruals — no debt, no fresh equity — is itself a statement of the balance-sheet quality. It's a cash-generative leader reinvesting its own cash into the runway. The trade-off minorities feel: that cash is being ploughed into growth rather than paid out — LG paid no dividend in FY26 — so the return comes via reinvested growth, not yield.
08

The royalty & the 100%-offer-for-sale nuance

The uncomfortable part of owning a controlled subsidiary — and what to actually watch.

Because LGEIL is ~85%-owned by its Korean parent, the parent is both its controller and its counterparty. LGEIL pays LG Electronics Inc. a royalty of ~1.9% of sales for technology, platforms and the brand — a legitimate, universal practice for licensed subsidiaries (Maruti pays Suzuki, Hyundai India pays Hyundai Korea the same way). But it is also a direct transfer from the listed company to the controlling shareholder, and therefore the governance issue minority investors watch most closely.

What the IPO actually was — and why it matters

  • A 100% offer-for-sale. The Oct-2025 IPO raised ~₹11,607 Cr — but every rupee went to the parent, which sold ~15% of its stake. The company received no fresh capital. No new factory, no growth fund came from the listing — it was a liquidity event for Korea, not a fund-raise for LGEIL. (LG is funding Sri City from its own cash instead.)
  • What minority investors bought. A minority stake in an excellent, cash-generative, market-leading business — but one where they are along for the ride on a parent-set agenda, having effectively paid the parent a full price (~35× earnings at IPO) for the privilege.
  • The debut pop. The stock listed ~50% above the ₹1,140 issue price and the IPO was subscribed ~54× — a sign of strong appetite for a scarce, high-quality durables leader, but also of a full valuation from day one.

The royalty mechanics — the number to track

  • The royalty runs around ~1.9% of sales — lower than Maruti's (~3.5%) or the ceiling Hyundai's structure allows. It is charged on revenue, so it comes straight off profit.
  • Why that matters: on ~₹24,600 Cr of revenue, each 1 percentage-point of royalty is roughly ₹250 Cr of pre-tax profit — meaningful against ~₹1,685 Cr of PAT. A creeping royalty is the clearest way value could shift from minority holders to the parent over time.
  • Watch also the related-party lines: LGEIL buys components and technology from LG group entities, and pays for shared services. A note of comfort in FY26: LG concluded a nine-year Advance Pricing Agreement with India's tax authority (CBDT) covering FY14–FY23, which removes a chunk of contingent tax/royalty liability and reduces transfer-pricing uncertainty.
How to think about it without over- or under-reacting

Don't treat the royalty as a scandal — it's normal, it's relatively low, and it pays for real, valuable technology and brand. But do treat it as a permanent charge on minority returns that the controller sets. The healthy way to hold this: watch the royalty-as-%-of-sales line each year (does it creep up?), watch related-party purchases and fees (are components bought from the parent at fair prices?), and watch dividend policy (LGEIL paid nothing in FY26 — is cash reinvested for all shareholders' benefit, or effectively routed to the parent via fees?). If royalty stays ~1.9%, related-party terms stay fair, and reinvestment earns good returns, the alignment is working. If royalty creeps up while growth stalls, the minority case weakens. This line — more than any operating metric — is where governance risk lives for LGEIL.

The honest tension: the parent gives LGEIL enormous value (technology, compressors, OLED, the brand) — and takes value back through royalty and related-party dealings. Most of the time these net out to a well-run, well-supported leader. The risk is structural, not day-to-day: in a controlled subsidiary, the controller sets the terms, and Indian minority protections, while real, don't equalise the relationship. Price it in; don't ignore it.
09

The industry and the rivals

A big, growing, moderately-concentrated market where LG leads — but is attacked in every aisle.

India's home-appliances market is large (~$55bn in 2026), growing (~5–6% a year), and moderately concentrated among a handful of multinationals and domestic players. LG is the clear overall leader, but it faces a different strong rival in almost every category — and, in ACs especially, aggressive Indian and Chinese brands have been gaining. Understanding the competitive set explains both the strength and the pressure.

The competitive tension to watch

The theme is clear: LG's breadth is its shield, but it has to defend leadership on many fronts at once. In fridges and TVs the battle is with Samsung; in ACs it's a scrum with Voltas, Blue Star and Lloyd (where LG has ceded a little share); in mass appliances it's Whirlpool, Godrej and Haier; and Chinese value brands keep TV/AC prices under pressure. LG's defence has three parts: (1) the moat — brand, distribution and service that rivals can't quickly copy; (2) the premium halo — dominating OLED, front-load and side-by-side, where margins are richest and challengers are weakest; and (3) breadth — no single rival attacks it everywhere, so a loss in one aisle is cushioned by leadership in another. The multi-year question is whether LG can keep widening the premium gap faster than value rivals compress prices in the mass and AC segments.

How to think about the ~60× multiple

At ~₹1,06,000 Cr market cap on ~₹1,685 Cr of FY26 profit, LG trades around ~60× trailing earnings — a very full multiple, made to look worse because it's on a trough-ish year. Why does the market pay it? Because this is a debt-free, ~30%-ROCE, market-leading franchise with a genuine multi-decade penetration runway, a hard-to-replicate moat, and self-funded capacity growth — a scarce combination among listed Indian durables. Consumer-durables peers (Voltas, Whirlpool, Blue Star, Havells) themselves trade at rich multiples, so a leader with better fundamentals commands a premium. The bear counter: FY26 revenue was flat, profit fell 24%, the AC summer disappointed, no dividend was paid, and a 60× multiple on soft earnings leaves very little room if the recovery is slow — plus the growth from the IPO went to the parent, not the company. The reconciliation is that LG is high-quality but expensively priced for a cyclical year — the multiple is a bet that penetration, premiumisation and Sri City re-accelerate earnings from here. Track two numbers each quarter: revenue growth / AC-season demand (is the plateau broken?) and operating margin (is the commodity/rupee squeeze reversing?).

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely excellent franchise — bought at a full price, after a cyclical stumble.

LG India is one of the highest-quality franchises in Indian consumer durables: category leadership, a hard-to-copy brand-distribution-service moat, a multi-decade penetration runway, high returns and a net-cash balance sheet. The investment debate is about price and control, not business quality — can it re-accelerate off a soft FY26, and how much value does the parent take along the way, all at a ~60× multiple?

The bull case

  • Category leadership & moat — #1 across fridges, washers, panel TVs and inverter ACs, defended by an unmatched dealer & service network.
  • Penetration supercycle — ACs in ~7–12% of homes, washers ~13%; a multi-decade first-time-buyer runway, with LG the trusted default.
  • Premiumisation — dominant in OLED (~63%), front-load (~37%) and side-by-side (~43%), the richest-margin segments, as buyers trade up.
  • Self-funded growth — ₹5,000 Cr Sri City plant from internal cash; debt-free, ~32% ROCE, deepening localisation as a margin cushion.
  • Parent technology — world-class compressors, OLED, AI/IoT and the LG brand, without the R&D burden.

The bear case

  • Very full valuation — ~60× earnings on a soft year leaves almost no room for a slow recovery.
  • Cyclical & weather-hit — FY26 PAT −24%; a cool AC summer and dearer commodities/rupee squeezed margins from ~13% to ~10%.
  • AC-share slippage — ceded a little share (~18%→~17%) to Voltas, Blue Star and Lloyd in its highest-opportunity category.
  • No dividend — FY26 payout was nil; return depends on reinvested growth, not yield.
  • Parent control & royalty — ~85%-owned; ~1.9%-of-sales royalty and related-party dealings can shift value to Korea; the IPO raised nothing for the company.
The bottom line

LG India is a structurally advantaged, market-leading consumer-durables franchise with a genuine moat, a multi-decade penetration runway and a top-tier balance sheet — the business quality is not in doubt. But FY26 exposed the two live questions: it is cyclical and just had a soft, weather-and-commodity-hit year, and it is a controlled subsidiary whose parent both empowers and extracts, listed at a very full ~60× multiple that prices in a lot of the runway already. The next few years will be written in a handful of numbers each quarter — revenue growth and the AC season (is the FY26 plateau broken?), operating margin (is the commodity/rupee squeeze reversing toward the ~13% FY25 level?), and the royalty and dividend lines (does the parent keep the deal fair, and does cash start returning to minorities?). Get those right and a premium multiple on a debt-free, high-return leader riding a penetration supercycle is defensible; disappoint on the recovery, and a 60× multiple on a cyclical name has a long way to fall.
