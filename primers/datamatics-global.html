Datamatics Global, the BPM house building its own bots

A full walk through one of India's oldest listed IT names — a diversified mid-cap that runs other companies' back offices, writes their software, and increasingly sells them the automation robots that do the back-office work — how those three businesses fit together, why the company's own products and its higher-value digital work are the whole re-rating argument, and why the very GenAI wave that threatens a paperwork business might also be the thing that saves it.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Datamatics Global Services Limited is a Mumbai-headquartered, mid-cap information-technology and business-process-management (BPM) company — one of the oldest names in Indian IT, founded in 1975 by Dr. Lalit Kanodia, a computing pioneer who was part of the founding team of Tata Consultancy Services before he was thirty. It does three things that sit next to each other: it runs other companies' back offices (document processing, data management, finance-and-accounting, publishing operations), it builds and runs their software (application development, cloud, data and analytics, engineering), and — the part that makes it more than a services shop — it sells its own automation and AI products, a family of platforms all prefixed "Tru": TruBot (robotic process automation), TruCap+ (intelligent document processing), TruAI, TruBI, and a smart-city / automated-fare-collection systems line.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a diversified mid-cap, not a large-cap major and not a pure BPO. At ~₹1,987 Cr of FY26 revenue it is a fraction of a TCS or Infosys, and unlike a pure back-office outsourcer it earns across services and software products. Roughly half the business is digital operations / BPM, about a third is digital technologies / IT services, and the balance is a publishing-and-experience arm (Lumina Datamatics). The blend is the point: the higher-value digital work and the products are where the margin lives.
  • FY26 was defined by margin, not growth. Revenue grew a respectable ~15% (helped by two acquisitions), but the headline was profitability: EBITDA jumped ~62% to ~₹372 Cr and the operating margin hit 18.7% — the highest in the company's fifty-year history, up more than five percentage points in a single year. Reported PAT of ~₹194 Cr looks flat-to-down, but only because the prior year carried a large one-off gain; underlying operating profit rose sharply.
  • It is cash-rich and cheaply rated. The company carries ~₹639 Cr of net cash and investments, no meaningful debt, and trades on roughly 18× earnings — a modest multiple that reflects exactly what the market sees: a well-run, well-diversified mid-cap whose growth has been lumpy and whose BPM core faces an automation question the whole industry is wrestling with.
In plain terms

Datamatics earns its living in three overlapping ways. First, it does the unglamorous, high-volume paperwork that large Western enterprises would rather outsource — scanning, keying, reconciling, publishing — the BPM business. Second, it writes and maintains their software and moves them to the cloud — the IT-services business. Third, and most interestingly, it packages the automation it built to do its own paperwork faster and sells that as a product to everyone else — the RPA and AI platforms. The investment argument is a simple migration: the more revenue Datamatics can shift away from cheap, commoditised keystrokes and toward software licences and complex digital projects, the richer and steadier its earnings become. FY26 was the first year that migration showed up emphatically in the margin. The open question is whether it can keep going while the ground under the BPM business shifts.

01

IT & BPM 101: the words you need first

No jargon left unexplained — services versus products, BPM versus IT, and where the money differs.

To follow Datamatics you have to hold two distinctions in your head. The first is services versus products: a services company sells people's time (an engineer, a data-entry operator), so revenue scales with headcount and margins are capped by wages; a products company sells a licence to software it built once, so each extra sale costs it very little and margins can be far higher. The second is BPM versus IT services: BPM (business-process management) means running a business function for a client — their invoice processing, their catalogue, their claims — while IT services means building and maintaining the software and systems. Datamatics straddles all of it. Five terms carry the rest of this primer.

The one structural fact that shapes the whole company

The defining tension at Datamatics is that its biggest business — commoditised BPM — is also its most vulnerable and its lowest-margin, while its most valuable business — owned software products — is still its smallest. Everything management does is an attempt to tilt the mix: automate the BPM so it needs fewer people and earns more per person, push clients up into complex digital projects, and turn the automation tools into sellable products. A large-cap major like TCS has already largely made this journey and sells mostly high-value services; a pure BPO has not made it at all and lives entirely on cheap labour arbitrage. Datamatics is deliberately mid-transition — which is exactly why the direction of its margin, not its top-line growth, is the number that matters.

Who pays Datamatics, and for what

  • Large Western enterprises — banks and insurers (BFSI), publishers, retailers and manufacturers, mostly in the United States and Europe, who outsource back-office operations and IT projects. The US is comfortably the largest market, at roughly half of revenue.
  • Software buyers — enterprises that licence TruBot, TruCap+ or TruAI to automate their own processes, sometimes without any accompanying services engagement. A different, higher-margin kind of customer.
  • Governments and infrastructure operators — metro-rail authorities, tolling and smart-city projects that buy automated-fare-collection and electronic systems. A lumpy, project-based, largely Indian order book that behaves nothing like the rest of the company (section 07).
02

The Tru- stack: the products that change the argument

A services company's software layer — small in rupees, outsized in what it means.

Most Indian IT mid-caps are pure services businesses: they sell hours. What separates Datamatics from an eClerx or a WNS is that it has spent two decades building a stable of proprietary software products, nearly all branded "Tru", born out of automating its own operations and then commercialised for outside sale. In rupee terms this layer is still a minority of revenue — but it carries software-like economics, it is what a re-rating case rests on, and it is why the company can plausibly argue it is more than a body-shop.

The strategic logic is a loop. Datamatics runs enormous volumes of document and data work for clients; that work forces it to build automation to stay competitive; the automation, once built, is generalised into a product; the product is then sold to the wider market and turned back on Datamatics' own operations to cut their cost. Each turn of the loop should, in theory, do two things at once — lift the margin on the services it already runs, and add a slug of high-margin licence revenue on top.

The device that only fits Datamatics: the automation it sells is the automation it eats its own lunch with

Here is the construct you will not find in any other primer in this library. For a normal BPM firm, automation is a threat arriving from outside — someone else's software that makes the client stop paying for people. For Datamatics, the automation arriving to hollow out its BPM business is a product on its own price list. When TruBot automates a client's invoice processing, Datamatics loses the data-entry contract and books a software licence instead — and the licence, if it holds, is worth more per rupee and needs almost no people. The company is, uniquely, on both sides of its own disruption: the arsonist and the fire brigade for the same building. Whether that nets out to a gain depends entirely on whether the licence revenue it captures exceeds the services revenue it cannibalises — which is the real subject of section 06.

Three engines, one bill: the segment and geography mix

How the revenue actually splits — and why the balance is deliberate.

Datamatics reports its business in three engines. Understanding their relative size, and where in the world the money comes from, tells you most of what you need about the risk and the opportunity. The mix is not accidental — it is the visible result of a long push away from single-service dependence and toward a diversified, cross-sold enterprise relationship.

Why the diversification is both a strength and a hedge

A pure BPO living on one service line in one geography is a fragile thing — one automation shock or one client loss can gut it. Datamatics deliberately spreads itself: three service engines, several verticals (BFSI, publishing, retail, transit), and four broad geographies. No single engine can sink the company, and a weak patch in discretionary IT can be partly offset by steadier operations or publishing work. The cost of that spread is focus — Datamatics is a generalist mid-cap competing against sharper specialists in each of its lanes, which is part of why it trades cheaply. The bet is that the diversification plus the owned products together beat the specialists' focus over a full cycle.

04

The company, the group, and the Kanodias

A fifty-year-old founder-run house with an unusually deep computing pedigree.

Datamatics is a founder-controlled company with a genuinely storied origin. Its founder, Dr. Lalit Kanodia, earned a Ph.D. from MIT in the 1960s and was part of the founding team of what became Tata Consultancy Services — arguably the birth of the Indian IT industry — before setting up Datamatics in 1975. That heritage matters less as trivia than as culture: this is a technically-rooted, conservatively-financed, closely-held business, run today by the founder's son Rahul Kanodia as Vice-Chairman & CEO, with the family holding roughly two-thirds of the equity.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter / founder family: the Kanodia family holds roughly 66% (approx.) of the equity, making this a firmly founder-controlled company. High promoter ownership aligns the family with minority shareholders but leaves control uncontestable — investors should confirm the exact figure in the latest shareholding filing.
  • Group companies & subsidiaries: the business is run through operating subsidiaries, notably Lumina Datamatics (the publishing / Digital Experiences arm, in which there are minority interests — which is why reported PAT is struck after non-controlling interests and can look lower than the operating strength implies).
  • Balance-sheet character: conservative and cash-generative — ~₹639 Cr of net cash and investments, negligible debt, a steady dividend and a history of periodic capital returns. This is a company that funds its own growth and acquisitions from internal cash rather than leverage.
Why the structure matters: founder control and a fortress balance sheet give Datamatics patience — it can invest through a downturn and buy capability (Dextrada, TNQ) without straining. But it also means minorities are along for whatever ride the family chooses: capital allocation, the pace of the products push, and the treatment of subsidiary minorities all sit with a promoter holding two-thirds of the votes. The cash pile is a genuine asset; the question shareholders should keep asking is whether it is deployed for growth and returns or simply accumulated.
05

The margin escalator: FY26's real story

The single most important idea in the case — and it lives in the margin line, not the revenue line.

For most of its listed life Datamatics was a steady, low-teens-margin services business — respectable, unexciting, priced accordingly. FY26 broke that pattern in one line: the operating (EBITDA) margin vaulted from around 13% to 18.7%, the highest in the company's history, and EBITDA grew ~62% on revenue that grew only ~15%. When profit grows four times faster than sales, something structural is happening beneath the top line. That "something" is the whole investment thesis, and it is worth taking apart carefully.

Three flat years around 13%, then a ~540 basis-point jump to 18.7% in FY26. A step of that size in a single year is rare in services and is the crux of the whole primer: is it a new plateau or a one-year peak? Figures approximate, derived from reported results.

Where did the five-plus points come from? Three sources, in roughly descending order. First, operating leverage and cost discipline — as revenue grew on a controlled cost base, more of each incremental rupee dropped through; management ran a deliberate efficiency and pyramid programme. Second, automation of its own delivery — using TruBot and TruCap+ internally to do the same BPM work with fewer people, which is the products-loop paying off on the inside. Third, mix — a slow tilt toward higher-value digital work and platform-run operations. The relative weighting matters: leverage and cost can reverse if revenue softens, but automation and mix are more durable. Which one dominated FY26 is precisely what a careful reader should want to know.

The metric that settles this company: incremental EBITDA margin, held across quarters

At ~18× earnings on a cash-rich balance sheet, Datamatics is not priced for heroics — the case doesn't need a re-rating so much as it needs the FY26 margin to stick. So the number that proves or breaks it is the EBITDA margin, tracked quarter by quarter, read together with two companions: the share of revenue from products and higher-value digital work (is the rich end of the mix actually growing?) and revenue growth (because a margin won by shrinking headcount in a soft-demand year is fragile, while one won by mix and automation while growing is real). If the margin holds near the high-teens through FY27 as revenue grows, FY26 was a re-basing and the modest multiple is too low. If it slides back toward 13% the moment demand or the rupee turns, FY26 was a cost-cycle peak dressed up as a structural shift. The margin line is the scoreboard; watch whether growth is paying for it.

06

The GenAI question: cannibalise or accelerate

The force that could hollow out a paperwork business — or hand it its best decade.

No section matters more for a BPM-heavy firm in the mid-2020s than this one. Generative AI can read a document, extract its data, answer a query and draft a reply — which is to say it can do, cheaply and instantly, a large share of exactly the work Datamatics has sold by the human-hour for fifty years. For a pure back-office outsourcer, that is an existential threat. For Datamatics the picture is genuinely two-sided, and the resolution decides whether this is a value trap or a coiled spring.

The threat is real and specific. A meaningful slice of Digital Operations revenue is high-volume, rules-based document and data work — precisely the tasks GenAI and IDP automate first. If a client can run its invoice processing with an AI agent for a fraction of what it paid Datamatics to run it with people, that revenue does not shrink gently; it can disappear at renewal. The industry euphemism is "productivity"; the blunt version is that a chunk of the BPM book is automatable, and some of it will be automated whether Datamatics supplies the tool or a rival does.

The opportunity is equally concrete. Datamatics doesn't merely consume AI — it sells it, through TruBot and the FY26 extension of TruAI into agentic automation (AI that carries out multi-step tasks, not just answers questions) and vertical apps like TruAI Underwriting. Every process it automates for a client can, in principle, be re-captured as a software licence and an implementation project. And the same tools cut its own delivery cost — part of FY26's margin jump. The bullish reading is that a firm which already lives in its clients' document and data workflows is unusually well placed to be the one that automates them, keeping the relationship and improving its economics.

The surprise a generalist misses about a "boring BPM" stock

The intuitive verdict on a BPM-heavy company in the age of AI is "short it — the robots are coming for the data-entry desks." The counter-intuitive reality is that FY26, the first full year of the GenAI shock properly landing, was the most profitable year in Datamatics' history. That is not what a company being disrupted to death looks like. The reason is that early-stage automation tends to help the automator's margin before it dents its revenue: you cut delivery cost immediately, while contract cannibalisation shows up slowly, at renewals, over years. So the danger is precisely that the near-term looks great — margins up, everyone reassured — while the slow erosion of the underlying BPM book builds beneath it. The tell to watch is not the margin (which flatters early) but whether Digital Operations revenue keeps growing. If the automation is net-accretive, that line grows even as it automates; if it is net-cannibalising, that line quietly stops growing while the margin story papers over it.

How to actually judge it: ignore the slogans on both sides and watch two things over the next several quarters — (1) Digital Operations revenue growth (accretion vs erosion of the BPM core) and (2) any disclosure of product / platform revenue and TruBot–TruAI traction (is the licence line big enough to matter?). Until product revenue is disclosed as a real, growing number, the "we sell the disruption" story is a plausible strategy, not yet a proven offset. That gap between strategy and proof is a large part of why the stock is cheap.
07

The toll-gate niche: a lumpy government order book

The one business inside Datamatics that behaves like infrastructure, not IT.

Tucked inside a services-and-products IT company is a business that looks nothing like the rest of it: smart-city and automated-fare-collection (AFC) systems — the electronics and software behind metro-rail ticketing, tolling and transit access, sold under the TruFare banner. It is small, it is largely Indian, and it runs on a completely different clock: government tenders, multi-year project execution, and revenue that arrives in lumps rather than a smooth monthly stream.

The work is real and reference-worthy — Datamatics has supplied automated-fare-collection systems for Indian metro networks (including recent Mumbai Metro lines), where its systems handle ticketing and gate access for large urban transit projects. This is a credible, hard-to-fake capability: fare-collection systems are safety- and reliability-critical, tightly specified, and awarded to a short list of qualified vendors. Winning them is a moat of sorts. But the economics are the opposite of the software dream the rest of the company is chasing.

Why a first-timer should mentally separate this business out

When you read Datamatics' quarterly numbers, some of the choppiness comes from this niche, not from the core. A big AFC project milestone can flatter a quarter; a gap between awards can make growth look weak even when the services engines are humming. It brings order-book visibility (a bidded pipeline of government work) and diversification away from Western discretionary spend, which is valuable when US IT budgets freeze. But it also brings the classic hazards of government infrastructure work: tender delays, execution and payment timing, and low-margin competitive bidding. The right way to hold it is as a small, differentiated, counter-cyclical appendage — useful ballast, occasionally a swing factor in a single quarter, but not the engine that will re-rate the stock.

The strategic value of the AFC line is less about its size and more about what it signals: Datamatics can build and deploy mission-critical, product-led systems in the physical world, not just process paperwork. It is a proof point for the "we are a products company too" argument — even if, on its own, it will never move the group's needle the way TruBot adoption or a Digital Operations re-acceleration would.

08

The discretionary-spend cycle and the client mix

Why a US-heavy mid-cap's growth stutters when Western budgets tighten.

Datamatics is a small company selling to large ones, mostly in the United States and Europe, and much of what it sells — new IT projects, digital transformation, incremental automation — is discretionary: exactly the spending an enterprise defers first when it turns cautious. That makes the top line cyclical in a way the steady margin story can obscure, and FY24 is the cautionary example every investor should keep in view.

In FY24 and into FY25, as Western enterprises reacted to higher rates and macro uncertainty, discretionary technology budgets froze and large-deal decisions stalled. For a mid-cap without the scale, breadth or must-have vendor status of a large-cap major, that shows up quickly: organic growth slowed to a crawl, some quarters saw revenue barely move or dip year-on-year, and the company leaned on cost discipline and acquisitions to keep numbers up. It is the structural vulnerability of the model — a diversified mid-cap is more exposed to a client's "let's wait" than a TCS whose contracts are larger, stickier and more embedded.

The reading discipline this section demands

Because acquisitions have propped up headline growth through a soft organic patch, the sharper question at each result is not "did revenue grow?" but "did it grow organically, in constant currency?" A 15% headline can be a healthy organic number plus a favourable rupee, or it can be a flat organic business plus an acquisition and a currency tailwind — and those are two very different companies. Pair that with the section-06 test (is Digital Operations growing as it automates?) and you have the two questions that cut through the noise: how much of the growth is real and recurring, and how much of the margin is durable. FY26 looked strong on both counts at the headline; the durability is what FY27 will decide.

09

The industry and the rivals

A generalist mid-cap boxed in by sharper specialists on every side.

Datamatics competes in three different arenas at once, and in each it faces opponents that are more focused than it is. That is the double edge of being a diversified mid-cap: it is never the obvious specialist choice in any single lane, but it can cross-sell across lanes in a way a pure-play cannot. Understanding who it is up against — and how it is not a large-cap major and not a pure BPO — is essential to placing the stock.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things follow. First, Datamatics almost never wins a beauty parade on pure focus — a client wanting best-in-class BPM may prefer eClerx, one wanting best-in-class RPA may prefer UiPath. Its edge is being already inside the client, running their operations, and cross-selling the software and the digital projects off that beachhead. That is a real but unglamorous moat: incumbency and switching cost, not technological supremacy. Second, the products are what let it punch above a services mid-cap's weight — but they compete with far larger platforms, so their role is to enrich and defend the services relationship, not to out-engineer UiPath. Judge Datamatics as a diversified incumbent monetising its client access, not as a category leader in any one category.

Financials & valuation

A record margin year on a modest multiple — the debate is durability, not price.

FY26 was, operationally, the best year in Datamatics' history: revenue up ~15% to ~₹1,987 Cr, EBITDA up ~62% to ~₹372 Cr, and a record 18.7% margin. Yet the stock trades at only around 18× earnings on a balance sheet stuffed with net cash — cheaper than most mid-cap IT peers and far below the large caps. That gap is the whole valuation conversation: the market is pricing in doubt about whether FY26's profitability lasts and whether growth can reaccelerate, not extrapolating the good year.

A few balance-sheet points sharpen the picture. The company is net cash, so enterprise value is well below market cap and the operating business is cheaper than the P/E alone suggests. Return ratios are solid — ROCE ~21%, ROE ~16% — the signature of an asset-light services model that needs little capital to grow. Collections are clean (days-sales-outstanding around the low-60s). The one number that settles the thesis: durable EBITDA margin, earned while growing

Strip the case to a single test. At ~18× on net cash, Datamatics does not need a re-rating to reward an owner — it needs to demonstrate that FY26's 18.7% EBITDA margin is a floor it can hold while the top line grows organically, not a peak it reached by cutting into soft demand. So the number to track is the margin, quarter after quarter, cross-checked against organic constant-currency revenue growth and any disclosed product-revenue traction. Hold the high-teens margin with mid-teens organic growth and the stock is materially too cheap — a cash-rich compounder mis-filed as a value trap. Give the margin back to a demand or currency turn, or keep growing only by acquisition, and 18× is about right for a lumpy generalist. The margin proves the story; growth proves the margin isn't borrowed from the future.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cash-rich, diversified mid-cap having its best year — asked whether it can prove the margin is real.

Datamatics enters FY27 in unusually good operational shape: a record-margin year behind it, a fortress balance sheet, a widening product suite led into agentic AI, two acquisitions bedding in, and a founder family fully aligned by a two-thirds stake. What it has not yet done is prove that the FY26 margin is a plateau rather than a peak, or that it can grow organically while the GenAI wave reshapes its BPM core. Those two proofs are the entire debate.

The bull case

  • Record margin, modest price — 18.7% EBITDA margin and ~21% ROCE for ~18× on net cash; little optimism is in the price if the margin holds.
  • Owned products, not just hours — TruBot, TruCap+ and TruAI give real software optionality and a way to monetise, rather than merely suffer, the automation wave.
  • Automating itself — using its own tools to run BPM with fewer people is a durable, structural margin lever, not a one-off cut.
  • Fortress balance sheet — ~₹639 Cr net cash funds acquisitions, products R&D and returns without leverage; patience through downturns.
  • Diversification & incumbency — three engines, several verticals and four geographies, plus a counter-cyclical government AFC book, spread the risk and defend client access.

The bear case

  • GenAI cannibalisation — the automation that lifts near-term margin can quietly erode the BPM revenue base at renewals; the net effect is unproven.
  • Margin durability — a ~540 bps one-year jump partly rests on cost and leverage that could reverse if demand or the rupee turns.
  • Discretionary-spend cyclicality — US/Europe-heavy and mid-cap, so exposed to budget freezes and slow large-deal decisions, as FY24 showed.
  • Growth quality — headline growth flattered by acquisitions and currency; organic, constant-currency growth is the truer test and has been patchy.
  • Sharper specialists everywhere — out-focused by eClerx/WNS in BPM and by UiPath/Automation Anywhere in RPA; a generalist rarely wins on focus.
The bottom line

Datamatics is a fifty-year-old, founder-run IT-and-BPM house that just posted the most profitable year of its life while sitting on a pile of cash — and the market has responded with a shrug and an 18× multiple, because it cannot yet tell whether the margin is a new normal or a good cycle, or whether the AI wave will grow this business or slowly drain its oldest one. The company's answer is to be on both sides of its own disruption: sell the robots that automate the paperwork it used to do by hand. That is a genuinely clever position, but it is still a strategy awaiting proof in the numbers. The next test is concrete and datable: whether the FY27 quarters hold the high-teens EBITDA margin while Digital Operations revenue keeps growing and product traction is disclosed as a real number. Deliver both and the cheap multiple looks like a mistake; hold the margin only by shrinking the core, and FY26 will read as the year the good cycle and the cost-cutting happened to peak together.
