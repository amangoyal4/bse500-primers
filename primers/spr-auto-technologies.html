SPR Auto Technologies, the piston-maker racing away from the piston

A complete walk through India's leading maker of the internal-combustion engine's core wear parts — the piston, the piston ring and the engine valve — a company formerly called Shriram Pistons & Rings that, having built a fortress in the three components a pure electric car throws away entirely, is now spending its cash buying its way into a future that no longer needs them.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

SPR Auto Technologies — until 2026 known as Shriram Pistons & Rings — is the company that makes the parts inside an engine that move, rub and wear. Its three flagship products are the piston (the plunger that fires up and down inside each cylinder), the piston ring (the springy seals wrapped around that plunger), and the engine valve (the poppets that let fuel-air in and exhaust out). It is India's largest maker of all three, supplying nearly every domestic engine builder — two-wheelers, cars, trucks, tractors, gensets — as both an original-equipment (OEM) supplier and a huge aftermarket (replacement-parts) business, and it exports to more than 45 countries.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It makes the most EV-exposed parts in the entire auto-component universe. A battery-electric car has no combustion chamber — and therefore zero pistons, zero piston rings and zero engine valves. Almost every other auto-part category (brakes, bearings, wiring, seats, castings) survives electrification in some form; SPR's three crown jewels do not. That single sentence is why this company trades on a different question from its peers.
  • Its answer is diversification-by-acquisition — and it is moving fast. Rather than wait, SPR has been buying its way into powertrain-agnostic and EV components: EMFi (EV motors and controllers, Coimbatore), a 75% stake in Takahata Precision (Japanese precision plastic injection moulding), and interior-lighting and interior-solutions businesses. The result is stark: core-ICE parts fell from ~96% of revenue in FY24 to ~79% in FY26 — the company is deliberately, rapidly shrinking its own core.
  • The legacy business is a cash machine, not a melting ice cube — yet. FY26 revenue was ~₹4,571 Cr, EBITDA ~₹989 Cr (a healthy ~21.6% margin), and PAT ~₹561 Cr, on returns (RoCE/RoE) around 18–19%. A big chunk of it is the aftermarket: engines wear out and get re-fitted with new pistons and rings for decades, a replacement annuity that keeps paying long after new-ICE sales fade.
In plain terms

Every petrol or diesel engine on the road slowly destroys itself: the piston hammers up and down millions of times, the rings scrape the cylinder wall, the valves slam shut thousands of times a minute. SPR makes the parts that take that beating — and then sells them again as replacements when they wear. That is a lovely business while engines dominate the roads. The problem is written into physics: an electric motor has none of these parts. So SPR is running a race against its own product. On one side, the slow decline of the car engine (and the fast, cash-rich present of trucks, tractors and the replacement market). On the other, how quickly the EV-motor, precision-moulding and non-engine businesses it is buying can grow into the gap. The whole investment case is the distance between those two lines.

01

The engine's three hearts: piston, ring, valve

No mechanic's licence needed — just the three parts that decide the whole business.

To understand SPR you need to understand what an internal-combustion engine actually is: a set of metal cylinders in which tiny controlled explosions push pistons down, and those pushes turn a crankshaft that ultimately spins the wheels. Three consumable parts do the hardest work in that process — and SPR makes all three. They wear because they are the moving, sealing, breathing surfaces of a machine that runs hot, fast and forever.

The only three parts you need to know
PartWhat it does (and why it wears)
PistonThe metal plunger that slides up and down inside each cylinder. The explosion of fuel-air pushes it down; that motion is the engine's power. It runs at extreme heat and force, thousands of strokes a minute — so it is precision-machined aluminium alloy and it eventually fatigues. SPR's founding product.
Piston ringTwo or three springy metal bands wrapped in grooves around the piston. They seal the gap between piston and cylinder wall (so the explosion's force isn't lost) and scrape excess oil back down. They are in constant sliding friction against the cylinder — the classic wear part that gets replaced when an engine is rebuilt.
Engine valveThe mushroom-shaped poppets at the top of each cylinder that open to let fuel-air in and exhaust out, then snap shut. They open and close thousands of times a minute against hot gas — subject to heat, impact and corrosion, so they too are a specialist, wear-prone part.
Why these three parts are a real business, not a commodity

You might assume a piston is a lump of metal anyone can stamp out. The opposite is true. These parts operate at the outer edge of what materials can survive — hundreds of degrees, enormous pressure spikes, relentless friction — and if one fails, the engine seizes. That makes them metallurgy and precision-machining problems, not commodity castings: alloy chemistry, coatings, micron-level tolerances and decades of validated field data. An OEM certifies a piston supplier onto an engine platform and then rarely switches, because a warranty claim from a bad piston is catastrophic. Those high stakes are exactly why a handful of specialists — SPR chief among them in India — own the category, earn ~20%+ margins on it, and why the aftermarket keeps coming back to a trusted brand rather than the cheapest bidder.

Where these parts go

  • Two-wheelers — scooters and motorcycles, India's largest engine population by unit count. Small single-cylinder engines, enormous volumes.
  • Passenger vehicles — petrol and diesel cars and SUVs. The segment most directly in the path of electrification.
  • Commercial vehicles & tractors — trucks, buses and farm machinery, overwhelmingly diesel, and the engines that will electrify last.
  • Stationary & industrial engines — gensets, pumps, construction equipment — a diffuse, resilient, ICE-for-longer market.

SPR's revenue splits into a legacy engine-parts core that is large, profitable and cash-generative, and a growing ring of newer businesses deliberately chosen to be indifferent to what powers the vehicle. The whole strategic story is the second ring eating into the first.

The core: the three flagship parts

The heart of SPR remains pistons, piston rings and engine valves — the segment on which it built market leadership across two-wheelers, cars, commercial vehicles, tractors and stationary engines. This is where the ~20%+ EBITDA margins come from, and where decades of OEM approvals and the trusted aftermarket brand create a moat. It is also, precisely, the part of the business a battery-electric drivetrain renders obsolete.

The adjacent engine parts

Around the flagship trio, SPR makes other engine and vehicle components — cylinder liners, piston pins, connecting-rod-area parts, gaskets and filters. These broaden the content SPR can sell per engine, but they are still fundamentally combustion-engine parts, sharing the same long-term electrification exposure as the core.

The new ring: agnostic and EV parts

  • EV motors & controllers (EMFi) — a Coimbatore business making electric-vehicle traction motors and controllers: the powertrain of an EV, the exact opposite end of the technology from a piston. SPR's clearest statement that it intends to sell into electric vehicles, not just survive them.
  • Precision injection moulding (Takahata Precision) — a 75%-owned Japanese-heritage business making high-precision plastic moulded parts used across automotive electronics, connectors and non-engine applications. Powertrain-agnostic content that grows whether the car burns fuel or electrons.
  • Interior solutions & interior lighting — cabin components and lighting fitted to any vehicle. A car interior needs the same parts whether it is an EV or an ICE — genuinely indifferent to the drivetrain question.
Why the portfolio shape matters more than its size

For most auto-component companies you can track revenue growth and be roughly done. For SPR you have to track where the revenue sits on that left-to-right map, because the two ends have opposite futures. Revenue growing on the left (more pistons) is high-margin cash today but a shrinking terminal market; revenue growing on the right (motors, moulding, interiors) is lower-margin and unproven today but a market that survives. So the headline "revenue grew X%" tells you almost nothing about whether SPR is winning its actual race. The mix does — which is why the ~96%→~79% shift in core-ICE share over two years is the most important number the company reports.

03

The razor-blade aftermarket: the annuity that outlives new engines

The quiet, sticky, high-margin business that many investors underrate.

Alongside supplying engine makers on the assembly line (OEM), SPR runs a large aftermarket business: selling replacement pistons, rings and valves to mechanics, garages and distributors for engines already on the road. This is the razor-blade model of the engine world — and it has a crucial property. It keeps paying long after new petrol and diesel vehicles stop being sold.

The mechanism is simple but powerful. An engine is a wear machine: over its life its pistons and rings degrade, and at overhaul they are replaced. Every one of India's tens of millions of existing petrol, diesel, CNG, two-wheeler, truck and tractor engines is a future customer for a set of replacement parts — regardless of what new vehicles are being sold that year. So even in a hypothetical world where India stops selling new ICE cars tomorrow, the installed base of engines already running would demand replacement pistons and rings for fifteen to twenty more years as those vehicles live out their lives.

The surprise buried in the aftermarket

Here is the counter-intuitive part. The aftermarket is not just a nice add-on — it is a structural hedge against SPR's own worst-case scenario. New-ICE-vehicle sales and aftermarket demand are driven by different clocks: new sales track this year's showroom, while the aftermarket tracks the entire vehicle parc built over the last two decades. When new-ICE sales eventually roll over, the parc keeps growing for years afterward before it plateaus — and the replacement annuity keeps flowing off it. The aftermarket is also structurally higher-margin and brand-driven: a garage rebuilding an engine wants a trusted brand it won't get a comeback on, not the cheapest ring. So the part of SPR most people would assume is "old and declining" is in fact the most defensive, brand-protected cash it earns — and the last part of the ICE story to switch off.

04

The company, the group, and the name that changed everything

A Shriram-group institution that just rewrote its own identity.

SPR Auto Technologies is one of India's oldest auto-component names, part of the broader Shriram (DCM-lineage) industrial group based in the Delhi/NCR belt, with manufacturing anchored around Ghaziabad and Bhiwadi (Rajasthan). It has been making engine parts since the mid-1960s under technical collaborations with global specialists, and for most of that history it went by a name that told you exactly what it did: Shriram Pistons & Rings. In 2026 it deliberately stopped telling you that.

Why dropping "Pistons & Rings" from the name is a strategy, not cosmetics

Corporate rebrands are usually vanity. This one is a thesis. A name like "Shriram Pistons & Rings" is a promise to the market that you are, and will remain, an engine-parts company — the very thing an EV world makes obsolete. By amending its charter and calling itself SPR Auto Technologies, the company is formally declaring that pistons and rings are no longer the definition of what it is, only where it started. It is the clearest possible signal from management that they see the electrification cliff coming and intend to be something else by the time it arrives. The tell is that they changed the legal object clause of the company (its permitted scope of business) at the same time — you don't rewrite your charter for a logo.

Ownership & control

  • Promoters: the Shriram-group promoter family and associated entities — including the Deepak Shriram Family Benefit Trust and Sarva Commercial Pvt Ltd — hold roughly 43–44% of the company, with no promoter shares under encumbrance (pledge) as of FY26. The rest is held by institutions and public shareholders; track the exact split in the quarterly shareholding filings.
  • Balance sheet: historically conservative and cash-generative. To fund the acquisition drive and capex, the company has raised ~₹1,000 Cr of non-convertible debentures (NCDs) — modest, serviceable debt rather than a stretched balance sheet, backed by ~₹989 Cr of annual EBITDA and strong cash generation.
  • Management: a professional leadership team (CEO/MD Krishnakumar Srinivasan) running the operating business, with the promoter group setting the diversification strategy.

05

The electrification cliff — sharpest here of anywhere in auto parts

The single force that defines SPR's long-term terminal value — and it is unique to this company's product.

Every auto-component company faces some version of the EV question. Almost none faces it as sharply as SPR. The reason is not opinion or valuation — it is the parts list of an electric car. Run the test.

The three-part throwaway test

A pure battery-electric car contains no combustion chamber, so it uses zero pistons, zero piston rings and zero engine valves. This isn't a demand dip — for SPR's core parts, a battery EV is a customer that ceases to exist. Hybrids, notably, keep the engine and all three parts alive.

Compare that to the rest of the auto-component shelf. A braking-system maker still sells brakes to an EV. A wiring-harness maker sells more wiring to an EV. A bearings or castings or seating company adapts its parts to the new drivetrain. Even a gearbox maker finds some reduced role. SPR's three flagships are the rare category with a terminal value of zero in a pure EV — there is no adapted version of a piston for a car with no cylinders. That is what makes SPR's electrification exposure the sharpest single case in the sector, and why its terminal value is genuinely uncertain in a way a brakes company's is not.

The device that fits only SPR: the throwaway-parts race

Hold two lines in your head. Line A is SPR's ICE-core revenue — pistons, rings, valves plus adjacent engine parts — which faces eventual decline as cars electrify, but which fades slowly because of three cushions: hybrids (which keep the engine), the hard-to-electrify segments (trucks, tractors, gensets), and the aftermarket annuity off the existing parc. Line B is SPR's agnostic-and-EV revenue — EMFi motors, Takahata moulding, interiors — which starts small and lower-margin but grows in a market that survives. SPR's terminal value is entirely the area between these two lines: how gently Line A descends versus how steeply Line B climbs. No other primer's company runs exactly this race, because no other company makes the three specific parts an EV throws away and is buying the parts an EV keeps. Track the two lines and you are tracking the whole thesis.

A genuine surprise: the company that makes the single most electrification-threatened part in the entire vehicle — the piston — is not slowly declining into it. It is actively racing to become an EV-components company, having already bought an EV-motor maker. The maker of the part electrification kills is spending its piston profits to sell into electrification. That inversion is the story.
06

Buying its way out of the engine: the M&A pivot

How SPR is trying to out-run its own product — and the risk that carries.

Sansera and others in the sector are hedging electrification through organic routes — precision forging that serves any powertrain, aerospace, defence. SPR has chosen a distinctly different path: diversification by acquisition. Rather than build EV and agnostic capability from scratch, it is buying companies that already have it, and bolting them onto a cash-rich engine-parts base. That choice defines both the opportunity and the danger.

In two years the core-ICE share of revenue fell from ~96% to ~79% — meaning the newer, agnostic and EV businesses rose from ~4% to ~21% of the company. The rate of that shift, and whether it keeps compounding, is the clearest read on whether the pivot is real.

This is a genuinely aggressive re-shaping. A ~17-percentage-point swing in revenue mix in two years is not a garnish — it is management restructuring what the company is. And it is being funded from a position of strength: the piston business throws off enough cash (and the balance sheet enough NCD headroom) to buy the future rather than being forced to defend the past. That is the ideal way to run this transition — cannibalise yourself while you are still profitable, not after.

The catch every acquirer knows and hopes to dodge

Buying capability is faster than building it, but it is also where value quietly leaks. Each of these deals carries the standard M&A hazards: integration (a Japanese moulding business and a Coimbatore EV-motor start-up run nothing like a Ghaziabad piston line), returns (were the prices paid sensible, and can these businesses earn SPR's ~20% margins or will they dilute them?), and goodwill (paying above book value creates goodwill that must be justified by future profits, and written down if it isn't). The bull case assumes SPR is assembling a coherent, agnostic platform at fair prices. The bear case is that it is buying revenue to change its story faster than it can earn a return on what it bought. Both are live; only the segment-level margins over the next few years will settle which is true.

07

The long ICE tail: why the engine outlives the car engine

The segments that keep piston demand alive far longer than the passenger-car headlines suggest.

The electrification cliff (Section 05) is real, but it is not uniform. "The engine is dying" is a headline about passenger cars in developed markets. Peel that apart and India's engine-parts demand has a much longer tail than the doom framing implies — and SPR is heavily exposed to exactly the segments that electrify last.

The tail that flatters the terminal value

Put the pieces together and SPR's ICE-core is not one market with one death date — it is a stack of markets each on its own clock. Passenger cars go first; trucks, tractors and gensets go last, potentially decades later; and layered under all of them is the aftermarket parc, which keeps buying replacement pistons and rings for 15–20 years after each engine is sold. So even a world that stops making new ICE cars still needs pistons for its trucks, its tractors, its gensets and its enormous fleet of already-built vehicles. That is the cushion under Line A from Section 05 — the reason the core doesn't fall off a cliff but glides down a long slope, buying SPR the years it needs for the acquired businesses to grow up.

The point that reframes the fear: SPR's exposure to the fastest-electrifying segment (passenger cars) is real, but it is one line in a book that also includes the slowest-electrifying segments on the road. A piston-maker weighted to trucks, tractors, gensets and the aftermarket has a materially longer ICE runway than a piston-maker weighted only to city cars. Where SPR's volumes actually sit across these segments is, quietly, as important as the EV headlines.
08

Exports, the OEM roster, and the technology heritage

The customer relationships and know-how that make the moat real.

SPR's leadership isn't just a market-share statistic — it rests on two things that are hard to replicate: a deep, sticky roster of OEM approvals, and decades of accumulated metallurgical know-how built on global technical collaborations. Together they are why an OEM keeps buying SPR pistons rather than switching to a cheaper unknown.

The customers

SPR supplies pistons, rings and valves to a broad base of Indian engine and vehicle makers across two-wheelers, passenger vehicles, commercial vehicles, tractors and stationary engines, on the assembly line as an OEM supplier. Getting onto an engine platform is a multi-year validation process; once approved, a supplier tends to stay for the life of that engine, because re-validating a new piston vendor is costly and risky for the OEM. That approval stickiness is a core part of the moat — and it repeats across every new engine platform SPR wins.

Exports

SPR exports to more than 45 countries across five continents, from an operating base of roughly nine manufacturing units and several assembly plants. Exports do two useful things: they diversify demand beyond the Indian cycle, and — because many export markets have older, more ICE-heavy vehicle parcs — they extend the engine-parts and aftermarket runway geographically, selling into places where the piston has a longer life ahead of it.

The heritage that is genuinely hard to copy

SPR did not learn to make world-class pistons, rings and valves overnight. Its capability was built over decades through technical collaborations with global specialists — the kind of alloy chemistry, coating and precision-machining know-how that is embedded in people, processes and field data rather than in a single patent. That is why the piston-ring-valve category has stayed a specialists' club worldwide: the barrier isn't a machine you can buy, it's the accumulated engineering to make a part that survives millions of firing cycles without failing. SPR's real asset in its core business is that institutional metallurgical knowledge — and the irony of the pivot is that this deep engine expertise is exactly what an EV motor or a precision-moulded connector does not draw on, which is precisely why it had to buy those capabilities rather than build them.

09

The industry and the rivals

A specialists' category at home, global majors abroad, and a new set of EV-component competitors.

SPR competes on two fronts that used to be one. In its legacy core, it faces a small club of domestic and global engine-parts specialists. In its new ring, it steps into far more crowded arenas — EV motors and precision moulding — where it is a newcomer against established players. Winning the first while surviving the second is the competitive task.

The competitive asymmetry to hold in mind

Notice the reversal. In pistons, rings and valves, SPR is the incumbent everyone else has to dislodge — decades of approvals, brand and metallurgy on its side. In EV motors and precision moulding, SPR is the challenger trying to dislodge others — buying its way to a starting position, not a leading one. So the same company is simultaneously the fortress-holder in a shrinking market and the insurgent in a growing one. That is an unusual and demanding place to stand: it must defend a category the world is leaving while attacking categories where it has no incumbency advantage. The margin profile shows it — the core earns ~20%+, the new businesses start lower — and closing that gap as they scale is the operational heart of the pivot.

Financials & valuation

A profitable, cash-generative core — valued on a question about mix, not a question about this year.

SPR's reported numbers are those of a healthy, well-run auto-ancillary: steady growth, ~20%+ margins, high-teens returns and a conservative balance sheet. The valuation debate isn't about whether the business works today — it plainly does — but about what fraction of it still exists in fifteen years, and what the pivot is worth.

That middle ground is the whole point: the market is pricing SPR as a cash-generative engine-parts leader plus some credit for a credible diversification, and the debate is whether that credit is too much or too little.

The one number that settles the thesis: the non-ICE revenue share and its trajectory

For most auto-ancillaries you'd anchor on revenue growth or margins. For SPR, neither settles the case — a company can grow revenue smartly while its terminal market quietly shrinks underneath it. The metric that actually proves or breaks the thesis is the share of revenue coming from EV / powertrain-agnostic / diversified businesses, and the rate at which it is rising (from ~4% in FY24 to ~21% in FY26), read together with three supports: the EBITDA margin of that new revenue (is the ~21% blended margin holding as lower-margin businesses scale, or diluting?), the resilience of the aftermarket and CV/tractor/genset ICE base (how gently does Line A descend?), and the returns on the acquisitions (is bought revenue earning its keep?). If the non-ICE share keeps compounding toward a third, then a half, of the company at defensible margins, SPR re-rates from "piston-maker with an EV hope" to "diversified auto-tech platform." If the share stalls in the twenties and the margins dilute, it stays a high-quality but structurally-challenged engine-parts company. Total revenue is a distraction; the mix is the scoreboard.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A fortress in a shrinking market, buying a stake in the market that replaces it.

SPR enters its new identity from a position most challenged companies would envy: leadership in its core, ~20%+ margins, high-teens returns, a manageable balance sheet, a defensive aftermarket annuity, and a diversification programme already at ~21% of revenue. The candid debate is not whether the business is good — it is whether the pivot is fast and profitable enough to matter before the passenger-car engine fades.

The bull case

  • Cash-rich core — India #1 in pistons, rings and valves at ~20%+ margins and ~18–19% returns, funding the transition from strength rather than distress.
  • Real, fast mix shift — non-ICE revenue up from ~4% to ~21% in two years; the pivot is measurable, not a slogan.
  • Defensive aftermarket + long ICE tail — a brand-driven replacement annuity plus CV, tractor and genset demand that electrifies last, cushioning the decline for years.
  • Buying EV-relevance — EMFi motors and Takahata moulding turn an "EV victim" into an EV and agnostic supplier.
  • Clean control — committed Shriram-group promoters, no pledged shares, and a charter deliberately widened for the new strategy.

The bear case

  • Sharpest EV exposure in the sector — the flagship parts have zero place in a pure EV; the core's terminal value is genuinely uncertain, unlike most auto parts.
  • Acquisition-integration risk — a Japanese moulder and a start-up EV-motor maker are hard to integrate, may dilute margins, and create goodwill that must be earned back.
  • New-segment execution — SPR is a challenger, not an incumbent, in motors and moulding; scaling them profitably is unproven.
  • Cyclicality & concentration — auto demand is cyclical and OEM-concentrated; a downturn hits the core cash that funds the pivot.
  • Capex, working capital & competition — the transition needs capital and faces MAHLE, Tenneco and domestic rivals in the core and crowded fields in EV parts, at a valuation already crediting the pivot.
The bottom line

SPR Auto Technologies is a company doing the hardest, most honest thing a legacy business can do: using the profits of its most-threatened product to buy its way out of that product before it has to. The engine-parts core is not dying tomorrow — hybrids, trucks, tractors, gensets and a two-decade aftermarket parc give it a long, cash-rich glide path — but it is, unambiguously, a market the world is leaving, and SPR makes the very parts it leaves behind. Whether that becomes a story of graceful reinvention or of a fortress defending a shrinking kingdom will not be told by revenue growth or by any single quarter's margin. It will be told by one line on the results deck, printed each year: the share of revenue that no longer depends on a piston, and how fast it is climbing at what margin. Watch that percentage march from the twenties toward a third and beyond — that, not the name on the door, is what actually changed.
