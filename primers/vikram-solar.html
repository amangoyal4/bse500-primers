Vikram Solar, India's module giant chasing its own cells

A complete walk through one of India's largest solar-panel manufacturers — how a sheet of silicon becomes a power-generating module, why a wall of import duties and approved-list rules has turned "Made-in-India" into a pricing advantage, and why a company that tripled its profit in FY26 watched its freshly-listed shares get cut in half at the same time.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Vikram Solar makes solar panels — the flat, glass-fronted rectangles of blue-black cells that sit on rooftops and cover utility-scale solar farms, turning sunlight into electricity. It is one of India's largest crystalline-silicon PV module manufacturers, a Kolkata-headquartered maker that has been building panels since 2009 and now runs roughly 9.5 GW of module-assembly capacity across West Bengal and Tamil Nadu. Alongside the panels it runs an EPC and O&M arm that designs, builds and maintains solar projects. Its customers are Indian utilities, C&I (commercial & industrial) buyers, rooftop installers, and — historically — export markets led by the United States.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It makes the panel, but not yet the cell inside it. A module is an assembly of solar cells, and Vikram — like most Indian module makers — has until now imported those cells, mainly from China and South-East Asia, then laminated them into finished panels. The single biggest thing happening at this company is the race to build its own 9 GW of cell manufacturing by December 2026, closing the one gap that has capped its margins and its access to protected demand.
  • A wall of policy protects the domestic maker. India has deliberately fenced off its solar market — import duties on foreign cells and modules, an Approved List of Models & Manufacturers (ALMM) that only lets enlisted Indian panels into most government-backed projects, production-linked incentives (PLI), and domestic-content (DCR) rules. For a locally-manufactured maker like Vikram, that fence is the whole opportunity: it makes an Indian-made panel structurally preferred over a cheaper Chinese one.
  • Profit tripled — and the stock still halved. FY26 revenue rose ~40% to ₹4,802 Cr and net profit jumped roughly 3× to ₹470 Cr. Yet the shares, listed in August 2025 at ₹338, trade near ₹173 a year later — roughly half. The reason is not the results; it is that the two things the market had priced in — booming US exports and ever-rising module prices — both went into reverse at once.
In plain terms

Vikram Solar is a manufacturer, not a power producer. It does not own solar farms and sell electricity for twenty years the way an IPP does; it sells the equipment — the panels — and moves on, plus a project-services arm on the side. Its economics live and die on two spreads: the price it gets for a module versus the cost of the silicon cells it buys to make it, and the premium an Indian-made, ALMM-approved panel commands over an imported one. The bet driving the whole story is backward integration — building its own cell (and eventually wafer) capacity so it captures the value it currently pays away to Chinese suppliers. The catch is that it is spending heavily to do this into the teeth of a global panel glut that has crushed prices worldwide.

01

How a solar module is made — the value chain in five words

No physics needed — just where Vikram sits, and where the money leaks out.

A solar panel is not a single thing you mine or refine; it is the last step of a long silicon supply chain. Understanding where Vikram Solar stands in that chain — and where it does not yet stand — explains almost everything about its margins, its risks, and its ₹1,000-crore-plus capex plans. The chain runs in five stages, and value (and profit) is concentrated at the middle steps, not the last one.

Vikram lives almost entirely at Stage 4 today, buying Stages 1–3 from abroad. The strategic push is upstream — into cells (Stage 3) and eventually wafers (Stage 2) — because that is where the margin and the "how green is it, really" question sit.

The tell is this: a finished module's price is dominated by the cost of the cell inside it. A module maker who buys cells is, in effect, a laminator and brander — it adds glass, frame, junction box, warranty and quality control, but the core value-adding chemistry happened one step upstream, in someone else's factory. That is why the whole Indian module industry is scrambling upstream into cells: staying a pure module assembler means forever renting your margin from a Chinese cell supplier.

The only terms you need

The economics that decide everything: the cell-to-module spread

Picture the profit in a panel as a thin slice sitting on top of a bought-in cost. When Vikram buys imported cells cheaply and module prices are firm, that slice is fat and it earns well. When Chinese cell prices spike, or module prices collapse, the slice is squeezed from both sides — and a module assembler has almost no shelter, because its biggest cost is a commodity it doesn't control. The entire logic of building 9 GW of its own cells is to convert that rented, volatile slice into an owned, structural one: make the cell yourself, and the margin the Chinese supplier used to keep becomes yours. This one spread — module selling price minus in-house cell cost — is the number that will define Vikram's profitability for the next five years.

02

What Vikram Solar actually sells

A panel factory with a projects business bolted on — read this once and the company clicks into place.

At heart, Vikram Solar is a volume manufacturer: it makes solar panels and sells them by the megawatt to whoever is building a solar project. But the same panel serves several very different buyers, under very different rules, and that mix — not the panel itself — is what an analyst watches. There are two businesses under the roof.

The two revenue lines, decoded

  • Module manufacturing — the overwhelming majority of revenue. Vikram assembles crystalline-silicon panels (Mono PERC and, increasingly, high-efficiency N-type TOPCon) at plants in Falta (West Bengal) and Oragadam and Vallam (Tamil Nadu), with the Tamil Nadu build-out being the newer, larger, TOPCon-era capacity. FY26 module sales were ~3,342 MW, up 76% on the year, on production of ~3,220 MW. This is the engine.
  • EPC & O&M services — the smaller arm that builds solar projects end-to-end (engineering, procurement, construction) and then maintains them. It captures more of the project value and can pull through module sales, but it is lumpier and lower-margin than a good panel order, and Vikram has deliberately kept it a supporting act rather than the main business.

The crucial split is not manufacturing-versus-services, though — it is which policy bucket a panel falls into. The same physical module can be sold three ways, and each carries a different price and a different eligibility rule:

One panel, three markets — the mix that sets the price

Non-DCR / ALMM domesticUtility-scale & C&I developersMust use an ALMM-listed module (imported cells allowed). The bread-and-butter Indian market; protected from Chinese finished panels.

Why the channel mix matters more than the tonnage

Two panels can roll off the same line and earn wildly different money depending on where they go. A DCR panel sold into a subsidised rooftop scheme commands a premium precisely because so few makers can supply fully-domestic cells-and-modules. An export panel to a US IPP once earned a fat margin because American buyers were desperate for non-Chinese supply. And a plain ALMM domestic panel earns the steady middle. So when you read that Vikram "sold 3,342 MW," the real question is the blend: how much rode the DCR premium, how much was ordinary domestic, and how much was export — because that blend, far more than the raw megawatts, drove the tripling of profit in FY26.

03

The company, the family, and a very fresh listing

A Kolkata promoter family that IPO'd into the top of the solar cycle.

Vikram Solar is a family-promoted manufacturer, built and controlled by the Chaudhary family of Kolkata, with Gyanesh Chaudhary as its long-time driving force (Vice Chairman & Managing Director). It is a genuinely home-grown industrial story — one of the earliest Indian companies to commit seriously to crystalline-silicon module manufacturing — that spent fifteen years private before a headline-grabbing August 2025 IPO.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoters: the Chaudhary family and associated entities hold roughly 63% of the company post-IPO — a firmly promoter-controlled business. A meaningful block of promoter shares had been pledged against project financing; the company has been unpledging shares (a ~26% tranche was released) alongside arranging a large (~₹3,200 Cr) credit facility to fund the capex. Track pledge levels directly in the shareholding filings.
  • Public float: retail and institutional investors hold the rest, with FIIs a low-single-digit percentage and domestic institutions only just beginning to build positions in a stock barely a year old.
  • What the IPO was for: the fresh-issue proceeds are earmarked squarely at the capacity build-out — the new Tamil Nadu module lines and, above all, the cell manufacturing facility. This is a company raising public money to fund a specific, capital-heavy industrial expansion, not to cash out a mature franchise.
A timing point worth sitting with: Vikram listed in August 2025, near the peak of enthusiasm for Indian solar manufacturing — right as US tariff politics and a global panel glut were about to turn. It raised its expansion capital at a good price for the company; public shareholders who bought the listing, by contrast, caught the top of the cycle. That mismatch — great capital-raise timing for the firm, poor entry timing for the new investor — is the backdrop to everything in the valuation section.
04

The policy fortress: why "Made in India" is a price, not a slogan

The single biggest reason a domestic module maker earns more than a Chinese one — decoded.

Left to a free market, Chinese solar panels — made at colossal scale and often below cost — would flood India and bury local makers. They largely did, a decade ago. What has changed is that India has built a deliberate, multi-layer protective wall around domestic manufacturing, and Vikram Solar's entire profitability rests on sitting inside that wall. This is not background policy colour; it is the business model.

The four walls protecting the Indian module maker
WallWhat it isWhy it helps Vikram
ALMMThe "Approved List of Models & Manufacturers" — only enlisted (largely Indian) modules can be used in most government-linked and utility projects.Structurally shuts cheaper Chinese finished panels out of the biggest slice of Indian demand. Vikram, as an enlisted maker, competes only with other Indian makers, not the world.
Import duties (BCD)Basic Customs Duty on imported modules (~40%) and cells (~25%), making foreign panels dearer at the border.Widens the price umbrella under which a domestically-made panel can sell profitably — and, tellingly, taxes imported cells too, nudging makers to produce cells at home.
PLI incentivesProduction-Linked Incentive subsidies for building integrated cell-and-module capacity in India.Directly subsidises the very backward-integration (into cells) that Vikram is undertaking, improving the returns on its capex.
DCR rules"Domestic Content Requirement" — flagship schemes (rooftop, farmer solar pumps) must use Indian cells and modules.Creates a premium sub-market only fully-domestic makers can serve. Management expects the DCR market to reach ~20–25 GW in FY27 — a big prize for whoever has home-made cells.

Notice how the four walls interlock. Duties and ALMM keep foreign panels out, so an Indian module maker can charge more. But the newest, sharpest tool — the extension of ALMM-style thinking and DCR to cells — targets the one thing Indian makers still import. Policy is deliberately herding the industry upstream: it is no longer enough to laminate imported cells into an Indian panel; to capture the richest, protected demand, you increasingly need to make the cell in India too. That is the government essentially writing Vikram's capex plan for it.

The insight most first-time readers miss

The protective wall is a double-edged gift. On one side, it is the reason a Vikram panel earns a premium a Chinese panel never could inside India — remove ALMM and the duties tomorrow and the domestic industry's margins would collapse overnight. On the other, that same wall means the demand is policy-created, and therefore policy-revocable. A future government that decides cheap solar matters more than domestic manufacturing could soften ALMM or cut duties, and the umbrella folds. So a large part of owning Vikram is a bet that India stays committed to building panels at home — a political judgement as much as an industrial one. The company's best defence against that risk is precisely to integrate into cells fast, so that even in a freer market it competes on real cost, not just protection.

05

The backward-integration bet — three different capacity numbers

The most important idea in the whole investment case, and it is specific to this company.

Every solar manufacturer quotes a capacity in gigawatts, and investors treat it like a single figure. For Vikram Solar, it is three figures — and the gaps between them are the entire thesis. There is the module nameplate (how many panels it can assemble), the ALMM-enlisted capacity (how much of that nameplate is actually cleared to chase protected demand), and the cell capacity (how much of the value inside those panels it makes itself). Today those numbers are miles apart. Watch how far.

Vikram can assemble ~9.5 GW of panels, but only ~2.85 GW was ALMM-enlisted (mid-2025) and it makes essentially none of its own cells today. The plan takes modules to ~15.5 GW and cells from zero to 9 GW by December 2026 — closing all three gaps at once.

This is the company in one picture. A big module nameplate is a promise of what it could ship. ALMM-enlisted capacity is what it is actually allowed to ship into the protected market — and that number has lagged the nameplate, because enlistment is a per-line, per-product approval process that takes time. And cell capacity of near-zero means that, for now, the most valuable part of every panel is bought from abroad. The FY26 profit surge happened despite all three gaps; the bet is that closing them turns a good manufacturer into a structurally advantaged one.

The device that fits only Vikram: nameplate GW → ALMM GW → cell GW

Most manufacturers you can size up with one capacity figure. Vikram needs three, and the distance between them is where the money is. If the December-2026 build-out lands — modules toward 15.5 GW, ALMM enlistment catching up, and 9 GW of home-made cells switching on — then Vikram stops renting its margin from a Chinese cell supplier and starts owning it, while unlocking the premium DCR demand it currently can't fully serve. If the cell lines slip, or ramp slowly, or arrive just as panel prices fall further, the company will have spent thousands of crores to run harder and stay roughly in place. The nameplate tells you the ambition; only commissioned cell GW and ALMM-enlisted GW tell you whether the ambition became an advantage.

06

PERC → TOPCon: the technology switch under the glass

Why a percentage point of efficiency reshapes the whole product line.

Solar cells are not static. The industry is mid-migration from the old Mono PERC cell to a newer, higher-efficiency N-type TOPCon design — and getting this transition right is as important to a module maker as the shift to bigger machines was for a wind company. Vikram has positioned itself as an early adopter of the newer technology, and its whole cell investment is being built for TOPCon, not the outgoing standard.

A few points of cell efficiency compound into meaningfully more watts per panel — a modern TOPCon module rates 590 Wp+ versus a lower-rated PERC one. More watts from the same glass area means lower cost per unit of electricity, which is what wins a competitive tender.

The reason efficiency matters so much is leverage on everything else. A higher-wattage panel means fewer panels, fewer mounting structures, less land, less wiring and less labour to build the same-sized project. So a developer will pay a premium for the more efficient module because it lowers the total project cost, not just the panel bill. For Vikram, moving its line to TOPCon lifts both the price it can charge per panel and — once its own TOPCon cells are running — the margin it keeps. It also future-proofs the huge cell investment: building a 9 GW cell plant for the outgoing PERC technology would be building tomorrow's obsolescence.

The risk hiding inside the upgrade

Technology leadership in solar is a treadmill, not a destination. TOPCon is today's frontier, but the industry is already talking about the next steps — heterojunction (HJT) and eventually tandem/perovskite cells. A manufacturer that pours capital into a specific cell technology is making a timing bet: that the plant will run long enough at good utilisation to earn its cost back before the frontier moves again. Vikram is spending heavily to catch up to a technology the Chinese majors have already mass-produced. Being an "early adopter" in India is not the same as being at the global frontier — and the gap between those two is a real, if unglamorous, risk sitting inside the otherwise upbeat efficiency story.

07

The order book and the export shock

A record backlog — and a rich export channel that quietly went dark.

Two facts about Vikram's demand sit in tension, and holding both at once is the key to understanding the stock. On one hand, the order book is at a record 8.2 GW — years of production already booked, heavy on domestic buyers. On the other, the channel that used to be the most profitable — exports to the United States — has, in management's own framing, thinned to almost nothing. The domestic book is booming while a premium export river ran dry, and the market has been pricing the loss more loudly than the gain.

An 8.2 GW order book is roughly two-and-a-half years of FY26 shipments already sold. FY26 module sales jumped ~76% year-on-year to 3,342 MW. The demand, on paper, is not the problem — the mix and margin of that demand is.

Here is what changed. For a couple of years, the US was scrambling for solar panels made anywhere but China — it had slapped anti-dumping and countervailing duties on Chinese and South-East-Asian modules, and Indian-made panels were suddenly a prized, high-margin alternative. Vikram exported into exactly that gap. Then the door swung shut: shifting US trade policy, new tariff lines that caught Indian supply too, and a domestic-manufacturing push in the US itself. In management's telling, exports from India to the US have "slimmed down to an almost negligible level." A channel that had been a profit centre effectively closed.

The genuine surprise: Vikram Solar tripled its net profit in FY26 — and its shares still roughly halved from the August-2025 listing, from ₹338 to about ₹173. That is not the market ignoring good results; it is the market re-pricing the quality of those results. The high-margin US export tailwind that helped justify the IPO valuation reversed, global panel prices fell, and investors marked down the multiple faster than the profits rose. A stock can fall by half in the same year its earnings triple — Vikram is a live case study in why the durability of a profit matters more than its size.
Why the domestic pivot is the real story now

With the US channel largely gone, Vikram's growth has to come from home — and that is where the policy fortress and the DCR premium (§04) come back in. The 8.2 GW order book is overwhelmingly domestic: utility-scale developers, C&I buyers, and increasingly the DCR-linked rooftop and farmer-solar schemes. The company is effectively swapping a volatile, politics-driven export margin for a protected, policy-driven domestic one — and betting that its cell investment lets it serve the richest domestic segment. Whether that swap is an upgrade or a downgrade depends entirely on how firm domestic pricing stays as every Indian maker races to add the same capacity at the same time.

08

The oversupply trap: a global glut Vikram can't opt out of

The one force that can undo everything — and it is entirely outside the company's control.

For all the domestic protection, there is a global reality Vikram cannot legislate away: the world is drowning in solar-manufacturing capacity, overwhelmingly Chinese, and that glut has crushed panel and cell prices to levels that have pushed even large Chinese manufacturers into losses. India's wall keeps the finished Chinese panels out — but it cannot keep the price signal out, and it cannot change the fact that the cells Vikram buys are priced off a brutally oversupplied global market.

Global module prices fell by roughly two-thirds in three years as Chinese overcapacity flooded the world (figures illustrative, $/W). India's duties cushion domestic panel prices, but Vikram's imported cell costs and its export competitiveness both track this global collapse.

This is the shadow over the whole growth story. The Indian market is protected, but Vikram is not sealed off:

  • Input dependence, both ways. Until its own cells switch on, Vikram buys cells from the same glutted global market — sometimes a blessing (cheap inputs fatten the module margin), sometimes a curse (a supply squeeze or an anti-dumping action on imported cells can spike costs and starve the lines).
  • The glut is coming home. Every Indian maker — Waaree, Premier Energies, Adani, Tata Power Solar and a dozen others — is adding module and cell capacity at once, chasing the same protected demand. India could manufacture its own domestic oversupply within a few years, compressing the very premium that makes the business attractive today.
  • Cyclicality is structural. Panel prices, cell prices and margins swing hard with the global cycle. FY26's fat profit came in a particular window of firm domestic prices and cheap imported cells; that window is not guaranteed to stay open.
The uncomfortable arithmetic of backward integration in a glut

Building your own cell capacity is the right long-run move — but the timing is fraught. Vikram is spending thousands of crores to make cells at the exact moment global cell prices are on the floor. If it can make cells at home for less than it now pays to import them, integration is pure margin gain. If domestic cell costs land above the rock-bottom import price — quite possible early in a ramp, at sub-scale utilisation — then for a while the company will be making something it could have bought more cheaply, and only the policy premium and duty on imported cells make the maths work. The wager is that duties, DCR rules and scale eventually tilt the arithmetic in Vikram's favour. That is a reasonable bet, but it is a bet, and the glut is the reason it isn't a certainty.

The valuation shape is unusual and worth naming plainly. After the fall, Vikram trades at a trailing P/E in the high teens — cheap-looking for a company that just grew earnings threefold. But the market is doing forward maths, not backward maths: it suspects FY26's margin was a peak (cheap imported cells, firm domestic prices, a not-yet-collapsed export tail), and that the coming years bring a heavy capex bill, ramp costs on new cell lines, and possible domestic oversupply. A low trailing multiple on a suspected-peak earnings number is not the bargain it first appears — which is exactly the tension the stock price reflects.

The one number that settles the thesis: gross margin per watt once cells come in-house

Strip away the noise and Vikram's case rests on a single measurable spread: the module selling price minus the fully-loaded cost of a cell it makes itself — read alongside the share of shipments backed by captive (in-house) cells and its ALMM-enlisted capacity. If, through FY27, the 9 GW cell plant commissions on schedule and Vikram's own cells cost less than the imports they replace, that spread widens structurally and today's high-teens multiple on "peak" earnings turns out to have been on a base. If the cell ramp slips or domestic prices sag as the whole industry adds capacity, the spread compresses and the earnings that looked cheap prove fragile. Everything else — the order book, the technology, the policy wall — feeds into that one spread. Watch captive-cell share and gross margin per watt each quarter; they are the scoreboard.

10

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, fast-integrating Indian field racing the same direction at once.

Indian solar manufacturing is no longer a cottage industry — it is a capital-arms-race between a handful of scaled players, all adding module and cell capacity as fast as capital and policy allow. Vikram Solar is a leading name, but not the largest, and the competitive question is less "can it win orders?" than "will everyone's simultaneous expansion compete away the premium?"

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, on the metric that matters most — in-house cells — Vikram is a catch-up player, not a leader; rivals like Premier Energies built cell capability earlier, and Waaree brings sheer scale. Vikram's ~9.5 GW of modules is real, but its zero-to-9-GW cell journey is one several peers are further along. Second, and more important, this is a rising-tide-then-flood dynamic: a protected, growing Indian market is lifting every maker today, which flatters everyone's numbers — but the same players are collectively building enough capacity to manufacture a domestic glut within a few years. The prize is being a low-cost, fully-integrated survivor when the tide of protected-but-abundant supply eventually laps over the premium. Scale and cell-cost, not brand, decide who that is.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A real manufacturer, a real policy tailwind, a real capex gamble — priced by a market that has already blinked.

Vikram Solar enters FY27 as a scaled, profitable module maker with a record order book, a supportive policy wall, and a clear, capital-heavy plan to integrate upstream into cells and wafers. It also enters it as a stock the market has already marked down by half, distrustful that FY26's fat margin can last. The debate is no longer whether the business is real — it plainly is — but whether backward integration lands before the cycle and the crowd compete the premium away.

The bull case

  • Policy fortress — ALMM, import duties, PLI and DCR structurally favour domestic makers; management sees a 20–25 GW DCR market in FY27.
  • Backward integration — 9 GW of cells by Dec-2026 (then wafers) would convert a rented margin into an owned one and unlock the premium DCR segment.
  • Record demand — an 8.2 GW order book, ~2.5 years of shipments, heavy on protected domestic buyers.
  • Technology & brand — an early TOPCon shift and a long-established, trusted panel brand across 39 countries.
  • Re-rated entry — after a ~50% fall, a high-teens trailing P/E on a fast-growing manufacturer, if FY26 proves a base not a peak.

The bear case

  • Peak-margin risk — FY26's profit leaned on cheap imported cells and firm prices; the market suspects it de-rates from here.
  • Global glut — Chinese overcapacity has crushed module and cell prices worldwide and is pushing the price signal into India.
  • Domestic oversupply — Waaree, Premier, Adani, Tata and a PLI tail are all adding capacity at once, threatening the very premium.
  • Execution & capex — thousands of crores staked on cell/wafer plants ramping on time and below import cost, into a glut; cells are Vikram's weakest link versus peers.
  • Policy & export dependence — the moat is government-granted and revocable; the once-rich US export channel has already gone dark.
The bottom line

Vikram Solar spent fifteen years as one of India's earliest serious panel makers, and FY26 was its loudest year yet — revenue up 40%, profit up threefold. But the market's verdict, a halving of the share price in the same twelve months, carries its own hard message: a module assembler's profit is only as durable as the spread between what it sells a panel for and what it pays for the cell inside. The next chapter is being built right now in Tamil Nadu, and it resolves into one checkable event — whether the 9 GW of cell capacity commissions on schedule by December 2026 and runs at a cost below the imports it replaces. Hit that, and Vikram becomes a genuinely integrated manufacturer that owns its margin; miss it, and it stays a well-run laminator renting profit from the same Chinese cell market that just halved its stock. Watch the cell lines switch on; that is where this company's next few years are decided.
