00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Gillette India makes and sells the things men (and increasingly women) use to remove hair and clean their teeth: razors, blades and shaving gels under the Gillette, Mach3, Fusion, Guard, Vector, Venus and Wilkinson Sword names, plus oral care under Oral-B and portable-power appliances under Braun. It is the listed Indian subsidiary of Procter & Gamble (P&G), the American consumer-goods giant, which owns 75% of the shares.

Three facts frame the whole business:

  • It is the archetype of the razor-and-blades model. Gillette sells the razor handle cheaply — sometimes at a loss — to hook a customer, then earns a high-margin, repeat, near-permanent income on the blade refills for years afterward. That model is literally named after this company. In India it commands roughly 45–50% of the organised razor-and-blade market and ~60–70% of cartridge razors.
  • The economics are almost freakishly good. FY26 (year ended March 2026) revenue was ~₹3,100 Cr, up 8%; net profit ~₹654 Cr, up 23%; operating margin ~30%. But the number that stops you is the ~90% return on capital employed and ~66% return on equity — a business that mints profit on a whisper of invested capital. It carries no meaningful debt and pays most earnings out as dividend.
  • The debate is growth and the shaving shift, not quality. The category is mature-ish, volume growth is modest, and a real structural change is underway — young Indian men are drifting from the wet blade toward the electric trimmer and stubble look, where Gillette is weaker. The stock, on ~38× earnings, has already slid from a 52-week high of ₹11,500 to ~₹7,600.
In plain terms

Gillette India is a toll booth on the Indian chin. Every few weeks, tens of millions of men pay a small, almost invisible sum for a fresh blade — and Gillette collects it, again and again, on a product that costs a few rupees to make. P&G supplies the brand, the global research and the manufacturing know-how; the Indian company supplies the factory, the salesforce and the shelf. The result is one of the most profitable consumer franchises in the country — but its core ritual, the morning wet shave, is exactly the habit that a new generation is quietly abandoning.

01

Razor-and-blades 101: the model named after this company

The single idea that explains why Gillette earns what it earns — with the maths worked out.

In business schools, the "razor-and-blades" strategy is a named category: give away (or heavily subsidise) the durable part of a product, then earn your money on the consumable refill that only works with it. Printers and ink cartridges, game consoles and games, coffee machines and pods — they all copy it. The original, the one the model is named after, is King Camp Gillette's safety razor of 1901. This company is that idea, listed on an Indian exchange.

The mechanism, step by step

  • Sell the handle cheap. The razor handle is the customer-acquisition cost. Gillette prices it low — even below cost on the mass end — because its only job is to install a proprietary blade-fitting in a customer's bathroom.
  • Lock in the refill. A Mach3 handle only takes Mach3 cartridges; a Guard handle only takes Guard blades. Once the handle is on the shelf at home, the customer is committed to your refills — not a rival's.
  • Collect the annuity. Blades wear out. The customer comes back every few weeks, for years, buying a high-margin consumable. That repeat purchase — predictable, frequent, sticky — is the actual product.
The Gillette Guard annuity — worked out in rupees

Consider Gillette Guard, the razor P&G designed specifically for India in 2010: the handle retailed for about ₹15 and each blade refill for about ₹5. Now do the arithmetic that a rival can't wish away. An Indian man who shaves a few times a week gets perhaps a month out of a blade — call it ~10–12 refills a year. Over a 30-year shaving life that is 300-plus refills. So the ₹15 handle is a one-time coupon; the ~₹5 blade, bought roughly monthly, is a rupee annuity that runs for decades — and it repeats across 600 million-plus adult Indian chins. The handle is arguably the cheapest customer-acquisition tool in all of Indian FMCG: a piece of moulded plastic that pays back every month for thirty years.

This is why gross margins in the category run ~55–58% and why the blades — not the handles — carry the profit. It also explains the moat. The switching cost is trivial in rupees but real in habit: a shave is a personal, slightly risky ritual (a bad blade means nicks and razor burn on your own face tomorrow morning), so people are reluctant to experiment. Trust, once earned, is remarkably sticky — and Gillette has had a century to earn it.

Why this device is non-transferable: you cannot paste this section into another FMCG primer. Soap, biscuits and shampoo are bought on price and promotion; there is no locked handle, no proprietary refill, no thirty-year annuity per customer. The razor-and-blades lock-in is specific to blades — and Gillette is the company the textbook names.

02

The India shaving market: huge, under-penetrated, trading up

Why the runway is long — and why most of the volume is still cheap steel.

India is, on paper, a shaver's paradise: 600 million-plus adult men and a culture where a clean shave still signals respectability at work and at weddings. Yet the branded, organised shaving market is only about ₹3,300 Cr (~$600m), and the narrower "razor" market is projected to grow from roughly $436m (FY25) to ~$700m by FY33 — a ~6% annual pace. The gap between "600 million men" and "a $600m market" is the whole opportunity: penetration and premiumisation.

The blade pyramid — where the volume actually is

Here is the fact that surprises most newcomers. Despite Gillette's cartridge dominance, the majority of Indian shaving volume is still the cheapest product on the shelf: the flat double-edge (DE) blade, the kind slotted into an old-fashioned razor and used with a mug of water. DE blades are roughly 60–70% of units; the pricier cartridge/system razors are a minority of units but the majority of value.

The genuine surprise: Gillette owns the cheap end too

You would assume Gillette is a premium-only brand fighting against the cheap DE blade. It isn't. Through Wilkinson Sword and 7 O'Clock, Gillette also dominates the humble double-edge blade — so as India trades up, the customer very often stays inside the same company's tent the whole way. Gillette both defends the bottom rung and owns the escalator up. That is why the trade-up story is so lucrative for this specific company: it captures the man at ₹2 and walks him to ₹30 without ever losing him to a rival.

The three growth levers

  • Penetration. Millions of men still use the barber, share a razor, or shave rarely. Bringing them into a branded, at-home shaving habit expands the base.
  • Premiumisation (trade-up). Moving existing shavers up the price ladder — DE → Guard → Vector → Mach3 → Fusion — is the single biggest value lever, and the one Guard was built to unlock.
  • Adjacencies. Women's grooming (Venus, and hair-removal), oral care (Oral-B) and appliances (Braun) extend the same distribution and brand into new baskets.

The company and who controls it

A P&G subsidiary with a tiny free float — and a recently reset financial calendar.

Gillette India is not an independent company in any meaningful strategic sense; it is P&G's listed Indian grooming vehicle. The American parent owns 75% — the maximum a promoter can typically hold while staying listed — leaving only a quarter of the shares in public hands. That structure shapes everything: the brands, the research, the pricing, and a set of related-party payments back to the parent (see §08).

How the two companies relate

  • P&G provides the intangibles. Global brand ownership, product R&D (the multi-blade cartridges, lubricating strips, flexible heads), and access to P&G's worldwide supply chain and formulations.
  • Gillette India provides the local machine. Manufacturing (a large plant at Bhiwadi, Rajasthan), a national salesforce and distributor network, and on-the-ground marketing tuned to Indian price points and habits.
  • Payments flow back. In exchange for brands, technology and shared services, Gillette India pays royalties and service charges to P&G affiliates — a genuine, recurring transfer that minority holders watch closely (see §08).

The brand portfolio and the price ladder

One company, a rung for every wallet — so no gap is left for a rival.

Gillette's cleverest structural defence is that it does not sell a razor; it sells a ladder of razors, one for every income and every stage of a shaver's life. Enter at the bottom on a ₹2 double-edge blade, and there is always a slightly better Gillette one rung up — which both traps value inside the company and starves competitors of an underserved niche to attack.

Beyond the men's razor

  • Venus — the women's shaving and hair-removal range. Small today but the fastest-growing grooming line (female grooming grew 20%+ in FY26), riding rising women's grooming spend.
  • Gillette shave prep — gels, foams and creams. Lower-margin than blades but a natural attach-sale that reinforces the ritual and the brand.
  • Oral-B — toothbrushes and oral-care products, the second reporting segment (see §05); a beachhead in a category with even more households to convert.

Why the ladder is a moat, not just a catalogue

A single-price rival — say a cheap-blade maker, or a premium D2C upstart — can only attack one rung. If it undercuts the bottom, Gillette meets it with Wilkinson/7 O'Clock; if it goes premium, Gillette meets it with Mach3 and Fusion. To dislodge Gillette you would have to out-compete it at every price point simultaneously, while also matching a distribution network built over decades. That full-ladder defence, more than any single product, is why the market share has proved so durable.

Two engines: grooming and oral care

Blades pay the bills; the toothbrush is the option on the next decade.

Gillette India reports in two segments: Grooming (razors, blades, shave prep, Venus) and Oral Care & Portable Power (Oral-B, Braun). Grooming is the overwhelming majority of revenue and nearly all of the profit; oral care is the smaller, faster-growing adjacency that uses the same trucks, the same salesforce and the same shelves.

The strategic logic is distribution leverage. Once your salesman is already visiting a million shops to sell blades, adding a toothbrush or a shave gel to his bag costs almost nothing incremental — the expensive part (getting on the shelf, being trusted) is already paid for. That is why oral care matters more than its ~20% share suggests: it is the cheapest possible way to grow, riding a distribution asset that already exists.

The mix question that matters

Grooming and oral care pull the blended margin in different directions. Blades are extraordinarily profitable; toothbrushes and appliances are good but not blade-good. So as oral care grows faster than grooming, it very gently dilutes the blended margin even as it adds revenue and diversification. Watch whether Gillette can keep grooming's premium mix rising (more Mach3/Fusion, more Venus) fast enough to offset that — it is the quiet tension inside an otherwise simple P&L.

06

Distribution — the real moat

The brand gets the credit; the delivery network does the work.

Ask why Gillette is so hard to unseat and most people say "the brand." The deeper answer is physical availability. A blade is a low-value, high-frequency, impulse-adjacent purchase: a man buys it at the nearest kirana, chemist or paan shop, on the way home, without planning. If your blade is not on that particular shelf at that particular moment, the sale is lost. Winning shaving is therefore a distribution war, and P&G's scale is the weapon.

  • Reach. P&G's system put Gillette into the order of a million-plus retail outlets — well beyond metros, into small towns and semi-urban India where the next wave of shavers lives.
  • Frequency & stock. Heavy investment in retailer incentives, logistics and warehousing keeps blades reliably in stock — the unglamorous discipline that quietly wins a repeat-purchase category.
  • Shelf gravity. A dominant share earns more shelf space, which earns more sales, which earns more shelf space — a flywheel a new entrant cannot buy its way into quickly.
Why a D2C challenger struggles here

A digital-first grooming brand can build a slick website and a loyal online following — but the average Indian blade buyer is not shopping online for a ₹5 refill; he is grabbing it at the corner shop. To reach him, a challenger must replicate a physical distribution network that took Gillette decades and P&G's balance sheet to build. This is the structural reason online-native rivals have grown fast in trimmers and premium kits (planned, higher-value purchases) but have barely dented the mass blade business — the terrain there favours the incumbent's trucks, not the challenger's app.

07

The competition — and the trimmer that threatens the wet shave

The real danger isn't a cheaper blade. It's men deciding not to shave at all.

Gillette faces three kinds of rival, and they are not equally dangerous. The cheap-blade makers and the D2C upstarts nibble at the edges; the genuinely structural threat is a change in what men do to their faces — the shift from the daily wet shave to the electric trimmer and the maintained-stubble look, a habit that needs far fewer Gillette blades, or none.

The trimmer, decoded — why it hits the annuity, not just a product

The razor-and-blades model is powerful precisely because the blade is a consumable bought forever. A trimmer breaks that engine. A young man who buys a ₹1,500 electric trimmer to maintain designer stubble has made a one-time purchase that ends his blade annuity — he no longer needs a fresh ₹5–30 refill every few weeks. Notice the tell in the D2C data: Bombay Shaving Company, a "shaving" company, earns nearly half its revenue from trimmers. The category's growth is migrating from the blade Gillette dominates to the device where it is weaker. P&G's answer is Braun appliances, but here Gillette is a challenger, not the king — reversing the usual dynamic.

This is the crux of the bear case, and it is not about a competitor stealing share of the wet-shave market — it is about the wet-shave market itself growing more slowly, or shrinking at the young end, as grooming habits change. Gillette can still win nearly every blade sold in India and yet grow slowly if fewer young men choose to shave with a blade in the first place. That is the difference between a share problem (which Gillette wins) and a category problem (which it cannot simply out-market).

The counter-argument: India is under-penetrated enough that new shavers entering the market may offset trimmer defection for years, and stubble still needs edging, trimming and the occasional clean shave. But the direction of travel among urban young men is real, and it is the one demand-side risk that Gillette's distribution and pricing power cannot neutralise.
08

The P&G parent — global muscle, and the royalty question

The parent is both the biggest advantage and the item minority holders argue about.

Being 75%-owned by P&G is, on balance, an enormous asset. But it comes with a structural feature every minority shareholder should understand: a portion of the profits earned in India flows back to the parent as royalties and service charges, in return for the brands and technology the Indian company uses but does not own.

What the parent gives

  • Brands. Gillette, Mach3, Fusion, Venus, Oral-B, Braun — century-scale trademarks the Indian company deploys but P&G owns.
  • R&D. The billion-dollar global research behind multi-blade geometry, lubricating strips, pivoting heads and blade metallurgy — Gillette India buys the output, it doesn't fund the lab.
  • Supply chain & systems. Global sourcing scale, formulations, and shared corporate services (finance, IT, procurement) that a standalone Indian firm could never match.

What the parent takes

In exchange, Gillette India pays the P&G group royalties and shared-service / cost-sharing charges as related-party transactions. This is entirely normal for an MNC subsidiary — but it is a real, recurring leakage of profit to the majority owner, and because P&G controls 75% of the votes, minority holders have little say over the rate. It is the standard governance tension in an MNC subsidiary: the majority owner sits on both sides of the payment.

A number to verify, not to trust from memory: the exact royalty/service-fee rate as a percentage of sales is set out only in the related-party notes of the annual report and has shifted over time; it is not cleanly disclosed as a single headline figure and should be read straight from the latest filing before relying on it. Treat any precise "X% of sales" claim (including from third-party summaries) as unconfirmed here. What is certain is the direction: it is a recurring transfer from the listed company to the parent, and a legitimate item to scrutinise.
The low-free-float twist

With P&G holding 75%, only about a quarter of the shares actually trade. A thin free float does two things: it makes the stock more volatile (a little buying or selling moves the price a lot), and it keeps alive the perennial minority-shareholder hope of a royalty cap, a special dividend, or a delisting/open-offer at a premium. None of these is promised — but the ownership structure is why the topic never fully goes away, and why governance and capital-return decisions sit entirely with the parent.

Why the returns are so extreme — and what that tells you

A ~90% return on capital employed is not a rounding error; it is the tell of a business that barely needs capital to grow. Gillette leases much of its brand equity from the parent, holds modest fixed assets relative to its profit, turns inventory fast, and gets paid quickly. So almost every rupee of profit is free — which is why it pays out most of its earnings as dividend (yield ~2.3%, a ₹60/share final for FY26 on top of interims) rather than reinvesting. The flip side: a business that returns ~90% on capital and still grows revenue only ~8% is telling you it has few high-return places to deploy money at home — the constraint is demand, not capital.

The one metric that settles the thesis

For a business this profitable, valuation turns on one composite question: can Gillette keep growing shaving volume while holding or lifting its gross margin through premiumisation? That single trajectory — volume up, mix richer, margin steady-to-higher — is what justifies a ~38× multiple; its opposite — flat volumes as young men defect to trimmers, forcing price-led rather than mix-led growth — is what has already knocked the stock a third off its 52-week high. Not occupancy, not order books: for Gillette it is units shaved × price per shave. If both rise together, the premium holds; if volume stalls and only price carries growth, the multiple keeps compressing.

10

The growth runway

Where the next decade of rupees comes from — if it comes.

Gillette's growth is not a single big lever; it is several small, compounding ones stacked on an already-dominant base. None is spectacular on its own, which is precisely the point of a steady FMCG compounder — the sum, over years, is what matters.

  • New shavers. India's under-penetrated base still adds young men to the shaving population each year — the raw fuel for volume, provided they choose the blade over the trimmer.
  • Trade-up. Migrating existing users up the ladder (DE → Guard → systems → Mach3/Fusion) lifts revenue per shave without needing a single new customer.
  • Women's grooming (Venus). A small base growing 20%+ — a category with far more headroom than men's as female grooming spend rises.
  • Oral care (Oral-B) & appliances (Braun). Riding the existing distribution asset at near-zero incremental cost — the cheapest growth Gillette can buy, and a partial hedge against the trimmer via Braun.
  • Pricing. Brand strength allows periodic price increases that the mass market largely absorbs — a reliable, if modest, top-line contributor.
The next chapter for Gillette is less about inventing a better blade and more about a simple race: bringing new shavers up the price ladder faster than the trimmer pulls the young ones off it.
What to watch, concretely: the grooming segment's volume commentary each quarter (not just value), the pace of Venus and oral-care scaling, any move by P&G on the royalty rate or a special capital return, and — the swing factor — whether Braun/appliances lets Gillette participate in the trimmer wave rather than merely lose blade sales to it.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A superb business with a slow-growth problem and a habit-change problem.

Gillette India is one of the highest-quality franchises on the Indian market: a category leader, capital-light, wildly cash-generative, backed by the world's premier consumer-goods parent. The questions are not about whether it is a good business — it plainly is — but about how fast it can grow, how much the parent extracts, and what a generation that prefers stubble does to the blade.

The bull case

  • Named-model moat. The razor-and-blades annuity, a full price ladder, and a century of trust — a franchise that is genuinely hard to attack at any single rung.
  • Best-in-class economics. ~30% margins, ~90% ROCE, net cash, generous dividends — a capital-light cash machine.
  • Long penetration runway. 600m-plus men, most of the market still cheap DE blades — decades of trade-up ahead, and Gillette owns both the bottom rung and the escalator.
  • P&G backing. Global brands, R&D and supply chain no local rival can match; adjacencies (Venus, Oral-B, Braun) ride the same distribution.
  • Optionality on the float. A 75% parent keeps alive the tail chance of a royalty cap, special dividend or premium delisting.

The bear case

  • The trimmer. A structural shift from wet shaving to electric grooming attacks the blade annuity itself — a category risk pricing power cannot fix.
  • Mature, slow growth. ~8% revenue growth against a ~38× multiple; the valuation needs premiumisation to keep working.
  • Royalty leakage & control. Recurring payments to P&G and a 75% parent that decides everything, with minority holders along for the ride.
  • Low free float = volatility. A thin tradeable base swings the price hard, as the slide from ₹11,500 to ~₹7,600 shows.
  • Input costs & D2C nibbling. Steel and packaging costs, plus agile online challengers in premium and trimmer niches.
The bottom line

Gillette India is a rare thing: a business that earns roughly ninety paise of return on every rupee of capital it employs, selling a product bought by hundreds of millions of people every few weeks. That quality is not in doubt. What is in doubt is the arithmetic of the next decade — whether enough young Indian men keep reaching for a ₹5 blade rather than a ₹1,500 trimmer to keep the annuity compounding. The number that will answer it is not in a boardroom or a valuation model; it is on ten million bathroom shelves each month, in the quiet choice between the blade and the buzz of a trimmer. Watch grooming volumes — the day they stop rising is the day the century-old model finally meets a habit it cannot price its way around.
