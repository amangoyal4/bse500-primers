Indian Oil Corporation, India's fuel-supply backbone

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Indian Oil Corporation (IndianOil, IOC) is India's largest downstream oil company — the state-controlled giant that buys crude oil, refines it into petrol, diesel, LPG, jet fuel and petrochemicals, and sells those fuels across the country through the biggest retail and distribution network in India. Where an "upstream" company like ONGC finds and pumps crude, IOC sits at the other end of the chain: it processes and delivers. It is the flagship of India's public-sector oil "OMCs" (oil marketing companies), ahead of peers BPCL and HPCL.

Three facts frame everything:

  • Two profit engines in one company — refining and marketing. IOC makes money twice on the same molecule: once as a refiner (the gross refining margin, or GRM — the gap between what its finished fuels sell for and what the crude cost) and again as a marketer (the marketing margin it earns retailing that fuel through its pumps). These two engines often move in opposite directions, which is the single most important thing to understand about IOC's earnings.
  • A record operating year on a margin recovery. FY26 (year ended March 2026) was a bumper year: consolidated net profit leapt ~210% to ₹42,096 Cr (standalone ₹36,802 Cr, up 184%) on ₹9.01 lakh crore of consolidated revenue — driven by a strong refining-margin upcycle and healthy fuel-marketing margins as crude stayed moderate while pump prices held. IOC also set records for refining throughput (75.45 MMT), pipeline throughput (105.3 MMT) and product sales (104.4 MMT).
  • A dividend PSU standing at an energy-transition crossroads. IOC is ~51.5% owned by the Government of India, paid a ₹8.25/share dividend in FY26, and is a reliable cash-return stock. But it is also a fossil-fuel refiner-marketer facing the long-run question every oil company faces — so it is spending on green hydrogen, ethanol blending, petrochemicals and EV charging to future-proof a ₹9-lakh-crore business built on petrol and diesel.
In plain terms

Think of IOC as India's giant fuel factory and corner-shop network rolled into one. It imports crude oil the country doesn't have enough of, cooks it in eleven refineries into the petrol, diesel, cooking gas and jet fuel the economy runs on, ships it through 20,000+ km of pipelines, and sells it through ~40,000 petrol pumps and to 15+ crore LPG kitchens. It earns a margin on the refining and another on the selling — but the government, which owns half of it, treats those pumps as a tool of consumer politics, so when crude spikes and pump prices are frozen, IOC's marketing profit gets crushed; when crude falls and prices hold, it fattens. That policy-driven see-saw, on top of the ordinary swings of the oil cycle, is why IOC's profits are among the hardest in the market to forecast.

01

Downstream economics 101: how a refiner-marketer earns

No chemical-engineering degree required — just the handful of levers that drive an OMC.

To understand IOC you first have to see where it sits in the oil chain. The industry runs in three stages — upstream (find & pump crude), midstream (transport it), and downstream (refine and sell). IOC is a downstream company: its raw material is crude oil (mostly imported — India imports over 85% of the crude it uses), and its products are the finished fuels an economy actually consumes. Two distinct businesses live inside that one word "downstream".

The simple profit equation

Roughly: IOC's operating profit ≈ (Throughput × GRM) + (Volume sold × marketing margin) − fixed costs ± inventory effects. So there are two "spreads" to watch, not one. The refining spread (GRM) is set by global product-vs-crude prices; the marketing spread is set by the gap between Indian pump prices (which the government influences) and IOC's product cost. Because these two spreads react differently to a change in crude — and because a moving crude price also creates paper gains or losses on the huge stock of oil IOC holds — a single oil-price move can push IOC's profit in three directions at once. That is the essence of why IOC earnings are volatile and genuinely hard to forecast.

02

Engine 1 — refining, the GRM and the crack spread

How IOC makes money turning crude into fuels — and why a single number, the GRM, drives it.

A refinery is a giant sorting-and-transformation machine. Crude oil is a messy mix of hydrocarbons; the refinery heats, distils and chemically "cracks" it into valuable, saleable products — petrol, diesel, jet fuel (ATF), LPG, naphtha, bitumen and more. The refiner's profit is the gross refining margin (GRM): the total value of the products it makes minus the cost of the crude and processing, expressed in dollars per barrel. It is the refining industry's single most-watched number.

The "crack spread" — decoded

The GRM is really a crack spread — a term from "cracking" heavy crude into lighter products. Picture buying a barrel of crude for, say, $70. You refine it and the basket of fuels it yields sells for $80. Your gross crack — before running costs — is $10 a barrel. Multiply that by IOC's 75.45 million tonnes of throughput (each tonne ≈ 7.3 barrels) and small moves in the per-barrel margin become enormous swings in profit. When global product prices (especially diesel, the biggest cut) run hot relative to crude, GRMs soar and IOC's refining arm mints money; when they compress, refining profit thins fast.

Two forces that muddy the GRM

  • Inventory gains and losses. IOC always holds a vast stockpile of crude and products in transit, tanks and pipelines. When crude prices rise during a quarter, that stockpile is suddenly worth more — a paper inventory gain that flatters the reported GRM and profit. When crude falls, the same stock loses value — an inventory loss. These swings are real accounting effects but not "core" refining skill, which is why analysts separate reported GRM from core GRM (the ~$9.2–9.9 figure above). A single sharp crude move can add or wipe out thousands of crore of paper profit.
  • Refinery complexity. Not all refineries are equal. A "complex" refinery with secondary units (cokers, hydrocrackers, FCC) can process cheaper, heavier, sourer crude and squeeze out a higher share of premium products (diesel, petrol) rather than low-value residue. Higher complexity → structurally higher GRM. IOC's refineries range widely in complexity, and much of its capex goes into upgrading them to lift the yield of valuable fuels and cut fuel oil.
Why the discount-crude story mattered

A quieter driver of recent OMC refining profits has been discounted Russian crude. After 2022, Russian oil traded at a discount to global benchmarks, and Indian refiners including IOC bought heavily — effectively lowering their crude cost and widening the GRM. That tailwind is real but political: it depends on sanctions dynamics and the size of the discount, both of which can shift with geopolitics. It's a reminder that a refiner's margin isn't just engineering — it's also who you can buy crude from, and at what price. Any narrowing of the Russian discount, or sanctions pressure to stop buying it, is a genuine risk to IOC's refining spread.

03

Engine 2 — marketing margins & the pump-price politics

The most distinctive, most policy-driven part of IOC's story: what happens between crude and the pump.

IOC's second profit engine is marketing — selling the fuel it (and others) refine, through its retail network, and pocketing a margin per litre. In a free market this margin would just track supply and demand. In India it is politically charged, because petrol, diesel and cooking gas are among the most price-sensitive items in the household budget, and the government — IOC's majority owner — leans on the state OMCs to keep pump prices stable regardless of what crude is doing. This creates a see-saw that defines IOC's marketing profits.

The under-recovery dynamic — the core mechanism

India's fuel prices are officially "deregulated" (freed in 2010–2014), but in practice the state OMCs move pump prices only when it's politically comfortable. That produces two opposite scenarios:

  • Crude spikes, pump prices frozen → margins crushed (an "under-recovery"). When global crude and product prices jump but IOC can't raise the pump price (often ahead of elections), it is selling fuel for less than it costs to make and deliver. The gap is an under-recovery — a marketing loss IOC absorbs. This is what happened in 2022 when oil surged after the Ukraine war and OMCs held prices: their marketing margins went deeply negative and profits collapsed.
  • Crude falls, pump prices held → margins fatten. The reverse is a windfall. When crude drops but IOC keeps the pump price where it is, the margin between cost and price balloons. Notably, IOC and its peers have not revised retail petrol/diesel prices in line with cost since May 2022 (bar a ₹2/litre pre-election cut in March 2024) — so as crude softened, marketing margins swelled to well above normal, and that fat marketing margin was a big part of why FY26 profits were so strong.

Why this is the hardest part to forecast

The marketing margin is where economics meets politics, and politics doesn't follow a formula. Two identical crude environments can produce very different IOC results depending on whether the government wants pump prices held, cut, or passed through — and that choice hinges on elections, inflation, and public mood. So an analyst can model the GRM off global product prices with reasonable confidence, but the marketing margin is a partly political variable. It is the biggest reason IOC's quarterly profit can surprise violently in either direction, and why the stock trades at a low, discounted multiple: the market cannot rely on a stable earnings stream from a business whose second engine is throttled by New Delhi.

The LPG legacy. Cooking gas (LPG, sold under IOC's Indane brand to 15+ crore kitchens) is the one fuel still effectively subsidised for households. IOC often sells LPG below cost as social policy, accumulating an "LPG under-recovery buffer" — which stood at ~₹23,101 Cr as of March 2026. The government periodically compensates the OMCs: in FY26 a ₹14,486 Cr subsidy was approved for IOC (paid in 12 monthly instalments from Nov 2025), of which ~₹6,036 Cr was recognised as revenue in FY26. LPG is thus a standing drag that the state occasionally, but not fully or promptly, reimburses.
04

The moat: refineries, pipelines & the retail-LPG network

The physical infrastructure that is genuinely hard to replicate — and the real reason IOC endures.

Refining and marketing margins swing with the cycle, but IOC's durable competitive advantage is physical: an infrastructure footprint so large, so capital-intensive and so tied up in land, permits and decades of build-out that no new entrant could recreate it. This is the classic moat of a downstream incumbent — you can undercut IOC on a single pump, but you cannot cheaply build a nationwide fuel-supply system.

The three pillars of the moat

  • The refining base. IOC operates 11 refineries with ~80.8 MMTPA of capacity (including subsidiaries) — about 31% of India's total refining capacity. Refineries are multi-billion-dollar, multi-year, permit-heavy assets sited near coasts and demand centres. That installed base is a barrier no start-up can leap.
  • The pipeline & logistics grid. Pipelines are the cheapest, safest way to move fuel at scale, and IOC's 20,000+ km network is the arteries of Indian fuel supply. In FY26 it moved a record 105.3 MMT. Rights of way across a country the size of India take decades to assemble — this is arguably the least replicable asset of all.
  • The retail & LPG last mile. ~40,000 filling stations and 60,000+ customer touchpoints put IOC within reach of virtually every Indian town and highway; Indane's LPG reach into 15+ crore kitchens is a distribution machine that took generations to build. Combined with lubricants (SERVO), aviation fuel and bulk sales, IOC touches the entire fuel economy.
Why the moat still matters in an EV world

The bear worry is that this fuel-retail moat becomes a stranded asset as vehicles electrify. The bull rebuttal is subtler: those ~40,000 forecourts are prime, high-traffic real estate — and IOC is converting them into energy-transition hubs (EV fast-charging, battery-swap, CNG, convenience retail). More than 10,000 of its outlets already host EV charging. So the network's value may migrate from selling litres of petrol to selling kilowatt-hours, gas and services — but the moat (the sites, the brand, the logistics) is the same asset either way. Whether that transition preserves the profit pool is the central long-term debate (see §08).

05

The company and who controls it

A 1959 nationalised distributor that became India's largest company by revenue for years.

IndianOil traces to 1959, when the state set up the Indian Oil Company to build indigenous fuel-distribution capacity and reduce dependence on the foreign "oil majors" that then controlled Indian marketing. It merged with the refining arm (Indian Refineries Ltd) in 1964 to become Indian Oil Corporation, and over six decades grew into a "Maharatna" — the top tier of PSU autonomy — and for many years India's largest company by revenue. It remains majority-owned by the Government of India (~51.5%).

  • 2010–2014

  • 2018

  • 2022–24

  • FY26
    Record throughput, pipeline & sales volumes; profit triples on a refining-margin upcycle and fat marketing margins; advances the Paradip petrochemical complex and green-hydrogen & EV-charging build-out.

Ownership & group structure

  • Government of India — ~51.5%. The controlling shareholder. This makes IOC's dividend, capital allocation and — crucially — its pump-pricing partly instruments of state policy. New Delhi relies on IOC's dividend and on its willingness to hold fuel prices for consumers.
  • Institutions & public — the rest. Domestic institutions (~20%) and foreign investors (~9%) hold much of the free float; LIC is a large holder. The government stake is the anchor and the constraint.
  • Subsidiaries & JVs. IOC holds refining/marketing subsidiaries (e.g. Chennai Petroleum — CPCL), pipeline and gas JVs, LNG and city-gas ventures, and overseas E&P stakes via IndianOil's exploration arm — but IOC is overwhelmingly a downstream story, unlike ONGC's upstream core.
The "PSU discount": because the government can direct IOC toward national objectives — freezing pump prices, absorbing LPG losses, sometimes cross-buying other PSUs — that a purely profit-maximising firm would avoid, the market values IOC at a low single-digit P/E. The flip side of that governance discount is a large, reliable dividend and an implicit sovereign backstop.
06

Crude-price sensitivity & why earnings are so volatile

The single force that explains most of IOC's quarter-to-quarter swings — and why it cuts three ways.

Crude oil is both IOC's biggest cost and the key input to its selling prices. So a move in the crude price ripples through IOC in three different directions at once, and they don't all point the same way. This is why IOC's earnings look erratic and are genuinely hard to model — far harder than a pure upstream producer whose profit simply rises and falls with oil.

What a crude-price move does to IOC — three channels, often opposing
ChannelWhen crude RISESWhen crude FALLS
Refining margin (GRM)Mixed — product prices may lag crude, squeezing the crack; but a rising market also creates inventory gains.Mixed — cheaper crude can widen the crack, but falling prices create inventory losses on stock held.

Read the table and the puzzle becomes clear: a rising crude price can simultaneously help IOC (inventory gain) and hurt it (marketing squeeze), while doing something ambiguous to refining. The net effect depends on how fast product prices move, whether pump prices are passed through, and how much stock IOC is holding. There is no simple "IOC is a bull/bear on oil" statement — it depends entirely on the configuration.

The "sweet spot" for a refiner-marketer

Counter-intuitively, IOC often does best in a stable-to-gently-falling crude environment, not a booming one. Its ideal setup is: moderate crude (so raw-material cost is contained and no under-recovery pressure), strong global product demand (so GRMs stay wide), and pump prices held steady by the government (so marketing margins are fat). FY26 was close to that ideal — hence record profits. A sharp spike in crude is usually bad for IOC (marketing squeeze, potential inventory whiplash), which is the opposite of an upstream producer like ONGC. When you hear "oil is up," don't assume IOC wins — it may well be the loser.

The forecasting trap: because three channels tug in different directions and the marketing channel is partly political, IOC's quarterly PAT can swing violently — a 500%+ jump one quarter, a sharp fall the next — with little change in the underlying business. Investors who treat IOC as a smooth compounder get whipsawed. It is best understood as a cyclical whose good and bad quarters average out over a full cycle.
07

Petrochemicals — moving up the value chain

Why IOC is pouring capex into turning fuel molecules into higher-value chemicals.

Refining alone is a thin-margin, cyclical, commoditised business. The strategic answer — pursued by every major refiner worldwide — is to push more of the barrel into petrochemicals: converting oil and gas feedstock into plastics, polymers, rubbers and chemical intermediates that sell at higher, steadier margins and enjoy structural demand growth as economies develop. IOC is investing heavily to raise its "petrochemical intensity" — the share of each barrel that becomes chemicals rather than burnt fuel.

Why the pivot matters

  • Higher, less cyclical margins. Petrochemicals (polymers, para-xylene, PTA, rubbers) typically earn better and more stable margins than motor fuels, and are less exposed to the pump-price politics that throttle IOC's marketing arm.
  • Structural demand growth. As India's per-capita plastics and chemicals consumption is still low versus developed peers, domestic petchem demand is a long-runway growth story — and IOC would rather serve it from its own molecules than cede it to imports or to Reliance.
  • An EV hedge. Critically, petrochemical demand does not disappear when cars electrify — plastics, packaging and chemicals keep growing. So lifting petchem intensity is also a hedge against the long-run erosion of transport-fuel demand (see §08). A barrel turned into polymer is a barrel that doesn't depend on the internal-combustion engine.

The catch — capital intensity and timing

Petrochemical projects like the ₹61,077 Cr Paradip complex are enormous, multi-year bets that lock up capital for years before earning a return, and their profitability depends on the petchem cycle — which has its own gluts and shortages (a wave of new global capacity can crush polymer margins just as IOC's plants come online). So the pivot is strategically right but execution- and timing-sensitive: get the cycle wrong and a huge investment earns thin returns for years. It also competes for the same cash as the dividend, refinery upgrades and the green transition — the four-way claim on IOC's cash flow that defines its capital-allocation challenge.

08

The energy transition — the existential question

What happens to a petrol-and-diesel refiner-marketer as the world (slowly) electrifies?

Every fossil-fuel company faces the same long-horizon question, and for a downstream refiner-marketer it is especially pointed: your entire business is selling transport fuel — what happens when vehicles stop burning it? Electric vehicles, tighter emissions rules, biofuel mandates and green hydrogen all chip, over decades, at the demand for petrol and diesel that IOC's refineries and pumps exist to serve. IOC's response is a broad, capital-hungry hedge across several transition vectors.

The honest paradox — and why India buys time

Like every incumbent, IOC must keep selling petrol and diesel to fund its escape from petrol and diesel. The cash for green hydrogen and EV charging comes from the very fuels it's hedging against. But India's situation gives IOC more runway than a Western oil major: India's transport-fuel demand is still growing (rising incomes, vehicle penetration, freight) and is expected to keep growing for years even as EV share rises — so IOC's core market isn't shrinking yet, it's still expanding. The existential question is real but distant: the risk is measured in decades, not quarters. The strategic bet is that by the time petrol/diesel demand plateaus, IOC will have shifted the barrel toward petrochemicals and the forecourt toward electrons and gas — keeping the infrastructure moat valuable through the transition rather than stranded by it. Whether that pivot earns adequate returns is the open, decades-long question at the heart of the IOC investment case.

09

Financials, valuation & the dividend

A record cyclical year, a cheap multiple, and a healthy dividend — with a big volatility caveat.

FY26 was an exceptional year for IOC — profit roughly tripled on a refining-margin upcycle, fat marketing margins (crude soft while pump prices held) and record volumes. But the very forces that made FY26 great are cyclical, so the headline growth flatters what is a lumpy, mean-reverting earnings stream.

The balance sheet & capital demands

A strong FY26 let IOC slash borrowings by ₹23,798 Cr, taking net debt-to-equity to a comfortable 0.32× — important, because IOC faces a heavy multi-year capex programme (refinery upgrades, the ₹61,077 Cr Paradip petchem complex, pipelines, green energy). The capital-allocation puzzle is a four-way split of one cash flow: sustain/expand refining, build petrochemicals, fund the green transition, and pay the dividend the government relies on. In lean years, something has to give — usually the balance sheet takes the strain, which is why IOC's debt swings with the cycle.

How to read the cheap multiple

IOC typically trades at a low single-digit P/E and near book value — far below the market — for three reasons: (1) earnings volatility, because the two-spread model plus inventory effects make profits lumpy and low-visibility; (2) the policy overhang, because the government can freeze pump prices, direct LPG losses, or lean on IOC for national ends, capping how much of a good cycle shareholders keep; and (3) the transition fear, because the market is reluctant to pay up for a fossil-fuel refiner-marketer facing long-run demand erosion. The bull rebuttal: a low-P/E, sub-book stock with a healthy dividend already prices in a lot of pessimism, and a normalised through-cycle earnings power well above the depressed multiple is upside the market isn't paying for. The key is to value IOC on mid-cycle earnings, not a peak or trough quarter.

10

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A cheap, dividend-paying national fuel champion — riding a violent margin cycle into an uncertain transition.

IOC is a paradox: an indispensable, cash-generative national champion — the physical fuel-supply backbone of the world's fastest-growing large economy — that the market values cheaply because its earnings are cyclical, its second engine is throttled by politics, and its core product faces long-run decline. The investment debate is whether the through-cycle cash generation, deep value and dividend outweigh the volatility, policy and transition headwinds.

The bull case

  • Deep value & a healthy dividend — low single-digit P/E, near book value; a lot of pessimism already in the price, with a reliable payout.
  • Irreplaceable moat — ~40,000 pumps, 20,000+ km of pipelines, 11 refineries (~31% of India), 15 cr LPG kitchens; nobody can rebuild this.
  • Growth in the core market — Indian fuel & petrochemical demand is still rising; IOC set records on throughput, pipeline & sales in FY26 and is gaining share.
  • Marketing windfall when crude is soft — held pump prices in a moderate-crude world produce fat margins, as FY26 showed.
  • Optionality — petchem ramp (Paradip), forecourts turning into EV/gas hubs, and green-hydrogen leadership are upside the market gives little credit for.

The bear case

  • Violently volatile earnings — two spreads plus inventory effects make profit lumpy and hard to forecast; peak-cycle FY26 flatters the run-rate.
  • Policy squeeze & under-recovery risk — a crude spike with frozen pump prices can crush marketing margins and inflate losses (as in 2022); LPG losses recur.
  • Government control — capital allocation and pricing serve the exchequer and consumers over minority holders; a persistent PSU discount.
  • Crude & geopolitics — dependence on imported crude and on the Russian-discount tailwind, both exposed to sanctions and price shocks.
  • Energy transition — long-run erosion of transport-fuel demand; the green & petchem pivots are costly, capital-hungry and unproven in returns.
The bottom line

IOC is best understood as a deep-value, dividend-paying downstream cyclical — not a growth compounder. You are buying India's irreplaceable fuel-supply infrastructure, and a healthy dividend, at a price that assumes little goes right. The story turns on three things to watch each quarter: refining margins (where is the GRM in the crack-spread cycle?), marketing margins (are pump prices being passed through, or is there an under-recovery squeeze?), and capital discipline (is the four-way pull of dividend, refining, petchem and green capex being managed without ballooning debt?). Get those right through a full cycle and a sub-book stock with a healthy yield is a sturdy income holding; get them wrong — a crude spike into frozen prices, a petchem glut, an over-leveraged transition — and the "cheap" multiple is cheap for a reason. The deepest question, unanswerable in any single year, is whether IOC's magnificent physical moat holds its value as the world it was built to fuel slowly changes.
