00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Aadhar Housing Finance is a mortgage lender for people the big banks find hard to lend to — lower-income and economically-weaker households buying their first home in small-town and semi-urban India. It writes small loans (average ticket ~₹10.7 lakh) against modest houses, mostly to families earning between roughly ₹5,000 and ₹50,000 a month, and it is the largest affordable-housing finance company (HFC) in India by assets under management, with ~₹30,571 Cr of AUM across 600+ branches in 20-plus states. It is controlled by private-equity giant Blackstone, which bought it in 2019 and floated ~24% of it on the market in May 2024.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It earns a fat spread for doing hard, manual work. Aadhar borrows at roughly 7.7% and lends at a portfolio yield of about 13.5% — a gross spread near 5.8 points, close to double what a prime home lender collects. That extra margin is the fee for underwriting borrowers who have real cash flow but no salary slip. It translates into a ~4.4% return on assets — exceptional for any lender.
  • The credit is far cleaner than the customer profile suggests. Despite lending down-market — roughly 64% of the book is to low-income (LIG) and economically-weaker (EWS) households — gross non-performing assets sit at just ~1.08% and annual credit losses run around 20 basis points of AUM. That counter-intuitive combination is the single most surprising thing about the company, and section 05 is dedicated to why it holds.
  • It compounds, and the market pays up for it. AUM has more than doubled since FY21; FY26 profit rose ~22% to ₹1,108 Cr. The stock trades near 3.2× book — a clear premium to prime HFCs — and the live overhang is Blackstone, which agreed in early 2026 to move its controlling block into a new fund vehicle, triggering an open offer.
In plain terms

A big bank's home-loan machine runs on documents: a payslip, a Form-16, a bank statement, a CIBIL score. Feed it those and it approves in minutes. But most of India doesn't produce those documents — the tailor, the vegetable seller, the auto-parts dealer, the daily-wage mason all earn real, steady money in cash, and none of it shows up on paper. Aadhar's entire business is a machine built to lend to exactly the person the document-machine rejects: it sends a field officer to sit in the shop, watch the till, count the sewing machines, judge the household, and decide how much this family can safely repay. That judgement is slow and expensive and can't be done from a screen — which is precisely why Aadhar can charge a wide spread for it, and why a bank generally can't be bothered to compete for a ₹10-lakh loan in a Tier-3 town.

01

Affordable-HFC economics 101: how a small-ticket lender earns

No finance background needed — just the handful of numbers that drive the whole business.

A housing finance company is, at heart, a spread business: it raises money at one interest rate and lends it out at a higher one, and the gap between the two — minus its running costs and minus the loans that go bad — is its profit. For an affordable HFC like Aadhar, five numbers tell you almost the entire story.

The only units you need
TermWhat it means (and Aadhar's number)

Ticket sizeThe average loan. Small ticket = down-market, higher-touch. Aadhar: ~₹10.7 lakh — versus ₹25-30 lakh+ at prime HFCs like LIC Housing or PNB Housing.
Yield & spreadYield = the interest it charges (~13.5%). Spread = yield minus its own cost of borrowing (~7.7%), so ~5.8 points. NIM (net interest margin, spread expressed over assets) is ~9%.
GNPA & credit costGNPA = share of loans that have gone bad (~1.08%). Credit cost = the money actually written off each year (~20 bps of AUM). The discipline gauge for a down-market lender.
RoA"Return on assets" — profit as a share of the loan book (~4.4%). It rolls spread, costs and credit losses into one number. Above ~2.5% is very good for a lender; 4%+ is elite.
The simple profit equation

Roughly: Profit ≈ AUM × (Yield − Cost of funds − Operating cost − Credit losses). So an affordable HFC grows profit three ways — grow the book (more AUM), protect the spread (keep the gap between lending and borrowing rates wide), and keep losses tiny (don't let the down-market book go bad). Aadhar's edge is that its spread is unusually wide and its losses are unusually small at the same time — a combination that most lenders have to trade off against each other. Get high yield and low losses together and you get a ~4.4% RoA. That is the entire investment case in one line.

Two products, one collateral

  • Home loans (~75% of the book) — money to buy, build or extend a self-occupied house. This is the core, the lowest-risk, PMAY-subsidy-eligible product.
  • Loan against property / LAP (~25%) — a mortgage-backed loan against a house the family already owns, often used by the self-employed for business needs. Higher yield, slightly higher risk, and something regulators watch closely.
  • The collateral is always the same idea: a house the borrower lives in or aspires to live in. That matters enormously for why the book stays clean — a family will sacrifice almost anything before letting the roof over their head be taken. More on that in section 05.
02

The business model: lending to income that has no payslip

One idea explains the whole company — read this section and the moat clicks.

A prime bank underwrites documents. Aadhar underwrites a household. The difference sounds small; it is the entire reason this business exists and earns what it earns. Roughly four in ten of Aadhar's borrowers are self-employed with informal, undocumented income; even its salaried customers are often on the informal-sector payroll — a factory hand, a shop assistant, a driver — where a formal salary slip may not exist. For all of them, the standard credit machine simply returns "no data, reject."

The underwriting funnel — how a loan actually gets made

Aadhar's process isn't a screen and an algorithm; it is people, on the ground, doing judgement work that doesn't scale cheaply. That slowness is the barrier to entry.

The surrogate-income method (the genuine craft)

Here is the part a spreadsheet can't do. When a tailor applies, Aadhar's officer doesn't ask for a salary slip — there isn't one. Instead the officer builds an assessed ("surrogate") income: how many machines are running, how many garments a day, what a garment fetches, how busy the shop looked across two visits, what the family's electricity bill and school fees imply about lifestyle, what neighbours and suppliers say. From that mosaic the officer estimates a monthly income, applies a deliberately conservative haircut, checks that the proposed EMI is a modest slice of it, and lends against a house worth well more than the loan. The whole method is judgement, physically gathered — which is why it can't be outsourced to a call centre or a bank's national credit engine, and why the affordable-HFC model is defensible even though the product (a home loan) is a commodity.

The branch is the factory — the A-B-C system

  • Category A — full-service hubs in larger towns and cities, with the widest product range and back-office depth.
  • Category B — mid-sized branches in semi-urban markets, the workhorses of the network.
  • Category C — lean, low-cost outlets pushed into rural and underserved pockets, designed to reach the customer where prime lenders never go.
  • The logic — density matters. Underwriting-by-visiting and collecting-by-relationship only work if a branch is genuinely local. Aadhar's 600+ branches span 20-plus states, reaching an estimated 90%+ of the country's population — deliberately spread thin and wide rather than concentrated, so no single town or state can sink the book.
03

The company and who controls it

A survivor of India's biggest financial collapse, now owned by the world's largest PE firm.

Aadhar's history has a genuine plot twist: it was owned by the Wadhawan family — the promoters of DHFL, the housing-finance lender at the centre of India's largest financial fraud and the 2018-19 NBFC crisis. When DHFL imploded, the Wadhawans were forced to sell their assets. Aadhar, a fundamentally healthy business that happened to have a toxic parent, was bought out of the wreckage by Blackstone in 2019 — arguably the best thing that ever happened to it, swapping a disgraced, cash-strapped owner for a global PE firm with deep capital and a professionalisation playbook.

Ownership & management

  • Promoter — Blackstone. Post-IPO, a Blackstone vehicle held ~76.5% (before the 2026 reshuffle). Blackstone brought professional management, board oversight, capital access and a clear scale-then-list playbook — but it is a financial owner, not a permanent one, which is the ownership question the market keeps pricing (section 10).
  • Management. A professional, non-promoter executive team runs the company (this is a PE-owned platform, not a founder-run one) — a deliberate contrast to the personality-driven NBFCs that dominated the pre-2018 era.
  • Balance sheet. Very well capitalised — capital adequacy around 44%, roughly triple the regulatory floor, a legacy of the IPO fund-raise and retained profits. That capital cushion is a real competitive asset in a business where a lender's own borrowing cost depends on how safe it looks.
The quirk worth remembering: Aadhar didn't fail in 2019 — its owner did. The lender's own loan book stayed healthy right through the DHFL blow-up. That distinction — a good business trapped under a bad parent — is exactly the mispricing Blackstone bought, and it is why Aadhar entered the listed markets as a clean, well-run platform rather than a distressed turnaround.
04

The loan book and the borrower

Who actually borrows from Aadhar — and why the mix is engineered for safety.

Aadhar's book is built to be small, spread out and low-leverage on the collateral. The average loan is ~₹10.7 lakh against a house typically worth 1.6-1.7× that, so the loan-to-value sits around 58-60% — the family has real equity in the home from day one, which is the first line of defence against default. The borrower base tilts deliberately toward the low end of the income scale, which is where PMAY subsidies and the biggest housing shortage both live.

The geography — wide, not deep

  • 600+ branches across 20-plus states and union territories — genuinely pan-India, with strongholds built out state by state (Gujarat alone crossed 80+ branches in 2025).
  • Semi-urban and rural weighting — the network is deliberately pushed into the smaller towns where a first-time buyer's ₹12-lakh house sits, not the metros where prime HFCs cluster.
  • No single-state dependence — unlike a regionally concentrated lender, Aadhar spreads its book so a local shock (a bad monsoon, a regional industry downturn) can't sink the whole portfolio. Diversification is a risk-management tool here.
Why the ticket size is a strategy, not an accident

Keeping the average loan around ₹10-11 lakh does three things at once. It keeps Aadhar in the subsidy-blessed, high-shortage EWS/LIG band where demand is structural. It keeps each loan small enough that no single default hurts — the book is thousands of tiny exposures, not a few large ones. And it keeps Aadhar in the zone banks won't chase, because the effort-per-rupee of a ₹10-lakh informal-income loan is uneconomic for a lender geared to ₹50-lakh salaried mortgages. Small is not a limitation here; small is the moat.

05

The asset-quality puzzle: risky customer, clean book

The most counter-intuitive fact about Aadhar — and the mechanism that makes it true.

Here is the fact that should stop a smart generalist in their tracks. Aadhar lends to informal-income, low-income, first-time borrowers — precisely the profile a banker would call "sub-prime." And yet its gross NPAs are ~1.08% and its annual credit losses are around 20 basis points of AUM — cleaner than several lenders who serve richer, salaried, metropolitan customers. Home First, a well-run peer, runs GNPA near 2%. How can lending down-market produce better credit outcomes? The answer isn't luck; it is four structural forces stacked on top of each other.

  • 1. It's the roof over their head. This is a self-occupied first home, not an investment property or a consumption loan. A family will default on almost anything else — a personal loan, a credit card, even a business debt — before they let the house they live in be repossessed. The collateral is emotionally senior, which makes the borrower's willingness to pay unusually high.
  • 2. Low LTV means the borrower has skin in the game. At ~58-60% loan-to-value, the family put down ~40% of the home's value. Walking away means torching their own savings first. And if repossession ever happens, the collateral comfortably covers the loan.
  • 3. Tiny tickets, thousands of them. The book is a vast granular spread of ~₹10-lakh loans. No borrower is big enough to matter alone, so the portfolio behaves statistically — a few will always sour, but the law of large numbers keeps the aggregate loss rate stable and predictable.
  • 4. Local, high-touch collection. The same physically-present model that underwrites the loan also collects it. A missed EMI triggers a personal visit, early, from someone the borrower knows — not a form letter 90 days later. Early, relationship-based follow-up stops a slip becoming a write-off.
The device that captures the whole company

Picture one loan. Aadhar's average borrower earns roughly ₹25,000 a month and borrows about ₹10.7 lakh against a house worth ~₹18 lakh — a house the family lives in. Aadhar funds that loan at ~7.7%, charges ~13.5%, pockets a ~5.8-point spread, and across the whole book loses roughly one rupee in a hundred to bad debt. Read those numbers together and the spread stops looking like danger pay for reckless lending. It is the wage of the field officer who sat in the tailoring shop and counted the sewing machines — the price of manual underwriting, not the price of risk. That single sentence is the difference between Aadhar and a payday lender, and it is why a wide spread and a clean book can coexist.

The caveat that keeps this honest: the low loss rate is a through-cycle claim that has held in a broadly benign economy. Informal-income households are genuinely more exposed to local shocks — a factory closure, a failed monsoon, an inflation spike that eats into thin cash flows. The model's defences (equity in the home, granularity, local collection) are strong, but they are defences against a normal cycle, not a guarantee against a severe one. Which is why the number to watch is not the current GNPA but whether credit cost holds when conditions turn (section 09).
06

The spread engine and the funding side

Aadhar makes money on both legs of the balance sheet — and the borrowing leg is where the risk hides.

The lending side we've covered: ~13.5% yield on a granular, well-collateralised book. But a lender is only as good as its funding, because an affordable HFC is wholesale-funded — it has no deposits, so every rupee it lends it must first borrow. The gap between what it borrows at and lends at is the spread, and both the width of that gap and its stability depend on the funding side.

Why the funding side is the real cyclical lever

Aadhar's yield is fairly sticky — its borrowers aren't rate-shopping between banks. But its cost of funds moves with the credit cycle and its own perceived safety. When the RBI cuts rates or Aadhar's rating rises (as CARE's 2026 upgrade to AA+ did), borrowing costs fall and the spread widens automatically — pure profit with no extra risk. The reverse is the danger: in a liquidity crunch — exactly what killed DHFL in 2018 — wholesale funding can dry up or reprice sharply, squeezing the spread from the wrong end. Aadhar's defences are a diversified lender base (40+ relationships), a fat ~44% capital cushion, the NHB line, and asset-liability matching. Being big and highly-rated is itself a moat here: the largest, safest-looking affordable HFC gets funded cheapest, which lets it lend cheapest or earn most — a scale advantage that compounds.

07

The opportunity: a housing shortage the size of a nation

The structural tailwind unique to this segment — and why the runway is measured in decades.

Every lender talks about "a large addressable market." For affordable housing finance the phrase is almost an understatement. India's housing shortage was estimated at roughly 61.5 million units in 2025, and the shortage is overwhelmingly at the bottom of the income pyramid — the EWS segment alone accounts for ~56% of it, and LIG for ~39%. That is Aadhar's exact customer. Layer on a mortgage market that has barely begun — India's mortgage-to-GDP ratio is only ~12%, versus 50-80% in developed economies — and you have a segment where demand structurally dwarfs supply for the foreseeable future.

The government is a tailwind, not a threat

Unlike many regulated industries where policy is a risk, in affordable housing the state is actively subsidising Aadhar's customer. The flagship scheme, Pradhan Mantri Awas Yojana (PMAY) — "Housing for All", was relaunched as PMAY 2.0 in September 2024, targeting 30 million additional homes through FY29. Under its urban interest-subsidy scheme, eligible EWS/LIG borrowers receive an interest subsidy on their home loan, disbursed over several years — money that lowers the effective EMI and directly expands the pool of families who can afford to borrow. When the government pays part of your customer's interest bill, it is enlarging your market for you.

The formalisation tailwind beneath the demographics

The headline demand story is the housing shortage. The quieter, more powerful story is formalisation — the multi-decade shift of credit away from moneylenders, chit funds and unorganised local financiers toward regulated lenders. An enormous share of low-income home-building has historically been funded informally, at punishing rates, off the books. As branches like Aadhar's reach deeper into Tier 3-4 towns, they don't just take share from banks — they convert borrowers who were never in the formal system at all. That is why the segment can grow at high-teens rates even in a year when overall home sales are soft: the market isn't just growing, it's being formalised, and organised affordable HFCs are the vehicle.

The counterweight: after years of ~25-30% growth, the affordable-HFC sector's pace has cooled toward the high-teens as competition intensified and the easiest customers were served first. A long runway is not the same as a fast one — the shortage guarantees demand for a decade, but the rate at which any single lender converts it depends on execution, funding cost and how crowded the field gets (section 08).

The competitive question that actually matters

The near-term rivalry is among the specialists, and Aadhar's answer is scale + safety: biggest book, most diversified geography, lowest-risk mix, cheapest likely funding as the highest-rated large affordable HFC. The longer-term question is whether banks and prime HFCs push down into the ₹10-15 lakh ticket. They have a huge funding-cost advantage (they take deposits). What they lack is the manual, branch-heavy underwriting machine — and history says banks repeatedly try the affordable/informal segment, find the per-loan economics and collection intensity unattractive, and retreat. Aadhar's bet is that this operational moat holds. If a bank ever cracks low-cost informal-income underwriting at scale, the whole segment's spreads compress — that is the tail risk sitting under every affordable HFC.

Financials & valuation

How to read a ~3.2× book multiple

Lenders are valued on price-to-book, not price-to-earnings, because their "product" is capital and their worth is roughly their net worth times how well they earn on it. At ~3.2× book, Aadhar trades at a clear premium to prime HFCs (which sit closer to 1-2×) and above the affordable-peer median (~2.3×). What justifies a premium P/B is a high, durable RoE with a long growth runway — and Aadhar's ~16% RoE, if it can be lifted as the fat capital base is deployed, plus high-teens AUM growth, is the argument. Note the quirk: Aadhar's RoE is actually held back by how well-capitalised it is (44% capital adequacy dilutes returns on equity); as that surplus capital gets lent out, RoE should rise even if nothing else changes — a built-in tailwind the multiple is partly anticipating.

The one metric that settles the thesis: credit cost. Growth is visible and the wide spread is structural — the affordable model reliably delivers both. The only thing that can turn this from a compounder into a value trap is the down-market book going bad. So the number to watch, quarter after quarter, is not AUM or NIM — it is credit cost as a share of AUM, currently a benign ~20 bps. Hold that near ~30-50 bps through a real economic wobble while AUM keeps compounding in the high teens, and the ~4.4% RoA — and the premium multiple — is earned. Let credit cost spike toward 100 bps+ in a downturn and the entire "risky customer, clean book" thesis is called into question at once. Everything else is secondary to that single line.
10

The Blackstone question & the growth runway

A private-equity owner is a feature and a countdown clock at the same time.

You cannot understand Aadhar's stock without understanding its owner. Blackstone is not a promoter family that will hold forever — it is a financial owner with a fund lifecycle, and PE funds exist to buy, improve, and eventually sell. That reality cuts two ways, and early 2026 brought the tension to the surface.

The surprise: Blackstone selling to... Blackstone

In February 2026, the market got its "PE exit" headline — but the details are more subtle than a straight sell-down. Blackstone's original holding vehicle agreed to transfer its controlling ~64% block to a different Blackstone-managed fund vehicle, which under Indian takeover rules triggered a mandatory open offer to public shareholders (priced near ₹470/share). In other words, this was less "Blackstone exits Aadhar" and more Blackstone recycling a maturing asset from an older fund into a newer one — resetting its own ownership clock while keeping control. For a first-time reader, that's the non-obvious bit: the "selldown overhang" everyone worries about turned out, at least in this round, to be an intra-house transfer rather than a market dump.

What the PE ownership actually means for a shareholder

The bull reading: Blackstone professionalised a business rescued from the DHFL wreckage, built its systems, capitalised it heavily, and listed it — a value-creation track record, and a large, sophisticated owner keeps governance tight. The bear reading: a large PE block is a persistent supply overhang — at some point those shares get sold into the market, capping the price and creating uncertainty about who the eventual anchor owner will be. Both readings will be resolved by watching one thing: who ends up holding the control block, and at what pace the stake is reduced. Until a stable, long-term owner emerges — whether a strategic acquirer, a broad public float, or a patient institution — the ownership question stays a live discount on the stock, independent of how well the loan book performs.

The runway the business is executing on

  • Book growth — high-teens AUM compounding from ₹30,571 Cr, with disbursements of ~₹9,556 Cr in FY26 (+17%) feeding the pipeline. The structural shortage supports years of this.
  • Branch expansion — pushing the 600+ network deeper into underserved states via lean Category-C outlets, each one a new local underwriting-and-collection node.
  • Operating leverage — a ~36% cost-to-income ratio that should grind lower as branches mature and technology takes on more of the process, lifting RoA further.
  • Capital deployment — the surplus ~44% capital adequacy is dry powder; lending it out lifts RoE without any new equity.
The engine is proven and the tank is full; the open question over the stock isn't whether Aadhar can lend — it's who will own it while it does.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality lender to a structurally under-served market — with real, specific things that could go wrong.

Aadhar is one of the cleaner franchises in Indian lending: elite returns, a genuine operational moat, a decade-long demand tailwind, and a balance sheet built like a fortress. The debate is split across two fronts — the credit cycle (does the clean book survive a downturn?) and the ownership overhang (who ends up holding it?) — neither of which is about whether the underlying business works.

The bull case

  • Elite unit economics — ~5.8% spread, ~4.4% RoA, ~16% RoE that has room to rise as surplus capital is deployed.
  • Operational moat — manual, branch-based informal-income underwriting that banks have repeatedly failed to replicate at these ticket sizes.
  • Clean book, down-market — ~1.1% GNPA and ~20 bps credit cost despite a 64% EWS/LIG mix; granular, low-LTV, self-occupied collateral.
  • Structural demand — a ~61.5M-unit housing shortage, ~12% mortgage-to-GDP, PMAY subsidies actively enlarging the customer pool.
  • Scale & safety — largest affordable HFC, AA+ rated, diversified across 20+ states and 40+ funding relationships.

The bear case

  • Credit is the whole thesis — informal-income borrowers are vulnerable to local shocks; a downturn that pushes credit cost toward 100 bps+ would undercut the premium overnight.
  • Ownership overhang — a large Blackstone block will eventually be sold; the eventual anchor owner is unknown.
  • Funding-cost sensitivity — wholesale-funded, no deposits; a liquidity crunch (the DHFL scenario) squeezes the spread from the funding side.
  • Crowded field + bank encroachment — many well-funded affordable HFCs plus prime lenders creeping down-market threaten long-run spreads.
  • Premium valuation — ~3.2× book already prices in durable high returns; execution or credit slippage would compress the multiple.
The bottom line

Aadhar Housing Finance is a well-built machine pointed at a market that will need what it makes for at least a generation: formal, affordable home credit for the households India's banking system was never designed to serve. The business quality is not really in question — a ~4.4% RoA on a 64%-EWS/LIG book is a genuinely rare thing. What a buyer is underwriting is narrower and more concrete than the usual valuation hand-wringing: that the credit-cost line stays low through the next real downturn, and that whoever inherits Blackstone's block is a steady hand. Track two things and you track the whole story — the quarterly credit-cost figure as AUM marches from ₹30,000 Cr toward ₹50,000 Cr, and the identity of the eventual controlling owner. The day a field officer's judgement in a Tier-3 tailoring shop stops translating into a rupee-in-a-hundred loss rate is the day the thesis needs re-writing; until then, the machine keeps compounding.
