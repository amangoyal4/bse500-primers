SKF India, India's default bearing, now splitting in two

A full walk through the country's leading maker of bearings — the small, precise steel rings that let every wheel, motor and gearbox in the economy turn without grinding itself apart — how a 100-year-old Swedish franchise built a near-annuity out of replacement demand, and why, in October 2025, the company sawed itself cleanly into an automotive half and an industrial half just as its parent did the same thing worldwide.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

SKF India makes bearings — the precision-ground steel rings, packed with balls or rollers, that sit between any two parts that spin against each other and carry the load while letting them turn smoothly. Wheels, electric motors, gearboxes, pumps, railway axles, wind turbines, machine tools: none of them rotate for long without a bearing inside. SKF India is the Indian arm of AB SKF of Sweden, the company that invented the modern self-aligning ball bearing in 1907, and it is the market leader in a tight domestic bearings oligopoly. Beyond bearings it also sells seals, lubrication systems, condition-monitoring mechatronics and engineering services — all the things that keep rotating machinery alive.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • It just cut itself in half. On 1 October 2025 SKF India completed a court-approved demerger, separating its industrial business into a brand-new listed company, SKF India (Industrial) Ltd. Every shareholder received one industrial share for each SKF India share held. What remains under the ticker SKFINDIA is now essentially the automotive business. So the historic ~₹4,920 Cr, two-segment company you'll read about in older reports no longer trades as one line — it is now two separate stocks with two separate stories.
  • Half of demand is replacement, not new sales. A bearing wears out. Every truck, tractor, factory motor and railway coach eventually needs its bearings changed, and when it does, the mechanic reaches for the brand he trusts. SKF's aftermarket / replacement channel — sold through a deep distributor network — is a high-margin, recurring stream that keeps earning even when new-vehicle and new-machinery sales slump. It is the shock absorber that makes a cyclical component maker far steadier than it looks.
  • It is priced like a franchise, not a part. SKF India historically earns strong returns, pays out most of its profit as dividend, carries almost no debt, and trades at a rich multiple — the automotive listco changes hands near 50× earnings. The market does not value SKF as a commodity steel-stamping shop; it values the brand, the technology licence from Sweden, and the annuity. Whether that premium survives the split into two smaller, more focused companies is the live question.
In plain terms

A bearing is a boring-looking doughnut of hardened steel that happens to be one of the most load-bearing objects in the entire industrial economy — get it wrong and machines seize, catch fire or fly apart. SKF India is the premium supplier of that doughnut, plus the grease, seals and sensors that go around it. Its quiet superpower is that customers keep coming back not once, when they build a machine, but again and again for the life of the machine, to replace the part. In 2025 the company decided its two very different customer worlds — carmakers on one side, railways and cement plants on the other — deserved to be run and owned separately, and split accordingly. The primer below is really about one franchise that has just become two.

01

Bearings 101: the part that lets things turn

No engineering degree needed — just why this unglamorous component is quietly critical.

Any time one part rotates relative to another — a wheel on an axle, a shaft in a motor — the two surfaces would otherwise rub, heat up, wear and eventually weld themselves together. A bearing solves this by placing a ring of hard, perfectly round rolling elements (balls or cylindrical rollers) between an inner and an outer ring, so the surfaces roll past each other instead of grinding. It sounds trivial. It is not: a bearing must hold enormous loads, spin thousands of times a minute for years, survive heat, dirt and vibration, and do it to tolerances measured in microns. That precision is the whole business.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
BearingThe core product: two steel rings with rolling elements (balls / rollers) and a cage between them. SKF's ball and roller bearings are the heart of everything it sells.
OEM vs aftermarketOEM = sold to a manufacturer to build into a new car or machine. Aftermarket = sold as a spare to replace a worn one. Aftermarket is smaller in volume but far richer in margin — and it is the annuity.
Content per vehicleThe rupee value of SKF parts inside one vehicle. A truck or an EV needs more (and bigger) bearings than a scooter, so richer product mix lifts revenue without selling more units.
Seals & lubricationA bearing dies if grit gets in or grease runs out. Seals keep contaminants out; lubrication systems keep the film of grease in. SKF sells these around the bearing to raise value per sale.
LocalisationThe share of what SKF sells in India that is made in India versus imported from the global SKF network. Higher localisation means better margins and less currency risk — a core capex theme.
Why a doughnut of steel commands a premium price

The reason SKF can charge more than a no-name maker is consequence of failure. If a ₹200 bearing seizes inside a ₹40-lakh machine tool, a wind turbine 120 metres up, or the axle of a railway coach carrying 1,000 people, the cost of that failure dwarfs the price of the part many times over. So the buyer of a critical bearing is not really shopping on price — they are buying the confidence that it will not fail. That is why bearings behave less like a commodity and more like a trust-based, brand-led product: reliability, engineering support and a century-old track record are worth paying up for. It is also why the industry stays concentrated — a new entrant cannot simply undercut on price into a market that fears failure above all.

Where bearings are used

  • Automotive — wheel hubs, gearboxes, engines, clutches, alternators; a modern car contains dozens of bearings, and EVs shift the mix toward the electric drivetrain.
  • Railways — axle-box bearings on every wagon and coach, a heavily-specified, safety-critical, long-life application that India is buying more of as it expands rail.
  • Heavy & process industry — cement, steel, mining, pulp and paper: giant slow-turning bearings under crushing loads, where downtime is ruinously expensive.
  • Energy & general machinery — wind-turbine main shafts and gearboxes, pumps, fans, compressors, electric motors and machine tools across every factory floor.
02

What SKF India actually sells: bearing plus everything around it

Read this once and the company's two-market shape clicks into place.

SKF India's revenue has two natural cuts. By product, it is bearings first, then the seals, lubrication systems and mechatronics that surround them. By end-market, it is two very different worlds — automotive and industrial — that behave on different cycles, sell through different channels, and have now been split into two companies. Understanding both cuts is understanding SKF.

By product, bearings do most of the heavy lifting — roughly three-quarters of revenue — with seals (~a tenth), lubrication systems and mechatronics & services making up the rest. Those surrounding "adjacencies" are small individually but strategically vital: they are where SKF raises the value of each customer relationship and defends against a pure-bearing rival. The more important cut, though, is by end-market — and that is the one the company just turned into two separate companies.

The two end-markets, decoded

  • Automotive — bearings and related parts for cars, two-wheelers, trucks, tractors and, increasingly, EVs. Sold to vehicle makers (OEM) and through a large auto-spares aftermarket. Higher volume, more price-competitive, tied to the auto cycle. This is what the listed SKFINDIA company now is.
  • Industrial — larger, more specialised bearings for railways, wind, cement, steel, mining and general machinery, plus a rich industrial distribution/aftermarket. Higher value per unit, stickier engineering relationships, tied to the capex and infrastructure cycle. This is now the separate SKF India (Industrial) Ltd.
  • Seals, lubrication & mechatronics — the adjacencies that ride along in both worlds. They deepen the relationship (a customer buying the bearing, the seal, the grease system and the sensor is far harder to displace) and lift the average value of every machine SKF touches.
Why "sell the system, not the part" matters

A rival can copy a single bearing. It is much harder to copy the whole rotating-equipment package — the right bearing, the seal that keeps it clean, the automatic lubricator that keeps it fed, the sensor that warns before it fails, and the engineer who specifies all four for a specific machine. By bundling, SKF turns a one-off component sale into a designed-in relationship that a purchasing manager is reluctant to unpick to save a few rupees on the bearing alone. It is also how a premium supplier justifies its price in a market where a cheaper ring is always available: it is not selling the ring, it is selling uptime.

03

The parent, the royalty, and who controls it

A Swedish multinational's Indian subsidiary — technology in, royalty out.

SKF India is a majority-owned subsidiary of AB SKF, the Gothenburg-based group that has made bearings since 1907 and operates in over 100 countries. The parent holds roughly 52–53% of SKF India (through group entities), which puts the company firmly in the camp of Indian-listed MNC subsidiaries: world-class technology and brand flow in from the parent; a royalty / technology fee and dividends flow back out. For an investor, that MNC structure is both the moat and the friction.

The road here

  • 1907
    AB SKF founded in Sweden; engineer Sven Wingquist invents the self-aligning ball bearing — the product that created the modern bearing industry.
  • 1920s–1960s
    SKF establishes an Indian presence, first through imports and then local manufacturing; the group's flagship Indian bearing plant is built at Pune (Chinchwad).
  • 1990s–2010s
    Listed on BSE & NSE; capacity expands across Pune, Bangalore and Haridwar; the business broadens from pure bearings into seals, lubrication and services.
  • Sep 2024
    Parent AB SKF announces it will separate its worldwide automotive business and list it independently — the global decision that sets the Indian split in motion.
  • Oct 2025
    SKF India completes the demerger of its industrial business (effective 1 Oct; record date 15 Oct). Shareholders get 1 share of SKF India (Industrial) Ltd for every SKF India share.
  • Nov 2025
    The industrial entity lists separately; the residual listed company becomes, in substance, SKF's Indian automotive business.
  • Mid-2026
    AB SKF targets a Nasdaq Stockholm listing of its global automotive arm — the parent-level mirror of the Indian split.

Ownership & the related-party question

  • Promoter: AB SKF and its group entities hold roughly 52.6%. The rest is public — with an unusually high domestic-institutional (mutual-fund/insurer) presence (~27%) alongside foreign investors (~7%), a sign of how much Indian institutions like this franchise.
  • The royalty / related-party flows: because the parent supplies technology, brand and imported product, SKF India pays a technology royalty and buys/sells with SKF group companies. These related-party dealings are legitimate and disclosed, but they mean minority shareholders should watch that royalty rates, transfer pricing and import sourcing stay fair — value can quietly leak to the parent if they don't. This is the standard MNC-subsidiary vigilance, not a red flag.
  • Balance sheet: classically MNC-conservative — essentially debt-free, cash-generative, and a high dividend payout (often the bulk of profit, and in some years boosted by special dividends). SKF India funds its own capex and still hands cash back.
Why the parent's ownership cuts both ways: AB SKF gives the Indian company a century of bearing science, a globally trusted brand, and access to a worldwide product range no domestic rival can match — a genuine, durable moat. But the same parent sets the royalty, controls the technology, and decides the corporate structure (including this demerger) from Gothenburg. Minority holders ride a superb franchise while accepting that the biggest strategic calls are made abroad, in the parent's interest first.
04

The 2025 demerger: one company becomes two

The single most important thing that has happened to this stock — and it is specific to this moment.

For decades SKF India ran automotive and industrial under one roof and one share. On 1 October 2025 that ended. The company executed a court-approved demerger, carving the industrial business out into a newly incorporated, separately listed company — SKF India (Industrial) Ltd — while the automotive business stayed inside the original listed shell that still trades as SKFINDIA. If you owned one SKF India share, you now own one share of each. This is not a cosmetic reshuffle; it re-cuts the entire investment case.

Why do it at all? The logic is focus and matched capital. Automotive and industrial are genuinely different businesses that happened to share a factory network. Automotive lives on car and two-wheeler volumes, tight OEM pricing, and the EV transition; industrial lives on infrastructure capex, railways, wind and long engineering sales. Run together, each was managed to a compromise. Run apart, each gets its own board, its own capex plan, its own currency (a listed share to fund growth or reward staff), and its own valuation — the market can now price a fast-growing industrial infrastructure play differently from a steadier automotive one, rather than blending them into one muddy multiple.

The device that only fits SKF India right now: one balance sheet, two tickers, three decisions

Most companies you value as a single stream of cash. From October 2025, valuing "SKF India" means holding three distinct questions at once. First, what is the automotive listco (SKFINDIA) worth as a standalone — a ~50× auto-component franchise levered to EVs and content-per-vehicle? Second, what is SKF India (Industrial) Ltd worth as its own listed infrastructure/railway/wind bearing play? Third — the subtle one — does the sum of the two parts re-rate higher than the old combined company did, because focused, separately-owned businesses often command better multiples than a conglomerate discount? The demerger is a bet that 1 + 1 > 2. An investor who still thinks of "SKF India" as one thing is now, literally, mis-counting.

So the familiar name and code did not keep the crown jewel; the newly-listed spin-off did. Anyone buying "SKF India" out of habit needs to know they are buying the auto business, not the industrial one.
05

The global mirror: SKF's worldwide automotive spin-off

The Indian split is a local copy of a decision taken in Sweden.

The Indian demerger did not happen in isolation. In September 2024, parent AB SKF announced it would separate its entire worldwide automotive business and list it independently — targeting a listing on Nasdaq Stockholm by mid-2026. India's carve-out is the local execution of that global strategy. Understanding the parent's move tells you where the Indian pieces are heading.

The global rationale echoes the Indian one, only larger. SKF's automotive and industrial arms serve different customers, invest on different horizons, and are valued by the market on different logic. As a combined group, SKF traded at a blended multiple that arguably flattered neither. By splitting worldwide, the parent aims to let a focused, publicly-valued automotive company and a focused industrial company each attract the investors, capital and management attention suited to it — and to give each the freedom to strike its own partnerships, including in the fast-moving EV space, without the other's constraints.

Why the parent's plan matters for the Indian shareholder

Two things follow for anyone holding the Indian stocks. First, alignment: with SKF worldwide organised into a clean automotive company and a clean industrial company, the two Indian entities slot neatly into their respective global parents — same technology pipeline, same brand, cleaner reporting lines. Second, the ownership question down the line: whenever a global parent restructures, minorities should watch how the eventual parent-level ownership of each Indian listco is set, what royalty each pays, and whether the split changes the terms of the relationship. The global separation is a tailwind for clarity, but it also puts the parent through a once-in-a-generation reorganisation that the Indian companies are, unavoidably, passengers on.

The timing tell: SKF India moved fast — completing its Indian demerger (Oct 2025) ahead of the parent's own targeted Nasdaq listing (mid-2026). Getting the Indian structure clean early means both Indian entities are already trading as focused companies by the time the global automotive spin-off completes, rather than being reshuffled afterward. It is a sign the group wants the India pieces settled and independently valued before the bigger global event lands.
06

The aftermarket annuity: why a cyclical maker isn't as cyclical as it looks

The replacement business is the ballast under both halves of SKF.

Sell a bearing to a carmaker and you earn once, on the day the car is built. Sell a replacement bearing through the aftermarket and you earn again, years later, when the original wears out — and again after that. Because every machine SKF's parts go into eventually needs those parts replaced, the company sits on a large, self-renewing replacement annuity that keeps generating revenue long after the original sale, and keeps generating it even when new-vehicle and new-machinery sales are weak.

The mechanism is what makes it valuable. New-equipment (OEM) demand is cyclical — it booms and busts with the auto cycle and the capex cycle. But the installed base of machines already running in the field only ever grows, and all of it needs servicing regardless of whether anyone is buying new. So in a downturn, when OEM orders fall, the aftermarket keeps humming: the trucks on the road still break down, the factory motors still wear out, the railway coaches still need their axle bearings changed. The aftermarket is counter-cyclical ballast bolted onto a cyclical hull.

The counter-intuitive bit about brand and distribution

In the OEM channel, a bearing is bought by a professional purchasing team that squeezes every rupee — so pricing is tight. In the aftermarket, the buyer is a mechanic or a plant maintenance chief choosing a spare for a machine that must not fail again, often under time pressure, from whatever the trusted distributor stocks. That buyer will pay for the SKF name. So the same physical bearing earns a thin margin sold to a factory and a fat one sold as a branded spare — which is why SKF guards its distribution reach and brand as fiercely as its factories. A century-old logo and a shop that always has the part in stock are, in the aftermarket, worth more than the steel.

07

Automotive & the EV question

What the residual SKFINDIA stock now is — and why EVs don't kill it.

Post-demerger, the listed SKFINDIA company is essentially the automotive bearing business: wheel-end bearings, transmission and engine bearings, two-wheeler and tractor bearings, sold to vehicle makers and through the auto aftermarket. The obvious worry for any auto-component maker today is electrification — but bearings sit on the right side of that shift, which is the crux of this segment.

Directional. Bigger, higher-load vehicles (a truck, a tractor) carry more and larger bearings than a scooter. Crucially an EV is not a lower-content vehicle for a bearing maker — it just moves the content from the engine to the e-axle and motor.

Why EVs are not a threat — the key insight of this segment. Many auto-part makers face extinction from electrification: no engine means no pistons, no fuel injection, no exhaust. Bearings are different. An EV still has wheels, an e-axle, a reduction gearbox and electric-motor shafts — all of which spin, and all of which need bearings. If anything, the high-speed electric motor and the quiet-running requirements of an EV demand more specialised, higher-value bearings and seals (to handle high rpm and to prevent electrical currents damaging the bearing). SKF is essentially drivetrain-agnostic: petrol, diesel or electric, the wheels still turn on its bearings.

The growth levers that don't need the market to grow

SKF's automotive business has two ways to grow faster than vehicle sales. First, content per vehicle: designing in more parts, and higher-value EV-specific bearings and seals, so each vehicle is worth more to SKF even if total vehicles are flat. Second, localisation: making in India what it used to import from the global network, which lifts margins and shields the business from a weak rupee — a core reason the automotive arm is committing fresh capex to Indian plants (Pune, Bangalore, Haridwar). Both levers let the company outgrow the underlying auto cycle rather than merely ride it.

08

Industrial & the railway pull

The half that left — and why it may be the more exciting one.

The demerged SKF India (Industrial) Ltd is the larger, higher-value-per-part business, and it is levered to exactly the parts of the Indian economy that are being built out hardest: railways, renewable energy, cement, steel, mining and general machinery. Where the automotive arm sells millions of small bearings into consumer vehicles, the industrial arm sells fewer, bigger, more specified bearings into infrastructure — with stickier engineering relationships and a rich industrial aftermarket alongside.

Directional. The industrial arm's appeal is that it sells into India's infrastructure and energy build-out — railways and wind especially — through both new-project supply and a deep replacement channel.

The railway exposure is the standout. India is expanding and modernising its rail network — more coaches, more wagons, dedicated freight corridors, faster trains — and every one of them runs on heavily-specified, safety-critical axle-box bearings that must last decades and cannot be sourced casually. That is precisely the kind of high-trust, long-life application where a premium supplier with a century of track record wins, and where price is a secondary consideration to reliability and approval.

Why the industrial arm is committing the bigger capex

The industrial company has signalled the larger investment programme — a multi-hundred-crore plan through 2030, including a new Pune plant — precisely because its end-markets (railways, renewables, heavy industry) are where India's structural growth and localisation push are strongest. Two forces reinforce each other: the government wants more of these critical components made in India, and the demand is genuinely growing. A focused, separately-listed industrial company can raise and deploy that capital, and be valued on that growth, in a way it never could buried inside a combined auto-plus-industrial group.

Keep the two straight: this primer's ticker, SKFINDIA (BSE 500472), is now the automotive company. The railway/wind/heavy-industry business described in this section trades as the separate SKF India (Industrial) Ltd. Both descend from the same parent and share the SKF brand, but they are two different shares with two different growth profiles — do not conflate their financials.
09

The bearings oligopoly and the rivals

A concentrated market where three or four names split the serious business.

Bearings is not a fragmented, race-to-the-bottom industry. Because failure is so costly and precision so hard, the credible domestic market is dominated by a handful of scaled, brand-led players — SKF alongside Schaeffler, Timken and NRB — with a fringe of cheaper unbranded and imported product competing only where reliability matters less. This concentration is the reason the leaders sustain high margins and premium valuations.

The shape of the rivalry to hold in mind

Two features define this market. First, it is a rational oligopoly: SKF, Schaeffler and Timken are all premium, technology-led, brand-defended players who compete on engineering and reliability far more than on headline price — which keeps industry margins healthy and is a large part of why all of them trade richly. Second, the competition is segment-specific: Timken is strongest where big tapered rollers rule (railways, heavy trucks), NRB where needle bearings go (auto transmissions), Schaeffler broadly across both. SKF's distinction is breadth — it plays credibly everywhere and wraps the bearing in seals, lubrication and services that a single-product rival cannot match. The demerger sharpens this: a standalone SKF industrial company now meets Timken more directly on its own turf, and a standalone SKF automotive company meets Schaeffler and NRB on theirs.

10

Financials & valuation

A high-quality, cash-rich franchise — priced richly, and now reported in two pieces.

SKF India's financial character is easy to summarise: steady mid-single-digit-to-teens revenue growth, high-teens-to-20% operating margins, mid-teens returns on equity, almost no debt, and a generous dividend. It is a quality compounder, not a cyclical trader — and the market prices it accordingly. The complication now is that the historic combined numbers below no longer map to one tradeable share: from FY26 the company reports the automotive business as continuing operations, with the industrial half hived off.

On the valuation itself: SKF India has long traded at 40–55× earnings, a multiple that reflects the brand, the near-annuity aftermarket, the clean balance sheet and the MNC pedigree rather than any explosive growth rate. That is a demanding price for a business growing revenue at high single digits — it only makes sense if you believe the quality, the localisation runway and the two-market breadth justify paying up. The demerger adds a fresh twist: you are now valuing two separate multiples, and the market's willingness to pay ~50× for the smaller automotive remainder will be tested quarter by quarter against its actual growth.

The one number that settles the thesis: localisation-led margin, not just revenue

For a premium, slow-growth, richly-valued franchise like this, the metric that proves or breaks the case is not headline sales growth — it is operating margin, and specifically how much of it comes from making more in India rather than importing. SKF's premium rating rests on the belief that it can keep expanding margin (FY24's ~16% moving toward and past 20%) by localising production, enriching mix toward EV-specific and higher-value industrial bearings, and holding aftermarket pricing — all while related-party royalty and import costs stay contained. If margin keeps climbing on localisation and mix, a ~50× multiple on a high-quality compounder is defensible. If margin stalls while imports and royalty eat the gains, the same multiple looks like paying franchise prices for component economics. Watch the margin line and the localisation commentary each quarter; that is the scoreboard, in both listcos.

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A superb franchise, freshly halved, asked to prove the parts are worth more than the whole.

SKF India enters its post-demerger life as two focused, debt-free, brand-led leaders in a concentrated market, riding India's vehicle demand on one side and its infrastructure build-out on the other. The candid debate is not whether the businesses are good — they plainly are — but whether the demanding valuation is justified for each half separately, and whether the split genuinely unlocks value rather than simply doubling the number of expensive stocks.

The bull case

  • Market-leading franchise — the strongest brand and broadest range in a rational bearings oligopoly, with pricing power rooted in the cost of failure.
  • Aftermarket annuity — a large, high-margin replacement stream that keeps earning through downturns and steadies both halves.
  • EV-agnostic — electrification moves bearing content, it doesn't destroy it; EVs may even need higher-value bearings and seals.
  • Structural end-markets — railways, wind and localisation drive the industrial arm; content-per-vehicle and Make-in-India drive the automotive arm.
  • Focus & balance sheet — two debt-free, cash-generative companies, each now able to invest and be valued on its own merits.

The bear case

  • Rich valuation — ~50× earnings for a high-single-digit grower leaves the automotive listco exposed to any margin or volume disappointment.
  • Cyclicality — OEM demand still swings with the auto and capex cycles; the aftermarket softens but does not erase the swings.
  • MNC / related-party leakage — royalty, transfer pricing and import sourcing to the parent can quietly divert value from minorities.
  • Demerger execution — value only unlocks if both halves re-rate; a poorly-received industrial listing or a de-rated auto remainder undoes the logic.
  • Competition & imports — Schaeffler, Timken and NRB press each segment, and cheaper imports cap pricing at the low-consequence end.
The bottom line

SKF India spent a century turning a small steel ring into a trust-based franchise with a self-renewing replacement annuity, a fortress balance sheet and a premium price to match. In October 2025 it did something genuinely consequential: it split that franchise cleanly into an automotive company (still trading as SKFINDIA) and a separately-listed industrial company, mirroring its Swedish parent's worldwide reorganisation. The next chapters are legible and specific — the successful trading debut and re-rating of SKF India (Industrial) Ltd, the parent's Nasdaq automotive listing due by mid-2026, and, above all, whether each Indian half keeps pushing operating margin higher through localisation and mix. If the two focused companies each compound margin and defend their premium, the split will have made shareholders richer than the combined company ever could. If they don't, investors will have paid franchise multiples, twice over, for parts of a business that were worth more together. Follow the margin line in each listco; it tells you which of those two outcomes is arriving.
