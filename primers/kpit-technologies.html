00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

KPIT Technologies writes software for cars and other vehicles — and almost nothing else. It is one of the world's largest independent specialists in the code that runs the modern vehicle: the software that controls electric powertrains and batteries, the middleware that lets a car's many computers talk to each other, the connected-and-cockpit systems a driver actually touches, and the driver-assistance / autonomous stack. Its customers are the global carmakers (OEMs) and their big Tier-1 suppliers. Headquartered in Pune, it employs roughly 12,500 engineers across India, Europe, the US and Asia.

Three facts frame everything:

  • A rare pure-play. Where most Indian engineering-services firms spread across aerospace, medical devices, telecom, plant and rail, KPIT points ~90%+ of its revenue at a single industry — automotive and mobility. That focus is exactly why it grew faster and earned fatter margins than diversified rivals for a decade. It is also why FY26 hurt so much.
  • The growth engine stalled in a single year. Constant-currency revenue growth collapsed from ~18.7% in FY25 to ~1.3% in FY26 as carmakers cut and deferred software spending. Nothing about KPIT's capability changed; the cheque-books it depends on simply closed for a year. Reported profit (PAT) fell from ~₹840 Cr to ~₹637 Cr, and earnings per share slid from ~₹30.63 to ~₹23.25.
  • The market darling de-rated hard. A stock that had traded near ~60× earnings — priced as a structural winner of the electric, software-defined car — fell roughly 55% in a year (from ~₹1,328 to ~₹556) and now trades around 24×. The whole investment question is now simply: was FY26 a pause, or a re-rating of what this business really is?
In plain terms

A modern car is turning into a computer on wheels — one that gets better after you buy it, through updates sent over the air, the way your phone does. Building that software is enormous, specialised work, and carmakers cannot hire fast enough to do all of it in-house. KPIT is the outside engineering team they hand a big slice of it to. For ten years that was a wonderful place to stand: the work only grew, and KPIT could charge well for scarce skills. Then in FY26 several of those carmakers, squeezed by a slower-than-hoped EV transition and their own cost problems, pressed pause on new software programs at the same time — and a company that lives off one industry felt the whole industry's flinch at once.

01

When a car becomes a computer: the software-defined vehicle

No engineering degree required — just the one shift that reorganised the whole car business.

For a century, a car was defined by its hardware: the engine, the gearbox, the metal. Electronics were bolted on as afterthoughts — dozens of small, single-purpose computers, each doing one job, wired together in a tangle. The software-defined vehicle (SDV) inverts that. In an SDV, the car's features live in software running on a few powerful central computers, and the hardware becomes the thing the software commands. What the car is — how the motor delivers torque, how the cabin behaves, what the driver-assist can do — is increasingly decided in code, and can be changed after the car is sold through over-the-air updates.

Three forces pushed this from a slide-deck idea into the axis the entire auto industry now turns on:

  • Electrification. An electric vehicle has no century of mechanical convention to inherit. Battery management, motor control, charging, thermal management and energy efficiency are all software problems — and getting them a few percent better directly extends range, the number EV buyers care about most.
  • The feature race. Buyers now compare cars the way they compare phones: the screen, the assistant, the driver-assistance, the app. Those features are software, and they are how a carmaker differentiates when two EVs have similar batteries and motors.
  • Consolidation of the computers. The industry is collapsing 100-plus scattered little control units into a handful of high-performance computers running a shared software platform. That re-architecture is a once-in-a-generation rebuild — and it is precisely the kind of deep, multi-year work an outside specialist gets hired for.
The one number that reframes the car business

A premium car today can carry well over 100 million lines of software code — more than a modern airliner or a desktop operating system — and the industry expects software to grow from a small slice of a vehicle's engineering budget toward roughly half of it over this decade. That single migration is the tailwind under KPIT's entire existence: every rupee that shifts from bending metal to writing code is a rupee that flows toward the firms who write the code. The catch, which FY26 made vivid, is that this budget is discretionary — a carmaker under cash pressure can slow it in a quarter, in a way it can never slow the assembly line.

Why "automotive software" is its own trade

This is not ordinary IT, and it is not ordinary product design. Vehicle software is safety-critical and heavily standardised: brake and steering code must meet functional-safety rules (ISO 26262), must run on constrained automotive chips in real time, and must be validated against standards like AUTOSAR — the industry's agreed way of structuring a car's software so parts from different suppliers fit together. It takes years to build engineers who can do it and reputations that carmakers trust with their safety systems. That difficulty is the moat; it is also why the talent is scarce and expensive, and why a firm that has spent two decades accumulating it — like KPIT — cannot be conjured overnight by a new entrant.

Open the hood on the revenue and you find four connected software practices, not a grab-bag of projects.

KPIT organises itself around the layers of the software-defined car. It is useful to see them as a stack — from the motor at the bottom to the driver at the top — because a single carmaker often buys across several layers, and because each layer carries a different growth rhythm and a different competitive set.

The domains, in plain language

  • Powertrain & electrification — the code that turns stored energy into motion: battery-management systems that keep cells safe and squeeze out range, motor and inverter control, charging, and the thermal software that stops a battery cooking. This is KPIT's heritage strength and its single biggest revenue pool, riding directly on the global EV build-out.
  • Vehicle OS & middleware — the least visible but arguably most strategic layer. Middleware is the plumbing that sits between the car's hardware and its apps, so software written by many suppliers runs together on shared computers. AUTOSAR is the standard here. Owning this layer makes KPIT hard to dislodge, because it becomes woven into the OEM's core platform.
  • Connected & digital cockpit — everything the driver sees and touches: instrument clusters, infotainment, telematics, and the over-the-air update machinery that keeps a car improving after purchase. This is where the "feels like a phone" competition plays out.
  • ADAS & autonomous — advanced driver-assistance (lane-keeping, automated braking, parking) climbing toward higher autonomy. The most complex, most safety-critical and most compute-hungry domain, demanding heavy validation and simulation.
Staffing versus platforms — the mix that decides the margin

Two ways to sell this work sit inside KPIT. The lower-value way is bodies-on-projects: renting skilled engineers by the hour, priced against wages. The higher-value way is IP-and-platform-led: KPIT brings its own reusable software building blocks, accelerators and platforms, so it charges for outcomes and re-uses the same asset across many carmakers. KPIT has deliberately pushed its mix toward the second — its own middleware stacks, its diagnostics and testing platforms, its integration frameworks. That is a big part of why its margins sat above most Indian engineering-services peers: it wasn't just selling time, it was selling a product wrapped in a service.

03

The company and who controls it

From a 1990s Pune systems shop to the demerger that created a pure-play — a founder-led story.

KPIT is a founder-built, founder-run company out of Pune. Its two central figures are Ravi Pandit, the co-founder and chairman who set the long-term automotive bet, and Kishor Patil, co-founder, CEO and managing director, who runs it. The business you can buy today is the product of a decisive 2019 corporate surgery that cut a diversified IT firm in two and kept the automotive half.

The 2019 demerger — the move that made KPIT a pure-play

Until 2019, "KPIT" was a broad IT and engineering company (long known as KPIT Cummins) that did both generic enterprise IT services and automotive engineering. In a three-way reorganisation with Birlasoft, the group split into two separately listed companies: the enterprise-IT-services business became today's Birlasoft, and the automotive engineering business was carved out as the new, focused KPIT Technologies. The point was concentration — freeing the auto-software business to chase the SDV wave without an unrelated IT-services arm attached. That single decision is why KPIT is a pure automotive-software play and its former sibling is a general IT firm.

Ownership & management

  • Promoters hold roughly 39.4%. The founder group — Ravi Pandit (chairman) and Kishor Patil (CEO & MD) — remains at the centre, giving KPIT the continuity of people who chose the automotive-only bet and have run it through a full cycle.
  • The rest is largely institutional — domestic and foreign funds that bought the SDV growth story. That ownership mix is part of why the FY26 de-rating was so sharp: a growth-priced stock held by growth investors re-rates violently when the growth disappears.
  • Balance sheet: KPIT runs net cash with high returns on capital (ROCE in the mid-20s%, ROE ~21%), funding acquisitions and platform investment from its own cash flow rather than debt — a cushion that matters most precisely in a down-year like FY26.

The client model: a few carmakers, held very close

KPIT's greatest strength and its sharpest risk are the same sentence — it depends on a handful of large OEMs.

KPIT does not chase thousands of small customers. Its stated strategy is to go deep with a concentrated set of the world's largest carmakers — internally framed around the top ~25 global OEMs ("T25") — and to embed itself so thoroughly in each that it becomes part of the OEM's core software team, on multi-year, expanding programs. A small number of anchor accounts therefore drive a large share of revenue, and KPIT's top clients each represent very substantial, sticky relationships.

The genuine surprise — this Indian IT company is really a European one

Ask most people to place an Indian software exporter and they picture US banks and US retailers. KPIT breaks the mould: its largest market is Europe, because that is where the deepest software-defined-vehicle programs — German luxury marques, European mass-market groups — actually sit. That flips two things a generalist would assume. First, KPIT's currency exposure leans on the euro as much as the dollar, so European exchange rates move its rupee revenue. Second, its fortunes are tied to European auto health — and it was exactly the pressure on European and global OEM budgets that drove the FY26 stall. The strength (proximity to the most advanced SDV work) and the vulnerability (dependence on a stressed European auto sector) are the same fact.

Why concentration cuts both ways

  • The upside of few, deep clients. Landing inside a top OEM's software platform means years of visible, expanding work; high switching costs (the OEM would have to unpick KPIT from its core systems); and the chance to sell across all four domains into the same account. Deep beats broad in safety-critical software.
  • The downside is symmetrical. When a few accounts carry most of the revenue, a budget cut at even one or two anchor OEMs lands disproportionately — there is no long tail of small clients to cushion it. FY26 was that risk made real: a synchronised pull-back at a cluster of large customers, and the whole top line went flat.
  • One industry, no hedge. A diversified engineering firm can lean on aerospace when autos wobble. KPIT, being ~90%+ automotive, has no other vertical to lean on. That is the price of the focus that made it special on the way up.
The concentration question: KPIT's whole design — few clients, one industry, deep integration — is a leveraged bet on the automotive software cycle. In an up-cycle that leverage is the reason it out-grew everyone. In FY26's down-cycle the same leverage is why it fell hardest. Watching whether revenue broadens beyond the top handful of OEMs is therefore not a footnote; it is central to de-risking the story.

KPIT's growth is built less on winning many small contracts and more on a few strategic, multi-year platform partnerships with major carmakers. These are structurally different from ordinary outsourcing: KPIT is chosen as a long-term software-scaling partner and dedicates hundreds — sometimes thousands — of engineers, its own IP, and its platforms to the OEM's roadmap. Notice the shape: these are not staffing contracts, they are strategic partnerships with defined horizons and dedicated headcount. The FY26 growth stall — and the de-rating

The crux of the entire story: a market-leading growth engine went from 18.7% to 1.3% in twelve months.

For years KPIT was the growth star of Indian engineering services — the firm that grew fastest and earned among the best margins because it had picked the one right vertical at the right moment. Then FY26 delivered the sharpest reversal in its listed life. Constant-currency revenue growth — the cleanest read on underlying demand, stripped of exchange-rate noise — fell from ~18.7% in FY25 to ~1.3% in FY26. Dollar revenue crept up just ~4.8% to $724.8M. Why it happened — the causes, honestly

  • The EV slowdown. The electric-vehicle transition, which had been the growth premise, ran slower than the industry hoped in key Western markets. When EV volumes disappoint, the software programs feeding them get re-timed.
  • OEM cost pressure. Several large carmakers — European ones especially — hit their own profitability and cash problems and responded by cutting discretionary R&D, including outsourced software. Software budgets are easier to defer than factories.
  • Program deferrals, not cancellations. Much of the lost growth is work pushed right rather than killed — SDV programs delayed, ramp-ups slowed. That is the bull's comfort and the bear's uncertainty: deferred demand can return, or can keep slipping.
  • Concentration amplified it. Because a few OEMs drive most of KPIT's revenue, these cuts did not average out across a broad base — they hit the top line almost directly.
A device worth sitting with

Here is the uncomfortable, clarifying fact: KPIT did not get worse at anything in FY26. Same engineers, same platforms, same deep OEM relationships, same industry-leading skills. Its margin held near ~20.8%; its balance sheet stayed net-cash; it kept winning deals. And yet growth fell ~17 percentage points, profit dropped from ~₹840 Cr to ~₹637 Cr, and the market took more than half the market value away. The whole move came from the outside — from a handful of customers deciding, in the same window, to spend less. That is the double-edge of a pure-play written in a single year: when you are this leveraged to one industry's discretionary budget, your own excellence sets the ceiling, but your customers' caution sets the floor — and in FY26 the floor moved.

The de-rating — from ~60× to ~24×

Markets pay up for growth and punish its absence, and KPIT had been priced for a lot of it. A stock that changed hands near ~60× earnings when it was compounding in the high-teens saw two things go against it at once in FY26: the "E" fell (EPS from ~₹30.63 to ~₹23.25) and the multiple the market would pay compressed. The share price fell roughly 55% — from around ₹1,328 to ~₹556 — and the trailing P/E now sits around 24×. The de-rating was not a small trim; it was the market re-appraising what kind of business this is.

The industry and the rivals

KPIT competes on three fronts at once — Indian ER&D peers, global auto-software specialists, and the OEMs' own in-house teams.

Automotive software is a crowded, well-funded arena, and KPIT's rivals do not all look alike. Some are broad Indian engineering-services firms that also do autos; some are global players built specifically for cars; and — most under-appreciated — the biggest competitor of all is often the carmaker itself deciding to build in-house. KPIT's distinctive claim is depth-of-focus: it is the one large listed name whose entire identity is the software-defined vehicle.

Where KPIT wins, and where it is exposed

KPIT's edge is being the specialist that a carmaker calls when the software job is big, safety-critical and central to the SDV rebuild — its focus, its accumulated IP in powertrain and middleware, and cost-competitive Indian engineering scale. Its exposure is the mirror image. Against diversified peers, it has no other vertical to soften an auto downturn. The FY26 stall was partly cyclical spend cuts; the longer question is how much of the SDV software value chain the OEMs ultimately keep for themselves.

08

The AI-in-engineering question

Does generative AI shrink KPIT's headcount-driven revenue, or make each engineer worth more?

Every engineering-services firm now faces the same interrogation: if AI can generate, test and validate code, does that erode the people-hours KPIT bills, or does it let the same engineers ship far more software and win more work? The answer is genuinely unsettled, and it matters more for a software-intensive firm like KPIT than for most.

The deflation worry

  • A meaningful chunk of revenue still tracks engineer count and hours; if AI makes coding and testing dramatically faster, per-project billing could compress.
  • Clients may demand AI-driven productivity be passed back as price cuts, squeezing the very margins that made KPIT special.
  • Commodity, lower-complexity work is the most automatable — and the first to be repriced.

The amplification case

  • Vehicle software demand is expanding faster than talent can be hired; AI helps close a shortage, not replace paid work.
  • Safety-critical automotive code needs heavy validation and human accountability — exactly where full automation is hardest and slowest.
  • AI tilts the mix toward KPIT's higher-value, IP-and-platform work and away from raw staffing — potentially margin-accretive.
How to weigh it without hand-waving

The most likely path is not "AI destroys the model" or "AI changes nothing," but a re-pricing: routine coding and test-generation get cheaper and more automated, while the scarce, defensible value migrates further up — to system architecture, functional safety, integration across the four domains, and the reusable platforms KPIT owns. A firm that had only sold hours would be threatened. A firm that has deliberately built middleware, diagnostics and integration IP has something to sell that AI augments rather than erases. The real test is observable in the numbers over time: if KPIT can hold or lift revenue per engineer and its EBITDA margin as it deploys AI internally, the amplification case is winning; if margins bleed as clients claw back the productivity, the deflation worry is.

How growth re-accelerates

The bull path is concrete — deferred programs restart, anchors scale, and the software share of the car keeps climbing.

The case for KPIT is not that FY26 was imaginary — it wasn't — but that the forces that stalled it are cyclical while the force under it is secular. If that is right, growth re-accelerates through a handful of identifiable levers rather than hope.

What could keep it stuck

  • A structurally slower auto industry. If Western OEMs remain cash-constrained and cautious for years, deferred programs keep slipping rather than restarting.
  • Insourcing. If carmakers decide the SDV software layer is too strategic to outsource and build it in-house, KPIT's addressable pie shrinks structurally, not cyclically.
  • Pricing pressure from AI. If clients capture AI productivity as lower prices, growth in value lags growth in volume.
The next chapter is not written in a new theme but in a cadence: quarter by quarter, does constant-currency growth climb back off the floor while the margin holds — and does the Honda-to-2030 ramp arrive on schedule?
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuinely good business, re-priced by a genuinely bad year — the debate is which one FY26 represents.

KPIT remains a high-quality operator: focused, cash-generative, best-in-class margins, embedded in the software rebuild of the global car, run by the founders who chose that path. FY26 did not break the business; it exposed how much of the business rides on other people's discretionary budgets. The investment question is whether the market's ~55% haircut over-corrected for a cyclical air-pocket or correctly re-rated a more cyclical, more concentrated firm than the 60× multiple had assumed.

The bull case

  • Structural tailwind intact — software's share of the car keeps rising for years; the SDV rebuild is a decade-long project, not a fad.
  • Deep, contracted anchors — Honda to 2030, Renault from 2026, embedded platform work that is hard to unpick.
  • Best-in-class economics — ~20.8% EBITDA margin held through the stall; net cash, high ROCE, self-funded.
  • Deferrals can return — much of the FY26 loss was work pushed right, not lost.
  • Cheaper entry — ~24× for a leader in automotive software is a fraction of the peak multiple.

The bear case

  • Client & vertical concentration — one industry, a few OEMs; an auto-software-spend cut hits disproportionately, as FY26 proved.
  • Cyclical, discretionary demand — OEM R&D budgets can slow fast; the growth may be structurally lumpier than believed.
  • Insourcing & GenAI — carmakers building in-house, or AI repricing engineering hours, could cap the pie or the margin.
  • Still not cheap — ~24× assumes re-acceleration; if growth stays near flat, that multiple is generous.
  • Europe/USD exposure — tied to a stressed European auto sector and to currency swings.
The bottom line

KPIT is the purest listed bet on the idea that the car is becoming a computer — and FY26 was the year that bet reminded everyone it runs on someone else's spending decisions. The business quality is not really in dispute; the growth durability is. The single verdict to watch is constant-currency revenue growth pulling off its ~1% floor while the margin stays in the ~20–21% band and the revenue base broadens beyond the top few OEMs. The concrete milestone that would prove the thesis is not abstract: it is the FY27 second half accelerating as management has guided, and the Honda software-defined-mobility program scaling toward its 2030 headcount on plan. If those arrive, the de-rated leader of automotive software looks mispriced; if the second half slips again, the market will have been right to treat FY26 as a reset rather than a pause.
