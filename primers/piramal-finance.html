00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Piramal Finance is a diversified non-bank lender (NBFC) controlled by the Ajay Piramal family. It borrows money wholesale and lends it out — increasingly as small, granular retail loans (home loans, loans against property, used-car, personal, small-business and gold loans) to customers in smaller-town, "Bharat" India, through a network of ~517 branches serving ~5.7 million customers across 26 states. It is classed by the RBI as an "upper-layer" NBFC — the largest, most systemically important tier.

Three facts frame everything about this company:

  • It's a business mid-transformation. Five years ago Piramal was a wholesale lender — big, chunky loans to real-estate developers and corporates. Today 85% of its ₹1,01,230 Cr book is retail, and the old risky wholesale pile has been deliberately shrunk. The whole investment case is: is the turn from a scarred wholesale lender into a clean retail one now essentially done?
  • The DHFL acquisition changed everything. In 2021 Piramal bought the bankrupt housing-finance giant DHFL through the insolvency courts (IBC) for ~₹34,250 Cr. Overnight it inherited a nationwide branch network and a million retail customers — instantly flipping the group from wholesale-heavy toward retail-led. It remains the pivot on which the modern company turns.
  • It just simplified and re-listed itself. In 2025 the listed parent Piramal Enterprises reverse-merged into its own lending subsidiary and renamed itself Piramal Finance, which began trading under the new NSE symbol PIRAMALFIN on 7 November 2025. FY26 profit tripled to ₹1,506 Cr (flattered by one-off gains), and the stock trades at roughly 1.5–1.7× book — the market's way of saying "we half-believe the turnaround."
In plain terms

Picture a lender that spent years cleaning up after a bad decade — it had lent too much to a handful of property developers, got burned, and decided to reinvent itself as a maker of thousands of small, boring, spread-out loans instead of a few giant risky ones. To get there fast, it bought a collapsed home-loan company out of bankruptcy court and used its branches as a ready-made retail machine. Today the clean-up is nearly finished and the retail engine is humming — profits are rising and the bad old loans are almost gone. The open question the share price is really asking: now that it looks like a normal, well-run retail lender, should it be valued like one? Note carefully — this is the financial-services Piramal. The old Piramal pharmaceuticals business was hived off years ago and lists separately as Piramal Pharma.

01

NBFC economics 101: lending without a bank licence

No finance degree required — just the handful of ideas that drive every non-bank lender.

A Non-Banking Financial Company (NBFC) does one core thing a bank does — it lends money — but it is not allowed to take ordinary public deposits the way a bank does. That single restriction shapes its entire economics. Understand it, and Piramal's numbers, risks and strategy all make sense.

The only ideas you need to read an NBFC
TermWhat it means (and why it matters for Piramal)
AUM"Assets Under Management" — the total pile of loans it has out earning interest. This is an NBFC's top-line scale metric. Piramal's is ~₹1,01,230 Cr (Mar '26), up 25% in a year.
No CASABanks fund themselves with cheap current & savings accounts (CASA) — money the public parks with them near-free. An NBFC has none of that; it must borrow the money it lends from banks, bond markets and other lenders, at a market rate. So its funding is dearer and it lives or dies on access to wholesale funding.
Spread / NIMThe NBFC lends at a higher rate than it borrows; the gap is the "net interest margin" (NIM) — its gross profit engine. Piramal's exit NIM was ~6.5% in Q4 FY26. A wider spread on retail loans is exactly why it is shifting toward retail.
ALM"Asset–Liability Management" — matching the timing of what it lends (often long, e.g. 15-year home loans) against what it borrows (often shorter). A mismatch is what actually killed DHFL and IL&FS. Getting ALM right is survival, not optional.
GNPA / Stage-3Loans that have gone bad. "Gross NPA" (or IND-AS "Stage-3") is the % of the book that's non-performing. Piramal's GNPA is ~2.3% — the health-of-the-book number.
RoA · RoE"Return on Assets" (profit ÷ total assets) and "Return on Equity" (profit ÷ shareholders' money). For an NBFC an RoA of ~2%+ is good; RoE shows how hard the equity is working. Piramal is climbing back toward both.
Why "borrow-to-lend" is the whole game

An NBFC is essentially an arbitrage on money: borrow at, say, 8–9%, lend at 12–16%, and keep the spread — after paying for staff, branches, and (crucially) the loans that go bad. Three levers drive profit: grow the book (more AUM), widen the spread (lend to higher-yielding retail segments while keeping borrowing cheap), and keep credit costs low (don't let too many loans default). Because it can't rely on sticky cheap deposits, an NBFC also has to obsess over two things a bank worries about less: liquidity (can it always roll over its borrowings?) and capital adequacy (does it hold enough equity cushion against losses?). Piramal's capital adequacy (CRAR) is a very comfortable ~19.8% — it is over-capitalised, which is both a safety feature and, as we'll see, a drag on RoE.

Why the RBI made Piramal list at all

In 2021 the RBI created an "upper-layer" (NBFC-UL) category for the biggest, most systemically important non-banks and required them to be publicly listed. Piramal's lending arm was an upper-layer NBFC but was itself unlisted (only its bonds were listed). That listing mandate is a direct cause of the 2025 merger described in section 04 — the group had to put the lender itself on the exchange.

02

The two-AUM story: one company, two very different books

Read this once and Piramal's entire strategy — and its messy accounts — click into place.

The single most important idea for understanding Piramal is that its loan book is really two books glued together, moving in opposite directions. There is a growth book (retail + a new, disciplined wholesale) that management is deliberately expanding, and a legacy book (old, risky, chunky wholesale loans) that management is deliberately running down to near-zero. Almost every quirk in the financials comes from the tension between the two.

Why "two books" matters for what you see in the accounts

The growth book generates the rising interest income and the compounding — it is what a healthy retail NBFC looks like. The legacy book generates provisions, write-offs and lumpy one-offs — when an old developer loan is finally recognised as bad, Piramal takes a provision (a charge against profit); when it later recovers more than expected on a written-down asset, it books a gain. That's why Piramal's reported profit can look jumpy: a good quarter for the growth book can be masked by a big legacy provision, or flattered by a legacy recovery. The mental trick analysts use: look at the growth book's steady progress separately from the legacy book's clean-up noise. As the legacy pile shrinks toward zero, the reported numbers should converge on the "true" underlying retail profitability — and that convergence is the entire re-rating thesis.

The "run-down" in one number

The clearest way to see the transformation: the group's legacy structured real-estate wholesale book fell from ~₹43,500 Cr in 2022 to under ~₹5,900 Cr — a deliberate, multi-year shrink of roughly 85%. Every rupee taken out of that risky legacy pile and redeployed into thousands of small retail loans lowers the company's risk and, eventually, lifts the multiple the market is willing to pay.

03

The DHFL deal — the acquisition that rewired the company

The single transaction that flipped Piramal from wholesale-heavy to retail-led overnight.

You cannot understand modern Piramal Finance without DHFL. Dewan Housing Finance (DHFL) was one of India's largest home-loan companies until a 2019 default and fraud allegations sent it into insolvency. In 2021, Piramal acquired the bankrupt DHFL through India's Insolvency & Bankruptcy Code (IBC) — the first-ever resolution of a financial firm under that law. In one move, Piramal bought itself a nationwide retail machine it would have taken a decade to build.

What Piramal actually got

  • A ready-made retail network — DHFL's ~300 branches, thousands of employees and roughly one million lifetime retail home-loan customers across two-dozen states. This is the seed of today's ~517-branch, ~5.7-million-customer retail franchise.
  • A large book of granular home loans — exactly the kind of small, diversified, secured retail lending Piramal wanted more of, in the semi-urban and small-town markets it was targeting.
  • A distressed-asset pool bought cheap — the deal (~₹34,250 Cr total: ~₹14,700 Cr upfront cash + ~₹19,550 Cr of 10-year bonds issued to creditors) valued much of DHFL's stressed and disputed assets at a deep discount. Creditors recovered only ~46% of their dues — meaning Piramal paid well below the face value of the assets it took on.
The accounting quirk: buying a business for less than its net assets

When you acquire a company for less than the fair value of its net assets, accounting rules make you book the difference as a one-time "gain on bargain purchase" (sometimes called negative goodwill or "badwill") straight into the profit & loss. Because Piramal bought DHFL out of bankruptcy at a steep discount, the deal produced exactly this kind of large, non-cash accounting gain in the year of acquisition — inflating that year's reported profit without any cash changing hands. On top of that, there is a recovery-upside tail: DHFL's stressed and disputed loans (including large "avoidance application" claims valued at essentially nil in the resolution plan) were taken onto the books at near-zero, so any cash Piramal later recovers on them drops in as pure gain. This is why Piramal's profit line carries lumpy, one-off items years after the deal — and why you must strip them out to see the underlying, repeatable earnings.

The reverse-transformation: before DHFL, Piramal's lending arm was wholesale-dominated — chunky loans to a concentrated set of developers. DHFL bolted on a huge, diversified retail book and the branch infrastructure to keep originating it. That is the mechanical reason the company's retail mix vaulted from a minority to today's ~85% — the acquisition did in one step what organic branch-building would have taken many years to achieve.
04

The merger & the rename — untangling "PEL" vs "Piramal Finance"

Why the ticker you may remember as PEL now trades as PIRAMALFIN — explained cleanly.

This is the part that confuses everyone, so here it is plainly. Until 2025 the listed entity was Piramal Enterprises Ltd (PEL) — a holding company that owned the actual lending business, Piramal Capital & Housing Finance / Piramal Finance, as an unlisted subsidiary. In 2025 the group collapsed that two-layer structure into one single listed NBFC, and renamed it. The result is the company you now see trading as PIRAMALFIN.

What happened, step by step

  • Pre-2025
    Listed Piramal Enterprises (PEL) is the parent; the operating lender Piramal Capital & Housing Finance (a housing-finance company, or HFC) sits underneath it, unlisted.
  • Apr 2025
    The lending arm converts from a housing-finance company (HFC) into a broader NBFC-ICC (Investment & Credit Company) and is renamed Piramal Finance Limited — a legal reflection of its shift beyond just home loans into multi-product retail and MSME lending.
  • Sep 2025
    The NCLT (bankruptcy/company court) approves the reverse merger: parent PEL merges INTO its own subsidiary Piramal Finance (hence "reverse"). One simplified listed NBFC replaces the old holdco-plus-subsidiary structure.
  • 7 Nov 2025
    The merged, renamed Piramal Finance begins trading on NSE & BSE under the new symbol PIRAMALFIN (BSE scrip code 544597) — a rare "listing without an IPO", achieved via the merger.
Why do this at all?

Three reasons stack up. (1) The RBI mandate — as an upper-layer NBFC, the lender itself was required to be listed, and merging it into the already-listed parent was the cleanest route. (2) Simplicity — a single listed operating lender is far easier for investors to value than a holding company sitting on top of a subsidiary (holdcos usually trade at a discount). (3) Focus & branding — with pharma long gone and the group now purely a financier, calling the listed entity "Piramal Finance" tells the market exactly what it is. Practically, for a shareholder: same underlying business, cleaner wrapper, new name and ticker.

Do not confuse the two Piramals on the exchange: Piramal Finance (PIRAMALFIN) is the lender covered here. Piramal Pharma is the separately-listed pharmaceuticals company that was demerged from the old group years ago. They share a founding family but are different listed companies in different sectors. This primer is exclusively about the financial-services entity.
05

The company and who controls it

A family-controlled financier with a professional banker at the operating wheel.

Piramal Finance is promoter-controlled by the Ajay Piramal family, who hold roughly 46% of the company. Ajay Piramal — one of India's best-known industrialists, who built the group by buying and selling a pharmaceuticals business and reinvesting into financial services — is Group Chairman. His son Anand Piramal is Chairman of Piramal Finance, and the day-to-day lender is run by a professional banking CEO.

Ownership & leadership

  • Promoter (Piramal family): ~46.2% holding (Mar '26) — a controlling, skin-in-the-game stake; promoters have declared no share encumbrance.
  • Group Chairman — Ajay Piramal: the architect of the group's pivot into finance; sets strategy across Piramal Finance, Piramal Pharma, Piramal Realty and the Piramal Foundation.
  • Chairman, Piramal Finance — Anand Piramal: oversaw the growth business and the multi-year run-down of the legacy wholesale book (from ~₹43,500 Cr to under ~₹5,900 Cr).
  • MD & CEO — Jairam Sridharan: a career retail-banker (ex-Axis Bank CFO) brought in to build and run the granular retail lending machine — a signal that Piramal takes the retail pivot seriously at the top.
  • Institutional register: foreign institutions ~14–15% and domestic institutions ~19% — a substantial free float alongside the family stake.
Why the leadership signal matters

The pairing tells you the strategy. A promoter family that has repeatedly reinvented the group (chemicals → pharma → finance) provides patient, long-horizon capital and appetite for big bets like DHFL. A professional retail-banking CEO provides the unglamorous operating discipline — underwriting standards, collections, branch productivity, technology — that granular retail lending actually requires and that the old wholesale Piramal never needed. Family ambition plus banker execution is the combination the whole turnaround rests on.

06

The retail engine — lending to "Bharat"

The growth machine: many small loans, spread thin, in smaller-town India.

The heart of the new Piramal is a multi-product retail lending platform aimed at what management calls "Bharat" — the metro-adjacent, semi-urban and rural India that big private banks under-serve. The philosophy is the opposite of the old company: instead of a few enormous loans, thousands of small, diversified, mostly-secured ones, originated through a physical branch network and increasingly through digital channels.

The moat is distribution and data: a physical network that reaches under-served borrowers, plus the underwriting to price them correctly — hard to replicate quickly, and exactly what DHFL's branches jump-started.

The product menu (jargon decoded)

  • Home loans & loan-against-property (LAP) — the secured core, inherited and scaled from DHFL; lower risk, lower yield, long tenor. Anchors affordable-housing lending.
  • Small-business / MSME loans — credit to tiny businesses and the self-employed; a strategic growth pillar, backed by multilateral funding (e.g. IFC/ADB lines earmarked for MSME and affordable housing).
  • Used-car loans — secured lending against second-hand vehicles; higher-yielding than home loans, popular in Bharat markets.
  • Personal & digital loans — unsecured, higher-yield, higher-risk consumer credit; grown carefully because unsecured is where credit costs bite in a downturn.
  • Gold loans — short-tenor loans secured against household gold; low-loss, quick-turnover, a classic Bharat product.
Why the "granular retail" mix is the point

A book of one million small loans behaves completely differently from a book of a hundred giant ones. If any single small borrower defaults, it barely dents the whole; risk is diversified across products, geographies and thousands of names. Retail loans also carry wider spreads than blue-chip wholesale lending, lifting NIM. And a secured-heavy mix (home, LAP, car, gold) means that even when a loan sours, there's collateral to recover against — capping losses. The trade-off is that granular retail is operationally intensive: you need branches, feet-on-street collections, technology and disciplined underwriting. This is precisely why Piramal hired a retail-banker CEO and leaned on DHFL's ready-made network — the model only works if the plumbing works. Retail AUM grew ~33% and disbursements ~34% in FY26, so the plumbing appears to be working.

07

The legacy clean-up & "Wholesale 2.0"

The other half of the story: shrinking the old risk, and re-doing wholesale the disciplined way.

If the retail engine is the growth story, the legacy clean-up is the risk story — and it is nearly complete. The old Piramal was, at heart, a concentrated real-estate lender: a relatively small number of very large loans to property developers. When that model came under stress, the company chose a hard reset — provision aggressively against the bad loans, stop making that kind of loan, and run the book down to almost nothing.

The clean-up, in mechanics

  • Provisioning & Stage-3 recognition. Under IND-AS accounting, loans are bucketed Stage-1 (healthy), Stage-2 (showing stress) and Stage-3 (bad/NPA). Piramal front-loaded provisions against its stressed legacy loans — charges that hurt reported profit in the clean-up years but leave a cleaner book behind. In Q4 FY26 alone, provisions on legacy assets jumped to ~₹1,787 Cr (from ~₹531 Cr a year earlier) as the last of the pile was scrubbed.
  • The run-down. The legacy structured real-estate book was reduced from ~₹43,500 Cr (2022) to under ~₹5,900 Cr — one of the largest deliberate de-riskings by any Indian NBFC.
  • Recovery upside. Where legacy or DHFL-linked assets were written down to near-zero, subsequent cash recoveries (including from stressed-asset/AIF investments) flow back as gains — the lumpy positives that partly offset the provisioning drag.

"Wholesale 2.0" — wholesale, but chastened

Piramal did not abandon wholesale lending entirely — it rebooted it. "Wholesale 2.0" is the new real-estate and corporate mid-market lending done from FY22 onwards under tighter rules: smaller ticket sizes, more granular exposure, better structuring, no repeat of the old concentration. It is kept deliberately modest — ~₹12,538 Cr, about 12% of AUM — a controlled complement to the retail engine rather than the main event.

Why the clean-up is central to the investment case

Here's the crux. For years, Piramal's reported profit was depressed by legacy provisions — the market couldn't easily see the healthy retail business underneath. As the legacy book approaches zero, two things happen at once: the provisioning drag fades (so more of the growth book's earnings reach the bottom line), and the quality of earnings improves (fewer one-offs, more repeatable retail spread income). That is the mechanical basis for a re-rating — the company is transitioning from "scarred lender with hidden earnings" to "clean retail NBFC with visible earnings." The bear's retort: legacy clean-ups often have a long tail, and one-off recoveries flatter the numbers on the way — so the "true" underlying RoA is lower than the headline suggests until the noise fully clears.

The number that matters most: RoA

For an NBFC, Return on Assets (RoA) is the truest single gauge of underlying quality — it strips out how much leverage or equity you use and asks "how much profit does each rupee of loan book actually earn?" Piramal's RoA is on a clear climb: management flagged an exit RoA of roughly ~2.1% at Q4 FY26, a target of ~2.5% (RoAUM) in FY27, and an aspiration toward ~3% by FY28. Reaching a sustained ~2.5–3% RoA would put Piramal in the same neighbourhood as India's best retail NBFCs. The catch is RoE (return on equity): because Piramal is heavily over-capitalised (~19.8% CRAR, ~₹28,000 Cr net worth), its RoE today is low (single digits) — a lot of idle equity dilutes the return. As the book grows into that capital, RoE should rise toward low-double-digits (brokers model ~12% by FY28). Watch RoA and RoE together: a good RoA with a rising RoE is the signature of a maturing, self-funding retail lender.

09

Valuation & the re-rating question

A transforming NBFC trading around book value — cheap turnaround, or value trap?

Lenders are valued primarily on price-to-book (P/B) — the share price relative to the accounting net worth per share — because a bank or NBFC essentially is its balance sheet. Piramal trades at roughly 1.5–1.7× book (on ~₹1,242 book value per share and a ~₹48,500 Cr market cap). Where a P/B lands tells you what the market thinks of a lender's returns: sub-1× says "we doubt it"; 1–2× says "decent but unproven"; 3×+ is reserved for the elite compounders.

How to think about the re-rating

The bull maths is simple to state: Piramal is guiding to grow AUM ~25% and profit ~50% in FY27, and to lift RoA toward ~2.5–3% and RoE toward ~12% by FY28. If it delivers a clean, mid-teens-growing, ~2.5%+ RoA retail book, the market should stop valuing it like a scarred wholesale lender (~1.5× book) and start valuing it like an established retail NBFC (~2–2.5×+ book). On a rising book value, a higher multiple is a double compounding — the classic re-rating trade. The bear's counter is equally clear: the RoE is still low, a chunk of FY26 profit was one-off gains, and the last legacy provisions were only just taken — so the "clean, high-return retail NBFC" the bulls are paying for is a forecast, not yet a track record. Below-2× book is either a cheap entry into a genuine turnaround, or a fair price for a lender that hasn't yet earned the premium multiple. The next 4–8 quarters of clean, one-off-free RoA and RoE will decide which.

10

The industry and the rivals

A crowded, fast-growing retail-credit market where scale, funding cost and underwriting win.

Piramal is competing in India's retail-credit boom — housing finance, MSME, vehicle and consumer lending — a large, structurally growing market being served by a mix of banks and specialist NBFCs. The winners are those with the cheapest funding, the best underwriting/collections, and the widest distribution into under-served markets. Piramal is a mid-sized challenger, not yet a category leader, in a field of formidable incumbents.

The competitive tension to watch

Two forces cut against Piramal. First, funding cost: because an NBFC borrows what it lends, it structurally pays more for money than a bank — so in secured, price-sensitive segments like prime home loans, banks can undercut it. Piramal's answer is to lean into segments and geographies banks under-serve (Bharat, self-employed, used-car, MSME), where the spread is wider and the competition thinner. Second, the credit cycle: retail lending looks wonderful while the economy is strong and defaults are low — the real test of underwriting comes in a downturn, and Piramal's retail book is young enough that it hasn't been fully stress-tested through a full cycle. Its edge, if it has one, is the DHFL-derived distribution plus a professional banking team; its vulnerability is that everyone — banks and NBFCs alike — is chasing the same Bharat borrower, which can compress spreads or tempt lenders to relax standards.

The regulatory backdrop: as an RBI upper-layer NBFC, Piramal faces bank-like scrutiny — tighter capital, liquidity and governance norms, plus periodic RBI tightening on unsecured/consumer lending and co-lending. That raises compliance cost but also cements its status as a serious, systemically-important lender rather than a fringe financier.
11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A genuine turnaround with the numbers finally turning — priced for it to keep proving itself.

Piramal Finance is one of the clearer turnaround stories in Indian financials: a scarred wholesale lender that has, over five hard years, rebuilt itself into a clean, growing, retail-led NBFC — and just simplified itself into a single listed entity to prove it. The debate is not whether the transformation happened (it did); it is whether the returns now justify re-rating it as a peer of the good retail NBFCs.

The bull case

  • The pivot is essentially done — 85% retail, legacy book run down ~85% to a small tail; the messy provisioning years are largely behind.
  • Numbers are inflecting — AUM past ₹1 lakh Cr (+25%), NII +32%, RoA climbing toward ~2.5–3%; guidance of ~25% AUM / ~50% PAT growth in FY27.
  • Fortress balance sheet — ~19.8% CRAR and a ~₹28,000 Cr net worth; over-capitalised, so growth is self-funded and there's room to lever RoE up.
  • Bharat distribution moat — a DHFL-derived branch network reaching under-served, wider-spread markets banks neglect; ~₹1.5 lakh Cr AUM targeted by FY28.
  • Re-rating optionality — at ~1.5–1.7× book vs 2.5–3× for proven retail NBFCs, a successful, clean, one-off-free track record could re-rate the stock and the book together.

The bear case

  • RoE is still low — the equity base is huge and profitability, ex-one-offs, is only just crossing into "good NBFC" territory; the premium multiple is a forecast, not a record.
  • One-off-flattered profit — a big slice of FY26 PAT came from asset-sale gains and legacy recoveries; the durable run-rate is lower than the ₹1,506 Cr headline.
  • Untested through a cycle — the young retail (esp. unsecured) book hasn't faced a real downturn; credit costs could rise sharply if the cycle turns.
  • NBFC funding & ALM risk — no cheap deposits; dependent on wholesale funding and market access, the exact vulnerability that felled DHFL and IL&FS.
  • Crowded field — banks and better-established NBFCs (Bajaj, Shriram, affordable-HFCs) chase the same Bharat borrower, pressuring spreads and underwriting discipline.
The bottom line

Piramal Finance has done the hard part — it survived a wholesale-lending blow-up, bought its way into retail via DHFL, cleaned the legacy book to a stub, and re-listed as a single, focused NBFC. What remains is to prove the returns are real and repeatable. The story from here is written in a few numbers each quarter: RoA and RoE excluding one-offs (is the underlying engine genuinely earning ~2.5%+?), retail asset quality (does the young book hold up as it seasons?), and the pace of the legacy tail (is the noise finally gone?). Get those right and a below-2×-book lender re-rates toward its clean-retail peers — a double engine of book growth plus multiple expansion. Stumble — a credit-cycle wobble, a funding scare, or a stubborn legacy tail — and a turnaround the market only half-believes stays stuck around book value. High reward, real risk, and unusually knowable milestones to track.

The transformation is no longer the question — the durability of the returns is. Watch RoA and RoE with the one-offs stripped out; that's the whole re-rating in two numbers.
