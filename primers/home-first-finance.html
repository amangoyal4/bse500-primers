00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Home First Finance is a housing finance company (HFC) that makes small home loans to first-time buyers — people in India's smaller cities and towns, on modest household incomes, buying or building their first home. The average loan is roughly ₹12 lakh, secured on the borrower's own house. In that respect it looks like every other "affordable-housing" lender. What sets it apart is how it does the job: Home First is built as a technology-first, data-driven lender — app-based origination, a centralised and largely standardised underwriting engine, and loan approvals in days rather than the weeks a branch-and-relationship lender takes. It sells modest secured home loans, but it operates more like a fintech than a traditional mortgage shop.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is growing fast, and widely. AUM (assets under management — the loan book it services) reached ₹15,878 Cr at March 2026, up ~25% year-on-year, spread across a pan-India footprint of 171 branches and 373 touchpoints. Where rivals such as Aptus and Five-Star are concentrated in South India, Home First deliberately fans out across West, North and South — a broader map that gives it a longer growth runway but also a book that is younger and less battle-tested.
  • The economics are good, and levered. FY26 delivered PAT of ₹540 Cr (+41%), a ~3.6% return on assets and a 15.7% return on equity — earned on a modest ~13.4% loan yield because the tech-lean model keeps costs and credit losses low. Crucially, Home First runs with more borrowing per rupee of equity than the ultra-capitalised Aptus, so it converts a similar ROA into a higher ROE. Leverage is a feature here, not an accident.
  • The whole debate is one number holding as it scales. Gross NPAs sit at just 1.8% and credit cost at ~40 bps. The bull case is a scalable, standardised engine compounding for a decade; the bear case is that a fast-growing, app-underwritten book across a wide geography has never been through a severe housing or employment downturn — and standardised underwriting can miss what a loan officer's eyes would catch. The stock trades near 3× book, a premium that assumes the growth continues and the credit stays clean.
In plain terms

Home First borrows money wholesale (from banks and bond markets) at around 8.4% and lends it to first-time home buyers at around 13.4%, secured on their houses. The gap between those two rates — the spread — is the raw material of its profit. What makes the company distinctive is that it runs that lending business through software: a salesperson or a "connector" sources the customer, the loan is assessed centrally against a standardised data-driven scorecard, and an answer comes back in days. That speed and low cost let it grow quickly and cover a wider map than the hands-on, branch-by-branch lenders it competes with. The reward is fast, profitable growth; the risk is that a book built this fast, this new, and this standardised only proves its credit quality when a real downturn tests it.

01

Affordable-housing finance 101: the corner of lending Home First lives in

No finance background needed — just the handful of ideas that drive the whole business.

India has tens of millions of families who earn a real, steady-ish living but sit outside the comfort zone of a big bank's home-loan desk. They may run a small shop, drive a taxi, tailor clothes, or work at a firm that pays partly in cash. They have income, but not the neat salary slips and long formal credit histories a bank wants. When they try to buy or build a first home, the mainstream mortgage system struggles to say yes. Affordable-housing finance companies exist to serve exactly this customer. Home First is one of them. To follow the business, you need five ideas.

The only terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)
HFC"Housing Finance Company" — a specialised lender that mostly makes home loans, regulated by the RBI (with the National Housing Bank as the sector authority). Home First is an HFC, not a bank; it can lend but cannot take deposits from the public.
AUM"Assets Under Management" — the total pool of loans on Home First's books (plus loans it has sold down but still services). This is the size of the business; Home First's AUM is ₹15,878 Cr. When AUM grows ~25% a year, the company is roughly growing that fast.
Spread / NIMSpread = lending rate minus borrowing rate (Home First: ~13.4% − ~8.4% ≈ 5.3%). NIM (net interest margin, ~5.7%) is the same idea measured against assets, and runs a touch higher because part of the book is funded by shareholders' own equity, which costs no interest.
GNPA / credit costGNPA (gross non-performing assets, 1.8%) is the share of loans where the borrower has stopped paying. Credit cost (~0.4%) is the money set aside each year for loans that go bad. For a lender, this is the line that separates a good year from a disaster.
ROA / ROEROA (return on assets, ~3.6%) is profit as a share of the loan book — how good the lending machine is. ROE (return on equity, 15.7%) is profit as a share of shareholders' money — ROA amplified by how much the company borrows. More borrowing turns a given ROA into a higher ROE.
Why a home loan is a safer bet than it sounds

Lending to a customer without a salary slip sounds risky. Two things pull the risk back down. First, the loan is secured — if the borrower stops paying, the lender can ultimately recover against the house, and losing the family home is the very last thing any borrower lets happen, so these loans default far less than unsecured ones. Second, the ticket is small (~₹12 lakh) and the loan-to-value is conservative, so even a forced sale usually covers the debt. The art of the business is assessing an informal income accurately — figuring out what a shopkeeper or a gig worker really earns and can really repay. That assessment is where affordable-HFCs live or die, and it is precisely the step Home First chose to solve with data and software rather than a loan officer's gut.

Who these lenders serve — and why banks don't

  • First-time home buyers — customers taking on a home loan for the first time, typically in tier-2/3/4 towns, with household incomes often under ₹50,000 a month. This is Home First's stated core customer.
  • A mix of salaried and self-employed — some work at small firms (salaried, but at employers a bank can't easily verify); others run micro-businesses (self-employed, informal income). Assessing both without formal paperwork is the whole skill.
  • Underserved by big banks — mainstream banks find these loans small, geographically scattered, and hard to underwrite with standard salary-slip logic, so they under-lend to the segment. That gap is the affordable-HFC's entire opportunity, and it is large and structurally growing as India urbanises and formalises.
02

What Home First actually lends: one product, done at scale

A deliberately narrow book — and why the narrowness is a strength.

Some lenders sprawl across dozens of products. Home First does close to the opposite: it keeps its book overwhelmingly in home loans and resists the temptation to chase higher-yielding but riskier lending. That focus is a design choice — a narrow, repeatable product is exactly what lets a standardised, software-driven engine work.

The borrower profile is a second deliberate choice. Roughly 68% of the book is salaried and the rest self-employed — a mix that tilts a little more toward salaried (if informally so) than some peers who lean hard into self-employed business loans. The customer is not the very poorest; it is the aspiring, first-time-buyer household that is creditworthy but paperwork-poor. That segment is enormous, and it is the sweet spot where a secured loan is genuinely safe but a bank still says no.

The surprise hiding in the borrower mix

The easy story about affordable housing is "lending to informal, self-employed borrowers banks won't touch." Home First's book quietly complicates that: about two-thirds of it is salaried — people with jobs, just at small or hard-to-verify employers. That matters because a salaried-tilted book behaves differently in a downturn from a purely self-employed one: salaried incomes are stickier when small businesses wobble, but they are also exposed to layoffs if the employer is fragile. It is a reminder that "affordable housing" is not one homogeneous risk — the exact borrower mix, which Home First has tuned toward salaried, is part of what its credit numbers are really measuring.

Why the narrow product is the point: a standardised, software-run underwriting engine only works if the thing being underwritten is repeatable. Twenty different loan types with twenty different risk shapes would break the model and force human, case-by-case judgement — the very thing Home First is trying to systematise. By keeping ~83% of the book in plain home loans, it keeps the product simple enough for a data-driven engine to handle at speed and scale. The narrowness is the strategy.
03

The tech-first engine: the one thing that makes Home First different

The single most important idea in the investment case — and the reason it can grow faster and wider than its peers.

Two lenders can offer the identical ₹12-lakh home loan at the identical ~13.4% rate to the identical borrower — and be completely different companies. The difference is the machinery between the customer walking in and the money going out. Home First's rivals largely run that machinery by hand: a loan officer meets the customer, builds a relationship, assesses income by sitting in the shop and eyeballing the cash flow, and takes weeks. Home First runs it as software: sourced through lean "connectors", assessed centrally against a standardised data-driven scorecard, and answered in days. That single architectural choice is the whole company.

Why "tech-first" is not a slogan — it is the economics

  • Lower cost-to-income. A connector network plus centralised underwriting means fewer expensive branches and fewer senior loan officers per rupee lent. Home First's cost-to-income ratio is ~32.5% (improved ~330 bps in FY26) — lean for a lender expanding this fast. Lower operating cost drops straight to ROA.
  • Faster growth and scalability. When the underwriting decision lives in software rather than in a veteran officer's head, you can open a new geography without first growing a new generation of loan officers. That is why Home First can push into West and North India faster than a relationship-heavy peer can.
  • Consistent credit decisions. A standardised scorecard applies the same rules in Surat and Salem. That consistency is the promise — every loan judged by the same logic, no rogue branch — and, as the bear case notes, also the risk, because a template can miss what a human would catch.
  • Speed wins the customer. To a first-time buyer juggling a builder's deadline, an approval in days versus weeks is decisive. Turnaround time (TAT) is a genuine competitive weapon, not a back-office nicety.
The trade it makes — speed and scale for deep relationship underwriting

There is a real cost to doing it this way, and naming it is the key to understanding the stock. The branch-heavy peers — Aptus, Five-Star — earn their credit quality through deep, manual, relationship underwriting: an officer who knows the local market, visits the borrower's business, and prices in things no scorecard captures. Home First trades some of that depth for speed, lower cost, and a wider map. In good times, the trade looks brilliant: it grows faster and cheaper across more of India. The unanswered question is whether a standardised, data-led engine underwrites as well as a seasoned officer when the economy turns — because a scorecard is only as good as the downturns baked into the data it was trained on, and this book has grown up almost entirely in good years.

The non-transferable device — hold this for the rest of the primer: most affordable-HFCs you can judge on the same secured-lending arithmetic. Home First forces a sharper question, because it has deliberately swapped relationship underwriting for a standardised software engine. So its scorecard is: does the cost-to-income stay low (is the tech genuinely cheaper?), does AUM keep compounding across a widening map (is the engine scalable?), and — the one that outranks the other two — does credit cost stay in its 30–40 bps band as the book ages and scales (does the standardised engine actually underwrite as well as the humans it replaced?). Two lenders, same loan; the difference is made in those three lines, and above all the third.
04

The company, its founders, and who owns it now

A Mumbai-based, professionally-run lender — with a surprisingly thin promoter stake.

Home First Finance was founded in 2010 and is headquartered in Mumbai. It was built from the start as an institution rather than a family shop — a professional management team (led by co-founder and MD & CEO Manoj Viswanathan, with veteran banker P.S. Jayakumar as chairman) backed by a rotating cast of private-equity and sovereign investors. That heritage shapes everything about how it is run, and it produces one genuinely unusual feature for an affordable-housing lender: there is barely a promoter at all any more.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter holding is very low (~7%, approx.). Unlike most affordable-HFCs — which are tightly held by a founding family or a dominant PE sponsor — Home First is essentially a widely-held, board-and-management-run company. Its shareholder register is dominated by institutions (domestic mutual funds and foreign investors) rather than a controlling owner. This is a real point of difference and cuts both ways (see below).
  • The PE backers have largely cycled out. True North trimmed over the years and Warburg Pincus's full exit in August 2025 marked the end of the marquee sponsor era. The float is now large and liquid, which is part of why the stock attracts heavy institutional interest and block-trade activity.
  • Clean, single-entity structure. There is no confusing holding-company overhang or listed subsidiary to value separately — what you buy is the lender itself. Net worth stood at ~₹4,357 Cr at March 2026, funding a ₹15,878 Cr book alongside ~₹10,590 Cr of borrowings.
A genuine surprise, and a double-edged one: most people assume an affordable-home lender is a founder's crusade with the founder's wealth locked in. Home First has almost the opposite — a ~7% promoter stake and a register full of professional investors. The upside is governance: decisions are made by a board and a professional team, not dictated by a controlling family. The flip side is that there is no large, aligned owner whose own fortune rides on the credit staying clean through the next cycle — the discipline has to come from management and the board, not from a promoter with everything at stake. When you judge this book, you are trusting a system and a team, not a founder's skin in the game.
05

The pan-India spread: wider than the peers, by design

Geography is a strategy here — and it separates Home First from the South-concentrated affordable lenders.

If you drew the branch maps of India's listed affordable-HFCs on top of each other, one would stand out. Aptus and Five-Star are essentially South-India franchises — deep, dominant and disciplined in a handful of southern states. Home First drew a different map on purpose: it spreads across West, North and South, treating a wide geography not as an accident of history but as the natural output of a scalable, software-run model.

The South-concentrated model earns deep local knowledge and pricing power in its home turf. The pan-India model trades some of that local depth for a much larger addressable market and a longer growth runway — which is exactly the bet a scalable, standardised engine is built to make.

The logic connects straight back to the tech engine. A relationship-underwriting lender is hard to expand geographically, because its edge lives in local officers who know their patch — you cannot copy-paste that into a new state overnight. A standardised, data-driven lender can, in principle, carry the same scorecard and the same app into a new town and be productive quickly. Geography and technology are two sides of one coin: Home First is pan-India because it is tech-first, and it is tech-first partly in order to be pan-India.

The catch inside the breadth

A wider map is a bigger opportunity and a more diversified book — a downturn in one state hurts less when you lend across many. But breadth has a cost that concentration does not. A South-focused lender knows its few markets intimately, through cycles, at the level of individual micro-markets and builders. A pan-India lender operating a standardised engine across dozens of unfamiliar micro-markets is, by construction, relying more on the model and less on local eyes. In benign conditions that is pure advantage. The test is whether the same scorecard reads risk correctly in a town the company has only lent in for three years as accurately as a local lender who has watched that town for fifteen. Breadth multiplies the growth and multiplies the places where a standardised model has to be right.

The ROA bridge: where a modest 13% loan becomes a 15.7% ROE

Follow the money from the interest a borrower pays to the return a shareholder earns — this walk is the tech dividend.

Here is the most useful thing you can do with Home First's numbers: walk a single rupee of loan book from top to bottom and watch where the profit is made and lost. A borrower pays ~13.4% interest. A shareholder ends up with a ~3.6% return on assets and a 15.7% return on equity. The whole company — and the whole tech-first thesis — lives in the steps between those numbers.

The margin (~5.3% spread, ~5.7% NIM) is respectable but not extraordinary for the sector. What lifts Home First to a ~3.6% ROA is that the operating-cost and credit-cost steps are small — the tech-lean model spends little to run and, so far, loses little to defaults. Then leverage does the rest: a ~3.6% ROA geared up becomes a 15.7% ROE.

Read the walk carefully, because it locates the tech dividend precisely. Home First's spread is good but not the widest in the sector — it lends on genuinely secured home loans, not high-yield business loans, so it does not chase the ~17%+ yields some peers extract from riskier books. Where it makes its money is lower down: the operating-cost step is thin because the connector-plus-software model is cheap to run, and the credit-cost step is thin because the loans are secured and (so far) well-selected. A modest margin plus low costs plus low losses equals a strong ROA — and that is the entire economic argument for doing lending as software.

Why leverage is a deliberate choice here — not a red flag

A ~3.6% ROA is only half the story; a shareholder cares about ROE, and Home First's is 15.7%. The bridge between them is leverage — how many rupees of loan book the company runs per rupee of its own equity. Home First deliberately runs more leverage than the famously over-capitalised Aptus (whose surplus capital drags its ROE down despite a high ROA). More borrowing per rupee of equity means Home First converts its solid ROA into a materially higher ROE. That is a feature: within prudent limits, gearing is how a well-run secured lender rewards shareholders. It is also the pressure point — the same leverage that lifts the ROE is what makes the company more sensitive to a funding squeeze or a spike in credit cost. Higher return, tighter rope.

The growth engine: AUM, disbursements and the branch build-out

The top line that the whole premium valuation rests on.

For a lender priced at ~3× book, the market is buying years of compounding. So the growth numbers are not a footnote — they are the thesis's fuel. FY26 delivered on that front: the book grew ~25%, quarterly disbursements hit records, and the network kept widening. The engine is running.

Why profit outran the loan book this year

PAT rose ~41% while AUM rose ~25% — profit grew faster than the book. That gap is the operating leverage of the model showing up. As the loan book scales, the fixed costs of the tech platform and the head office spread over more loans, so the cost-to-income ratio falls (it dropped ~330 bps in FY26 to 32.5%), and each incremental rupee of margin drops more efficiently to the bottom line. This is the flywheel the tech-first design is supposed to produce: grow the book, dilute the fixed costs, widen the return — and do it again. The FY26 result is that flywheel working. The bear's question is simply whether it keeps working when growth eventually slows and the credit cycle turns.

08

Asset quality and the fast-growth-at-scale test

The numbers look excellent today — the entire debate is whether they hold as the book ages.

Everything above — the growth, the ROA, the premium multiple — rests on one foundation: the loans get repaid. On today's numbers, they emphatically do. Gross NPAs are just 1.8% (improved 20 bps in the quarter), net NPAs ~1.4%, and credit cost a contained ~40 bps, guided to stay in a 30–40 bps band. For a lender to informal-income, first-time buyers, that is a genuinely strong book. And yet this is exactly where the honest investor has to slow down.

These are strong numbers for the segment — a secured book, conservatively underwritten, with losses running at less than half a percent of assets. The question is not whether the book is good today. It is whether a book this young, growing this fast, has simply not been old enough to reveal its true through-cycle losses yet.

Here is the uncomfortable structural fact. A fast-growing loan book is, by definition, a young loan book — a large share of the loans were written in the last couple of years and have not yet had time to go bad. Loans typically sour a few years in, not a few months in, so a book compounding at ~25% always looks cleaner than it will ultimately prove, because the denominator (new loans) grows faster than the problems (which take time to surface). Add two Home-First-specific wrinkles — a standardised, data-driven underwriting engine that has been calibrated mostly on benign years, and a connector-led sourcing model where the originator is not the ultimate risk-taker — and you have a credit book whose true quality is genuinely untested through a severe housing or employment downturn.

What a downturn would actually test

Picture a real shock — a regional employment slump that hits the small employers Home First's salaried borrowers work for, or a property-price wobble in a few of its newer markets. Three things get tested at once. First, whether the standardised scorecard selected borrowers as well as a local officer's judgement would have — a template optimised on good-year data can systematically miss a risk a human would have smelt. Second, whether connector-sourced volume was as carefully screened as it looked when everyone was being paid to grow. Third, whether the collections machine, which has mostly practised on a cooperative book, can recover in a stressed one. None of these are reasons to assume the worst — the security on the loans is real and the numbers today are excellent — but they are the specific places where a fast, tech-led, wide-geography model carries risk a slow, manual, concentrated one does not. This is the price of the growth.

The thesis-settling metric, named: of everything in this primer, watch credit cost through the next real downturn. If it stays inside the ~30–50 bps range while AUM keeps compounding, the tech-first, standardised, pan-India model will have proven it underwrites as well as the humans it replaced — and the premium multiple is justified. If a stressed year sends credit cost to 100–150+ bps, the market will conclude the clean numbers were a function of a benign cycle and a young book, and both the earnings and the multiple re-rate together. Growth, cost-to-income and spread all matter — but this one line is the scoreboard.
09

Funding and leverage: the other half of a lender

A lender is only as strong as the money it borrows — and Home First borrows more, per rupee of equity, than its most cautious peer.

Every lender is really two businesses stacked together: an asset business (the loans it makes) and a liability business (the money it borrows to fund them). The asset side gets all the attention; the liability side is where lenders quietly die. Home First funds its ₹15,878 Cr book with roughly ₹10,590 Cr of borrowings on ~₹4,357 Cr of net worth — a diversified pool from around 31 banks and financial institutions, plus bonds and refinance lines from the National Housing Bank.

Leverage (debt/equity)~2.4×Higher gearing than the over-capitalised peers — the reason a similar ROA becomes a higher ROE, and the reason funding risk bites harder.

Two features of the funding book are worth understanding. First, diversification: drawing from ~31 lenders plus the bond market and NHB refinance means no single funding line can strangle the company, which is the classic way an HFC gets into trouble. Second, the asset-liability mismatch every mortgage lender lives with: Home First borrows for a few years but lends for 15–20, so it must continuously roll over funding while its loans run long. In calm markets this is routine. In a liquidity crunch — the kind that froze parts of India's NBFC sector in 2018–19 — it is the pressure point, and a more-levered lender feels it first.

The rate-cycle sensitivity in one sentence

Home First earns a spread — the gap between a ~13.4% lending rate and a ~8.4% borrowing rate — so its profit is exquisitely sensitive to how those two rates move relative to each other. If funding costs spike (a rate shock, a liquidity squeeze) faster than it can reprice its loans, the spread compresses and profit falls; the higher leverage magnifies the hit. If rates fall and it holds its lending rates, the spread widens. Because it runs more borrowing per rupee of equity than the ultra-capitalised peers, this rate-cycle sensitivity is real and worth watching — the spread through the rate cycle is one of the numbers that tells you whether the funding engine is as well-run as the lending one.

The industry and the rivals: a crowded, competitive niche

Affordable housing is a good business — which is precisely why everyone is piling in.

The structural case for affordable-housing finance is genuinely strong: a huge under-served customer base, a government keen on "housing for all", rising incomes and urbanisation, and the steady financialisation of India's informal economy. The problem with a good business is that it attracts competition — and this niche now has a lot of it, from focused specialists to giant banks moving down-market.

The competitive threat that actually matters

The specialist rivals — Aavas, Aptus, India Shelter, Aadhar — compete for the same borrower, and in an expanding market there is room for several to grow. The more serious long-run pressure comes from above: large banks and giants like Bajaj Housing Finance, which fund themselves far more cheaply than any affordable HFC. Home First's whole ROA rests on a ~5.3% spread; a well-capitalised bank that decides to chase the affordable segment can undercut on rate because its cost of funds is lower. The specialist's defence is exactly the thing this primer is about — underwriting the informal-income customer better and faster than a big bank can be bothered to. Home First's tech-first, standardised engine is a bet that it can serve this customer at a cost and speed a lumbering bank won't match. That edge is real today; whether it survives a determined bank push is the competitive question underneath the credit one.

The valuation — roughly 3× book and ~24× earnings — is a premium, and for a lender the relevant lens is price-to-book set against ROE and growth. At a 15.7% ROE growing the book ~25% a year, a ~3× multiple is defensible if both continue; it is expensive if either the growth slows or the credit cracks. The margin of safety is thin, and it lives entirely in the credit book behaving.

What the price-to-book multiple is really pricing

A price-to-book of ~3× says the market believes Home First's equity will keep earning a mid-teens (and rising) return for many years and keep compounding the book at a strong clip — the classic maths of a durable, high-ROE lender. Two things can break that. A growth slowdown — as the base gets larger, ~25% gets arithmetically harder, and if AUM growth drifts toward the low teens the compounding case weakens. Or, more decisively, a credit shock — a stressed year that lifts credit cost sharply would cut ROE and puncture the premium simultaneously, a double hit. So the P/B is not really pricing this year's clean numbers; it is pricing a continuation — of both the growth and, above all, the low losses — that a young, fast-growing, standardised book has not yet earned the right to be assumed. Whether it earns that right is what the next cycle decides.

12

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A high-quality growth lender riding a real tailwind — asked to prove its credit model works when the weather turns.

Home First enters FY27 firing on the metrics that matter: ~25% growth, rising returns, falling costs, clean credit, and a structural tailwind under India's affordable-housing demand. The case against it is not that the business is weak — it plainly is not — but that it is priced for a continuation that a fast-grown, tech-underwritten, wide-geography book has never been asked to deliver through a downturn.

The bull case

  • Scalable tech-first engine — standardised, app-based underwriting plus connector sourcing keeps cost-to-income low (32.5%) and lets the model expand into new geographies faster than branch-heavy peers.
  • Long, pan-India runway — a wide West/North/South footprint on a structurally growing affordable-housing market; ~25% AUM compounding with plenty of white space left.
  • Strong, levered returns — ~3.6% ROA and 15.7% ROE, with deliberate gearing turning a solid secured-lending margin into a genuinely attractive shareholder return.
  • Clean, secured book — 1.8% GNPA and ~40 bps credit cost on conservatively underwritten home loans; losses well contained so far.
  • Professional, well-capitalised, liquid — board-run with diversified funding from ~31 lenders and a large institutional float.

The bear case

  • Untested through a cycle — a young, fast-growing, standardised book has never faced a severe housing/employment downturn; a scorecard can miss risks a manual underwriter catches, and credit cost could spike.
  • Borrower-segment fragility — informal-income, first-time buyers are more exposed to an economic shock than prime borrowers.
  • Crowded, price-competitive niche — Aavas, Aptus, India Shelter, Aadhar and, most seriously, cheap-funded banks and Bajaj Housing moving down-market can compress spreads.
  • Higher leverage = more funding/rate sensitivity — greater gearing than peers means a funding squeeze or rate spike bites the spread harder.
  • Rich valuation, thin promoter skin — ~3× book prices in continuation, and there is no large aligned owner whose own wealth guards the credit through the cycle.
The bottom line

Home First has done something quietly radical: it turned the hardest part of affordable-housing lending — judging an informal-income borrower — into a fast, cheap, repeatable software process, and used that to grow faster and wider than lenders who still do it by hand. On every metric an investor can measure today, the model is working beautifully: the book compounds, the costs fall, the returns rise, and the loans get repaid. The one thing no metric can yet show is how that standardised, connector-sourced, pan-India book behaves the first time a real downturn hits the small employers and micro-businesses its borrowers depend on. That answer arrives in a single, checkable line each year — credit cost — and the moment to watch is when it is finally read against a stressed cycle rather than a benign one. Until that print lands, Home First is a superb-looking lending machine whose most important number has only ever been measured in fair weather.
