Capri Global Capital, the multi-product NBFC that also brokers car loans

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Capri Global Capital (CGCL, "Capri Loans") is a Mumbai-based non-bank lender (NBFC) that borrows money wholesale and lends it out across a spread of secured retail products — small-business loans against property, affordable home loans, gold loans, and construction finance — and, alongside that lending book, runs two capital-light fee businesses: it originates and distributes car loans for banks and sells insurance, earning commission without putting its own balance sheet at risk. It is controlled by founder Rajesh Sharma, and in FY26 it was among the fastest-growing lenders of its size in India.

Three facts frame everything that follows:

  • Growth, and lots of it. Consolidated assets under management (AUM) reached ~₹36,623 crore at March 2026, up ~60% in a single year; net profit nearly doubled to ~₹949 crore (+98%). Management is guiding for ~₹46,000 crore of AUM and ~₹1,300 crore of profit in FY27, and ₹57,000 crore of AUM by FY28. Few lenders its size are compounding this fast.
  • A five-product book, tilting hard toward gold. The old core was MSME loans (secured lending to small businesses). But the breakout engine is gold loans, which more than doubled to ~₹16,965 crore and is now the single largest business — built out from almost nothing to a 1,000-plus-branch network in barely three years.
  • A lender that also runs a brokerage. The quirk a generalist should carry away: CGCL arranged ~₹11,910 crore of car loans in FY26 that it never funded — it originates them and hands them to 14 partner banks, keeping a fee and none of the credit risk. A "lender" whose franchise includes one of the larger bank-channel car-loan desks in the country.
In plain terms

Start with the picture of an ordinary lender: borrow at one rate, lend at a higher one, keep the gap. CGCL does that across four secured products — but it has bolted on a second, unusual layer. In some businesses it puts up its own money and takes the credit risk (MSME, housing, gold, construction). In others it acts as a matchmaker — finding the borrower, doing the paperwork, then routing the loan to a bank that funds it, while CGCL simply pockets a commission. That mix of own-book lending plus fee-for-arranging is the whole story, and it is what lets a mid-size firm grow its franchise faster than its own capital would normally allow.

01

The NBFC model, 101: how a non-bank lender actually makes money

No finance degree required — just the handful of mechanics that drive every line in CGCL's accounts.

An NBFC — Non-Banking Financial Company — is a lender that is not a bank. The distinction matters for one blunt reason: an NBFC cannot take your deposits. A bank funds its loans with cheap current- and savings-account money from the public; an NBFC has no such deposit tap, so it must borrow wholesale — from banks, bond markets, and other institutions — and then re-lend that money at a higher rate. The gap between what it earns on loans and what it pays to borrow is the engine of the whole business.

The vocabulary that runs an NBFC — with CGCL's numbers
TermWhat it means (and CGCL's figure)
AUM"Assets Under Management" — the total book of loans the firm has out (whether on its own balance sheet or arranged with a partner). CGCL's consolidated AUM is ~₹36,623 Cr.
NIM / spread"Net Interest Margin" — the rate earned on loans minus the cost of borrowing, as a % of assets. This is the core profit spread. CGCL's net interest income (NII) was ~₹1,998 Cr in FY26 (+50%).
Cost of borrowingThe average rate the NBFC pays on its own funding. Lower is better; it fell modestly through FY26 and a credit-rating upgrade could shave off more.
GNPA / credit costBad loans as a % of the book ("Gross Non-Performing Assets"), and the money set aside for them. CGCL's GNPA is ~0.9% — low, because most of its book is secured.
RoA / RoEReturn on Assets / Return on Equity — how much profit the firm squeezes from its assets and its shareholders' capital. The numbers that decide whether growth is worth it (see §07).
Fee incomeMoney earned without lending — commissions from distributing car loans and insurance. ~29% of CGCL's net total income now comes from non-interest sources.
Why "secured" is the word that matters most

Almost everything CGCL lends is backed by an asset it can seize and sell if the borrower stops paying: property behind an MSME or housing loan, gold jewellery behind a gold loan, the project behind construction finance. That single design choice is why its bad-loan ratio sits under 1% while it grows at 60% a year. Unsecured lending — personal loans, microfinance — grows fast too, but when a credit cycle turns it goes bad with brutal speed (the story that blew a hole in several peers in 2024–25). CGCL made a deliberate bet to stay secured and collateralised, trading a little yield for a lot of resilience. The trade-off: secured loans need appraisal, paperwork and branches, so they are slower and costlier to originate — which is exactly why CGCL has been spending so heavily on people and branches (again, §07).

The two ways CGCL earns — and why the second is the interesting one

  • Interest income (its own book). The classic NBFC engine: fund a loan, hold it, earn the spread, carry the credit risk. This is MSME, housing, gold and construction finance — ~71% of net income.
  • Fee income (other people's books). Originate a car loan or an insurance policy, pass it to a bank or insurer, keep a commission, hold no loan and take no credit risk. Capital-light, high-return-on-equity, and the reason CGCL's franchise reaches further than its balance sheet (see §05).
02

Five businesses on one balance sheet

CGCL is really a small conglomerate of lenders — here is what each one does, and how big it is.

CGCL began life as a wholesale and construction-finance lender, then spent the last decade turning itself into a granular retail book spread across products that rarely go bad at the same time. That diversification is the point: a small-business slowdown, a housing wobble and a gold-price dip are unlikely to strike together, so a spread of secured products smooths the ride. The four own-book lenders, decoded

  • MSME loans — secured loans to small businesses, mostly loan-against-property (LAP): a shopkeeper or small manufacturer pledges a home or shop and borrows working or growth capital. This was CGCL's retail foundation; disbursements rose ~65% in FY26 to ~₹2,550 Cr. A newer micro-LAP variant (tiny-ticket property loans, AUM ~₹824 Cr) pushes the same model down-market.
  • Gold loans — short-term cash lent against household gold jewellery, the fastest-scaled business (see §04). AUM ~₹16,965 Cr across 1,000-plus branches, GNPA a remarkable ~0.3%.
  • Construction finance — larger, wholesale loans to real-estate developers to build projects. AUM ~₹5,708 Cr across 282 active projects, average sanction ~₹48 Cr. This is the legacy business, higher-ticket and lumpier than the retail books.
  • Affordable housing finance — home loans to lower-income borrowers, run through a dedicated subsidiary (Capri Global Housing Finance), average ticket ~₹21 lakh. Grew ~43% in FY26; the housing arm is separately capitalised at a ~27.7% capital-adequacy ratio.
The shape of the whole thing

Picture CGCL as a lending platform with five taps and two commission desks. Four taps are its own money going out as secured loans (MSME, gold, construction, housing); the fifth is capital it on-lends or arranges through partners. The two desks — car loans and insurance — earn fees on volume routed to banks and insurers. What ties it together is a shared branch and customer base: a gold-loan branch can cross-sell an insurance policy; an MSME borrower can be offered a car loan through a partner bank. The strategy is to own the customer relationship and then monetise it in as many capital-efficient ways as possible.

03

The company and who controls it

A founder-built, promoter-controlled lender — with an old ticker and a new identity.

CGCL is very much a founder's company. It is controlled by Rajesh Sharma, a finance professional with roughly three decades in the industry, who built the group from a Mumbai-based wholesale financier into today's multi-product retail lender. The promoter group — Sharma and Capri Global Holdings Private Limited — holds ~59.9% of the equity, with zero shares pledged, an unusually clean and concentrated ownership structure for a listed NBFC.

Ownership & structure

  • Promoter & MD: Rajesh Sharma — founder, largest shareholder, and the strategic driver. Promoter holding ~59.9%, no pledge.
  • Group entities: the listed parent runs MSME, gold, construction finance and the distribution businesses; Capri Global Housing Finance is the separately-regulated affordable-housing subsidiary.
  • Balance sheet: well-capitalised — standalone capital adequacy ~25.8% (housing subsidiary ~27.7%), far above regulatory minimums, and modest leverage of ~3.3×, leaving ample room to grow the book without immediately raising equity.
  • Funding: a mix of bank loans and bonds, recently broadened with a maiden ~$500 million overseas dollar bond and a raised borrowing limit (₹35,000 Cr), aimed at lowering and diversifying the cost of funds.
Read the concentration both ways. A ~60% promoter stake with no pledge signals conviction and aligned incentives — the founder's wealth rides on the same shares an outside investor buys. It also means minority holders are, in practice, backing one person's judgement: capital allocation, the pace of the branch build-out, and the choice of which businesses to scale all run through Rajesh Sharma. That is a strength while the calls are right and a governance risk if they are not.
04

The gold-loan sprint: nothing to No. 1 in three years

The clearest evidence of how fast this company can build a business from scratch.

The single most striking operational fact about CGCL is the speed of its gold-loan build-out. Gold lending — cash advanced against a family's gold jewellery, secured by the metal locked in a branch vault — is one of India's oldest and safest lending products. CGCL entered it from a standing start in 2022. By March 2026 the gold book was ~₹16,965 crore, up ~111% in the year, the largest of its five businesses, run through a network that crossed 1,000 branches across 16 states. Muthoot and Manappuram took decades to build their gold franchises; CGCL built a top-tier-sized one in a handful of years.

Why gold was the right business to sprint into

Gold lending suited a company in a hurry for three reasons. It is secured by an asset already in hand, so credit losses are tiny (gold GNPA ~0.3%) even when you scale fast — the metal is in the vault from minute one. It needs no credit history, so a new branch can start lending to walk-in customers immediately rather than waiting years to build underwriting data. And it is short-tenure and high-yield, so a young book generates cash and seasons quickly. The catch is that gold lending is branch-heavy and price-sensitive: you need physical outlets with vaults and trained appraisers (hence the spending), and the collateral value rides on a gold price nobody controls. CGCL keeps a conservative buffer between loan and gold value, but a deep, sustained gold-price fall is the structural tail-risk of any gold lender.

05

The car-loan desk: a lender that lends none of its own money

The most unusual — and most misunderstood — part of CGCL, told in full.

Here is the part almost nobody expects from a company filed under "lender." CGCL runs one of India's larger bank-channel car-loan distribution businesses. It does not lend its own money for cars. Instead it finds the car buyer, does the underwriting paperwork, and routes the loan to a partner bank that actually funds it — and CGCL keeps a commission. In FY26 it arranged ~₹11,910 crore of car loans this way (+13% YoY), across 14 partner banks and financial institutions, from a pan-India network of hundreds of locations. Not a rupee of that ₹11,910 crore sits on CGCL's balance sheet, and CGCL bears none of the credit risk if a borrower defaults — that is the bank's problem.

Why this business is worth more than its size suggests

A rupee earned this way is worth more than a rupee of interest income, and here is the mechanism. Own-book lending consumes capital — regulators require an NBFC to hold shareholder equity against every loan it funds, which caps how fast it can grow before it must raise more equity (and dilute holders). Fee income consumes almost none: CGCL earns a commission on ₹11,910 crore of car loans while tying up virtually no capital against them, because the loans live on the banks' books. So the car-loan and insurance desks lift the return on equity of the whole company and let its franchise reach far beyond what its own balance sheet could fund. It is the closest thing an NBFC has to earning money for free — which is exactly why a "lender" bothers to run a brokerage at all.

The genuine surprise, stated plainly: most readers picture Capri as a gold-and-MSME lender. Yet in FY26 the volume it arranged for other banks in a single product — ₹11,910 Cr of car loans — was comparable in scale to its own hard-won gold book (~₹16,965 Cr), and it earned on that volume without lending a paisa. A distribution engine hiding inside a lender.
06

Co-lending: sharing the loan, keeping the growth

The other capital-light lever — and the regulatory change that briefly jammed it.

Between "lend all of it yourself" and "arrange it for a bank and keep none," there is a middle path CGCL leans on heavily: co-lending. In a co-lending arrangement, CGCL and a partner bank fund the same loan together — the bank puts up the larger share (typically ~80%), CGCL the rest — and CGCL usually keeps the customer relationship, services the loan, and earns a fee plus a spread on its slice. The borrower gets a single loan at a blended rate; CGCL grows its franchise while using far less of its own capital than if it funded the whole loan.

CGCL has co-lending tie-ups with public-sector banks including Central Bank of India and Bank of Maharashtra, used to extend its MSME and gold books deeper into the country than its own funding alone would allow. The bank gets priority-sector-friendly retail assets it struggles to originate itself; CGCL gets reach and fee income. It is a genuine win-win — and a core reason a mid-size lender can chase a ₹57,000 crore AUM target without a matching mountain of fresh equity.

The dependency, and the FY26 hiccup: co-lending's power is also its vulnerability. It relies on partner banks staying willing — their appetite, their priorities, and the rules governing the arrangement. In FY26 the RBI issued revised co-lending guidelines, and the transition temporarily slowed CGCL's co-lending disbursements while partners re-papered arrangements, with full resumption guided for the second quarter of FY27. A reminder that a chunk of CGCL's growth model sits at the mercy of both a regulator and a handful of bank treasuries it does not control.
07

The J-curve: paying today for tomorrow's book

Why a company growing 60% a year still isn't earning its target returns — and why that's the whole debate.

Fast growth flatters the top line and hides a cost: you spend the money to build a branch, hire the staff and open the book before those loans earn you anything. A gold branch opened in FY25 costs its rent, salaries and set-up from day one, but its loan book — and its profit — take a year or two to fill up and season. Multiply that across the hundreds of branches CGCL has opened, and you get a company whose returns are depressed precisely because it is investing ahead of its own growth. That is a J-curve: profitability dips as you invest, then bends sharply upward as the investment matures.

The non-transferable device — the two-rupee spread

Here is the way to hold CGCL's puzzle in one image. Consider the gap between its two headline return numbers. Its return on equity (RoAE) has already recovered to ~16.5% — a respectable figure that says the business is working. But its return on assets (RoA) still sits roughly a full percentage point below the 4–4.5% management is guiding to for FY28. That gap is the J-curve, quantified: the young branch cohorts and unseasoned gold and MSME loans are dragging asset returns down even as leverage and fee income prop equity returns up. The thesis on CGCL comes down to whether that RoA gap closes as the books season — while GNPA stays under 1%. If RoA climbs to 4%+ without credit costs spiking, the growth was worth the spend. If RoA stalls, or if GNPA rises as the fast-grown books age, the market paid a premium for growth that never converted into quality returns.

Name the metric that settles it: not AUM growth (that is already spectacular and largely in the price), but RoA reaching and holding ~4% while GNPA stays sub-1% as the FY24–26 branch cohorts season. Growth is the promise; RoA-with-clean-credit is the proof. Everything else in this primer is context for that one convergence.

How to read a ~2.6× book on a mid-size NBFC

For a lender, Price-to-Book is the honest yardstick, because it tells you what you pay for each rupee of the firm's own capital — and NBFC earnings swing too much with provisions to trust P/E alone. At ~2.6× book, CGCL trades well above a typical mid-size NBFC and much nearer the elite compounders. The bulls' arithmetic: a firm growing AUM ~60% with sub-1% bad loans and a rising RoE deserves a premium multiple, and if RoA bends to 4%+ the book value itself compounds fast enough to catch up with the price. The bears' arithmetic: ~2.6× book already assumes the J-curve completes cleanly, so the stock is paying today for a return profile CGCL has not yet delivered — and any wobble in credit cost, co-lending, or the gold price as the young books season would leave the multiple exposed. Same 2.6×, opposite conclusions; the referee, as §07 argued, is RoA-with-clean-credit.

A note on the returns numbers: different data providers quote CGCL's return ratios on different bases (trailing vs. reported, on-book assets vs. AUM), so you will see RoE figures ranging from the low-teens to ~16.5%. The company's own FY26 disclosure puts consolidated RoAE at ~16.5% (up from ~11.8%). Treat all point-in-time ratios here as approximate and confirm against the latest results and the shareholding pattern before relying on them.
09

The industry and the rivals

CGCL competes in four lending arenas at once — against a different set of specialists in each.

There is no single "CGCL competitor," because CGCL is really four lenders wearing one name. It faces the giant diversified NBFCs in retail secured lending, the pure-play small-business specialists in MSME, and the dedicated gold financiers in gold. Understanding who it is measured against in each ring explains both its opportunity and why its multiple invites scrutiny.

The competitive tension CGCL lives inside

Diversification is CGCL's defining feature and its strategic gamble. A focused rival — Five-Star in MSME, Muthoot in gold — pours everything into one product and tends to run higher returns and deeper expertise in that lane. CGCL spreads itself across four, betting that the diversification lowers cyclical risk (the books rarely sour together) and that a shared branch-and-customer base plus the fee desks make the whole worth more than the parts. The open question every competitor poses is whether a mid-size firm can be excellent in four lending products at once — or whether the specialists out-earn it in each, leaving CGCL a jack-of-all-trades priced like a master of one.

10

Risks: what could go wrong

A growth story carries growth's risks — here they are, without softening.

CGCL's strengths and risks are the same coin viewed from two sides. The speed that makes it exciting is exactly what makes its books young and unseasoned; the diversification that lowers cyclical risk also spreads management thin; the capital-light levers that lift returns also create dependencies on outside partners. None of these is a red flag today — the numbers are clean — but each is a place the story could crack.

  • Unseasoned books, untested through a downturn. A loan book growing 60% a year is dominated by new loans that have not lived through a full credit cycle. GNPA at ~0.9% looks pristine, but young books almost always do — the real test of the fast-grown gold and MSME loans comes when the economy, or the gold price, turns. Credit cost rising as the book ages is the most important thing to watch.
  • Returns below target — the J-curve might not complete. RoA still sits below the guided 4%+. If operating leverage stalls, or new-branch productivity disappoints, the premium multiple loses its justification (see §07–08).
  • Gold-price and gold-concentration risk. With gold heading toward half of AUM, a sharp, sustained gold-price fall would hit collateral values, slow disbursements and pressure the fastest-growing book — a concentration CGCL did not have three years ago.
  • Partner & funding dependence. Co-lending relies on partner banks staying willing (the FY26 RBI co-lending transition already caused a temporary slowdown); the car-loan and insurance fees rely on 14 banks and 20-odd insurers continuing to route through CGCL. As a non-deposit lender, it also lives on wholesale funding — a rate spike or rating slip would squeeze the spread.
  • MSME / small-borrower cyclicality. Small businesses and low-income housing borrowers are more sensitive to economic stress than large corporates; a downturn hits these books first.
  • Promoter concentration. ~60% in one promoter group means capital allocation and strategy rest heavily on a single decision-maker; minority holders are backing Rajesh Sharma's judgement as much as the franchise.
  • A rich multiple leaves little slack. At ~2.6× book for a firm still earning below its target RoA, the valuation already banks the good outcome — so a stumble on any of the above would likely hit the multiple as well as the earnings.
11

The future, and the bull/bear

A genuinely fast-growing, cleanly-run, diversified lender — priced as if the best case is already in hand.

CGCL enters its next chapter as one of the standout growth stories among mid-size Indian lenders: a five-product secured book compounding at ~60%, a gold franchise built at remarkable speed, two capital-light fee engines lifting returns, and a clean balance sheet with room to grow. Against that sits a valuation that already assumes the plan works and a set of books too young to have proven they will hold up when conditions turn.

The bull case

  • Rare growth — ~60% AUM growth with a guided path to ₹46,000 Cr (FY27) and ₹57,000 Cr (FY28); few lenders its size compound this fast.
  • Clean credit — GNPA ~0.9%, gold GNPA ~0.3%, a secured-only book that has stayed pristine through the sprint.
  • Capital-light levers — car-loan distribution (₹11,910 Cr arranged, zero credit risk), insurance fees and co-lending lift RoE and stretch the franchise.
  • The J-curve bending up — RoAE already recovered from ~11.8% to ~16.5% as branches season; RoA guided toward 4%+.
  • Aligned owner, strong capital — ~60% promoter stake with zero pledge, ~25.8% CRAR, low leverage, and freshly diversified funding.

The bear case

  • Priced for the good outcome — ~2.6× book while still earning below its target RoA; the premium banks a J-curve that hasn't fully completed.
  • Unseasoned books — 60%-a-year growth means most loans are new; the real asset-quality test comes only through a downturn.
  • Gold concentration — a business heading to ~50% of AUM inherits full exposure to a gold price at elevated levels.
  • Partner dependence — co-lending and fee income hinge on banks and insurers whose appetite (and RBI's rules) CGCL doesn't control; FY26 already saw a co-lending slowdown.
  • Jack-of-four-trades — focused specialists out-earn it in each product; can a mid-size firm be excellent in four lending lines at once?
The bottom line

CGCL is that unusual thing: a lender doing several things at once and, so far, doing them well — growing faster than almost any peer its size, keeping its bad loans under 1%, and layering fee income on top of a secured lending core. The reason it is not a simple buy is that the market already knows all of this and has priced it at ~2.6× book — a multiple that will only be vindicated if the young books season into their target returns rather than souring first. The next two years hand the company a specific test it has set itself: hit the FY27 markers of ₹46,000 crore of AUM and ₹1,300 crore of profit, and push return on assets toward the 4% it has promised for FY28 — all while gross NPAs stay under 1% as the fastest-grown book in its history, gold, matures. Clear that bar and a premium was cheap. Miss it — on credit cost or on the RoA convergence — and the premium was the story.

The gold desks and the branch signs went up fast. Whether the loans behind them are as good as they look is a question only the seasoning of the book — and the RoA line — can answer.
