Tips Music, own the songs, collect forever

A complete walk through one of India's largest music-IP companies — the label that owns roughly 30,000 songs and now, instead of pressing cassettes, simply licenses that back-catalogue to Spotify, YouTube, JioSaavn and the rest. How a music label actually earns money when nobody buys music any more, why a decades-old song can earn more this year than the year it released, and why almost all of the profit comes from a library that was paid for long ago.

00

The 30-second snapshot

What you're looking at, before we go deep.

Tips Music Limited is a music label and music-IP company — the owner of one of India's larger song catalogues, built by the Taurani family's Tips brand over four decades of Hindi film and regional music. It makes almost nothing physical. Its business is to own the master rights to songs and then license them to the platforms where India now listens: Spotify, YouTube, JioSaavn, Apple Music, Gaana, and the short-video and social apps (Instagram/Meta reels and their peers). Every time one of its ~30,000 songs is streamed, played on radio or TV, or used in a reel, Tips collects a royalty. It is, at heart, a toll-collector on a library it already paid for.

Three facts frame everything:

  • It is a royalty-annuity business, not a hit factory. Because Tips owns the recordings, an old song keeps paying every year with no fresh cost — and, remarkably, often pays more each year as more Indians stream and platforms charge more. The catalogue behaves like a bond that keeps raising its own coupon. New releases matter, but the ballast is the back-list.
  • The economics are extraordinary because the songs are sunk cost. The tenth-million stream of a 1990s Tips hit costs the company essentially nothing to serve — the recording was made and paid for decades ago. That is why an entity of modest size turns ~₹376 Cr of revenue into ~₹217 Cr of profit: an EBITDA margin around 73% and a net margin near 58%, with returns on capital that look almost implausible (RoCE well over 100%).
  • It is now a pure music company — the films business was hived off. The listed entity was Tips Industries until the film-production division was demerged into a separate listed company, Tips Films, in 2022; the remaining music-IP business was renamed Tips Music Limited in September 2024. What trades under TIPSMUSIC today is the pure catalogue-and-licensing company.
In plain terms

Picture a landlord who owns 30,000 apartments — except the apartments are songs, the tenants are streaming platforms, and the rent is a royalty paid every time somebody presses play. Tips built or bought those "apartments" over decades; the construction bill is long since paid. So most of what comes in each year drops through to profit, and the whole enterprise runs on a handful of people rather than a factory. The catch is that its tenants are a very small number of very powerful platforms — one of them, Spotify, pays over half the rent — and to keep the estate growing it must keep buying new "apartments" (fresh song rights) that may or may not fill up. But the core is unusually simple and unusually profitable: own the songs once, and collect for a very long time.

01

How a music label earns when nobody buys music

No cassettes, no CDs, no downloads — and yet more money than ever. Here is the mechanism.

To follow Tips you have to unlearn how the music business used to work. There was an era when a label made money by selling copies — cassettes, then CDs, then paid downloads. That era is over. Today almost no Indian buys a copy of a song. And yet the people who own songs are earning more than they did in the cassette age. The reason is a shift from selling copies to licensing access, and it rests on one legal fact.

The one thing that matters: who owns the master

When a song is made, two separate rights are created. The composition (the tune and lyrics, owned by writers/publishers) and the sound recording — the actual recorded track, known as the master. A label like Tips typically owns the master rights to the songs in its catalogue. Owning the master is the whole game: it means that anyone who wants to play that specific recording — a streaming app, a radio station, a film, a brand's ad, a person making a reel — must pay the owner. Tips does not need to sell you anything; it simply owns the recording and collects whenever it is used.

The only music-rights terms you need
TermWhat it means (and why it matters here)

Sync licensingA one-off fee to "synchronise" a song to picture — in a film, an ad, a web series, a game. Higher-value, lumpier, and a way to monetise the catalogue beyond audio streaming.
Public performanceRoyalties when a song is played in a "public" setting — radio, TV, events, restaurants — usually collected through licensing bodies. Part of the non-digital slice.

Why the streaming era is better for owners than the sale era ever was

In the cassette age, a label earned once — when you bought the tape — and then never again, no matter how many times you played it. In the streaming age, the label earns every single time the song is played, by everyone, forever. A hit that would have sold you one cassette in 1996 now earns a sliver of a rupee every time any of hundreds of millions of listeners streams it, decade after decade. The same catalogue that was a one-time sale has become a recurring toll. That inversion — from a single sale to a perpetual per-play royalty — is why owning masters went from a fading business to one of the most attractive annuities in Indian media.

Where the money physically comes from

  • Audio streaming platforms — Spotify, JioSaavn, Apple Music, Gaana, Amazon Music and the like. They pay Tips a share of their subscription and ad revenue, allocated by how much its songs are streamed. This is the largest bucket.
  • YouTube & video/social — official song and video views on YouTube, plus usage of Tips' audio in short-form video and reels on Meta's platforms and others. Monetised through ad-share and content-ID systems that detect and pay for the use of owned recordings.
  • Sync & brand licensing — songs placed into films, ads, series and games for a negotiated fee; a way to earn from the catalogue outside the streaming pipe.
  • Public performance & non-digital — radio, television, live events and "brand publishing," typically routed through collection societies. Steadier, lower-growth, and roughly a third of the mix.
02

The catalogue annuity: own it once, collect for decades

The single most important idea in the investment case — and it is specific to a music-IP owner.

Most people value an entertainment company by its latest hit. For a catalogue owner that is exactly the wrong lens. Tips is not primarily a bet on this year's releases; it is a bet on a library that keeps earning without being remade. The right way to hold the company in your head is as a portfolio of thousands of tiny, perpetual royalty streams — an annuity whose coupon actually rises over time as more Indians stream and platforms monetise them better.

The cost sits only in the first box, and only once. Every box after it is pure collection — which is why the catalogue behaves like an annuity that quietly raises its own payout.

Here is the non-transferable maths of this specific business. A Tips song recorded in, say, the 1990s cost whatever it cost to make back then — and that money is long gone. This year, that same recording required zero fresh spending, yet it earned a stream of royalties that is very possibly larger than in any year of its life, because the number of Indians streaming music and the revenue each platform earns per listener have both climbed. The song did not change. The world around it monetised it better. Do that across ~30,000 songs and you have a library whose aggregate earnings grind upward almost regardless of what is released this quarter.

The device that only fits a catalogue owner: value = catalogue × monetisation × new adds

Forget "did they have a hit?" The number that runs Tips Music is (size and quality of the catalogue) × (how well each stream is monetised) × (how much fresh catalogue they add). The first term is the ~30,000-song back-list, already paid for. The second term is India's streaming boom doing the work — rising listeners, rising subscriptions, rising ad rates — lifting the earnings of songs that never change. The third term is the only one that costs money: buying new hits to keep the library growing. A music-IP compounder is precisely a company where the first two terms carry the business and the third is the discretionary bet. Get comfortable with that decomposition and the rest of this primer is detail.

03

What Tips owns and sells: the library and the revenue mix

A deep Hindi-film-and-regional catalogue, monetised mostly through digital pipes.

Tips' asset is its catalogue — roughly 30,000 songs accumulated over four decades, drawing on the music of around 40 Hindi films the group made plus a wide sweep of regional-language and non-film music. Its revenue is what that catalogue earns across a handful of channels, and the mix has swung decisively toward digital.

The two halves of revenue

  • Digital (~70% of revenue) — audio streaming, YouTube and video, and social/short-form usage. This is the growth engine, expanding at double-digit rates as India's streaming base and platform monetisation rise. Within it, audio streaming platforms dominate, with YouTube a large second pipe.
  • Non-digital (~30% of revenue) — public performance (radio, TV, events), sync/brand licensing and "brand publishing." Steadier and lower-growth than digital, but a real cash contributor and a diversifier away from pure streaming.

The audience reach behind the numbers

The scale of the pipe shows up in Tips' owned channels. Its cumulative YouTube subscriber base runs into the tens of millions across channels — the flagship channel alone counted well over a hundred million subscribers — a reminder that a music label today is also, in effect, a large video publisher. Those subscribers are not the product being sold; they are the meter that turns catalogue ownership into a steady stream of ad and streaming royalties.

Why the digital tilt is the quality of the earnings, not just the growth

The shift toward digital does two things at once. It grows revenue, because streaming in India is still early and expanding fast. And it improves the character of that revenue, because streaming royalties are recurring, granular and tied to a structural boom rather than to any single hit or release calendar. A label that earned lumpily from cassette drops and film soundtracks now earns a smoother, compounding flow from a library on tap. The ~30% non-digital slice adds ballast. The combination is what lets Tips grow at a healthy clip while keeping margins that most media businesses can only envy.

04

The company, the family, and the great split

A Taurani-family label that separated its films from its music — and became a pure catalogue.

Tips has been a Taurani-family enterprise since the cassette era, when the Tips label built a reputation for popular Hindi film and pop music. For most of its listed life it was Tips Industries, a company with two quite different activities under one roof: producing and distributing films, and owning and exploiting music rights. The single most important corporate event in its recent history was the decision to pull those two apart.

Why the split is the reason the stock is what it is

Films and music are opposite businesses wearing the same industry label. Film production is lumpy, hit-driven and capital-hungry — a few big bets a year, any of which can flop. Music-IP is the annuity described in section 02: steady, high-margin, compounding. Bolted together inside old Tips Industries, the annuity's quality was obscured by the films' volatility. Separating them let the market see — and price — the music catalogue for what it is: a rare, asset-light, extremely high-margin royalty machine. What you are analysing when you look at TIPSMUSIC is that clean, de-risked music company, with the film-production risk moved next door into Tips Films.

Ownership & control

  • Promoters: the Taurani family retain control, holding roughly 64% of the company as of mid-2026. Notably, the promoter stake has been reduced from around 75% in FY23 — the family has trimmed its holding as the stock re-rated, which is worth watching as a signal.
  • Institutions: foreign institutional investors have built a position (around ~8% by mid-2026), reflecting growing interest in the listed music-IP theme.
  • Balance sheet: essentially debt-free — an asset-light IP owner with little need for borrowed capital, generating cash it can return through dividends and, prospectively, buybacks.
Two governance points to hold: first, this is a family-controlled company, so capital allocation — how much is spent on new music, how much returned to shareholders, how related entities like Tips Films are dealt with — sits largely with the promoters; the buyback under consideration and the steady dividend are encouraging, but minorities should watch that discipline holds. Second, the promoter sell-down from ~75% to ~64% is a double-edged fact: it improves free float and liquidity, but insiders reducing into a re-rating is always a data point, not a non-event.
05

The streaming tailwind: why the coupon keeps rising

The structural force that turns an old catalogue into a growing one.

A catalogue owner does not need to do anything new for its earnings to grow, provided the world around the catalogue monetises it better each year. In India, that is exactly what is happening. The country is in the early-to-middle innings of a music-streaming boom, and Tips is a direct, leveraged owner of the content that boom consumes. This is the tailwind that makes a static library a growth asset.

What is actually driving it

  • Hundreds of millions of listeners, cheaply connected. Cheap mobile data and smartphones put streaming in reach of a vast population that never bought a CD. The number of people who can stream a Tips song has exploded and is still rising.
  • Rising monetisation per listener. The early Indian streaming market was overwhelmingly free/ad-supported and lightly monetised. Over time, paid subscriptions, higher ad rates and better rights enforcement lift the revenue each stream generates — the per-play coupon climbs. At Tips, paid-subscription-linked revenue is a growing slice of digital, compounding at a faster clip than the whole.
  • New surfaces for music. Short-form video and reels turned every social feed into a place where owned recordings get used — and, through content-ID systems, paid for. A song can now earn from a viral reel it was never "released" for.
  • Better rights enforcement. As platforms mature and piracy is squeezed, more of the value of a play actually reaches the owner. Formalisation of the market is itself a tailwind for whoever owns the masters.
The catalogue doesn't grow. The market's ability to pay for it does — and that is enough.
Why Tips is a leveraged way to own the boom

You could buy the streaming boom by owning a platform — but platforms fight for subscribers, spend heavily on technology and marketing, and often struggle for profit. Tips offers a different exposure: it owns the content the platforms compete to carry, and it collects a share of their revenue without bearing their customer-acquisition or infrastructure costs. When streaming grows, Tips' royalties grow; when platforms raise prices, the master owner's cut rises too. It is a way to be long the boom while sitting on the high-margin side of the value chain — the side that owns the songs rather than the side that rents the customers.

The dependency runs the other way too, and it is important not to romanticise it: Tips' growth is derived from platform growth and platform economics. If Indian streaming monetisation stalls, or the platforms squeeze the rates they pay owners, the coupon stops rising. The tailwind is real and structural, but it is not Tips' to control — a theme we return to in section 07.

06

New music: the only real "capex," and the only real product risk

How a catalogue grows — and how the money spent growing it can fail to pay.

If the back-list is an annuity that runs itself, the one active, money-spending decision Tips makes is acquiring new music — buying or producing the rights to fresh songs and film soundtracks to keep the catalogue enlarging. This is the company's version of capital expenditure, and it is the place where genuine risk lives. Everything else is collection; this is the bet.

How new-music acquisition works

Tips adds to the catalogue in two broad ways: producing music itself (commissioning songs and albums, and acquiring the music rights to new films) and buying existing rights outright. Either way it pays up front — sometimes substantially — to own the new masters, which then join the library and, if they land, begin their own decades-long royalty life. The company has been stepping this spending up: in recent quarters content cost has grown far faster than revenue as management front-loads investment in new releases. In Q1 FY27, for instance, content spend jumped roughly 90% year-on-year even as revenue grew ~21%, as Tips released dozens of new film and non-film songs.

The honest tension at the centre of the model

New-music spending is both the growth engine and the risk. Spend well — acquire songs that stream for years — and each rupee of content cost seeds a fresh, perpetual royalty stream that widens the annuity. Spend badly — overpay for songs that never catch — and the money is simply gone, because unlike the back-list, a new release carries real hit-or-miss uncertainty. The near-term arithmetic makes this visible in the P&L: a quarter of heavy front-loaded content spend can depress reported profit even though it is meant to build future earnings. So the investor's job is to judge not how much Tips spends on new music, but whether that spending is earning its keep — turning into durable streaming revenue a few years out.

This is also why the model is not a pure "do-nothing" annuity. A catalogue that is never replenished slowly ages: tastes move on, and while old hits keep paying, the freshest, fastest-growing streams come from recent music. Tips must keep feeding the library to stay relevant to the platforms and to younger listeners — which means it must keep taking the one risk the back-list is free of. The skill of the business, beyond simply owning great old songs, is buying the right new ones at the right price.

The metric to watch: the ratio of new digital/licensing revenue growth to content spend over a couple of years. Rising content cost is fine — welcome, even — if it converts into a widening royalty base. If spending accelerates while digital revenue growth does not, the new-music bets are not paying, and the market's willingness to fund a high multiple will fade. This is the number that separates a compounding catalogue from an expensive treadmill.

07

The platform question: one tenant pays over half the rent

The genuine dependency a first-timer never sees — and the royalty-rate risk beneath it.

Here is the fact that surprises most people who look at Tips for the first time: for all its 30,000 songs and hundreds of millions of listeners, its revenue leans heavily on a very small number of platforms — and one of them, Spotify, accounts for roughly 55% of streaming revenue. A business that looks pleasingly diversified across a huge catalogue is, on the demand side, concentrated on a handful of powerful counterparties who set the terms of the deal.

Why does this matter so much? Because the master owner does not set the royalty rate — the platform does, within the bounds of its licensing deals. Tips owns the songs, but the price per stream, the terms of renewal, and the pace at which a platform grows its Indian subscriber base are decisions made in the platform's boardroom, not Tips'. If a dominant platform renegotiates rates downward, changes its payout model, or simply grows more slowly than hoped, a large share of Tips' revenue is exposed to that single decision.

The counter-intuitive truth to sit with

The strength of a catalogue business — owning irreplaceable content — coexists with a real weakness on the other side of the table. A platform cannot easily replace Tips' specific hit songs, which gives Tips negotiating power. But Tips also cannot easily replace the platforms that reach India's listeners, which gives them power. The result is a mutual dependency in which a handful of global streaming companies are, collectively, Tips' customer base — and one of them alone pays the majority of the streaming bill. Record profits and a concentrated customer list can be true at the same time. The health of the business is therefore never captured by the catalogue's quality alone; you must also ask how the platform relationships and their royalty rates are trending.

Why this reframes the company: Tips carries a concentrated counterparty exposure that a typical consumer business does not. The upside is that its main partners are large, growing and improving their monetisation — which is exactly why the coupon has been rising. The risk is that the timing, the rate and the growth of that payment are set by a few powerful platforms whose interests are not identical to Tips'. Read the platform mix and the direction of per-stream realisation together; they tell the story the catalogue size hides.
08

The rivals: Saregama, T-Series, and the scale gap

A small club of catalogue owners — Tips is the high-margin specialist, not the biggest library.

Indian recorded music is a consolidated field, because the thing that matters — owning a deep back-catalogue — cannot be built quickly or cheaply. A newcomer cannot simply create fifty years of hit songs. That scarcity protects the incumbents, of which Tips is one of a small handful. But Tips is neither the largest nor the most diversified; it is the most focused and highest-margin.

The competitive shape to hold in mind

Two things stand out. First, competition here is not a price war for listeners — it is a contest for ownership of catalogue and for the best terms with platforms. The incumbents' moat is their irreplaceable back-lists; the live battleground is bidding for new music, where deep-pocketed global majors can raise the price of fresh rights. Second, Tips has deliberately chosen focus over sprawl: where Saregama diversified into devices and events, Tips stayed a near-pure licensing business — which is why its margins are dramatically higher on a fraction of the revenue. Whether that focus is a virtue or a fragility depends on how you weigh purity against diversification, and on how the platform relationships in section 07 evolve.

09

Why the margins are almost implausibly high

A company that makes nothing physical earns ~73% EBITDA margins — here is exactly why.

The single most striking number about Tips is not its growth; it is its profitability. An EBITDA margin around 73%, a net margin near 58%, and a return on capital employed comfortably above 100% are figures you almost never see in a media company — or in most companies at all. This is not an accounting quirk. It is the direct, mechanical consequence of the annuity model, and it is worth understanding precisely.

The three reasons the margin is so wide

  • The catalogue is a sunk cost. The recordings were paid for years or decades ago. Serving one more stream of an existing song costs Tips essentially nothing — there is no raw material, no factory, no unit cost that scales with volume. Revenue from the back-list is very close to pure gross profit.
  • The cost base is tiny and fixed. A catalogue-and-licensing business runs on a modest team managing rights, platform relationships and new-music deals — not thousands of workers or a plant. Costs barely rise as royalty revenue grows, so incremental revenue drops almost straight to the bottom line: enormous operating leverage.
  • Almost no capital is tied up. Because it owns intangibles, not machines, Tips needs very little balance-sheet capital to operate. With minimal capital employed and large profit, the return on that capital is arithmetically huge — hence a RoCE that looks almost like a typo. It is real, and it is the signature of an asset-light IP owner.

The genuine surprise worth pausing on

A company can earn a higher net margin than most software firms — while owning no software, no factory and no shops — purely by owning old songs. It sounds like it cannot be right, and it is one of the least intuitive facts in Indian equities: the most profitable slice of the music value chain is not the platform that reaches millions of customers, nor the artist who performs, but the quiet entity that owns the master recording and collects a toll on every play. Tips is a clean example of that inversion. The value did not accrue to whoever has the users; it accrued to whoever has the rights.

Two cautions keep this from being a fairy tale. First, these margins are a reason the stock is expensive, not a free lunch — the market has noticed, and prices the company accordingly (section 10). Second, the margin can be diluted deliberately, and healthily, by heavy new-music spending (section 06): a year of aggressive content investment will show lower margins even as it builds the future annuity. Judge the margin alongside what it is being traded for — growth in the catalogue and the digital base.

Financials & valuation

Fast growth and extraordinary returns — priced richly, so the question is durability of the compounding.

FY26 was a record year: revenue up ~21% to ~₹376 Cr, profit up ~30% to ~₹217 Cr, with margins expanding as digital grew. The multi-year picture is a clean compounding curve — revenue and profit have both roughly tripled over four years. Unlike a cheap cyclical, Tips is not priced as an afterthought: at around 38–39× earnings, the market already rewards the annuity and the growth. The debate here is not whether it is cheap; it is whether the compounding is durable enough to justify a premium multiple.

The quality metrics are unusual across the board: a return on equity in the 90s (percent), a return on capital employed above 100%, a healthy dividend payout (around three-quarters of earnings) and a debt-free balance sheet. This is what a genuine annuity looks like on a spreadsheet — high, cash-generative, capital-light. It is precisely because those numbers are so good that the shares command ~38–39× earnings. A premium rating is the price of admission to a business like this; the risk is entirely in whether it is sustained.

The one number that settles the thesis: digital licensing revenue growth, read against content spend and per-stream realisation

At ~38–39× earnings, Tips needs to keep compounding to justify its price — a stumble in growth, not just a bad quarter of margin, is what would hurt. So the metric that proves or breaks the case is digital/licensing revenue growth, tracked against two companions: the content spend feeding it (is heavy new-music investment converting into durable streaming revenue, per section 06?) and the direction of per-stream realisation (are platforms, led by Spotify, raising what they pay owners, per section 07?). If digital revenue keeps growing at a healthy clip while content bets pay off and platform monetisation rises, the premium multiple is earned and earnings compound into it. If digital growth decelerates while content spend keeps climbing — or a dominant platform trims rates — the multiple has a long way to compress. Catalogue times monetisation, minus what the new music costs to acquire: that is the scoreboard.

11

The future, the risks, and the bull/bear

A rare, asset-light royalty machine on a rich multiple — asked whether the compounding lasts.

Tips Music enters FY27 in enviable shape: a debt-free balance sheet, ~73% margins, a ~30,000-song annuity, a structural streaming tailwind, and enough cash to both invest in new music and return capital (a buyback is under board consideration). What no catalogue owner can promise is the two things that decide the next decade — that India's streaming monetisation keeps rising, and that its new-music bets keep paying. That is the frame for both cases.

The bull case

  • A compounding annuity — a paid-for catalogue whose royalties rise each year as India's streaming base and monetisation grow, with essentially no incremental cost to serve.
  • Extraordinary economics — ~73% EBITDA margin, >100% RoCE, debt-free; incremental revenue drops almost straight to profit on a near-fixed cost base.
  • Early-innings tailwind — Indian music streaming is still under-monetised versus the West; rising subscriptions and ad rates lift the coupon on a static library.
  • Focus & capital return — a pure licensing model (unlike Saregama's hardware/events), a healthy dividend, and a prospective buyback signalling confidence and shareholder focus.
  • Scarce, irreplaceable asset — decades of hit catalogue cannot be recreated; platforms must license it, giving Tips durable relevance.

The bear case

  • Platform concentration & rate risk — one platform (Spotify) pays over half of streaming revenue; royalty rates and terms are set by a few powerful counterparties, not by Tips.
  • New-music / hit risk — the fast-rising content spend can fail to pay back; overpaying for songs that never catch is where money is actually lost.
  • Premium valuation — ~38–39× earnings leaves the case dependent on continued fast compounding; a growth stumble, not merely a soft quarter, would hurt.
  • Smaller library than rivals — Saregama and T-Series own deeper catalogues and outbid for new rights; scale in acquisition matters.
  • Family control & promoter sell-down — capital allocation sits with the Tauranis, whose stake has fallen from ~75% to ~64%; related-party dynamics with Tips Films bear watching.
The bottom line

Tips Music is one of the cleanest expressions in Indian equities of a simple, powerful idea: own the songs once, and collect for decades while the market around them learns to pay more. The economics are genuinely rare — an asset-light catalogue throwing off cash at margins most businesses cannot approach — and the streaming tailwind behind them is structural, not a fad. But the same purity that makes the model beautiful also concentrates its dependencies: a handful of platforms, led by one, decide the rate; and a rising content bill has to keep buying hits that earn their keep. The next test is concrete and datable — whether digital licensing revenue keeps compounding at a healthy clip through FY27 as content spend steps up and platform monetisation rises, and whether the promised buyback confirms the family's confidence in the cash the catalogue throws off. Keep the annuity compounding and the premium multiple looks deserved; let growth stall or a platform trim its rate, and a 38× stock has a great deal of room to re-rate downward.
